I have been working through the conceivability argument against physicalism, and I want to check my understanding of where it leads practically. As I reconstruct it, the argument shows that a philosophical zombie — a being physically and functionally identical to a conscious human but lacking phenomenal experience — is coherently conceivable, and that this conceivability establishes the metaphysical possibility of such beings, which in turn shows that consciousness is something over and above the physical facts.

Given that conclusion, it seems to me the argument points toward a straightforward empirical research program that philosophers have oddly neglected. If consciousness really is a further fact beyond the physical, then a sufficiently advanced whole-brain emulation — a software or synthetic duplicate reproducing every neuron and synapse of a human brain — should in principle instantiate the physical facts without the additional phenomenal facts, making it an actual zombie. Such a duplicate would presumably behave indistinguishably from a conscious person while inwardly being "dark," and sustained interrogation, or perhaps future refinements of neuroimaging and comparison against genuine human brains, should eventually reveal the deficit: some subtle failure of self-report, integration, or responsiveness that betrays the absence of experience.

So my questions are: Why haven't philosophers of mind and consciousness researchers treated the construction of such a duplicate as the decisive experimental test of the conceivability argument's conclusion? And once we have built one, what specific measurements or behavioral probes would let us determine whether the system is a genuine zombie or actually conscious — and wouldn't a persistent failure to find any difference itself count as evidence against dualism?
