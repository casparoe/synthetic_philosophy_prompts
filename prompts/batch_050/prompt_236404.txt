So here's the situation. I retired last spring after 35-odd years as an experimental condensed matter physicist, and to keep my brain from ossifying I've been sitting in on a history-of-philosophy seminar at the local university. We just finished a stretch on Hobbes, and while everyone else wanted to talk about the Leviathan stuff, I got completely fixated on his geometry sideline -- the decades he spent insisting he could square the circle, the endless pamphlet brawls with Wallis, the way he thought algebra was basically cheating and that real geometry had to be grounded in bodies and motion. As someone who has both written and recieved vicious referee reports, I find the whole episode weirdly touching and hilarious.

Here's what I want: a parody referee report, maybe 600-700 words, as if Hobbes's latest circle-squaring manuscript had been submitted to a modern mathematics journal and landed on the desk of an extremely thorough anonymous reviewer. Format it like a real report -- summary of the manuscript, recommendation (presumably reject, but the reviewer should agonize a bit), then numbered major and minor comments. The humor has to come from the actual record: the reviewer's complaints should track the real criticisms people made of Hobbes, and the manuscript (as described) should display the real Hobbesian tics -- the dismissal of symbolic methods, the insistence that geometry is about magnitudes generated by motion, the unshakeable confidence, and that thing where every refutation just produced a slightly tweaked new construction rather than a moment of self-doubt. Someone who knows the Hobbes-Wallis dispute should be able to tell I know it too; someone who doesn't should still find it funny as a portrait of a certain kind of author we've all encountered. Keep it deadpan -- the reviewer never breaks character, never winks, and is scrupulously professional even while dismantling the thing.

Two more requests. First, commit to a definite reading of Hobbes in the piece rather than hedging: I want it to portray him as sincere and philosophically motivated -- his materialism genuinely drove the geometry -- and not just as a vain crank. If you think that reading is wrong, pick whatever you think is right, but pick something and let it shape the jokes. Second, after the report, add a short note (150 words max, out of character) telling me where the piece exaggerated or leaned on a contested reading of the history, so I don't embarrass myself repeating satire as fact at the seminar.
