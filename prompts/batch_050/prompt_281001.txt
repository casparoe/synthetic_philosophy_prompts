I was at a small meetup of people from an online philosophy Discord last weekend -- mostly we argue about AI risk, but two members, Priya and Marcus, fell into a sustained fight about origin essentialism that outlasted everything else on the agenda, and I left genuinely unsure who had won. The trigger was Kripke's discussion in Naming and Necessity: he argues there, in effect, that a table made from a wholly different block of wood would not be this table, and that a person originating from entirely different gametes would not be this person, even if the later biography were stitched to match. Priya defended the thesis; Marcus attacked it. Their exchange went roughly four rounds. Round one: Priya said Kripke's point is that origins are intrinsic to identity in a way later accidents are not -- you could have become a carpenter instead of a lawyer, but the person produced by that other zygote at that other conception simply isn't you, whatever he resembles. Marcus replied that this makes personal identity hostage to facts nobody can ordinarily verify -- mix-ups at fertility clinics, disputed paternity -- and that it licenses wild skepticism about who anyone is; besides, ordinary speech treats "I could have been born to different parents, in another country" as plainly possible. Round two: Priya said epistemic fallibility doesn't touch metaphysics, names are rigid designators, and the ordinary sentence is loose talk or picks out a mere counterpart. Marcus then produced what he called his trump card: monozygotic twins come from one sperm and one egg, so origins demonstrably do not determine which person results -- and if the same origin can yield two different people, why insist a different origin couldn't yield the same person? He added that "could I have been my twin?" seems like a real question origin essentialism answers by fiat. Priya answered that necessity doesn't require sufficiency -- the thesis says only that you can't come from a wholly different origin, not that your origin singles you out -- and both twins share the origin, so no counterexample has been produced. Round three: Marcus pressed a vagueness argument. Same egg, different sperm? Same sperm, one meiotic division earlier? A zygote differing by a single chromosome? Either the identity-conferring boundary is precise, which is incredible, or vague, which he said is a bad look for a metaphysical necessity; he also suggested counterpart theory dissolves the whole thing by treating modal talk about persons as context-sensitive similarity talk. Round four: Priya said heaps are vague but real, vagueness is everywhere and not fatal, and counterpart theory changes the subject rather than answering the question -- it makes "I could have died in childhood" literally false of me, which costs more than Kripke's intuition does. At that point the host made everyone go home.

Neither of them would concede anything, and I deliberately haven't told you which side I found more persuasive, because what I want is an adjudication of the arguments as actually made, not a referendum on the conclusion. I'd also like to know whether the dispute is really metaphysical or partly semantic in disguise, and what each side would need to concede to make progress.
