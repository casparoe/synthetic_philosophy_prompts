Two colleagues of mine in a philosophy of physics reading group got into a long argument about what Noether's theorems do and do not explain, and they asked me to referee. I could not settle it, so I am reproducing the exchange as faithfully as I can and asking you to adjudicate it instead.

The dispute began over energy conservation in a closed mechanical system.

(1) Ana: Time-translation symmetry explains why energy is conserved. Noether's first theorem is precisely the result that derives the conserved quantity from the symmetry, so the symmetry is the reason the conservation law holds.

(2) Bruno: The theorem proves a conditional -- given a Lagrangian dynamics with such-and-such variational symmetry, here is a quantity constant along solutions. A mathematical equivalence cannot explain either of its relata by deriving the other, and the derivation runs both ways: from the conservation law one can recover the symmetry. What does the explaining is the particular Lagrangian, that is, the laws themselves. The symmetry is at best a redescription of them.

(3) Ana: Even if derivability runs both ways, explanatory priority need not track derivability. Symmetries constrain which laws are possible: demanding local gauge invariance fixes the form of the electromagnetic interaction and forces the existence of a force carrier. That is a symmetry doing explanatory work on laws, not the reverse.

(4) Bruno: Two mistakes there. First, a constraint is not an explanation, and the laws constrain the symmetries in exactly the same sense, since a transformation only counts as a symmetry relative to a given dynamics. Second, your best case is actually your worst: gauge symmetries are the one kind Noether's first theorem does not apply to -- local symmetries fall under her second theorem, which yields identities holding off-shell rather than genuine conservation laws. If the paradigm explanatory symmetry produces no conserved quantity, the symmetry-to-conservation explanatory story has things backwards.

(5) Ana: Then account for spontaneous symmetry breaking. The electroweak laws are gauge symmetric but the vacuum is not, and this fact -- that the symmetry lives in the laws rather than in the solutions -- explains how gauge bosons can acquire mass while the underlying symmetry is preserved. You need the symmetry to be prior to the solutions to state this at all.

(6) Bruno: That example cuts against you. What does the physical work is the structure of the solutions: the degenerate vacuum manifold and the selection of a ground state. The symmetry is a feature of the formalism that the world systematically fails to instantiate, and calling something the explainer of phenomena in which it does not itself appear is exactly the overreach I object to.

They agree on the mathematics and disagree only about what it explains. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six moves above, say whether it is clearly right, defensible but not decisive, or clearly mistaken, with a brief justification in each case. Then identify what, if anything, both parties are missing -- in particular, whether either has offered an adequate account of what explanation by symmetry would have to consist in for the dispute to be resolvable -- and say who is more right overall. State how confident you are in your overall assessment and single out the specific adjudication you are least sure of. End with a final line of the form: Verdict: <one sentence naming who is more right overall and on which central point>.
