I am a professor of philosophy working in formal epistemology, and I am drafting a sabbatical application to my university's internal research fund — one term of leave plus a modest budget for a small workshop. The committee includes scholars from outside philosophy, so the proposal has to be intelligible to non-specialists without talking down to the philosophers on it, and I would like help producing a strong draft.

The project grows out of an exchange in my graduate seminar on the pragmatics of credence. We were working through the classical Dutch book argument for probabilism — the result that an agent whose credences violate the probability axioms will accept, at her own fair prices, a set of bets on which she is guaranteed a net loss — when a student asked why the bookie always gets to be the adversary. Pressing that question turned up what I now think is an underappreciated symmetry: the very same incoherence that allows a bookie to assemble a guaranteed-loss package also allows the assembly of a guaranteed-gain package from bets the agent would accept, depending only on which side of each bet she takes at her fair prices. (For instance, credences in a proposition and its negation that sum to less than one make her willing to buy bets on both sides for a sure profit; summing to more than one, she will sell both for a sure loss — but her willingness runs in both directions either way, since she stands ready to buy or sell at her own fair prices.) I am provisionally calling this the Windfall Problem. My working suspicion is that it is fatal to the traditional exploitability reading of Dutch book arguments — if incoherence were irrational because it makes one vulnerable to sure loss, the same incoherence could not equally make one the beneficiary of a sure gain — and that such arguments must instead be read as diagnostic: the book is a witness to an internal defect in the agent's evaluations, not a practical threat. But I do not yet know whether the symmetry holds for the full theorem rather than toy cases, nor what exactly the diagnostic reading should identify as the defect (inconsistent evaluation of equivalent prospects? a structural flaw in the credence function that is independent of any outcome?), nor how the whole issue stands to the now-standard non-pragmatic, accuracy-based defenses of probabilism, which abandon betting talk altogether and may either dissolve my problem or inherit a version of it.

Please write the proposal, roughly one page — about 700 words — with a working title. It should state the central question precisely; explain why the two dominant ways of defending probabilism, the pragmatic reading of Dutch books and the accuracy-dominance approach, leave this question open rather than already settling it; lay out the method, which combines formal work (the symmetry conjecture needs a proof or a counterexample) with conceptual analysis of what normative conclusion a Dutch book can actually support, and argue for that method against the obvious alternatives, such as empirical study of actual betting behavior or simply extending the accuracy programme; describe what a positive result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and keep the scope honest for a single term of leave, including the planned workshop. Refer to positions and arguments by description rather than by citation — I will supply the references myself — and do not include a bibliography.
