Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about the Four–Seven debate in Joseon-dynasty Korea, and I came away unsure how much of what he told me to trust. I had read a little about the debate beforehand; he claims to have studied it seriously, and he summarized it for me in roughly the following terms:

"Yi Hwang (Toegye) and Ki Daeseung (Gobong) spent years exchanging letters in sixteenth-century Korea over how to classify two groups of feelings within Zhu Xi's Neo-Confucian framework of li (principle) and qi (material force). The Four Beginnings -- the sprouts of compassion, shame, deference, and approval-and-disapproval that Mencius identifies with the four virtues -- and the Seven Emotions of joy, anger, sorrow, fear, love, hate, and desire. Toegye's innovation was to say that the Four issue directly from li, which makes them purely good, whereas the Seven issue from qi and are morally neutral, capable of going either way. Gobong pushed back that both groups arise through qi, with the Four merely being the Seven viewed under a specific moral description. What the debate really came down to, though, was whether human emotions are trustworthy: Toegye thought our spontaneous feelings are basically reliable guides to the moral pattern, and Gobong thought they need constant discipline and suspicion. The debate settled in Toegye's favor, which is why his elevation of li over qi became the orthodox Korean position."

I have a few suspicions already. I thought I remembered that li, for Zhu Xi, is not the kind of thing that acts or generates activity on its own, which would make talk of li "issuing" anything more subtle than my friend let on. I also thought the Four were sprouts of the virtues rather than the virtues themselves, and that even Toegye admitted the Four could be corrupted if one's qi was unbalanced, which would undercut calling them purely good. But I may be garbling things, and my friend was confident throughout.

Could you go through his summary and audit it? For each claim, tell me which parts a scholar of the debate would accept, which are defensible simplifications, and which misrepresent either Toegye's or Gobong's actual position -- including any place where he attributes to both sides something only one of them held. Where my suspicions above are right, confirm and explain; where they are wrong, correct me. Then tell me whether his concluding characterization of what the debate was "really about" survives once the misrepresentations are fixed, and how a supporter of Gobong would object to that framing even if every factual claim in the summary were accurate.
