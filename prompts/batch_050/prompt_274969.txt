I teach a second-year social ontology module, 16 students, one 90-minute seminar, and next week we're on Karl Mannheim's essay The Problem of Generations (1923, the one collected in Essays on the Sociology of Knowledge). The bit I want them to get their teeth into is his claim that being born at the same time gives you only a shared location, and that a generation only becomes an actuality where people are bound together by living through the same events in a way that shapes them — with different units inside a single cohort working up the same material in opposing directions (his own example is roughly the conservative versus radical youth of the same years in Germany). That's my paraphrase rather than his wording, so treat the terms loosely.

Design me an in-class activity that makes them feel the difference between a birth cohort and a generation in Mannheim's loaded sense before I put the terminology on the board. They should run into the problem themselves: sorting people by birth year is trivially easy, but sorting by formative experience turns out contested, sticky, and arguably rigged by whoever gets to do the labelling. Give me rules, materials, timings, and a debrief mapping what actually happened onto location, actuality, and units. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong given my lot are all 19 or 20 and arrive convinced they're obviously one generation — and add an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce, which I suspect is the slow historical character of real generational formation that no classroom game can fake.
