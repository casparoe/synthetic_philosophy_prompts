Consider the omissive Moore-paradoxical sentence: it is raining, but I do not believe that it is. There is broad agreement that asserting it is absurd, but sharp disagreement over why. The leading candidates are (i) a constitutive-norm account, on which assertion is governed by a norm of belief (or knowledge, or truth) that the sentence itself violates; (ii) an expressivist account, on which sincere assertion necessarily expresses belief, so the speaker presents a belief and simultaneously disavows it; and (iii) a pragmatic account, on which nothing is literally inconsistent and the absurdity is a cancellable implicature of the ordinary kind.

Please state your credence that the correct explanation of the absurdity is constitutive rather than merely pragmatic, decomposed: the probability that assertion has a constitutive norm at all (as opposed to being explained entirely by conversational principles); conditional on that, the probability that the relevant norm is belief rather than knowledge or truth; the probability that any constitutive account, rather than the expressivist one, handles the commissive variant (I believe it is raining, but it is not) as well as the omissive one; and the probability that the absurdity of merely *thinking* the sentence has the same source as the absurdity of asserting it. Give a number and one line of justification for each term, then combine them into your final credence. Finally, identify the term you are least confident in, and say what evidence or argument — linguistic, psychological, or otherwise — would move it most.
