Before I commit to this in a chapter draft I want to know if my position on dispositions actually hangs together or if I'm papering over something. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Dispositions are intrinsic. Whether this glass is fragile is a fact about the glass itself: any perfect duplicate of it, in any surroundings whatsoever, is fragile iff it is. This is also why I think finks work the way they do -- Martin's electro-fink can only strip the wire of being live by changing the wire itself, cutting the current the very instant it's touched.

(2) The simple conditional analysis (fragile = would break if struck) is dead, killed by finks in both directions: a dead wire hooked to a device that would electrify it the moment it's touched would conduct if touched but isn't live, and a live wire hooked to a device that would cut the current on touch wouldn't conduct if touched but is live. I take Lewis's repaired version to be basically right: fragile means having some intrinsic basis B such that being struck while retaining B would cause breaking. And I want the analysis outright, not hedged with "in normal conditions" -- that phrase either means "conditions where it manifests," which is circular, or it's too vague to do any work.

(3) Masks are different from finks, and the disposition survives them. The glass swaddled in bubble wrap is still fragile even though striking it now gets you no breaking; the wrap blocks the manifestation without touching the disposition. Same deal with poison and antidote -- the poison is still deadly while the antidote neutralizes it in the bloodstream.

(4) Fragility is multiply realizable. The microphysical basis of fragility in the glass is nothing like the basis in a dry autumn leaf or a cracker, yet it's one and the same disposition in all three. So the disposition is not identical with any of its bases.

(5) Dispositions do causal-explanatory work. When the unwrapped glass breaks, part of the correct explanation of the breaking is that the glass was fragile -- not merely that its molecular bonds were arranged such-and-such.

(6) Disposition ascriptions are still fundamentally tied to conditionals -- to say what fragility IS you have to talk about breaking-upon-being-struck. I just deny you can convert that into a reductive analysis without residue.

So: audit me. Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do? I suspect someone's first move will be to say (1) and (3) are in tension, but I'm not so sure. Rank the real tensions by severity, say for each one what would have to give and what that costs, and if there's a single revision that buys the most consistency for the least damage, name it. I'd rather find the rot now than in referee reports.
