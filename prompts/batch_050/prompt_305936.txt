I keep running into the claim that the Kelly criterion is the "objectively rational" way to size bets, because maximizing log wealth maximizes your long-run geometric growth rate, and that this somehow justifies log utility without needing to assume risk aversion -- you just point out that time averages beat ensemble averages for a single repeated bettor. But I've also seen pushback (I think Samuelson was the famous skeptic?) that this is sleight of hand: maximizing growth rate isn't the same as maximizing what you should care about, and a Kelly bettor can still have a really bad time over any finite horizon.

Can you explain where exactly the disagreement sits? Specifically: what does the geometric-growth argument actually prove (and under what assumptions -- iid bets? infinite repetition? no consumption along the way?), what does it quietly smuggle in, and does the "ergodicity" framing genuinely add something beyond log utility or is it mostly repackaging? I'd like you to clearly separate the settled math from the contested interpretation, because both camps seem to talk as if their side is just a theorem and I suspect that isn't true for at least one of them.
