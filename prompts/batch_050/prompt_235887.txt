I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about corruption scandals, which keeps forcing me up against a view I've held for years without really stress-testing it. My position is roughly this: there's a universal moral line between public office and private interest, and practices like an official steering contracts to a cousin, taking gifts from people with business before their office, or trading public jobs for electoral support are corruption wherever they happen -- the local vocabularies for it (nepotism as "taking care of your own," gifts as "respect," patronage as "loyalty") are just euphemisms that rationalize the same underlying violation. Whenever I read the anthropological counter-literature, or talk to people who grew up in clientelistic systems, I get the pushback that I'm smuggling in a specifically post-Enlightenment, WEIRD-population notion of the impersonal state and treating its absence as a defect rather than a different moral ecology -- one where impartiality toward strangers can itself read as coldness or betrayal, and where the gift relationship has obligations and reciprocities my framework can't even see. I've always suspected this is motte-and-bailey stuff from people who don't have to live under the strongman's nephew, but I'm not confident.

So: what would count against my view? I want a map of its exposure, not a seminar. Which findings or cases would be genuinely decisive -- for instance, would it matter if it turned out that in some societies gift-mediated governance delivers roughly what impersonal bureaucracy delivers elsewhere, in predictability and welfare, or would I reply that it's still corruption even if the outcomes are fine, and is that reply a strength or a tell that my view has stopped risking anything? Would it matter if the "universal" impersonal standard turned out to be historically shallow even in the West, something like two centuries old and held inconsistently at that (think lobbying, revolving doors, campaign finance -- where exactly does my line put those, and can it put them anywhere without embarrassing me)? What do defenders of my position -- development economists, anti-corruption people -- themselves concede as the strongest counterevidence, as opposed to what the cultural-relativist critics say? And here's what would move me most, personally: a case where the locally-embedded practice and my universal standard give opposite verdicts, the local verdict is clearly the right one, and the difference isn't explainable as my standard being applied badly. If you think my view survives all this more or less intact, say that too -- a position that turns out to be well-insulated is worth knowing about, but I want to know whether the insulation is earned or just what happens when you define corruption as whatever violates a standard you refuse to put at risk.
