I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long feature about whether history has a discernible direction — whether, as the phrase goes, its arc bends toward justice. A historian I interviewed told me that most modern arguments about moral progress are, without realizing it, footnotes to an essay Immanuel Kant published in 1784, the Idea of a Universal History on a Cosmopolitical Plan. I've been reading it in Thomas De Quincey's 1824 translation, and I've gotten stuck on the Second Proposition, which strikes me as the load-bearing move of the whole essay. It runs as follows:

In Man, as the sole rational creature upon earth, those tendencies which have the use of his reason for their object are destined to obtain their perfect development in the species only, and not in the individual. Reason in a creature is a faculty for extending the rules and purposes of the exercise of all its powers far beyond natural instinct; and it is illimitable in its plans. It works, however, not instinctively, but tentatively, by means of practice, through progress and regress, in order to ascend gradually from one degree of illumination to another. On this account, either it would be necessary for each man to live an inordinate length of time in order to learn how to make a perfect use of his natural tendencies; or else, supposing the actual case that Nature has limited his term of life, she must then require an incalculable series of generations (each delivering its quota of knowledge to its immediate successor) in order to ripen the germs which she has laid in our species to that degree of development which corresponds with her final purpose. And the period of this mature development must exist at least in idea to Man as the object of his efforts: because otherwise his own natural predispositions must of necessity be regarded as objectless; and this would at once take away all practical principles, and would expose Nature, the wisdom of whose arrangements must in all other cases be assumed as a fundamental postulate, to the suspicion of capricious dealing in the case of Man only.

Three things puzzle me about this passage. First, its logical status: Kant seems to argue that history's goal must "exist at least in idea" because otherwise nature would stand accused of "capricious dealing" — but is that an empirical prediction about where history is heading, a methodological rule telling historians how to look at their material, or a providential faith presented as philosophy? Second, the ethical cost: earlier generations appear to be cast as rungs in a ladder only their descendants will climb, which seems to sit uneasily with Kant's own famous insistence that humanity must never be treated merely as a means — is this a real contradiction or only an apparent one? Third, a worry that matters for my article: if the development unfolds over "an incalculable series of generations," does the providential reading of history retain any testable content at all, or is it constructed so that no stretch of events, however bloody or regressive, could ever count against it?

Please respond as a scholar of Kant and of the philosophy of history writing for an intelligent lay reader. Think carefully, and before settling on your interpretation, briefly weigh the strongest alternative reading of the passage against your own. I do not need citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I need an explanation I can translate into plain, accurate prose for my readers. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — your one-sentence answer to what Kant is actually doing in this passage — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
