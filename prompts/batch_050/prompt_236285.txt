So me and my flatmate (still my friend, somehow) wasted basically an entire Sunday arguing about something that started ridiculously small: she forgot to water my fern while I was visiting my parents, the fern died, and when I half-jokingly said she'd killed it she went full philosopher on me. Her view: her not watering the fern didn't cause anything, because an omission isn't an event, it's an absence, an nothing — and nothings can't make things happen; what killed the fern was entirely positive stuff, cells drying out, whatever biologists say happens. My view: that's mad, of course the omission caused it — if she'd watered it, the fern would be alive, and that counterfactual is all causation ever is. Three hours in, we'd reinvented half the standard moves from scratch, including the bit where a gardener forgets to water the flowers and so does a passing tourist, yet everyone blames the gardener and nobody blames the tourist, even though the flowers' survival counterfactually depends on both of them.

We eventually agreed we're stuck not because we disagree about the fern but because we can't even formulate where we actually disagree. So: write the joint statement we'd produce if we ran this as a proper adversarial collaboration. I want (1) a numbered section of claims we can both sign — my guesses are that these include that causal talk in courts and everyday blame works perfectly fine with omissions, that bare counterfactual dependence can't be the whole story or the tourist becomes a cause too, and that "cause" might be doing different jobs in physics than in the kitchen — but push well beyond my guesses; (2) a table of the remaining disagreements, each side's position phrased in words that side would genuinely accept, no strawmen; (3) the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move one of us; and (4) for each crux, a case or argument we both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance, so neither of us can shift the goalposts afterwards.

As grist for (4), here's a case we built at 1am and couldn't crack: a village is protected by an automated tide-gate with two independent watchdog programmes, either of which can trigger the gate to close. During a storm, Watchdog A sends no signal because a developer disabled its alarm during an update six months earlier; Watchdog B sends none because a cleaner unplugged its sensor the previous night. The gate stays open, the village floods. My flatmate says no omission caused the flood; I say both omissions did, jointly or severally; and we both have a nagging feeling that the fact that the cleaner would probably get prosecuted while the developer merely gets fired tells us something about causation itself — or maybe only about norms, which is itself one of the things to sort out. If it turns out part of our fight is purely verbal, say exactly which part and why. Oh, and please write the whole thing in British English.
