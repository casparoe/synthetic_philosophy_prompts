I run internal training sessions at a consultancy, and my next workshop includes a twenty-five-minute segment on escalation of commitment for an audience of newly promoted project leads. I want to build the segment around the dollar auction. Everyone in the room completed a module on cognitive biases last quarter, so they will arrive confident that escalation is simply the sunk cost fallacy and that they personally are immune to it. I don't want you to write the explanation itself; I want a plan for it, covering the following.

First, the order in which the components have to be introduced, with the dependency reasons for that order. In particular: what must the audience already understand about the auction's payoff structure — above all that the runner-up also pays — before the phenomenon of locally rational escalation can even be stated, and where does the psychological layer belong relative to the strategic one? I believe the interesting point is that the trap works even on agents with no sunk-cost bias whatsoever, because the incentive structure itself shifts once two bidders are inside; the plan should make that point land before biases are ever mentioned. If you think I have this backwards, say so.

Second, the carrying example. I am torn between actually running the auction live in the room for small stakes and narrating a transcript of a past auction. Recommend one, given that the live version has a predictable failure mode: participants walk away having learned to avoid silly games from the outside, when the skill their jobs require is recognizing and exiting traps from the inside.

Third, the misconception map: the four or five misreadings this audience will predictably produce, in the order they will produce them — my guesses are that the bidders were simply irrational, that the first bidder is at fault, that the correct play is to open at a dollar or just under, and that the whole demonstration is the sunk cost fallacy in fancy dress — with the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact. If any of my guessed misconceptions is actually the right reading, tell me.

Fourth, one end-of-segment check for understanding: a modified auction I can hand them (only the winner pays, say, or the runner-up forfeits only half their bid) on which they must say whether the trap survives the modification and why — something that separates having understood the mechanism from having memorized the moral.

Fifth, what to leave out at this level while saying aloud that I'm leaving it out. I assume the formal equilibrium analysis belongs on that list; tell me if some piece of it is indispensable.

One constraint throughout: the transfer target is their real work — they sponsor multi-year projects and decide each quarter whether to keep funding them — so the plan must connect the auction's structure to that setting without pretending the two are identical, and should mark exactly where the analogy breaks.

If any premise of this request is confused, or the specifications cannot be met in twenty-five minutes, say so plainly and tell me what to cut rather than papering over the problem. Keep the whole plan to 700 words or fewer.
