So I've been chewing on the dark forest thing again and noticed something odd about how the SETI/METI community actually behaves, as opposed to what it says. The de facto practice seems to be: passive listening is completely uncontroversial, but anyone who wants to deliberately beam a message at a nearby star gets pushback as reckless, and the post-detection protocols floating around say we shouldn't reply to a detected signal without broad international consultation. Meanwhile nobody objects to planetary radar astronomy, which for decades has blasted signals orders of magnitude stronger than any METI message at specific targets, and nobody pretends our TV and military radar leakage isn't already announcing us to anyone within ~100 light years with a decent enough antenna.

What does the "listen but don't shout" norm actually presuppose, philosophically? It can't straightforwardly presuppose that silence keeps us hidden, since we aren't silent and haven't been for a century. Does it presuppose a dark-forest-style prior where hostile civilizations are rare but the downside is extinction, so even a tiny probability increase dominates the expected value calculation? Or is it smuggling in a distinction between deliberate messages and incidental noise that only makes sense if aliens respond to our intentions rather than our mere existence? Reconstruct the implicit decision theory, say whether the practice strictly needs it or is compatible with several rival views, and tell me whether it's even internally consistent — because if you actually bought the dark forest prior, wouldn't you ban the radar first and worry about METI later? Also curious what a practice built on a genuinely dark-forest prior would have to change.
