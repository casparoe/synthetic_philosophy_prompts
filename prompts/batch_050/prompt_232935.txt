At a philosophy department happy hour last week, I listened to two colleagues — a decision theorist and a moral philosopher — argue for nearly an hour about what rationality demands when two options are exactly equally good. The occasion was a hiring case: their search committee had interviewed two finalists judged, after exhaustive discussion, to be indistinguishable in expected contribution, and the committee had deadlocked for a week before someone proposed drawing lots. I report the exchange as a bystander and genuinely cannot tell who won. Here are the four central moves, as best I can reconstruct them:

(1) The decision theorist held that the week of deadlock was straightforwardly irrational: once the committee had concluded the options were tied in expected value, further deliberation had negative expected value, since it consumed time and goodwill while being incapable of producing a better outcome. Drawing lots immediately, he said, is the uniquely rational response to a genuine tie; treating arbitrary selection as a failure of rationality confuses the psychology of choosing with the logic of it, and the old Buridan's ass parable merely dramatizes a failure mode that a coin flip dissolves at once.

(2) The moral philosopher replied that this begs the question. A hiring decision, she said, is not just an outcome to be produced but an act performed for reasons, and a committee that picks by lot has no reason that applies to this candidate rather than that one. Something is lost when a selection between people is made arbitrarily: the selected candidate, were she to learn she had been chosen by a coin flip, would have a legitimate complaint that she had not been treated as an individual whose merits mattered. Randomization is an admission of failure, not a solution, and the discomfort people feel about tie cases tracks a real feature of agency rather than a confusion to be engineered away.

(3) The decision theorist conceded that randomization sometimes carries social costs but denied it could be intrinsically irrational, on the grounds that rationality concerns the selection of means to ends: if both means are equally good, selecting either is rational, and any costless mechanism that selects one is rational as well. Moreover, he argued, her position proves too much. If choices between near-equals require candidate-specific reasons, then since perfectly exact ties are rare, her view commits her to indefinitely prolonged deliberation in every near-tie, hunting for the tiny difference — and yet she, like everyone else, grabs the first acceptable toaster on the shelf rather than comparing them all.

(4) The moral philosopher answered that her view requires no such thing. In the consumer case a tie-breaking reason is readily available: minimizing search costs is itself a reason, so grabbing the first toaster is done for a reason, not arbitrarily. The hiring case is different because the affected parties are owed reasons grounded in their own merits rather than in the selector's convenience. Where such reasons are genuinely absent, she said, the honest response is not to pretend the coin flip itself confers a reason, but to acknowledge a limit of reason-giving and to be transparent with the candidates about it — a transparency the decision theorist's brisk lottery tends to elide.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible but open to challenge, and briefly justify your verdict. Then say who, on balance, has the better of the dispute about the original hiring case, and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing. In your answer, please distinguish carefully between points on which there is broad agreement among philosophers who work on rational choice, agency, and the ethics of lotteries, and points that remain genuinely contested; do not present a contested position as the received view.
