I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a courtroom drama, and I've hit a problem I don't have a word for and it's driving me a bit nuts.

The setup: a doctor does something that saves her patient and, in the process, foreseeably causes another man's death (the plot's messier than that but that's the skeleton). My defense attorney character argues the death was a side effect, not the point of what she did, and so it's a different moral animal from killing him on purpose. Fine. That distinction is obviously real — everyone feels the difference between aiming at a harm and accepting one.

But here's what keeps happening across drafts. Whether that argument sounds compelling or completely unhinged depends entirely on how finely I describe the action. Coarse description: she rerouted the hospital's limited oxygen supply knowing the man in bed six would suffocate. Sounds monstrous, sounds like his death is part of her plan. Fine description: she adjusted a valve, the valve changed pressure distribution, the pressure change altered flow, an unfortunate downstream consequence of the altered flow... and now it reads like the death happened to her plan rather than through it. Nothing physical changed between the two tellings. Just the grain of the narration. And I've realized my villain can exploit this in-story: he narrates his own acts to himself in absurdly thin slices to keep his conscience quiet — I'm not poisoning him, I'm moving a glass from one shelf to another; whatever the liquid does after that is chemistry. Take it to the limit and it seems like with enough clever slicing nobody ever intends any harm ever, which can't be right, or the distinction everyone feels is just a spell cast by word choice.

I gather there's an old principle in this exact neighborhood, something about harms-you-aim-at versus harms-you-merely-foresee, and trolley stuff is floating around nearby, though my case isn't really a dilemma — nobody's picking between two body counts, that's not what's doing the work here. My questions: does this weakness — the principle being only as solid as whatever stops you redescribing the act thinner and thinner — have a name? Is the version I'm dealing with, where it's the narrator or the reader doing the re-slicing rather than the agent rationalizing to himself, the same problem or a seperate one? And is my villain's trick a genuine loophole that people take seriously, or is there a standard way of slamming it shut that I can hand to my prosecutor so she doesn't sound naive? If there are two or three rival accounts of where the slicing has to stop and why, I want those, because right now the smartest character in my book is the murderer and that's a structural problem.
