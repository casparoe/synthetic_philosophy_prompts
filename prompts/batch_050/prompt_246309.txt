I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, and my dissertation defends a position I will call the interventionist thesis about wild animal suffering. Roughly: (1) suffering in the wild is vast in scale and morally urgent, given that the overwhelming majority of sentient animals live in nature and most live short lives ended by predation, starvation, disease, or exposure; (2) we therefore have strong pro tanto reasons to intervene in natural processes to reduce that suffering whenever effective means exist or can be developed; (3) the burden of proof falls on those who oppose intervention, and appeals to the value of nature, naturalness, or species integrity do not by themselves defeat these reasons; and (4) consequently, conservation priorities and public policy should be substantially reoriented toward individual welfare rather than ecosystem or species preservation.

I am writing a chapter that anticipates objections, and I do not want the usual strawmen. I want a map of what this position actually risks: what would have to be true for it to be wrong, and how exposed it really is. Specifically, I would like you to:

Distinguish empirical defeaters from theoretical ones. On the empirical side, consider, for example, what follows if most wild animals turn out to have net-positive lives, if cross-species welfare measurement proves too unreliable ever to act on responsibly, or if large-scale interventions predictably backfire given ecological complexity. On the theoretical side, consider whether the thesis has consequences in population ethics that I cannot accept, whether it commits me to an imperative to re-engineer or replace ecosystems wholesale (and whether that is a reductio or a bullet I should bite), and whether the strongest versions of the naturalness and relational objections merely complicate the view or actually defeat it.

Identify evidence that arguably already counts against the thesis — well-known cases where well-intentioned interventions plausibly worsened welfare — and assess how much weight it should carry against a position that explicitly conditions intervention on effectiveness.

Say what the thesis predicts that its rivals do not, so that its defenders and opponents are not just talking past one another.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each, say whether a sophisticated proponent of the thesis would concede it as counting against the view or dismiss it as answerable from within.

Finally, assess honestly whether the position has been qualified into practical unfalsifiability — intervention is always deferred to a future when our science is better — and whether that matters for a thesis that is part normative claim and part empirical bet. For what it is worth, I would personally find it most damaging if you could show that no intervention regime can be reliably expected to be net-positive, or that the scale premise collapses once the sentience of the most numerous wild animals is properly scrutinized.
