I write a column on science and big ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a piece on weird theories of rational choice. I want to get one section right in my own head first, so here's what I need explained properly, at a level that respects the actual mechanics rather than the pop-science version.

Superrationality (the Hofstadter idea) says roughly: when you're up against another agent who reasons like you, choose on the assumption that you'll both reach the same conclusion, because anyone doing the same reasoning as you will land where you land. This famously yields cooperation in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, where standard causal decision theory defects. I get the sales pitch. What I *don't* have a clean grip on is the load-bearing move: by what right does my choice count as information about theirs? Nothing I do causes their decision, and yet the superrational agent seems to reason as if choosing to cooperate somehow drags the twin along. Is that just evidential decision theory in a trench coat -- my choice as *evidence* for a shared deliberative output -- or is it a genuinely different thing? And if it's evidence, why does it survive where EDT normally gets into trouble?

Here's where I really want the explanation to dig in, because this is where I'd guess the idea strains. First: in anti-coordination games like chicken, "whatever we both do, we do the same" is a disaster -- both swerve or neither does. So the symmetry constraint can't be the whole story; something has to break symmetry between identically-reasoning agents. What does the superrationalist actually say here, and is it principled or ad hoc? Second: the move has a vaguely Kantian flavour -- act as you'd will all relevantly similar agents to act -- so is superrationality ultimately a *moralised* decision rule rather than a purely prudential one, and if so, does it still count as a solution to the dilemma or just a refusal to play it?

Please explain this in American English (house style, sorry), aimed at a sharp reader who knows what the prisoner's dilemma and Newcomb's problem are but has never thought about whether rationality could be indexed to a *type* of agent rather than an individual. Also: end by telling me what would change your verdict -- which consideration, if it held up, would make you say superrationality is just a confusion rather than a live option, and where in your own explanation you think the reasoning is most likely to give way.
