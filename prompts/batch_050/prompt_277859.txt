I'm a staff software engineer, and I own my company's incident-review process. We've had a "blameless postmortem" culture for years: after an outage we reconstruct what happened and fix systems, not people. I've defended this for a long time, but a fight with a colleague last month broke my confidence in it. A senior engineer ignored a documented deprecation, skipped the review gate, and took production down for six hours; when I said the postmortem would, as usual, assign no individual blame, my colleague replied that blamelessness had become a superstition -- that there's a real difference between a junior engineer who was never trained on a runbook and a senior one who decided the runbook didn't apply to him, and that our process is willfully blind to the difference. She pointed me to P. F. Strawson's essay Freedom and Resentment, which I've now read twice and think I mostly understand: Strawson separates cases where a bad act didn't express ill will at all (he didn't know, he was pushed, it was an accident) from cases where we stop treating someone as a participant in adult moral life altogether (he's a child, he's ill, he's been under extraordinary strain), and he insists that our practices of resentment, indignation, and blame do not await a verdict from the free-will debate. I have no formal philosophy background, so I want help turning that framework into an instrument my team can actually run.

Rebuild our incident-review rubric as a decision tree that an on-call reviewer can walk through during a postmortem, with the following requirements. First, every branch node must be a question answerable from the incident record alone -- timelines, chat logs, what training the engineer received, what warnings the tooling emitted, what the team had agreed to -- never a question that requires knowing Strawson's terminology, and never a metaphysical question. In particular, do not include any node asking whether the engineer's choices were "really free"; the tree's silence on that point should itself embody Strawson's claim that responsibility practices don't wait on metaphysics, and I'd like one short paragraph explaining why that omission is faithful to him rather than evasive. Second, the leaves must be verdicts with teeth: not merely "excused" or "exempted," but what the postmortem then owes -- a training fix, a tooling fix, a frank conversation about the engineer's regard for his colleagues, a change to how the team distributes on-call load. Distinguish at least: full blame within the participant stance; excuse, where the act didn't express the quality of will we assumed; exemption, where we temporarily or chronically treat the person's conduct as something to manage rather than answer to; and at least one leaf marking cases where Strawson's framework itself yields no verdict. Third, somewhere on the tree you must land the case that started this: the senior engineer who "should have known," whose ignorance looks self-manufactured. Say whether Strawson's quality-of-will criterion condemns him, excuses him, or exposes a gap in the framework. Fourth, the person running a postmortem is rarely the person whose night was ruined; note where, if anywhere, the tree should treat a bystander's verdict differently from a victim's, given Strawson's distinction between attitudes on one's own behalf and attitudes on others'. Fifth, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, and diagnose whether that's a flaw in your tree or a genuine blur in the framework; likewise, if the space of possible answers contains a combination that seems to land nowhere -- a verdict nobody in this debate would defend -- say so and say why it's empty.

Finally, run three invented two-sentence incident vignettes through the finished tree to show it working: include at least one where the blameless instinct gets the answer wrong, and at least one where the instinct to blame gets the answer wrong. Keep the whole thing under a length I could paste into our internal wiki.
