For the past several years I have belonged to a small decision-theory reading group, and its most opinionated member — I will call her Mara — has over time given me a long series of verdicts on Newcomblike problems. Next month I intend to put to her a case we have never discussed, and I would like to go in with a well-founded prediction of her answer rather than a guess.

Her recorded verdicts, with the reasoning she offered at the time:

1. Standard Newcomb's problem, predictor correct in 999 of 1000 past trials: one-box, without hesitation.
2. The smoking lesion problem: smoke. Since the correlation runs through a common cause rather than through the act of smoking, abstaining in order to manage the evidence would be, in her view, a category error.
3. Parfit's hitchhiker: pay the driver the $100 on arrival in the city, even though by then nothing depends on it.
4. Counterfactual mugging: pay, provided the coin flip and the predictor's counterfactual reasoning are credibly established.
5. One-shot prisoner's dilemma against an exact copy of herself instantiated in a simulation: cooperate.
6. One-shot prisoner's dilemma against a stranger with whom she shares no source code, no enforcement mechanism, and no channel of influence: defect. She insists she is no universal cooperator; what moves her is prediction and logical dependence, not the bare hope that the other party resembles her.
7. Having promised a friend $50 conditional on losing a bet, with no witnesses and no future dealings at stake: she would pay, but she describes this as ordinary integrity rather than anything decision-theoretically interesting, and concedes that a pure payoff-maximizer would not pay.

The new case is Kavka's toxin puzzle, in this precise form. An eccentric billionaire offers her $10 million on the following terms: a perfectly reliable scanner will read her brain at midnight tonight, and the money is wired immediately if, and only if, she then sincerely intends to drink a certain toxin at noon tomorrow. The toxin induces one day of intense misery and no lasting harm. Whether she actually drinks is neither scanned nor enforced; there are no penalties for abstaining, no observers, no future interactions, and nothing further turns on whether she drinks. Assume she believes all of this completely.

Please give me the following:

(a) Your best prediction of her verdicts on the three sub-questions: whether she would attempt to form the intention at all; whether she would, or by her own lights should, actually drink at noon; and whether she thinks a fully reflective agent could collect the money without drinking.

(b) Which rule she is implicitly running across the whole track record — updateless policy selection, resolute commitment-keeping, cooperation only where a prediction or scan mediates the payoff, or some fourth rule I have not named. For each candidate you consider live, show how it accommodates verdicts 1 through 7 and what it predicts about the toxin case, and state honestly where the track record underdetermines the choice among them.

(c) The single further case — a variant of the toxin puzzle, or a new Newcomblike problem — that would most sharply tell the surviving rules apart, with each rule's prediction about that case stated explicitly.
