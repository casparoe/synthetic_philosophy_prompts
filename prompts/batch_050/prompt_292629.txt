Before I let myself be persuaded by any further arguments about evolutionary debunking, moral progress, or the authority of intuitions, I want to know what I should be asking of a method for telling genuine moral preferences apart from mere biases. Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of the preference–bias distinction in morality must deliver, with the case or consideration motivating each requirement, and flag which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which look like the prejudices of one camp.

To fix the purpose: the account should be usable by an individual moral agent trying to audit her own convictions, not merely by a psychologist classifying other people from the outside.

The list should address at least the following tensions, and you may add more:

- Robustness versus responsiveness. A genuine preference should presumably survive reflection and new information, but an account that makes survival the sole criterion cannot distinguish a principled conviction from a prejudice too rigid to update. Say what the right middle condition would look like.
- The symmetry problem. Disgust at incest and horror at cruelty may have equally disreputable causal histories; most people want to keep the second and discard the first. State what a theory needs to deliver in order to draw that line without simply ratifying current opinion.
- Intelligibility to the subject. Must a bias be one the agent could in principle come to see as a bias from the inside, or may the correct verdict be available only to a third party with a causal theory of her mind? Take a position.
- Consistency with the possibility of moral learning. The criterion must not be so demanding that it rules out changing one's mind for good reasons, nor so lax that every confident feeling counts as a preference.
- Neutrality across moral theories. Assess whether the distinction can be drawn without presupposing the truth of consequentialism, deontology, or sentimentalism, and say whether full neutrality is achievable or a mirage.

Then, briefly, score three contenders against your list: a coherence-based reflective equilibrium approach, a causal-genealogical approach that classifies convictions by how they were formed, and an idealised-counterpart approach that asks what the agent would endorse under full information and vivid imagination. If you suspect some pair of desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, say so explicitly and explain which you would sacrifice; I regard that as the most valuable part of the answer.
