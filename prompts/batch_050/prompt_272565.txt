For a reading group I've been asked to write down where I stand on predictive processing so the others can poke at it. Roughly:

1. Perception is controlled hallucination: what I consciously see is the brain's best guess at the causes of its sensory input, and the guess is doing the real work.
2. There's no deep perception/action divide: acting is just the brain making its proprioceptive predictions come true, so motor control is prediction fulfilment, not commands.
3. The dark room problem is a pseudo-problem. An agent minimizing prediction error won't curl up in a dark corner, because being alive and embodied means it carries deep expectations (about blood sugar, novelty, social contact, whatever) that the dark room violates, so plain old error minimization already explains exploration and curiosity.
4. Attention is nothing over and above precision-weighting of prediction errors.
5. For all that, I'm still a direct realist about ordinary objects: I see the coffee mug itself, not a mental model of a mug.
6. I like the free energy principle partly because it seems almost like a theorem -- anything that keeps on existing as the kind of thing it is must be minimizing free energy -- and I also think it gives a substantive, testable account of why some brains end up psychotic or autistic and others don't.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which just look like they are. For each real tension, say how severe it is, what the cheapest fix would be, and what that fix costs. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Don't just flag everything -- I want to know what actually has to give.
