A colleague and I have been arguing about a streaming series that depicts a real, recently deceased novelist as having plagiarized her most famous book -- a claim the showrunner admits, in interviews, is invented for dramatic effect. I find the series repugnant; my colleague thinks my reaction is squeamishness dressed up as ethics, and he has written out the following argument to prove it.

(1) An act can wrong a person only if it makes that person worse off than they would otherwise have been -- worse off in their experiences, their health, their opportunities, their projects, or something else that matters to them.

(2) A dead person cannot be made worse off in any of these respects. There is no longer a subject whose experiences could be degraded, whose plans could be frustrated, or whose welfare could decline. Nothing that happens after a death reaches the person who died.

(3) Therefore no act performed after a person's death can wrong that person.

(4) If portraying a real person in a biopic or historical drama could wrong anyone, the primary candidate for the wronged party is the person portrayed. Other affected parties -- surviving family, misled viewers -- have at most incidental grievances, since the family is wronged only insofar as the subject is, and viewers of fiction are on notice that they are watching a dramatization rather than a documentary.

(5) So a filmmaker owes no duty of accuracy, fairness, or respect to the real dead person being portrayed. As far as the subject is concerned, a scrupulously researched portrayal and a malicious fabrication are morally on a par.

(6) An act that wrongs no one and produces the ordinary goods of storytelling -- entertainment, emotional insight, artistic achievement -- is permissible.

(7) Therefore the streaming series is permissible, and indeed any dramatization of any dead person, however false and however cruel, is permissible provided it makes for good television.

I cannot accept (7). It seems to license a smear campaign against anyone, the moment they die, so long as it entertains. But when I press on the premises, each one looks like something I already believe. I especially want to keep (2) -- I do think the dead cannot suffer, and I don't want to commit to anything spooky about a persisting subject who can be harmed from beyond the grave. And (1) seems like a platitude about what wronging someone even is. I suspect the problem is somewhere in the middle of the argument, but I can't pin it down.
