Two of my coworkers keep relitigating an argument about Stoicism and I got dragged in as referee. Background: Dan got passed over for a promotion he really wanted and now keeps saying he's "using the dichotomy of control" about it. Priya thinks he's doing it wrong, or that the whole framework doesn't survive scrutiny. Their exchange went roughly like this:

(1) Dan: Some things are up to us, some aren't. Whether I got promoted was never up to me -- the committee, office politics, the budget. What was up to me was my work and my reaction. So I'm not upset, and not being upset is simply the correct response.

(2) Priya: That dichotomy is just false as a description. Almost nothing falls cleanly in either bucket. You partially controlled your work, you even partially influenced the committee -- you could have schmoozed more, or made your results more visible. Once you admit partial control everywhere, "focus only on what you control" stops giving you any real guidance about where to put your effort.

(3) Dan: The Stoics knew that. Their own example was the archer, who controls everything about the shot but not the wind. The point isn't carving the world into two zones, it's where you hang your goal: the archer's job is to shoot well, not to hit. I did everything to deserve the promotion, that's the win, hitting the target was never mine to promise.

(4) Priya: That's the part I don't buy. Either you actually wanted the promotion, in which case "I only ever wanted to shoot well" is sour grapes in toga cosplay -- or you genuinely only wanted to do your best, in which case you're some kind of motivational alien and your advice is useless to normal humans. There's no stable spot between lying to yourself and not caring.

(5) Dan: There is, and it's the whole doctrine of preferred indifferents. Health, money, promotions -- these are genuinely indifferent to whether your life goes well in the deep sense, but you still pursue them with full effort, because pursuing them well is what virtue looks like. Preferring isn't needing. I can go after the promotion the way I'd go after a sunny day: try for it, enjoy it if it comes, and my life isn't wrecked if it rains.

(6) Priya: Even granting that, your "up to us" bucket keeps shrinking. You say your judgments and reactions are up to you, but you were visibly gutted on Friday and couldn't just choose not to be. Tiredness, habit, mood -- your inner life is hostage to fortune almost as much as the committee is. So the dichotomy shrinks to "bare assent in the next five seconds is up to you," and you can't build a whole ethics on that.

(7) Dan: "Up to us" never meant "up to your willpower at this exact instant." It means the kind of thing that yields to practice over time. My reaction on Friday wasn't up to me; my reactions a year from now are, because I can train them. That's still a real asymmetry with the committee, which no amount of practice will ever put under my control.

Then they both turned to me and I said something cowardly like "you've each got a point." Settle it properly: go through all seven moves, say who's right or wrong on each and why, and tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing.
