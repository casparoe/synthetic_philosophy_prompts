Last year I agreed, rather naively, to write a history of my family's firm for its centenary, and the project has quietly turned me into a sceptic about historical objectivity. Working through the archive — payroll ledgers, a founder's self-serving correspondence, minutes that clearly record decisions after they were taken, employees' recollections that shift with each telling — I found that I could not even begin without deciding what mattered, and every such decision seemed to be mine rather than the archive's. Reading Carr and then Hayden White gave my unease a doctrinal form: the past as it happened is gone; what the historian produces is a construction in which selection is already interpretation and narrative form does argumentative work that no amount of archival diligence can neutralize. I have come to accept this position, and I want to know what would count against it.

I ask because of a specific passage I keep circling back to. In the preface to Leopold von Ranke's Geschichten der romanischen und germanischen Völker (1824), immediately after the endlessly quoted remark that history merely wants to show how it essentially was, Ranke adds a sentence that receives far less attention — to the effect that the strict presentation of the event, however conditioned and unbeautiful it may be, is without doubt the supreme law, and that only afterward does the comprehension of the unity and development of the whole come into question. (I paraphrase the German rather than quote it.) What strikes me is the concession packed into the sentence: Ranke admits in advance that strict presentation will be constrained, partial, and aesthetically unsatisfying, and insists it is binding anyway, in a way that the later grasp of larger unities apparently is not. My sceptical reading says this concession swallows the claim — a presentation that is conditioned all the way down by selection cannot be strict in any sense that constrains the historian, and the supreme law is unenforceable. But I recognize that I may be reading the concession more strongly than Ranke intended, and that his distinction between a binding discipline at the level of the particular and a free construction at the level of the whole might survive my objection entirely.

So: what would have to be true for my position to be wrong? Please map its exposure honestly, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Which observations, cases, or arguments would count decisively against the Carr–White picture — and which would merely embarrass it? Are there historical works or episodes of inquiry (my archive included) that a thoroughgoing constructionist cannot plausibly describe without smuggling in the very distinction between constraint and invention that the view denies? What would Carr's or White's own defenders concede as counting against them — I suspect White would treat some defeaters as resting on a confusion between chronicle and history, and I want that move stated precisely and assessed, not just gestured at — and which criticisms would they dismiss as missing the point? Does the position make any risky claim at all, or has it been qualified, between strong and weak readings, into something no archival practice could falsify — and if so, is that a defect in a view of this kind or just the shape of any thesis about the conditions of a discipline? Finally, bring it back to my case: if strict presentation of the event is achievable as a discipline even where the whole is constructed, what would that discipline look like concretely for a firm history built from compromised sources, and what test, applied to my own finished chapters, would actually discriminate between the two positions? I should say that I would find a worked example — a case where the constructionist reading demonstrably fails to capture what a careful historian did — more persuasive than any argument about the concept of objectivity in the abstract.
