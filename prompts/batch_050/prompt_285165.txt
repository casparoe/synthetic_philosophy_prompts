1. Death is bad for the person who dies (when it is bad) because it deprives them of goods they would otherwise have had — more life, more experiences, more projects finished. It's not bad because dying hurts, it's bad because of what you miss out on.

2. I think the Epicureans had at least this much right: for something to be bad for a person, it has to be bad for them at some time at which they actually exist. Harms need a subject who exists at the time the harm holds.

3. The asymmetry between prenatal and post-mortem nonexistence is fine and rational. I dread dying early but I feel basically nothing about not having been born in 1650 instead, and I don't think that's a bias I need to correct. Lucretius can say what he wants.

4. Case verdict: someone who dies painlessly in their sleep tonight, totally unexpectedly, with no dependents and no fear beforehand — that death is still bad for them. So a death can be bad for someone even if they never experience anything bad and never know it's coming.

5. On the other hand, I don't think anything that happens after you're dead can be bad for you. If someone destroys my life's work or trashes my reputation after I die, that's unfortunate for the world maybe, but nothing bad happens to *me*, because there is no me anymore.

Which of these are in genuine tension and which only look like they are?
