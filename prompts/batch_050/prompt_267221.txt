I'm making a flowchart for a biology-and-society course I'm TAing, to go with a unit where students read a pop-science piece claiming their genome is a blueprint/code/recipe (pick your metaphor) and I want them to figure out what they actually think before they learn what anyone else thinks. So: a 'where do you land on the DNA-as-information question?' decision tree, 6 to 8 branch nodes, for undergrads who've had intro bio and zero philosophy.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names. The nodes should be questions someone can answer without knowing any labels, and wherever possible they should be concrete cases rather than abstract principles. Some cases I'd like used somewhere in the tree (pick whichever do real diagnostic work, you don't have to force all of them in): prions, which inherit stuff with no nucleic acid involved; a stretch of sedimentary rock whose layers correlate with past climate, which some people would call information about the past; a virus that hijacks the cell's machinery to copy itself; a mutation that changes which protein gets made, where 'misread' or 'mistake' seems like a natural thing to say; and epigenetic marks that get passed down a few generations without any sequence change. The point of these is to smoke out things like whether the student thinks 'information' is just correlation, whether they think DNA is special compared to other causes of the trait or just one cause among many, and whether they think talk of 'mistakes' in DNA is literal or loose.

The leaves should include at least: the view that code/information talk about DNA is a useful metaphor and nothing more; the view that genes carry information in the bare statistical sense of correlating with traits (and that rocks, smoke, tree rings etc. carry information the same way); the view that what's real is that genes are causally specific causes of traits in a way other causes aren't; the view that DNA has genuinely semantic content, grounded in what the sequence was selected for; and the full deflationary position that there's no privileged informational role for genes at all because development is a system with lots of equally information-y inputs. If your tree ends up needing an extra leaf or merging two of mine, fine, but say so and why.

Two more things. First, note any combination of answers nobody seems to occupy, and give a one-line guess why (incoherent? unattractive? just unexplored?). Second, flag any pair of leaves your questions can't actually separate — I'd rather know that than have a fake-clean tree. And please phrase every node so a student who's never heard the words 'teleosemantics' or 'Shannon' can answer it honestly.
