So here's the position I've settled on after a philosophy of science reading group, and I want it stress-tested: Hume's problem of induction is genuinely unsolvable, and the no-free-lunch theorems are basically the proof of that. My reasoning: Wolpert's results show that, averaged over all possible target functions, every learning algorithm performs exactly as well as every other — random guessing included — so any attempt to justify induction has to smuggle in a prior assumption about which environments are the likely ones, which is precisely the move Hume said can't be made non-circularly. Induction is a local habit that our corner of the universe happens to reward (so far), not a rationally privileged method. Full stop.

What would count against this view? I want a map of its exposure, not another explanation of the theorems themselves. Concretely:

- Which ingredients of the NFL framework are doing the sceptical work, and what would things have to look like for each to fail? Take the uniform averaging over all possible environments: is that itself a substantive assumption, a mere modelling choice, or simply what "assuming nothing" looks like? And if it is an assumption, does that cut against my position or actually entrench it?
- Potential defeaters that have arguably already materialised. Suppose someone proves that a meta-strategy of simply tracking whichever prediction method has performed best so far, and following it, is provably optimal across literally all possible worlds in some precise long-run sense — without presupposing that the world is regular at all. Does that refute me, or just shove the problem one level up? Please don't gesture at this; tell me what such a result would and wouldn't establish.
- Reichenbach-style pragmatic vindication — induction works if anything does, so you're rationally obliged to use it. Dead on arrival given NFL, or does something survive?
- What would defenders of my position themselves concede as counting against it, as opposed to what only its critics consider a defeater? I suspect there's a gap here and I want it named.
- Rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. And give me an honest word on whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every apparent justification of induction gets waved off as still circular or as secretly assuming a prior, does the position risk anything at all — and if it doesn't, is that a defect, or just what being a theorem feels like from the inside?
