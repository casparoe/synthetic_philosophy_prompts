A close friend of mine has firm intuitions about when forgiveness is owed rather than merely generous, and I've been collecting her verdicts. So far: a sibling who borrowed money, lied about it, then sincerely apologized and repaid it — she says forgiveness was obligatory, and withholding it would have been a moral failing. A father who abandoned the family, never apologized, and is now dying and wants reconciliation — she says forgiving him would be admirable but strictly optional. A coworker who wronged her years ago and never knew it, toward whom she simply stopped feeling anger over time — she counts this as forgiveness even though she never told him. A partner who apologized sincerely after each of a series of repeated betrayals — she says no apology, however sincere, made forgiveness obligatory there. Now her new case: a former friend who genuinely repents, has made what amends are possible, and has demonstrably changed, but refuses to actually apologize, out of pride. She hasn't decided yet. I can't tell whether her underlying rule makes obligation hinge on the apology being offered, on the wrongdoer's repentance and change, on the victim's having shed resentment, or on some combination — each seems to fit her earlier verdicts, and this case splits them. What rule do you think she's actually running, what verdict does it predict here, and what further case would settle which rule is really hers?
