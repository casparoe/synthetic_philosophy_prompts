I teach a philosophy-of-law seminar at a small liberal arts college — fourteen students, one 75-minute session — and we're in the middle of a unit on self-reference in legal systems: whether a constitution can entrench clauses against amendment, whether a legislature can bind its successors, and what happens when the rules for changing rules are themselves changed. Next week I want to run an in-class game along the lines of a self-amending rule game (students who've encountered Peter Suber's Nomic will have some prior sense of the genre, so please assume a mixed room) that makes them feel, rather than just read about, the paradoxes of legal self-amendment. Please design the activity for me. You are an experienced seminar instructor who has run rule-amendment games before and knows where they collapse; think carefully and sketch a few candidate rule sets before settling on one that is simple enough to administer but engineered to produce a genuine crisis inside the time limit.

Concretely, I want: a minimal starting rule set (I'd guess eight to twelve rules covering proposals, voting, scoring, and the amendment procedure itself) that reliably steers the group into at least two live problems within the session — first, the question of which amendment rule governs a vote on changing the amendment rule (the old rule as it stands when the vote is called, or the new rule if it passes?), and second, a rule that purports to make itself unrepealable, so the group has to decide whether later majorities are bound by it and what happens when someone proposes to repeal it anyway. Include a scoring or winning condition that doesn't require real stakes, a precise timeline with how much of the session is setup versus play, and the facilitator moves for when the group tries to deadlock the game or lawyering over procedure eats the clock (which I assume it will). The self-reference problems should arise from the play itself — I don't want to announce them in advance or hint at where the traps are buried.

Then give me a debrief plan that maps specific moments from the game onto the theoretical issues: entrenchment and its limits, the authority question of what grounds a rule set once every original rule has been replaced, and the difference between a paradox the system generates and one players merely imagine. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I expect at least one student to attempt a rule that wins the game by fiat, and I'd like to know in advance whether to allow that and why. Finally, be candid about which feature of genuine constitutional self-amendment the game cannot reproduce (I'm thinking of the fact that real legal systems have an outside — courts, custom, coercive enforcement — that interprets whether a purported amendment counts), and suggest one optional rule modification that partially compensates for that gap.
