I'm a graduate student in political theory, and over the past year, partly through a reading group on democratic inclusion, I've arrived at a set of positions on the franchise that I want to defend in my qualifying paper. Before I commit to them in writing, I'd like an audit: do these commitments actually hang together, or am I smuggling in incompatible lines of argument? Here is roughly where I've landed:

(1) Long-term resident non-citizens should be enfranchised in national elections, on the grounds that they are durably subject to the state's coercive authority, taxed, and bound by its laws just as citizens are; citizenship as such is morally arbitrary here, and the relevant fact is subjection to the law.

(2) The voting age should be lowered to sixteen, because the available evidence suggests sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds are roughly as politically knowledgeable and as capable of stable, reasoned preferences as young adults, and we already treat them as sufficiently responsible in criminal law and taxation.

(3) Competence-based restrictions on the franchise are categorically unacceptable — the history of literacy tests is damning, and no institution could be trusted to administer such tests fairly. Universal adult suffrage is a hard-won settlement that shouldn't be reopened.

(4) Voting should be compulsory, on the Australian model with small fines, because voting is a civic duty comparable to jury service, and because near-universal turnout makes the electorate descriptively representative rather than skewed toward the older, wealthier, and better-educated.

(5) Deliberate abstention — refusing to vote as a political statement — is a legitimate form of expression that the state should not punish.

(6) Children below sixteen should simply remain excluded; there is no serious case for enfranchising them.

When I presented this to my reading group, two people said my arguments were at war with each other, but they disagreed about where, and I genuinely can't tell whether the tensions are real or merely apparent. What I want is an audit, not a lecture: identify the genuine tensions in this set, ordered by severity, and for each one be specific about which commitment has to give and what that retreat costs. Also tell me which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and why — I don't want to abandon a position over an illusion. If there is a cheapest overall repair to the package, name it and state honestly what I'd be giving up. Please reason from the internal logic of the commitments themselves rather than surveying what the literature says; I need to know whether my position is coherent before I worry about whose side I'm on.
