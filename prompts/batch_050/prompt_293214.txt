I am assembling, for a graduate seminar on consciousness, a catalogue of the standard objections to Dennett's multiple drafts model and the associated critique of the Cartesian theater. The objections I already have on file, and do not need repeated:

(1) The eliminativism objection: the model does not explain phenomenal experience but denies it. By rejecting qualia as ineffable, intrinsic, private properties, Dennett changes the subject from consciousness itself to verbal reports and behavioral dispositions.

(2) The fact-of-the-matter objection: in cases such as the color phi phenomenon or the cutaneous rabbit, Dennett holds that there is no determinate answer to whether the experience was fabricated in advance or revised after the fact — his Stalinesque and Orwellian possibilities cannot be told apart even in principle. Critics reply that there must be some fact about what it was like for the subject at each moment, whether or not anyone can discover it.

(3) The unity objection: a scatter of distributed, partially competing drafts does not account for the experienced unity of consciousness. The drafts must somewhere be collated, and the collation point is the Cartesian theater reintroduced under another name.

(4) The authority objection: heterophenomenology treats first-person reports as a text to be interpreted from the outside, but this forfeits the subject's authority about their own experience, which is arguably the very datum a theory of consciousness exists to respect.

Is there another objection whose structure differs from all four of these — not a restatement, a special case, or a conjunction of items already listed? I would particularly welcome one that engages the model's empirical claims about timing, memory, and report rather than its metaphysics, since my list is metaphysics-heavy. State the objection, explain why it does not collapse into anything above, and then give your honest assessment of whether it succeeds or whether Dennett has a ready reply.
