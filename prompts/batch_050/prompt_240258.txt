I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory, and my dissertation has a chapter defending a moral right to strike -- not the legal right, the underlying moral one that the legal right is supposed to track. At our department's political philosophy reading group last week, one of the postdocs (a lovely guy who clearly enjoys ruining my life) presented an argument that I havn't been able to crack, and it's been eating at me because it seems to prove way too much. Roughly as he gave it:

1. It is morally wrong to deliberately inflict harm on innocent third parties as leverage to extract concessions from someone else. (His motivating cases: extortion rackets, hostage-taking, that sort of thing.)
2. A strike does exactly that. The whole mechanism of a strike -- the thing that makes it a bargaining instrument rather than just a mass resignation -- is that it imposes costs on people who aren't party to the dispute: commuters stranded by a transit walkout, patients whose procedures get postponed, customers, downstream businesses. If it imposed no such costs, it would move nobody.
3. So striking is morally wrong, full stop.
4. But the same reasoning condemns a boycott (it harms the boycotted firm's innocent employees), condemns quitting a job without generous notice (your coworkers absorb your work), and frankly condemns ordinary market competition (opening a better, cheaper shop across the street deliberately harms the established shopkeeper, and "winning" just means that harm materializes).
5. So if 1-3 go through, everyday participation in a market economy is one long string of extortionate acts, which is absurd -- hence either the argument breaks somewhere or strikes are fine.

His response was to deny it proves too much: he said to accept 3 and bite most of 4's bullet (boycotts and abrupt quits are often genuinely wrong and we've just never noticed), and that strikes are actually the *clearest* case because there the third-party harm isn't incidental, it's the point. When I tried "in a strike the harm to bystanders is a side effect, not the means," he pointed at transit strikes and asked what I thought was putting pressure on the city council if not precisely the public's misery. When I tried "competitive harm doesn't count because nobody is entitled to my staying out of the market," he said that's exactly what he'd say about the employer's workforce -- nobody is entitled to anyone's labor, which is why his argument only needs the harm itself, not any rights violation. So my two cheap exits are blocked and I'm going in circles.

So: where does the argument actually go wrong, if it does? I'd most like to keep some version of premise 1, since it seems obviously true that you can't get what you want by squeezing bystanders, and I obviously want to keep the conclusion that striking can be permissible. Tell me which premise or step to reject, why that step looked fine when he said it out loud, and what it would cost me to reject each of the other options instead -- including the option of just accepting his conclusion and restricting the right to strike to cases where bystanders barely notice. Please answer in American English.
