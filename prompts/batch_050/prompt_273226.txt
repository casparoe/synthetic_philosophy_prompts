I am a journalist covering science and ideas, writing a feature on whether recent progress in artificial intelligence vindicates the familiar claim that the brain is a computer. My draft argues that, just as a simulation of a storm can reproduce the storm’s dynamics without being wet, a simulation of a brain might reproduce its cognitive organization without being made of neurons. A scientifically minded editor objects that this argument runs together four distinct claims: that brains process information, that neural activity can be usefully modeled computationally, that cognition is independent of biological substrate, and that any system performing the right computations would thereby be conscious.

Audit the analogy carefully. Begin with an explicit correspondence table: on the computer side, consider hardware, program, memory, state transitions, inputs and outputs, and levels of implementation; on the brain side, consider neurons and synapses, developmental constraints, neuromodulation, continuous physical dynamics, embodiment, and interaction with an environment. In particular, assess what could plausibly play the role of the program: the connectome, learned synaptic strengths, genetically supplied developmental rules, an abstract cognitive architecture, or nothing at all.

Then identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion. Address the differences between digital and analog processes, discrete and continuous change, designed and evolved systems, and a replaceable hardware-software distinction versus the brain’s entanglement of computation with metabolism, development, and behavior. Also examine a deeper problem: whether calling a physical process computational is a claim about the process itself or about a description imposed by an interpreter. Avoid the unhelpful extremes that everything is a computer and that computation is merely metaphorical; state what would count as a principled criterion of computational implementation.

Use three test cases to discipline the analysis: a model that predicts neural activity without reproducing the brain’s internal causal organization; a neuron-by-neuron silicon replacement that preserves input-output behavior while omitting intracellular chemistry; and a complete real-time simulation whose simulated neurons have no effects outside the computer. Which, if any, would instantiate the same computation as the original brain, and would that settle whether it has experiences?

Conclude by evaluating each of the four claims separately. State what the brain-computer analogy genuinely supports, what it merely suggests, and whether it can justify either mind uploading or artificial consciousness without an additional theory of consciousness.
