The exam question:

In Book III of An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Locke distinguishes the real essence of a substance (its inner constitution, from which its observable qualities flow) from its nominal essence (the abstract idea we use to sort things into kinds). Locke argues that we classify by nominal essences, so the boundaries of our sortal terms reflect our classificatory practice rather than divisions we simply read off nature. Using Locke's distinction, discuss whether a modern science such as chemistry or biology can be said to carve nature at its joints. A good answer will (i) explain the real/nominal essence distinction clearly, (ii) apply it to at least one modern kind, and (iii) take and defend a position on whether Locke's account survives modern science.

The student's answer:

Locke's real essence is the hidden microstructure of a thing, so for gold something like its atomic structure, while the nominal essence is the bundle of observable properties we use to decide what counts as gold, like being yellow, malleable, dissolving in aqua regia, and so on. Locke's key move is that we sort things by nominal essences, since we cannot perceive real essences directly.

Locke thought that because kinds are defined by our ideas, the boundaries between them are drawn by us and not by nature. As he famously puts it, a good classification carves nature at its joints, by which he means our classifications are basically arbitrary: any grouping of particulars by shared observable features would do just as well, so there is no fact of the matter about whether a whale counts as a fish or a mammal. On Locke's view, then, nature itself contains no kinds at all, only individual things.

Modern science might seem to refute this. When chemists worked out that gold is the element with atomic number 79, they arguably discovered the real essence of gold. Locke just could not have anticipated that we would one day get access to microstructure. Kripke and Putnam formalized this point: natural kind terms are rigid designators whose reference gets fixed by samples, and this vindicates Locke's skepticism, since it shows reference does not depend on any essence at all. So chemistry partly confirms and partly refutes him.

Biology, though, confirms Locke fully. Since Darwin we know that species evolve and have no essences; there is no inner constitution that all and only tigers share. This kills the ancient idea that species have real essences, but it leaves Locke's nominalism about kinds untouched, since on his view the species boundary was always our workmanship anyway.

So my position: Locke's account survives in biology but fails in chemistry, where real essences turned out to be discoverable after all.
