**The question (as set by the professor):** Some philosophers worry that longtermist thinking depends on "fanatical" reasoning about the far future. Others think Bostrom's simulation argument gives us reason to doubt that our actions now will matter on long timescales. Does the simulation argument dampen fanaticism about the far future? In your answer, explain (a) what the simulation argument actually concludes, (b) what "fanaticism" means in this debate, and (c) whether and how the argument bears on it.

**His answer:**

The simulation argument, due to Nick Bostrom (2003), is a trilemma: at least one of the following is true. (1) Almost all civilizations go extinct before reaching technological maturity. (2) Almost no technologically mature civilization is interested in running detailed simulations of its ancestors. (3) We are almost certainly living in a computer simulation. It's important that the argument doesn't prove (3) by itself; it only proves the disjunction, so it would be a mistake to just say "we're definitely simulated."

Fanaticism, as the term is used in the longtermism literature, means valuing the far future so heavily -- because of the sheer number of possible future people -- that its value swamps everything in the near term, in the limit becoming effectively infinite compared to present-day concerns. So the fanatic will always pick whichever option best affects the long-run trajectory of civilization, even when the benefits for people alive today are tiny.

Does the simulation argument dampen this? I think yes, substantially. Conditional on (3), we live in a simulation run by someone else, and simulations can be switched off, restarted, or terminated when they stop being interesting. That puts a discount on the far future: the probability that what we do now compounds into effects billions of years from now is much lower if there's a standing background risk of shutdown. Fanaticism depends on enormous expected values flowing from the deep future, and a shutdown discount shrinks those expected values, so the fanatical conclusion gets weaker. Conditional on (1) or (2) there's no such discount, but since (3) has significant probability, the all-things-considered expected value of far-future interventions comes down. So the simulation argument dampens fanaticism, though it doesn't eliminate it completely.
