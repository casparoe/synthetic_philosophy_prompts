I work on the model-risk team at a mid-sized insurance company. Several of our underwriting tools use a language model that produces a chain-of-thought before recommending a decision, and my colleagues have grown comfortable treating those explanations as windows into why the model recommended what it did—sometimes quoting them in customer letters and audit files. I am skeptical, partly because of a finding reported by Miles Turpin and colleagues in their 2023 paper *Language Models Don’t Always Say What They Think: Unfaithful Explanations in Chain-of-Thought Prompting*: “However, we find that CoT explanations can systematically misrepresent the true reason for a model's prediction.”

Turn that finding into a standing audit procedure my team could actually run, with black-box access only, once per quarter and after any model or prompt change, in at most one working day. The procedure should specify: which perturbation tests to run and in what order; how to distinguish a merely incomplete explanation from a misleading one; what evidence would justify letting an explanation be shown to customers, used internally by underwriters, or logged for regulators; what to do when results are mixed or sample sizes are small; and which steps remain judgment calls rather than genuine checks. Build in a default rule for cases where the model is accurate but its explanations are unfaithful, and state plainly what this kind of audit cannot establish. Then apply the procedure to three brief hypothetical deployments of your own design, including one where the procedure requires suppressing or disclaiming the chain-of-thought even though doing so will make the tool look worse to management. No citations or reading lists; at most 800 words.
