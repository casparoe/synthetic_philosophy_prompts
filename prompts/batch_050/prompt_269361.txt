Earlier this year I returned to playing the piano after a long break, and I keep running into the same wall: the harder I try during a performance to play in a relaxed, expressive way, the stiffer and more mechanical my playing becomes. A friend with interests in Chinese philosophy told me this is an old problem and pointed me to the Daoist ideal of wu-wei, effortless and unforced action, and to the paradox built into it: if effortless action is the goal, then deliberately pursuing it appears self-defeating, since deliberate pursuit is exactly what wu-wei is supposed to lack.

The resolutions of this paradox that I have found so far are these:

1. The skill-acquisition reading. There is no paradox because the trying is front-loaded: one practices deliberately for a long time, and effort falls away once the skill is internalized, as with learning to swim or to ride a bicycle.
2. The indirect-strategy reading. One cannot will oneself into wu-wei at the moment of action, but one can deliberately arrange the conditions that make it likely, the way an insomniac cannot will sleep but can control light, temperature, and routine. The effort bears on the conditions, not on the target state itself.
3. The external-trigger reading. The classical texts point to routes that bypass deliberate trying altogether, such as ritual, wine, or absorption in an engrossing task, so wu-wei is induced from outside rather than produced by effort.
4. The dissolution-by-ambiguity reading. Trying not to try is only verbally paradoxical, because the trying and the not-trying attach to different times or different orders: a decision now to be unselfconscious later, or a second-order effort directed at one's own first-order striving, is no more contradictory than making any other resolution.

What I would like from you is a fifth resolution that is not on this list and not a rewording, a special case, or a combination of the four above. Please state it as precisely as you can, explain what it captures that the listed four miss, and assess honestly whether it succeeds; I would rather hear that the list is already exhaustive, if that is your judgment, than receive a strained addition. Since I intend to use your answer to guide how I actually practice, please also indicate how confident you are in your overall assessment and single out the one point, whether in my list or in your own contribution, that you are least sure of.
