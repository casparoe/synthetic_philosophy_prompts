You're a philosopher of science who's spent years thinking about long-run predictions, and you've got a bit of a bee in your bonnet about sloppy uses of falsification. Think carefully before you start -- maybe do a quick brainstorm of the moving parts first, then write the essay.

Here's the setup I want you to work with. People often say Malthus was "right but early": the population-versus-food mechanism he described is real enough, the story goes, it just hasn't bitten yet, and maybe it will once soils degrade, aquifers drain, or the climate turns. Critics reply that a prediction you can keep pushing into the future isn't a prediction at all -- it's a snooze alarm. Every time the crisis fails to show up, you just set a new date and call the theory confirmed-in-advance.

To make this sharper, here's a distinction I'd like you to use (I'm more or less coining it, so run with my definitions rather than anything you might half-remember). Call a prediction **clock-bound** if the theory itself specifies the timing mechanism -- it tells you which observable variable is the ticking clock and roughly what value of it triggers the crisis. Call it **calendar-bound** if the timing is just a date or a vague "eventually" stapled on from outside. A clock-bound prediction can genuinely be early: the clock just hasn't reached the mark, and you can check that. A calendar-bound one can only be postponed.

Now the essay question: is "right but early" a coherent status for a theory, or is it always either (a) a legitimate claim that the triggering conditions haven't yet held, or (b) an immunising dodge dressed up in patient language? Use Malthus as your central case: was his theory clock-bound or calendar-bound, given that he actually described the mechanism (geometric population growth versus arithmetic food growth) but got the effective clock wrong because he didn't foresee yields rising? Then apply your framework to one or two other long-run predictions of your choosing (ones you actually know something about -- don't invent facts).

Some things I want the essay to do:
- Take the snooze-alarm objection seriously and say exactly what it gets right and where it overreaches, since clock-bound theories can also be snoozed if proponents keep redefining the clock.
- Say what observable evidence would distinguish "the clock hasn't reached the mark" from "there is no clock."
- Reach an actual verdict on Malthus -- was he right but early, wrong, or is the question itself ill-posed? Defend it. No wishy-washy "it's a bit of both" ending, though a conditional verdict with teeth is fine.
