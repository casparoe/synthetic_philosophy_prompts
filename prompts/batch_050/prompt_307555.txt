I'm in an effective altruism reading group and last week we got into a surprisingly heated argument about the "hinge of history" idea -- roughly, the claim that we're living at an unusually influential time, where what happens now could lock in the long-run trajectory of humanity. The thing we kept tripping over is that there seem to be two different claims hiding inside this, and people kept sliding between them. Call the first the *leverage claim*: actions taken now have more causal influence over the far future than actions taken at most other times. Call the second the *resolution claim*: we're currently facing unusually large uncertainties about which trajectory civilization will go down, and these are about to get settled one way or the other, after which later generations won't have much room to steer.

Can you explain whether these two claims can actually come apart, and if so, which one the standard arguments for the hinge hypothesis (the ones based on existential risk, transformative AI, etc.) really support? It would help to think through concrete cases: could a period be high-leverage without resolving much uncertainty (my friend suggested a hypothetical civilization that faces one big fork per century, forever, each equally consequential)? And could a period resolve tons of uncertainty without being especially high-leverage? Also, one person in the group insisted that "the most important century" talk is about which era matters most in absolute terms, while someone else said hinge-ness is only ever a claim about where the *next* unit of effort or money does the most good -- a quick note on whether those are the same thing would be great.
