Consider the dispute between Mozi’s impartial care and Confucian graded love in light of a distinction I will call motivational impartiality versus institutional impartiality. Motivational impartiality requires people to feel approximately equal concern for kin, friends, and strangers. Institutional impartiality permits people to retain graded attachments, while social roles, reciprocal duties, and public mechanisms channel those attachments into outcomes that give everyone’s interests comparable weight. Call such a mechanism a care transducer: it converts naturally partial motivations into relatively impartial treatment.

What is your credence that, in large-scale human societies, institutional impartiality is more reliable than motivational impartiality as a way of durably securing the moral benefits Mozi wanted from impartial care? Treat this as a question about feasible human societies, not about what is logically possible for ideally transformed agents. Give one overall probability, then decompose it into the subquestions doing the real work: how plastic human concern is across social distance; whether genuine care must be felt directly to count as morally valuable; whether Confucian graded love can be extended outward without being diluted or captured by favoritism; whether Mozian impartiality underappreciates the motivational role of special relationships; and whether care transducers tend to discipline partiality or merely launder it into apparently neutral institutions.

Assign a rough probability to each relevant subquestion, explain briefly how those probabilities combine without pretending that they are statistically independent, and identify which term is doing the most work in your final credence. Then say what philosophical argument or empirical evidence would most change that term. Please answer in American English.
