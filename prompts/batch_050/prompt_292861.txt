I am preparing the final examination for my upper-level undergraduate seminar on fiction and reference, and I want one essay question built around the following passage, the opening of Arthur Conan Doyle's "The Adventure of the Empty House," in which Dr. Watson addresses his readers:

"Let me say to that public, which has shown some interest in those glimpses which I have occasionally given them of the thoughts and actions of a very remarkable man, that they are not to blame me if I have not shared my knowledge with them, for I should have considered it my first duty to have done so, had I not been barred by a positive prohibition from his own lips, which was only withdrawn upon the third of last month."

Design a question a strong candidate could answer in 60 minutes, closed book, worth 30 marks. Its crux should be the feature the passage makes explicit: within the Holmes fiction, Watson's narratives are themselves published writings addressed to a reading public inside that fiction, and one of those narratives, "The Final Problem," informed the public that Holmes died at the Reichenbach Falls — a report the later narrative reveals to have been false. The question should therefore force candidates to take and defend a position on what fixes truth in a fiction whose narration exists inside the fiction: is it true in the earlier story that Holmes died, or only true that Watson reported his death, and should the Holmes canon be treated as one fiction that corrects itself over time or as many mutually inconsistent fictions? It should also require candidates to connect this to the status of the name "Sherlock Holmes": whether it refers, and, if it does not, how anything Watson writes — or we say — about Holmes can be true or false at all. Do not let the question be answerable by reciting a generic summary of theories of fictional truth without applying them to this case.

Along with the question, supply a model answer of roughly 600 words and a grading rubric: four components a full-marks answer must contain, the marks each is worth (totaling 30), and for each component a one-sentence note on the most common way candidates mishandle it. Add a capping rule for answers that speak interchangeably of Doyle and Watson, and state what an answer must do beyond the four components to earn first-class marks.
