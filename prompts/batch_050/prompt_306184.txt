I am drafting a literary novel whose plot turns on a sustained, cordial disagreement between two characters -- an elderly philosopher and her former student -- who meet weekly over a year, talk with complete honesty and mutual respect, and yet part at the end still disagreeing about whether one of them, years earlier, acted wrongly in a way the other witnessed. Early readers keep telling me this is unrealistic, citing Aumann's agreement theorem: two rational agents with common priors who honestly exchange their views cannot, it seems, agree to disagree. I have therefore been collecting the standard ways this result is dodged, so that I can decide which one my characters are permitted to rely on.

The escape routes I already know and do not need explained again are these: (1) different priors -- my characters simply do not share a prior, so the theorem never gets a grip; (2) failure of common knowledge of rationality or honesty -- each privately suspects the other of self-deception or evasion, even while being perfectly polite about it; (3) bounded rationality -- neither can actually compute the Bayesian update that full rationality demands, so both remain stuck at their untidily updated views; (4) equivocation -- they are not really disagreeing about the same proposition, because the moral terms in play mean subtly different things to each of them; and (5) insincerity in the exchange -- one or both is not reporting a genuine posterior but managing the conversation, so what is traded is not really the agents' considered views.

What I want from you is a further escape route that is genuinely distinct from these five -- not a rewording, special case, or combination of them -- and that could plausibly sustain an honest, affectionate, years-long disagreement between two intelligent people. Then I ask you to be decisive rather than even-handed: tell me which of the escape routes, including your new one, you judge to be the most dramatically and psychologically credible for my two characters, and defend that choice. I do not want a menu of options with the selection left to me; I want your considered verdict, with its reasons, and a candid statement of the strongest objection to the route you endorse -- including whether that objection is serious enough to make me restructure the novel.
