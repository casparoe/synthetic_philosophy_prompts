A friend and I recently had a long argument about whether major historical outcomes turn on small accidents or merely use those accidents as occasions for deeper forces to operate. She defended a version of for-want-of-a-nail history; I argued that social systems usually correct for local disruptions. We eventually realized that we were conflating several distinctions: sensitivity to initial conditions, absence of a sufficient cause, persistence of a difference, and the importance of a cause in an explanation.

Please construct a decision tree that sorts theories of contingency in history according to these distinctions rather than according to whether their adherents describe history as contingent or determined. Begin with the neutral commitment that some actual event had a significant consequence. Each branch should be a diagnostic question that someone could answer without already knowing the name of the position toward which it leads. Where useful, employ concrete fictional cases, such as a delayed messenger, a ruler’s unexpected death, a failed harvest, a mutation in a pathogen, or a military order lost in transit. Do not rely on disputed historical facts.

The tree should include leaves corresponding to at least the following positions, although you may rename or subdivide them when precision requires it:

1. robust-process structuralism: large-scale outcomes are relatively insensitive to individual accidents;
2. trigger theory: a structural condition makes some event likely, while accidents determine its timing, form, or agent;
3. path-dependence theory: an early contingent difference is stabilized and amplified by later mechanisms;
4. chaotic-history theory: tiny differences can produce large and enduring divergences without any privileged threshold;
5. restoring-mechanism theory: accidents matter temporarily, but selection, replacement, imitation, or institutional redundancy returns the system to a similar trajectory;
6. scale-relativist pluralism: an outcome can be contingent at one temporal or social scale and robust at another;
7. historical underdeterminism: prior conditions do not settle the outcome even when all laws and structural tendencies are fixed;
8. counterfactual skepticism: claims about what would have happened are generally too unconstrained to support a taxonomy of contingency.

Distinguish clearly between a cause’s being replaceable, its being improbable, and its being explanatorily important. Also distinguish deterministic contingency, in which a system is highly sensitive but still deterministic, from genuine causal openness. If two leaves cannot be separated by answers about cases alone—for example, if chaos and underdeterminism differ only over a metaphysical claim that the case does not test—flag that limitation explicitly.

After the tree, provide a brief residue section identifying any coherent combinations of answers that have no obvious defenders, such as a view combining global inevitability with radical sensitivity at every scale, and explain whether each is incoherent, merely unattractive, or potentially viable but underdeveloped. Conclude with four test cases designed to expose disagreements that the usual rhetoric of accidents versus structures tends to hide.
