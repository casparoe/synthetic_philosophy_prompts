I teach a seminar on the ethics of deception, and in a recent session a student pressed me on whether Augustine's notoriously uncompromising position on lying — every deliberately deceptive utterance is sinful, even one told to save an innocent life, yet lies differ enormously in gravity — has anything systematic to say about the texture of modern deception. I would like to put that to the test, so here is the view as I presented it to the class: what makes an utterance a lie is the intention to deceive, so jokes and tall tales in which no one is actually meant to be taken in do not strictly count as lies; all genuine lies are wrong, but their gravity depends on what they concern and on whom they help or harm, with lies that corrupt the teaching of religion at the top of the scale and so-called officious lies told to protect another person at the bottom; and no end, however weighty, can license a lie, since a good outcome cannot purify a corrupt means.

With that framework in hand, consider the following eight cases: (1) a hospice nurse tells a dying patient that his estranged son has been contacted and is on his way, though she has not managed to reach him; (2) a homeowner hiding two refugees in the cellar tells the militia officer at the door that no one else is inside; (3) a pastor, fearing a schism, knowingly teaches her congregation a doctrine she privately believes to be false; (4) a job candidate claims fluency with a software package he has used twice, expecting to learn it properly before the start date; (5) a comedian opens her set with an anecdote delivered as personal experience that the audience plainly understands as a bit; (6) a shopping app displays a countdown timer warning that a sale ends in ten minutes, though the timer resets for every visitor; (7) an undercover journalist adopts a false name and an invented biography over several months to infiltrate a wildlife-trafficking network; (8) a physician, persuaded by the evidence on placebo effects, hands a patient an inert capsule while describing it as a proven painkiller.

Please rank these eight cases from most to least gravely wrong as Augustine himself would rank them, giving two or three sentences of justification for each placement and noting explicitly where two cases share a rank or where the ordering between two of them is underdetermined by his criteria. Then answer two follow-ups. First: which item, if any, would Augustine refuse to classify as a lie at all, and does your ranking change once that item is removed? Second: which single placement in your ranking does Augustine's own apparatus handle most awkwardly — where do his criteria pull in opposite directions or run out of discriminative power — and what does that strain reveal about whether his ordering of lies tracks the matter lied about, the intention of the liar, or the consequences for the deceived?
