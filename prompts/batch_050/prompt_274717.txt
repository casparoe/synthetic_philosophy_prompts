You are both a political philosopher specializing in Hobbes and a literary critic familiar with William Golding’s Lord of the Flies. Think carefully about both sides of the comparison before answering, and write in polished American English.

In the dedication to Leviathan, Hobbes writes:

“Besides, I speak not of the men, but (in the Abstract) of the Seat of Power, (like to those simple and unpartiall creatures in the Roman Capitol, that with their noyse defended those within, not because they were they, but there) offending none, I think, but those without, or such within (if there be any such) as favour them.”

Use this neglected passage as an interpretive key to Lord of the Flies. Begin with a brief scene map identifying the episodes you consider most relevant, and then develop a sustained reading of the novel through Hobbes’s account of the state of nature, covenant, sovereignty, and political obligation.

First, explain what follows from Hobbes’s distinction between particular men and the abstract Seat of Power. In particular, consider how the passage complicates any simple identification of sovereignty with the strongest or most charismatic individual. Then apply that distinction to the island. Does the conch represent a public office or seat of power without adequate force behind it? Does Jack eventually acquire force without becoming a genuine sovereign? Does Ralph retain legitimate authority after he loses the capacity to enforce collective decisions? And does Jack’s tribe constitute a primitive commonwealth because it provides food, membership, and protection, or is it better understood as organized domination within an ongoing state of war?

Address at least the election of Ralph and the establishment of the conch, the deterioration of the assemblies, Jack’s separation from the original group, the movement to Castle Rock, the killing of Piggy together with the destruction of the conch, and the arrival of the naval officer. For each stage, ask whether Hobbes would classify the boys’ condition as a state of nature, an attempted commonwealth, a stable commonwealth, or a reversion to war. Pay particular attention to fear of the beast: does it function like the mutual fear and anticipation that drive Hobbesian conflict, or does its imaginary character expose a limitation in Hobbes’s more rationalistic explanation?

Your reading should resist treating the novel as a straightforward confirmation of Leviathan. Consider at least three complications: the boys enter the island with inherited social ranks and moral habits rather than as free and equal adults; Jack’s authority depends partly on ritual, appetite, cruelty, and theatrical fear rather than only on rational self-preservation; and the final rescue comes from an outside power rather than from a covenant formed among the boys themselves. Does the ending support Hobbes by showing that only overwhelming external force can end the conflict, or does it challenge him by implying that no genuine social contract was possible among the children?

Conclude with a measured judgment about what the novel adds to an understanding of Hobbes that philosophical argument alone may miss. Do not invent quotations or minor textual details; rely only on the novel’s well-known plot and major scenes. Finally, state your overall confidence in the reading and single out the one interpretive or philosophical claim about which you are least sure.
