Here's his argument as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) If many-worlds is right, then whenever something happens that could kill me, as long as there's some nonzero chance of survival, the world splits and there's a branch where I survive.
(2) I can only ever experience branches where I'm alive. A branch where I'm dead has no experiences in it for me, so from my own point of view it might as well not exist.
(3) So no matter what I do, the only future I'll ever actually experience is one where I survived. Basically I'm subjectively immortal.
(4) A risk can only count against doing something if there's a chance I'll experience the bad outcome. By (3), the bad outcome (death) is one I'll never experience, no matter how likely it is "objectively."
(5) So for me the expected cost of any deadly risk is zero, and I should take arbitrarily large lethal risks for even small payoffs. His example: play quantum russian roulette where a quantum device kills you with 99.9% probability and pays you $500 the other 0.1%, since you'll only ever experience the branch where you collect.
(6) But that's obviously insane advice, so either many-worlds is false or one of the steps above is broken.

He's a physics guy so he insists (1) is fine, and honestly (2) sounds right to me too when I squint at it -- of course I can't experience being dead, that's what dead means. But the conclusion in (5) feels like it has to be a scam somewhere, because by his logic literally no safety measure is ever worth taking, seatbelts included. Also I noticed it seems to prove too much even beyond risk stuff: if I'll always experience surviving, why would I ever save for retirement or bother with health insurance in this branch at all? Something's off but I keep going in circles.
