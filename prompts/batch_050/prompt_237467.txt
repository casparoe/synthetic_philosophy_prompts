You’re a professional political philosopher who’s good at getting people who disagree to actually say what they disagree about. Think carefully and do some explicit brainstorming first (list possible agreed claims, candidate cruxes, and cases) before drafting the final document.

So my reading group has two people who have been fighting all semester: a negative-liberty liberal (roughly Berlin’s negative camp — freedom is the absence of interference by others) and a republican in the neo-roman / domination sense (freedom is the absence of subjection to another’s arbitrary will, even if that will never actually interferes). They keep talking past each other and I’ve convinced them to do an adversarial collaboration. Write the joint statement they should come out with.

The document needs:

1. A numbered list of claims both can actually sign. Not mush like “freedom matters” — I want real content, e.g. about what coercion, law, and dependence each do or don’t do to a person, where both can agree on the facts while disagreeing on whether to call it unfreedom. Aim for at least 6-8 of these, and be honest if some of them are thinner than they look.

2. A disagreements table: each row states the residual disagreement in wording that BOTH would accept as a fair statement of their own view. No strawmen. Flag which disagreements you think are actually verbal (I have a strong suspicion that part of this fight is just two uses of the word freedom, and part of it is genuinely normative — tell me which part is which and why).

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions where an answer would force at least one side to give ground. For each crux, give a concrete test case both agree bears on it, and record each side’s prediction/verdict in advance. Use these three cases at minimum, which I made up so they can’t have rehearsed answers:

(a) The Watchful Landlord: a tenant whose landlord has the legal right to evict her at any time for any reason, has had this right for 20 years, has never used it, and is a genuinely nice guy. She organizes a tenants’ union anyway. Is she free during those 20 years? Does anything change if the landlord dies and a faceless property company inherits the same right?

(b) The Benevolent Algorithm: a city run by a prediction system that could override any citizen’s decision at any moment, is known to have overridden no one ever, and whose code no citizen can contest or vote on — but its objective function is just “let people do what they were going to do.” Versus a neighboring city with harsh, occasionally enforced, but constitutionally entrenched and contestable laws that every citizen had a vote on. Which city’s residents are freer, and is the comparison even well-defined?

(c) The Friendly Bouncer: a private club where the owner personally stops you from doing certain things (smoking, discussing politics) but you can leave anytime, versus a state that never stops you from anything but reserves the legally unchecked power to do so. The negative guy says the first involves more interference so less freedom; the republican presumably says the opposite is worse. Does the exit option dissolve the republican objection or not?

For each case, say what each side is committed to saying, and — the important part — whether their verdict follows from their theory or whether they have to add something extra (intuitions, empirical claims, whatever) to get there. If one of my cases doesn’t actually discriminate between the views, tell me and replace it with one that does.

4. Finally: what, if anything, would each of them count as a reason to abandon their view? If one side turns out to have no possible defeater, note that — it might mean their position is a definition rather than a thesis, which is itself a finding worth putting in the statement.
