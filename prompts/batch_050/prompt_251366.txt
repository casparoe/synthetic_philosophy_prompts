Number theorists routinely assess conjectures using probabilistic models that rest on what might be called a presumption of independence: treat the primality of nearby numbers, or the residues of primes in different congruence classes, as independent random events, then check whether the conjectured phenomenon occurs with probability one. Sometimes this works remarkably well — random-model heuristics for Goldbach's conjecture and for the distribution of Mersenne primes track the data closely once corrected for congruence obstructions. Sometimes it fails instructively: the naive independence estimate for the density of twin primes is off by a factor of about 1.32, because residues modulo small primes are not in fact independent, and fixing the model requires the Hardy–Littlewood singular series. And sometimes symmetry-based reasoning is quietly wrong in a way the data only partially reveal, as with Chebyshev's bias, where primes congruent to 3 mod 4 lead those congruent to 1 mod 4 far more often than naive independence would suggest.

Suppose you are advising a grant panel that can fund two or three philosophers of mathematics, for three years, to work on the epistemology of such heuristic arguments. Which open questions should the panel prioritize, and in what order? Candidates include, but are not limited to: whether heuristic arguments genuinely raise credence in a conjecture and by what right; whether the presumption of independence can be formalized as a defensible inference rule (via maximum entropy, reference-class selection, or something else) or is irreducibly a matter of judgment; how to characterize the defeaters that indicate when independence fails; how to adjudicate when two plausible probabilistic models deliver conflicting verdicts; and whether heuristic confidence can legitimately guide the allocation of proof-search effort. For each question you prioritize, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers rather than working number theorists, and what developments over the next few years — in mathematics or in formal epistemology — would move it up or down the list. I am less interested in the list itself than in the reasoning that generates the ordering.
