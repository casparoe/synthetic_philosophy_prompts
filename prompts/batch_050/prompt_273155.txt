Ok so I write novels, and I'm deep into a new one where the whole cast is, for lack of a better word, reasoners. There's a detective who's basically a human theorem prover, an AI, a saint who's supposed to be close to all-knowing about moral truths, and a teenage protagonist who's smart but very much finite. My problem: I keep catching myself writing all of them the same way -- they deduce everything instantly, they never miss a consequence of what they know, and then a philosopher friend told me that real epistemologists have been fighting about exactly this for decades under the name "the problem of logical omniscience." Something about how standard modal logics of knowledge force you to know all logical truths and every consequence of everything you know, which nobody and nothing actually does.

So I want to steal the structure of that debate for my character sheet.

Here's the task. Act as an expert on formal epistemology and modal logic who also consults for fiction writers (you've helped novelists get the philosophy right before, so you know the difference between getting the ideas right and info-dumping). Think carefully before you answer -- first do a quick brainstorm of the main responses to logical omniscience in your head and check that each one gives you a genuinely different *way a mind can fail to be omniscient*, then build the deliverable.

What I want: a taxonomy of the main positions on how to model agents who aren't logically omniscient, written as a branching tree where every split is labeled by the question that separates the two sides. Start the tree at "the agent knows some things" and branch on stuff like: do we keep the normal modal logic and just accept omniscience as a harmless idealization, do we split "belief" into two notions (the stuff you'd avow vs. the stuff buried in your head), do we let in impossible worlds so an agent can fail to know a tautology, do we index knowledge to time or steps of reasoning so the agent gets the consequences eventually, and so on -- you know the space better than I do, add whatever splits belong. Each leaf gets the name of the position (impossible-worlds accounts, awareness-style approaches, whatever the real names are) plus two things: one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and one sentence on what a character modeled on it would look like *in scene* -- like, what I'd actually write. Also flag any pair of positions the tree can't really pull apart, because I don't want two characters who are secretly the same guy with different labels. And if there's a combination of answers that's logically open but nobody defends, note it and tell me whether it'd be incoherent to give that position to a character anyway -- honestly incoherent characters are fine for fiction, I just want to know.
