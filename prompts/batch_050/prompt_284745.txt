Ok so I'm a novelist, I write literary fiction, and I have a problem which is that my hard drive is basically a graveyard of half-finished books. Three novels abandoned somewhere between page 80 and page 250, plus two I actually finished but couldn't sell and one I'm currently 4 years into and starting to hate.

The thing that always gets me is this: I drop a project and some well-meaning friend says "sunk cost fallacy! the years are gone either way, good for you for walking away." But then I pick an old draft back up and someone ELSE says I'm only doing it because I can't stand having wasted the time. It seems like literally any choice I make can be diagnosed as the sunk cost fallacy, which makes me suspect the diagnosis is doing no work at all.

So here's what I want: a decision procedure I can actually run, once or twice a year, whenever I'm staring at a stalled or resurrected manuscript wondering whether to keep going. Not a vibe, a procedure. Questions in order, what counts as a real answer to each one vs. me lying to myself, where it stops with a clear "abandon" or "finish," and where it has to admit the call is a judgment call no checklist can make. The hard part, philosophically, is sorting out when past investment is a legitimate input to the decision (like, the three years taught me things about this book nobody else knows, or I've built up a readership that expects a certain kind of book from me) versus when it's just loss aversion wearing a trench coat. The procedure has to be able to tell those apart, because that's the whole ballgame for me. Keep in mind I have no special discipline, limited free time around a day job, and my agent only ever sees finished things, so "ask my agent" is not a step.

Then run the procedure on these three cases from my own drawer, including my current one, and be honest even where I wont like the answer:
1. A 200-page draft I abandoned 5 years ago when the plot collapsed; the prose is the best I've ever written and the plot is still broken.
2. My current novel, 4 years in, structurally sound, but I now find the premise faintly embarassing and I've recently had a much better idea for a different book.
3. A finished, unsellable quiet novel that two small presses passed on, that I keep thinking I should rewrite from scratch.

Also tell me straight up what the procedure can't do -- where it will misfire, and what decision about a book is simply not the kind of thing a procedure can settle.

End with the verdict on my current novel (case 2), stated as a single last line beginning "Verdict:" followed by finish, abandon, or one of your procedure's own output labels if you gave it others.
