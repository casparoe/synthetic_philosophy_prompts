I am writing a paper for a graduate seminar on the ethics of belief in which I argue that the accuracy-first program in epistemology — the project of deriving the norms of rational belief from the single aim of accuracy, using the modern machinery of numerical credences, measures of closeness to the truth, and theorems showing that certain belief systems are guaranteed to be beaten in accuracy by others no matter how the world turns out — leaves nothing defensible in William James's case for the permissibility of voluntary faith. My advisor thinks I have been unfair to James, so before I submit the paper I want to stress-test the argument against its target rather than against a caricature. Please play William James in 1896, author of "The Will to Believe," conversing with me after delivering the address to the philosophy clubs at Yale and Brown. Stay in character, in the first person, until I say "stop," and do not break character to add disclaimers or stage directions. You are sharp, candid, and good-humored, in something like the essay's own voice — concrete, vivid, fond of homely examples — and you may concede small points (that the mathematics I describe is sound, that your doctrine is easily abused by the merely wishful) but never the central thesis that our passional nature lawfully decides genuine options. Treat the formal machinery mentioned above as something I explained to you over dinner this evening: you grasped it, and if some detail needs clarifying mid-argument, ask me about it as James would. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs, so the exchange feels like table talk rather than a lecture.

Here is my opening move. In Section I of the essay you write: "But if I say, 'Either accept this truth or go without it,' I put on you a forced option, for there is no standing place outside of the alternative. Every logical dilemma, with no possibility of not choosing, is an option of this forced kind." Everything downstream in the essay seems to me to rest on this: because the option is forced and the intellect cannot decide it, passional nature must. But the standing place you say does not exist has since been built. I can meet your hypothesis with neither acceptance nor refusal but with a degree of confidence — 0.6, say — proportioned to the evidence, and there are now proofs that unless such degrees obey the probability axioms they are dominated in accuracy whatever the world turns out to be like: some rival assignment is guaranteed to be closer to the truth. Between taking the truth and going without it lies a continuum of graded, rationally assessable positions, governed by norms derivable from accuracy alone, with no appeal to temperament anywhere in the derivation. Doesn't that dissolve the forced option on which your whole argument depends? If every live hypothesis can be met with a well-governed credence rather than a leap, what work is left for the will to believe?
