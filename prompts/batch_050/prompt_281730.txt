A historian friend ended a long argument with me last night by declaring that "the Renaissance was invented in the nineteenth century," as though that settled whether the Renaissance was a real historical period. I suspect the sentence trades on ambiguity, but I couldn't say exactly where. Please lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making: the near-trivial observation that all period labels are retrospective, since nobody in 1450 dated their letters "the Renaissance"; the stronger historical claim that contemporaries had no self-conception of a rebirth of learning, and that any such sense was projected backward by later writers; the historiographical claim that the period's content and boundaries were drawn to serve a later narrative about the birth of modernity; and the practical claim that scholars should simply retire the label. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or straightforwardly false, and indicate which readings would actually justify ending an argument about the Renaissance's reality rather than merely about our vocabulary. Then note briefly whether the analogous slogans about "the Middle Ages" and "the Scientific Revolution" support the same verdicts, or whether the cases come apart.
