im putting together the final exam for my upper-level philosophy of physics seminar and i need one essay question on noether's theorem and explanation by symmetry. the problem with the usual version of this topic is that students just recite it: continuous symmetry of the action, conserved current, time translation gives energy conservation, done. i want a question they cant answer that way.

the crux i want them to confront: the theorem is a piece of mathematics connecting two things, and under suitable conditions the connection runs in both directions — so why does anyone get to say the symmetry explains the conservation law, rather than the conservation law explaining the symmetry, or neither explaining anything? and the theorem is fussier than the slogan suggests. it wants a symmetry of the action, not just of the equations of motion, and it has failure modes that are philosophically interesting: dissipative systems where the equations are time-translation invariant but energy visibly isnt conserved, and general relativity, where matter on a spacetime with no timelike killing vector has no global energy conservation at all (cmb photons really do lose energy as the universe expands, and it doesn't "go" anywhere). if "energy is conserved because of time-translation symmetry" is supposed to be the paradigm payoff of the theorem, students should have to say what these cases do to the paradigm, and whether "explanation by symmetry" survives contact with the actual statement of the theorem.

so, the deliverables: the exam question itself (something a strong student could answer in about 75 minutes of a 3-hour final), a model answer of around 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit moves, with a one-line note for each on how students typically fumble it. the scheme should make explicit what seperates an A from a B — i.e. which specific moves a B answer is missing — and a student who just recites the slogan version should be looking at a C at best.

one more thing: at the end, tell me where this is most likely to be unfair or just wrong. what consideration or counterargument would make you rewrite the question or reweight the scheme? i'd rather hear it now than in the middle of grading.
