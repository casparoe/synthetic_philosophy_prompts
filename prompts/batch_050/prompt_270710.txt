Okay so I just spent like three hours arguing with my friend about this and we got absolutely nowhere, so I'm outsourcing it. The fight started because I mentioned that a bunch of US states (I want to say close to half?) still have these old "filial responsibility" laws on the books — statutes that can make adult children cover an impoverished parent's nursing home or medical bills, even if the kid hasn't spoken to the parent in decades. Mostly they're not enforced, but every so often a care facility actually sues the kids and wins. My friend said, good, your parents raised you, you owe them, that's just the bill coming due. I said that's insane — I never signed a contract at birth, and the law doesn't seem to care whether your parents were loving or basically checked out, which is a weird foundation for "you owe them."

What I want to know is what these laws actually presuppose, philosophically. What theory of filial obligation do you have to hold for this practice to make sense? My friend's line is basically a debt view — care received creates a repayable obligation. But the laws also have this odd feature where they only kick in when the parent is actually poor, which makes it look less like debt collection and more like the state treating family as an insurer of last resort, and I'm not sure those presuppose the same thing. There's also a pretty well known article by the philosopher Jane English arguing that grown kids don't owe their parents debts at all — what they owe is closer to what friends owe friends, which depends on the actual current relationship and can shrink toward nothing if the relationship is dead. So: which rival accounts of filial duty (debt, gratitude, friendship, special-relationship-whatever) does this legal practice quietly rule out, and which could still license it? Are the laws even internally consistent with the rest of what we do — like, we don't let the state enforce debts between friends, we let parents terminate their obligations via adoption, but kids can't terminate theirs? And what would the law have to look like if we rebuilt it on English's friendship model instead?

One more thing: please be upfront about what's genuinely settled in this literature versus what's still contested among people who work on it. Don't present the friendship model as the consensus if it isn't, and don't present the debt view as dead if it has serious defenders.
