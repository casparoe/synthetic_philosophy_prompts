Next month I've got the Machiavelli week in my big intro political theory survey (90-odd students, mostly non-majors there for the distribution requirement), and I want to do it differently this time, because every year it goes wrong in exactly the same way. Half the room arrives with the pop-culture Machiavelli pre-installed: ends justify means, better feared than loved, history's great villain, etc. I spend the fifty minutes swatting that down, and by the end they've just swapped one cartoon for a second cartoon, roughly "Machiavelli was secretly a warm-hearted republican all along." What never happens is them actually learning to think with the virtù/fortuna framework, which is the only part of the lecture I really care about. The concepts seem simple enough that I keep assuming they'll just get them from the reading, and they definately do not.

So plan the lecture for me — not the content, I know the content, the pedagogy. What's the right order of ideas? My hunch is that something has to be in place before the fortune half of the framework even makes sense to them: maybe the thought that the political world has causal structure that's partly stable and partly not, or that political success and moral goodness can come apart, but you tell me what the real dependency is. Then the carrying example. I've always built the whole thing on the river-and-floods image from the fortune chapter, but I'm no longer sure it's doing the work — it might be too quaint, and it makes fortuna sound like weather, i.e. stuff nobody can do anything about, which is close to the opposite of the point. Should I replace it with a modern case (a startup founder in a recession? a general whose enemy's army mutinies?), and more importantly, what would a good carrying example have to have and have to lack in order to show fortune as something you can partly prepare for and partly master rather than as dumb luck?

Then the misconception map, which is the part I most want. The two I can predict are "virtù just means virtue, so Machiavelli is contradicting himself when he praises cruel but effective rulers" and "fortuna means luck, so he's saying political success is random and skill doesn't matter." Tell me exactly where in the sequence each of those should be headed off, because I've noticed that correcting them after they've formed is nearly hopeless. And flag the confusions I'm probably not anticipating — I suspect a chunk of the room will slide into "so morality is just irrelevant to politics," which is not quite the claim, at least not as I read him. Finally, one exit-slip question I can ask at the end that would tell me whether they've internalized the framework as a tool, rather than just memorized that Machiavelli wasn't a Disney villain.
