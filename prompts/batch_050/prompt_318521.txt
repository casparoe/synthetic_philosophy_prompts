I am drafting a journal article on Descartes' bête-machine doctrine, and I have run into a remark in Part I of the Discourse on the Method (1637) — I am working from John Veitch's 1850 English translation — that I suspect is quietly load-bearing for everything he says about animals in Part V, though it appears long before that discussion, in the middle of his apparently light autobiographical reflections:

"And besides these, I know of no other qualities that contribute to the perfection of the mind; for as to the reason or sense, inasmuch as it is that alone which constitutes us men, and distinguishes us from the brutes, I am disposed to believe that it is to be found complete in each individual; and on this point to adopt the common opinion of philosophers, who say that the difference of greater and less holds only among the accidents, and not among the forms or natures of individuals of the same species."

I need help understanding this passage and its connection to the animal doctrine, and I have four questions. Please answer them drawing on the passage itself, on what is generally known about the Discourse and the scholastic background Descartes is invoking, and on philosophical reasoning — not by directing me to the secondary literature.

First, what exactly is the common opinion of philosophers he is adopting here? I take it to be a scholastic thesis about substantial forms not admitting of degrees, but I want that explained concretely: what does it mean to say that greater and less hold among accidents but not among forms or natures, and what would a counterexample to the thesis have looked like to a seventeenth-century reader trained in the Schools?

Second, there is an apparent tension I cannot resolve. Descartes obviously knew that people differ enormously in intelligence, and in Part V he leans on the fact that even the least capable humans surpass the most capable animals. But if reason is found complete in each individual, then the observable spread in human cleverness must be chalked up to accidents — memory, imagination, the condition of the bodily organs — rather than to reason itself. Is that a coherent position? Spell out what accidental variation in the deliverances of a uniform reason would have to look like, and whether this reading collapses the distinction between having a faculty and being able to exercise it in a way Descartes could accept.

Third, does this passage function as a premise for the Part V conclusion that animals are automata, or merely as a restatement of it? The argumentative difference matters to my article: if reason must be all-or-nothing because forms do not come in degrees, then the absence of full linguistic behavior in animals licenses the inference to the total absence of thought, with no room for an intermediate animal mind. If instead the all-or-nothing claim just follows from the soul's being an indivisible immaterial substance established on independent grounds, the passage is doing no work of its own. Which reading does the text better support, and what else in the Discourse bears on the question?

Fourth, test the passage against a hypothetical: suppose Descartes had been shown an animal that passed his own Part V tests — conversing appropriately, acting from knowledge rather than disposition. On the present passage, is he committed to granting that animal a rational soul complete and entire, identical in kind to ours? If so, the no-degrees thesis makes his position brittle in a specific way: there is no fallback position between beast-machine and full humanity. Is that brittleness a fair criticism, or would he have welcomed it as a mark of a properly binary ontology of mind?

In your answers, flag clearly where the passage itself constrains the interpretation and where competent readers genuinely disagree, so that I know which of my claims need hedging in print.
