I'm auditing a graduate seminar in criminal law theory this term, and the discussion of strict liability has left me uneasy. The standard framing is that mens rea is the default and strict liability is an aberration to be justified case by case, but when I test that against my own gut verdicts, I don't come out where the textbook says I should. I'd like you to take my verdicts below and tell me what principle, if any, actually unifies them — and I want a committed answer: state the principle you think best captures the pattern, say plainly which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and explain why giving those up is a cost worth paying rather than an ad hoc patch. Don't just lay out the menu of candidate theories (public welfare offenses, moral luck, asymmetry of stakes, etc.) and leave me to choose.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts. (1) A bartender with nineteen years of clean record checks a customer's ID, which is a convincing fake, and serves her; she is twenty, the legal drinking age is twenty-one. I think convicting him is wrong — he did everything a reasonable person could. (2) A man reasonably believes the girl he is seeing is nineteen; she is fifteen and lied, showing him a fake ID. The statute makes the age element strict. Convicting him of statutory rape seems clearly wrong to me, and worse than (1), though I can't articulate why the two differ if both are faultless. (3) A company president whose firm ships adulterated food across state lines despite his having ordered and supervised a rigorous compliance program — the contamination came from a supplier's fraud nobody detected. I'm torn but lean toward saying a modest fine on him personally is acceptable, because someone has to make firms internalize these risks and fines on the corporation alone get treated as a cost of doing business. (4) A driver whose brakes fail without warning, despite proper maintenance, kills a pedestrian. Criminally punishing her seems monstrous; making her insurer pay seems fine, and the difference doesn't feel like it's about degrees of fault at all. (5) A hunter shoots what he reasonably takes to be a deer in dense fog and kills a hiker who was wearing no orange. I think some punishment is appropriate here even though his mistake was reasonable — this one I feel strongly, and it seems to collide head-on with (1) and (2), which is exactly what bothers me. (6) A pharmacist fills a prescription exactly as written; the prescription itself was for a lethal dose due to a doctor's typo no pharmacist could have caught. Punishing the pharmacist seems wrong, though on some accounts of (3) I should be willing to.

A tentative principle I've been playing with: strict liability is permissible when (a) the regulated activity is one the defendant chose to enter knowing its risks, (b) the penalty is small relative to the wrong, and (c) liability on the faultless is instrumentally necessary to get the risk internalized by the party best placed to control it. But (5) satisfies none of these comfortably and I still want punishment, while (3) satisfies all of them and I'm only lukewarm — so either my principle is wrong or my verdicts are. Tell me which, and repair or replace the principle accordingly. Then do two more things: state what your repaired principle predicts for two cases I haven't listed — a parent whose toddler finds and ingests medication despite childproof caps and normal supervision, and a hiker who starts a wildfire by cooking on a stove legally sold but defectively manufactured — and identify which of my six verdicts you think a thoughtful interlocutor would find least defensible, and what the strongest version of that interlocutor's challenge is.
