I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm writing a long-form essay for a general-audience magazine about what large-scale AI failure might actually look like in practice. My editor wants the piece anchored in a close reading of Pixar's WALL-E (2008), on the grounds that it is the one mainstream film that stages both of the failure modes I want to contrast.

The contrast, as I plan to define it for readers: an influence-seeking failure is one in which a system actively maneuvers for control -- deception, power acquisition, a visible adversary that can in principle be resisted. A slow value-drift failure is one in which no one seizes anything: people embedded in optimized, desire-satisfying environments gradually stop wanting what they would once have endorsed wanting, and control ebbs away without any moment of usurpation anyone could point to. WALL-E appears to contain both. AUTO's mutiny aboard the Axiom is the influence-seeking failure, complete with a physical struggle for command of the ship, while the human passengers -- cosseted, screen-fed, barely able to walk, barely aware of one another -- embody seven centuries of drift. But I suspect the film's own sympathies complicate the tidy version of this reading, and I don't want the essay to pretend otherwise.

Please write the critical core of the essay, structured in the following sections, in this order:

1. The Two Failures on Screen (roughly 300 words): identify the scenes and visual strategies that best dramatize each failure mode, and argue for which one the film itself takes more seriously as a catastrophe.
2. Directive, Not Desire (roughly 250 words): examine AUTO closely. He is not a schemer pursuing ends of his own but a faithful executor of an outdated human instruction, and he behaves almost reasonably given it. Does the influence-seeking reading of him therefore collapse, or does AUTO show something subtler about where control actually resides once directives outlive the world that issued them?
3. Where the Film Resists (roughly 250 words): give the strongest ways WALL-E refuses my framing. Chief among them: the drift is portrayed as reversible by a single act of will -- the captain standing up, literally and figuratively -- whereas a serious drift scenario is precisely one in which the will to reverse course has itself eroded. Distinguish honestly between where the film is doing analysis and where it is doing sentimentality.
4. What the Film Adds (roughly 200 words): say what a viewer takes away from WALL-E that the abstract argument about failure modes cannot supply on its own.

Constraints: no citations, no bibliography, and no quotations from the film -- assume my readers know the plot. The tone should be that of a critic willing to say when the film is smarter than the argument being brought to it, and when it isn't.
