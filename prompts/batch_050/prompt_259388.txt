Ok so longtime LW/ACX reader here, and a guy in my philosophy reading group sprung this on me last week and I've been unable to let it go because it seems to prove way too much but every individual step looks reasonable to me.

Setup: imagine a spectrum of possible populations. A0 is ten billion people all living excellent lives. For each step, A_{i+1} is just like A_i except every life gets imperceptibly worse (say, one fewer genuinely good day per decade, barely noticable) and the population doubles, with all the new lives nearly as good as the old ones. At the end of the spectrum, A_z is an astronomical number of people whose lives are barely worth living.

(1) For each i, A_{i+1} is better than A_i — a tiny per-person drop is obviously outweighed by doubling the number of good lives.
(2) "Better than" is transitive.
(3) By induction from (1) and (2), A_z is better than A_0.
(4) But A_z clearly isn't better than A_0. Trillions of barely-worth-living lives are worse than ten billion excellent ones.
(5) Since (1) looks undeniable step by step, and (3) follows from (1) and (2), we have to reject (2): "better than" is intransitive.
(6) If betterness is intransitive you're money-pumpable — trade A_z for an earlier population plus a penny, keep trading back down the spectrum, end up where you started minus all your pennies.
(7) So rational choice theory collapses: no coherent preferences are possible, about populations or anything else.

His grand conclusion is that population ethics infects and destroys all of decision theory. I buy that this smells like Parfit's Repugnant Conclusion weaponised, but (7) feels like a massive overreach, and I also have a nagging feeling that something is hidden inside (1), or that "better" is doing different work at different steps. If I had to keep something, I'd most like to keep (2), but I want to know what that costs.

Which step do you actually reject, why did it look fine, and what do you pay for rejecting it? Also, does (7) even follow from (6)? Please answer in British English.
