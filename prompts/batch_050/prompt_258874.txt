Here is an argument I put together for a philosophy of mind reading group, and it seems to prove something absurd, so I assume it goes wrong somewhere -- but each step looked innocent when I wrote it down. (1) To know a proposition is, at least to a good approximation, to rule out the possible ways things could be that are incompatible with it: your knowledge corresponds to a set of possible worlds, and you know p just in case p holds in all of them. (2) Mathematical truths, if true at all, are necessarily true: they hold in every possible world. (3) So every mathematical truth holds in every world compatible with anyone's knowledge, which means everyone knows every mathematical truth -- including whoever is currently stuck on a problem set and including all of us with respect to unresolved conjectures. (4) Worse, an inconsistent set of beliefs rules out every possible world, and a proposition true in zero out of zero worlds counts as true in all of them, so anyone with inconsistent beliefs knows everything whatsoever. (5) These consequences are absurd, so (1) must be false. What troubles me is that something like (1) seems to underpin the best going treatments of information, epistemic logic, and the semantics of knowledge attributions, so rejecting it wholesale feels expensive. Which step do you actually reject? If it is (1), what do you replace it with that still does the work that made it attractive? If you keep (1), which of (2), (3) or (4) breaks, and why did it look like it followed? I would like the diagnosis, the price of each available escape, and a verdict on which escape costs least.
