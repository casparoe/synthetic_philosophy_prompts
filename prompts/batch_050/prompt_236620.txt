I'm a second-year philosophy student, and a disagreement in my logic seminar last week has been nagging at me. We had been working through the MU-puzzle from Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach: you have a tiny formal system with the symbols M, I, and U, four string-rewriting rules, and the question of whether the string MU can be derived starting from MI. Once someone in the room noticed that the only rules affecting the number of I's in a string either double that number or delete I's in blocks of three, it became clear that the I-count's failure to be divisible by three is preserved under every rule application — and since MI contains one I while MU contains none (zero being divisible by three), MU can never be derived. So far, so good. But then one of my classmates drew a much bigger lesson. His claim was that the puzzle captures something essential about the difference between minds and machines: a purely mechanical reasoner confined to the MIU-system could apply the four rules forever without ever concluding that MU is underivable, whereas we, by stepping outside the system and reasoning about it, settle the question in minutes by spotting the invariant. He took this as a small-scale model of why human thought outruns any formal system — there is always a standpoint outside the rules that the rule-follower as such cannot occupy.

I think that lesson is wrong, and I'd like you to argue the case against it. The core of the argument, as I see it, is that the so-called outside perspective is no less mechanical than the inside one: checking the I-count's divisibility by three across rule applications is itself a finite, specifiable, rule-governed procedure, and a suitably equipped theorem-prover could run it as easily as we do. Jumping out of the system doesn't deliver you from mechanism; it relocates you into another rule-governed activity, namely reasoning at the meta-level, which is just another system one could in principle be stuck inside. And if the rejoinder is that one can always jump out of that system too, the argument should explain why this regress of ever-higher meta-levels does not rescue my classmate's conclusion — at no stage of the regress is there a standpoint that is not itself, from some higher vantage point, merely another system with its own rules.

Please also engage seriously with the strongest version of my classmate's position rather than a caricature: his best move, I think, is to concede that any particular jump is rule-governed once made, but to insist that recognizing when a jump is needed, and selecting which of indefinitely many possible meta-levels will prove fruitful, is not itself derivable within any of the systems involved — and that this capacity for well-timed, well-aimed jumping is precisely what minds have and mere systems lack. If the argument is to succeed, it has to say something substantive about whether that selection problem is genuinely different in kind from the rule-following it supposedly transcends, or whether it only looks different because we don't yet see the rules. Finally, having dismantled the inflated moral, please close by stating what the MU-puzzle does legitimately show about the difference between working within a formal system and reasoning about one — because I don't want to overcorrect and deny that there is any philosophically interesting distinction there at all.
