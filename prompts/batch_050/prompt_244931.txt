Last week I posed a puzzle to two friends over dinner, and the argument that followed has been nagging at me. The setup: you are playing a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a perfect psychological duplicate of yourself -- a freshly made copy of your brain that is, right now, deliberating about the same problem by the same process you are. The payoffs are standard (3 each for mutual cooperation, 1 each for mutual defection, 5 for defecting against a cooperator, 0 for cooperating against a defector). There is no communication, no iteration, no enforcement, and no further interaction; the copy is then destroyed or sent far away, take your pick. I asked each of them what rationality recommends, and they disagreed immediately and stayed disagreed.

Mara's opening move was that defection is obviously correct by dominance: whatever the copy does, you end up better off having defected, and rational choice means not leaving payoff on the table. Jonas replied that dominance reasoning is inapplicable here because it requires holding the other player's move fixed while varying yours, and the whole point of the setup is that this cannot be done -- since the copy deliberates by the very same process, the only reachable outcomes are mutual cooperation and mutual defection, so the real choice is between 3 and 1, and you should cooperate. Mara countered that this conflates evidence with causation: your decision does not cause the copy's decision, it merely indicates it, and if the argument is just that cooperating is good news about what the copy is doing, then the puzzle is nothing more than the old evidential-versus-causal decision theory dispute with a sci-fi coat of paint, and causalists already know what they think about that. Jonas said the argument never needed causation or even correlation; it needs only that he cannot coherently deliberate to defection while regarding the copy's cooperation as a live possibility, because his deliberation just is the copy's deliberation -- so the two-move picture dominance relies on simply fails to describe the situation, whatever one's prior decision-theoretic allegiances.

At this point Mara tried a different tack: the argument proves too much. If symmetry of reasoning licenses cooperation against a duplicate, does it license cooperation against someone merely very similar -- same values, same habits of thought, same conclusions nine times out of ten? If yes, there is a continuum of similarity with no principled cutoff, and the view collapses into "cooperate with people who think like you," which is a recipe for being exploited; if no, then the view depends on perfect identity of process, which suggests the "choice" was never really open, and it is odd to give advice about what one should do in a situation where one could not have done otherwise. Jonas answered that the continuum worry confuses two different things: against a merely similar person, your reasoning is fallible evidence about theirs, and ordinary game theory applies with adjusted probabilities; against a genuine duplicate, there are not two events to be correlated in the first place, so no continuum ever gets started.

I've heard this general line of thought called superrationality, but I don't want to know what the literature says -- I want to know who is right about what. Please go through the exchange move by move: Mara's dominance argument, Jonas's reachability objection, Mara's evidence-versus-causation objection, Jonas's reply, Mara's slippery-slope argument, and Jonas's final response. For each, say whether it succeeds, fails, or succeeds only against a weakened version of the opposing view. Then tell me: is this dispute really just the causal-versus-evidential decision theory debate relocated, or does the duplicate setup generate a genuinely new question? And what, if anything, would actually settle it -- is there a fact of the matter here, or two internally coherent notions of rationality talking past each other?
