For the past three years I've run a small philosophy society at my university, and next month it's my turn to lead the session. I chose consequentialist cluelessness -- the problem that, because almost any action ripples forward to alter which future people exist and what happens to them, we may be systematically unable to estimate which available act has the best consequences, even in mundane cases. My audience is second- and third-year undergraduates who have done exactly one ethics course: they know the maximizing structure of act consequentialism, they've done trolley cases and Singer's pond, and a few have absorbed the effective-altruist habit of treating expected-value calculation as the obvious answer to any epistemic difficulty. I have about an hour, and I do not want to stand up and lecture the problem at them; I want a plan for how the session's explanation should unfold.

Please design that plan rather than giving me the explanation itself. Specifically, I want: (1) the dependency order -- which ideas must be securely in place before the problem can even be stated, and why each has to precede the next (I'm assuming the causal reach of trivial actions, including effects on the timing of conception and hence on the identity of future persons, has to be made vivid before the epistemic collapse will seem like anything more than ordinary ignorance, but tell me if the sequencing should differ); (2) the one example that should carry the whole session -- I was considering building it around an everyday choice like driving across town to a party, whose traffic effects ramify into who conceives which children decades later, but I'm worried that framing invites the "just run the expected values" response too early, so advise me on what the carrying case needs to exhibit and what it must avoid suggesting; (3) the misconception map -- the objections these particular students will raise, in roughly the order they'll raise them (my guesses: that this is just ordinary uncertainty and expected value handles it; that better forecasting would dissolve it; that it's simply a reductio of consequentialism rather than a challenge to it; and that cluelessness symmetrically favors inaction or the status quo), together with the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than answered ad hoc at the end; (4) a closing check that would show they've grasped that the problem is meant to undermine our grip on which act is right at all, not merely to recommend epistemic modesty about our estimates; and (5) what I should openly bracket for this audience -- I'm inclined to exclude formal treatments of imprecise credences and the technical nonidentity literature, but I want to be told what to leave out and how to say so without making the problem seem artificial.
