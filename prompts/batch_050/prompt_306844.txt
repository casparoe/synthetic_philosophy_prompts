I am a retired molecular spectroscopist who spent forty years using quantum mechanics as an instrument without ever worrying about what it describes, and who has spent the last three years, in retirement, reading the foundations literature I once waved away. I have become stuck on one dispute in particular, and I would like a diagnosis of the dispute itself rather than another argument for one side.

The dispute is over whether the wavefunction is real. On one side are the psi-ontic views: the wavefunction, or something isomorphic to it, is part of physical reality. On the other are the psi-epistemic views: the wavefunction encodes an agent's knowledge or information about an underlying reality whose actual state it does not uniquely determine. The formal side of the debate is usually framed by the ontological-models classification: a model is psi-ontic if every preparation of a distinct pure quantum state corresponds to a distinct physical state, and psi-epistemic if distinct quantum states can correspond to overlapping distributions over the underlying ontic states. The case for psi-epistemic views has always struck me as intuitively strong: toy models with overlapping distributions reproduce a surprising number of quantum phenomena -- the impossibility of distinguishing non-orthogonal states, something like interference, something like teleportation -- from a simple restriction on what can be known. And the case against seemed, for a while, to have been settled by the PBR theorem of Pusey, Barrett, and Rudolph, which rules out psi-epistemic models given an assumption of preparation independence.

Yet the debate plainly did not end. Defenders of epistemic views attack the preparation-independence assumption, or retreat to frameworks the theorem does not touch, or adopt retrocausal or perspectival machinery that resists the classification altogether. Meanwhile some writers on both sides now question whether the ontic/epistemic dichotomy carves the space of interpretations at its joints at all -- pointing out that Everettians, Bohmians, and relationalists each get sorted awkwardly, and that the classification presupposes a measurement-independent ontic state that several live views deny.

Here is my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the debate keeps stalling because a formal question and a metaphysical question are being run under one heading. The formal question -- can an ontological model of a specified structure reproduce quantum statistics with overlapping epistemic states? -- is the kind of thing that admits of theorems, and the theorems keep arriving. The metaphysical question -- is the wavefunction the sort of thing that could be knowledge, given that it collapses, that it lives in configuration space rather than physical space, and that it plays a dynamical role nothing else called information plays? -- is not touched by those theorems, yet each new no-go result is announced as if it settled both.

Is this the right diagnosis? I would like you to tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what the two camps in fact say: is it substantive or partly verbal; is the hidden work being done by divergent standards of what a physical explanation must deliver; is it really several different questions (about ontology, about explanation, about the status of agents) sharing one label? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a further formal result, a distinction that dissolves part of the dispute, a change in the question -- or an argument that the impasse is itself evidence that the ontic/epistemic framing should be abandoned.
