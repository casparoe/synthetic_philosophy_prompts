Last week my reading group (we've been doing AI safety stuff this term, though I'm the token philosophy student so half the technical discussion goes over my head) got into a surprisingly heated argument about what "the alignment problem" even is, and I want an outside verdict. Maya's position was that alignment is at least three separate problems that get lumped together: first, getting a system to actually do what its operators intend in novel situations rather than just on the training distribution; second, keeping it aligned as it gets more capable or gets modified and retrained, since alignment doesn't automatically survive scaling; and third, aligning the deployers themselves, because a perfectly obedient powerful system in the hands of reckless or malicious humans is still a catastrophe. Her complaint was that treating it as one problem makes people talk past each other and misallocates talent -- someone great at interpretability might have nothing to contribute to the third one.

Daniel pushed back hard and I couldn't tell who was winning. He said Maya's taxonomy smuggles in its own conclusion. The third problem, he said, isn't an AI problem at all -- it's ordinary politics and ethics, thousands of years old, and relabeling it "alignment" is just branding. And the first two aren't really separate either: the only reason stability under capability gain matters is that we can't robustly specify intent, so if you genuinely solved problem one, problem two comes for free, since an intent-aligned system would itself want to stay aligned. Maya replied that wanting to stay aligned doesn't guarantee being able to -- humans want to keep their values too and still get corrupted, drift, or rationalize, and a self-modifying system could break its own values while trying to preserve them. Daniel said that just makes it a capability problem -- can the system verify its own changes -- not an alignment one. Maya said that was question-begging, since the whole dispute is about where the capability/alignment line even falls. By that point the moderator moved us on and it never got resolved.

Can you go through the exchange point by point and say who's right at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? Where either of them is conflating things or missing an obvious move the other could've made, flag that too. And then give your own take on the underlying question: is alignment one problem or several, and does anything practical actually hang on the answer -- e.g. how a small safety team should divide its effort, or whether "solving alignment" is even a coherent goal to announce? I don't want a reading list, I want the actual arguments sorted out.
