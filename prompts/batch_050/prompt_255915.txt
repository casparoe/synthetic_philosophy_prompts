I am working through the philosophical literature on moral testimony — the question of whether, and why, forming a moral belief simply because someone told you that p seems epistemically or morally defective in a way that forming an ordinary empirical belief on testimony does not. Before I commit to any of the positions on offer, I want the specification first. Write the desiderata that an adequate account of moral testimony must meet, taking the purpose of the account to be normative guidance: telling us when, if ever, one ought to form moral views on another's say-so rather than working them out for oneself.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it. At minimum, I want the list to address the following:

- The core asymmetry to be explained: why deferring to a friend on whether it is wrong to lie to a dying relative feels suspect, while deferring to the same friend on whether the bridge ahead is closed does not — and whether the account should treat this asymmetry as a datum to be vindicated or an intuition to be explained away.
- Cases the account must get right: the child who acquires moral beliefs almost entirely from parents and teachers; the agent who does the right thing on advice but cannot say what makes it right; the person with no independent view who takes a trusted interlocutor's word on an unfamiliar moral question; and the distinction between treating testimony as evidence to weigh and treating it as settling the matter outright.
- Distinctions the account must not collapse: moral knowledge versus moral understanding; testimony about first-order moral claims versus testimony about what one's peers or tradition believe; the epistemic deficiency of deference versus its moral deficiency, if these come apart.
- Findings the account cannot contradict: that testimony is in general a legitimate and indispensable source of knowledge, and that apparent episodes of moral progress (in how societies came to regard slavery, say, or the treatment of animals) seem sometimes to have proceeded by people accepting moral claims on the word of others rather than by independent reflection.
- A harder case I have not seen treated in the literature: the scrupulous deferrer — someone who, out of intellectual humility, systematically outsources every contested moral judgment to the considered consensus of people they take to be more thoughtful than themselves, and who by ordinary measures ends up with admirable views. State explicitly whether an adequate theory must condemn this practice, merely diagnose it as epistemically suboptimal, or license it — and say which desideratum forces the verdict.

Mark which desiderata you take to be non-negotiable and which are the signature demand of one camp in the debate (roughly, pessimists who hold that moral deference is generally problematic versus optimists who deny this). Flag any pair or subset of desiderata that appear to be jointly unsatisfiable, since if the specification itself is inconsistent that is the result I most care about. Finally — and only briefly, since the criteria are the deliverable — indicate how the main families of accounts in this area (those grounded in the special value of moral understanding, those grounded in the alleged unreliability of moral testimony, and those grounded in considerations of autonomy or authenticity) fare against the list.
