I'm in my final year of secondary school and co-run our school's philosophy society, which has about fourteen members aged sixteen to eighteen and meets for seventy-five minutes once a week. We've spent the last two sessions on Kant's ethics, working through the case of the lying promise and the test of whether a maxim can be universalized. The discussion went badly in an interesting way: one member argued that the test is toothless, because you can always describe what you're doing narrowly enough that universalizing it causes no problem -- not lying when convenient, but lying to this particular person on this particular afternoon in these circumstances. Another replied that the test only bites if you're forced to state your maxim honestly, at which point it seems to rest on a rule about how you're allowed to describe your own actions, which struck several people as cheating. I think they're both circling something real, and I'd rather have them collide with it than have me explain it.

So: design a game for next week's session, no lecture beforehand and no mention of Kant's name until the debrief, in which some participants try to get self-serving maxims past a universalization procedure and others try to break those maxims by universalizing them. I want the rules and scoring (points or tokens, not money) arranged so that players discover for themselves, in play, the two things my club stumbled into: that some maxims collapse because the practice they rely on couldn't exist if everyone acted on them, while others fail only in the weaker sense that no one could consistently will the resulting world -- and that the whole game hinges on who controls the level of detail at which a maxim gets stated, so the group feels the pull of gerrymandered descriptions and the need for a principled constraint on them. Give me concrete rules, roles, a scoring scheme that creates real (small) stakes, a round-by-round timeline for the seventy-five minutes, and the exact constraint on maxim-formulation you'd have the enforcers apply, since that's clearly the load-bearing piece. Then a debrief script that maps what happened in each phase onto the distinction between the two kinds of contradiction and onto the maxim-specification problem, plus a frank list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (students treating it as a debate exercise, collusion between roles, the game stalling if someone refuses to commit to a description) and how to handle each.

Please commit to one specific design rather than offering a menu of variants, and give your reasons for the choices you make -- especially the scoring incentives and the maxim-formulation rule, where I'd like to know why your version beats the obvious alternatives. I'd also like you to be honest at the end about which feature of Kant's actual view the game cannot reproduce (for instance, I suspect it will make the test look like a matter of bad consequences rather than of what rational willing requires) and what I'll have to supply in the debrief to compensate, rather than overselling the activity.
