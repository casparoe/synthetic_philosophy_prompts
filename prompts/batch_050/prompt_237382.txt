As a journalist who has covered artificial intelligence and the alignment field for the better part of a decade, I keep returning to one debate that seems permanently stuck: whether aligning AI systems with "idealized" human preferences is a coherent research program at all. The proposal, in its general form, is old and well known: rather than training systems on our actual, messy, inconsistent preferences, we should aim at what we would want if we were better informed, more reflective, more consistent -- some idealized extrapolation of current human values. Every few years a new paper or essay cycle restages the same confrontation. One side insists the proposal is empty or circular: any procedure for idealizing preferences has to decide which of our dispositions count as distortions to be corrected and which count as values to be preserved, and those decisions cannot themselves be read off our actual preferences without smuggling in exactly the substantive normative judgments the idealization was supposed to deliver. The other side replies that this is just a demand for an impossible level of precision at the conceptual stage, that idealization is something moral psychology and ordinary reflective deliberation already do informally, and that the alternative -- deferring to unidealized, manipulable, incoherent revealed preferences -- is strictly worse. Neither side seems to have moved. The critics keep producing variants of the same circularity argument; the defenders keep reiterating that the objection proves too much, since it would indict ordinary moral reflection too.

I am writing a long-form piece on why this particular impasse persists when so much else in the field has changed. My working hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis, is that the two sides are disagreeing about whether an idealized preference is something discovered -- in which case the critics' demand for a specification is fair and the defenders owe one -- or something constructed by the idealization procedure itself -- in which case demanding that the output be certified by pre-idealization preferences misunderstands the proposal from the start. But I could also imagine the real fault line being elsewhere: a disagreement about how much normative theory must be settled before an engineering program can legitimately begin, or about whether reflective endorsement under idealized conditions is even empirically stable enough to be a target.

Diagnose the disagreement for me. Using what each side actually argues -- not caricatures -- tell me whether this is a substantive philosophical dispute, a partly verbal one resting on divergent uses of terms like preference, value, and idealization, a hidden empirical disagreement about the psychology of reflection, or two parties answering different questions under one heading. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a formal result, an empirical finding, or a reframing of the problem -- or explain why, on your diagnosis, no such resolution should be expected.
