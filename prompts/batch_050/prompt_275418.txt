I've been defending the idea of supererogation in a reading group and someone hit me with this argument and I haven't managed to shake it off. (1) Whenever you've got several options, you morally ought to take the best one available. (2) If you morally ought to do something, then not doing it is wrong -- that's just what 'ought' means. (3) So whenever there's a best option on the table, anything short of it is wrong. (4) A supererogatory act is supposed to be good -- better than required -- but not required: skipping it isn't wrong. (5) So there are no supererogatory acts. Worse, (6) since there's almost always a better option than whatever any of us actually does, pretty much everything we do is wrong. But surely the person who runs into a burning building to pull out a stranger, at real risk to themself, isn't merely doing their duty -- nobody would be blamed for staying outside. So one of these has to be false and I genuinely can't see which. I badly want to keep the burning-building intuition, and if forced I'd rather drop (1) than (2), but every option feels like it costs something I thought I understood. Tell me which premise to give up, explain why it looked so safe in the first place, and price the alternatives -- including what the bullet-biters on each side end up saying about heroes, saints, and the rest of us. British English in your answer please.
