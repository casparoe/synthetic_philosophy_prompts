I've covered bioethics for a long time, and one dispute I keep watching go in circles is the fight over the therapy/enhancement distinction. The pattern at every conference is identical: everyone concedes the boundary is vague — vaccinations, eyeglasses, growth hormone for short but healthy children, stimulants for students without a diagnosis all sit awkwardly on it — and yet one camp keeps insisting the distinction marks no moral boundary while the other insists something essential is lost if we abandon it. Twenty years on, nobody has moved. I don't want a verdict on enhancement; I want a diagnosis of the impasse itself. Is this a verbal disagreement, with the two camps using "enhancement" to answer different questions — one about the concept of health and disease, the other about what medicine is for? Is it a disguised empirical disagreement about how enhancement technologies would actually distribute in practice? Or is it a collision of unargued starting intuitions about the normal and the natural, dressed up as conceptual analysis? My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the debate survives because each side needs the distinction to do a different job — one side wants a line usable for policy and reimbursement, the other wants a thesis about human nature — and so they never actually meet. Test that against what each side really says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a sharper account of disease, an agreement to split the question into two, or a case that the question itself is ill-posed.
