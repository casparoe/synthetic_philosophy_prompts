So some background: I'm a retired molecular biologist (protein folding, mostly) who got into philosophy about three years ago when my wife signed us up for a reading group at the local university and I couldn't get out of it. Now I've somehow agreed to give a talk to that same group, which has grown to include a few actual philosophers, including two philosophy-of-biology people who I suspect know more about the replicator/interactor debate than I ever will.

My talk defends a version of the memetic theory of culture, but with a twist I've been developing, and I want to know if the twist actually buys me anything or just sounds clever. The standard meme picture, as I understand it, treats cultural items as replicators analogous to genes, spread by imitation, often with a vaguely parasitic or viral connotation. My twist: I argue the better biological analogy for a meme isn't a virus or a gene but a domesticated organism or an endosymbiont -- something like gut bacteria or cultivated wheat. The idea being that most cultural replicators co-evolved with us over so long that the host/symbiont line is blurry, they're mostly beneficial or neutral rather than predatory, and thinking of them as domesticates explains why their "fitness" is so entangled with ours, which the virus picture can't do. My pitch is that this reframing survives the usual objections to memetics (no clean genotype/phenotype split, fuzzy copying fidelity, no discrete units) because domestication in biology is also messy in exactly those ways, and yet nobody denies dogs and wheat are real evolved things.

The audience: the two phil-of-bio people mentioned, a couple of continental-leaning folks who will be suspicious of scientism on principle, a Wittgenstein person, and some generalists. No one else with a biology background. The talk is 30 minutes, then Q&A.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections I'll actually get from this particular crowd -- not the ones a genetics seminar would ask -- ordered roughly by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do. Phrase each one the way that kind of philosopher would actually put it (the phil-of-bio people will talk about replicator theory and lineage formation; the Wittgenstein person won't). For each: what should I concede gracefully, what should I hold the line on, and a reply I can deliver in about two minutes. Flag where my replies are soft.

Also tell me honestly whether the domestication analogy is doing real work or whether it's just a prettier metaphor with the same problems underneath. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, because I'm sure someone will.
