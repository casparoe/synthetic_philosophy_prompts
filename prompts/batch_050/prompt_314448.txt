Okay so this got real for me last week: my roommate found out our landlord is running an unpermitted apartment in the basement, and he told the guy he'd report it to the city unless he got a rent cut. I said dude that's literally blackmail, and he said both things are legal, reporting code violations is legal, asking for a rent cut is legal, so where's the crime. And I actually couldn't answer him. So now I've been stress-testing my own intuitions and I want to know what principle, if any, is behind them:

(1) The roommate case: wrong, clearly blackmail to me.
(2) Same roommate, but instead he says: I'll report the unit unless you fix the mold in the other apartments and stop jacking up everyone's rent. This feels completely fine, maybe even admirable. Same threat, different demand.
(3) A journalist finds out a company is polluting and tells them: pay me $50k or I publish. Feels way more wrong than (4) below, even though publishing is legal and striking is legal.
(4) A union says: raise our wages or we strike. Totally fine.
(5) A paparazzo offers a celebrity first refusal on embarrassing photos -- pay me what the tabloids would, and I won't sell to them. Framed as a generous offer, not a threat, and it still feels exactly like blackmail to me. So the offer/threat framing doesn't seem to do the work.
(6) Threatening to quit your job unless you get a raise: fine, obviously.

So my demand-for-money verdicts are inconsistent (3 vs 4), my demand verdicts are inconsistent (1 vs 2 vs 6), and framing as an offer doesn't launder anything (5). What's the principle here, stated precisely enough that it actually sorts cases? Which of my six verdicts does it force me to give up, and if the answer is "several," tell me honestly which ones are most expendable. Also: what does the resulting principle say about threatening to leave a one-star review of a restaurant unless they refund your meal? I genuinely can't predict where it lands on that one.
