So I'm teaching a grad seminar this fall on explanation in the life sciences, and we've spent the last couple weeks on the Machamer-Darden-Craver tradition -- mechanisms as organized collections of entities and activities whose operations regularly produce some phenomenon, with all the usual examples (protein synthesis, the sodium-potassium pump, long-term potentiation, etc.). In the course of prepping I started writing what was supposed to be a short blog post and it has ballooned into something between an essay and a rant. Before I inflict it on anyone, I'd love comments. I'm especially unsure about (a) whether the dormancy case actually puts pressure on the activities-centered picture or whether there's an obvious reply I'm missing, (b) whether the individuation argument in the third paragraph equivocates between token mechanisms and mechanism types, and (c) whether the whole thing is interesting or whether I've just rediscovered something everyone in this literature already knows and has a name for. Be blunt. Here's the draft:

---

THE QUIET MECHANISM PROBLEM

The mechanistic tradition in philosophy of biology tells us that to explain a phenomenon is to show how it's produced by a mechanism: entities doing things, organized in the right way, running from some set-up condition to some termination condition. The emphasis on activities -- on the entities actually doing stuff -- is usually presented as the view's great virtue. It's what supposedly saves the account from the emptiness of just citing dispositions or capacities. A mechanism doesn't explain because its parts are disposed to produce the phenomenon; it explains because the parts are busy producing it, right now, in an organized fashion.

But consider dormancy. A tardigrade in the tun state has, as far as anyone can tell, essentially no metabolism going on. Nothing is happening inside it, and that's precisely how it survives being boiled, frozen, irradiated, and launched into space. Or take a seed sitting in a seed bank for fifty years, or a bacterial spore, or a quiescent stem cell that just sits in its niche not dividing. These are paradigm cases of biological phenomena that cry out for explanation -- biologists really do want to know how the tardigrade survives desiccation -- and yet the explanatory work seems to be done not by what the system's parts are doing but by what they're conspicuously not doing, plus how they're arranged. The trehalose glass just sits there. Nothing is "productive of regular changes" because there are no regular changes. If the activity-centered picture is right, it looks like the most impressive survival trick in the animal kingdom either has no mechanistic explanation or has one only in some degenerate, promissory sense.

Now, the mechanist has an obvious move: say that dormancy is explained by the mechanisms that produced the dormant state (the tun-formation machinery, the sporulation cascade) and the mechanisms that will be reactivated on exit (germination, recovery). But this creates a strange problem of individuation. If mechanisms are individuated by their activities and their start and end conditions, then what exactly is the thing that persisted through the fifty years in the seed vault? On a strict reading, no mechanism existed during that time -- there was just a pile of organized stuff. But then it seems wrong to say the mechanism of germination explains how the seed survived, since that mechanism didn't exist yet, and it's equally wrong to say the seed's survival was explained by a mechanism that stopped existing decades ago. We seem forced to say either that mechanisms can exist while doing nothing (which abandons the activity emphasis in all but name, and collapses the view back toward "organized stuff with dispositions"), or that dormancy survival is a genuine biological phenomenon with no mechanistic explanation at all (which seems like a serious incompleteness in a framework that advertises itself as capturing how biologists actually explain things).

There's a third option I find more interesting: treat persistence itself as something that sometimes needs producing. On this reading, the lesson of dormancy isn't that mechanisms can be idle but that the framework misidentifies the phenomenon. The explanandum was never "survival through the frozen decades"; it's "resumption of function on rehydration," and that is produced by a mechanism that runs continuously -- it's just that its continuous operation, in the dormant period, consists largely in nothing going wrong. Absence of perturbation isn't activity, though. A guard who falls asleep still counts as guarding, legally speaking, but we might wonder whether "guarding" names an activity or a status. My suspicion is that the mechanist needs a notion of "working constancy" that the original entities-and-activities picture doesn't have the resources to state, and that supplying it pushes the view toward something more structural than its founders intended. But I haven't worked out whether this is a friendly amendment or a replacement.
