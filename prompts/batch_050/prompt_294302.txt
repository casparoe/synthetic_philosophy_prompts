I'm applying for a one-semester undergraduate research fellowship in my philosophy department (the expected output is a paper of roughly 6,000 words) and I need a two-page proposal. My topic is Harsanyi's aggregation theorem — both the version built on expected utility theory plus an ex ante Pareto condition, and the later impartial-observer construction. The standard presentation I keep encountering treats the theorem as nearly a proof of utilitarianism: if individuals are rational, society is rational, and society respects unanimous individual preferences, then social welfare must be a weighted sum of individual utilities. My half-formed suspicion is that this reading mislocates where the normative work happens. The theorem's conclusion is additive separability given a fixed cardinalization of each person's utility, but that cardinalization is not unique — rescale one person's utility function and the weights shift correspondingly — so the move from "some weighted sum represents social welfare" to "sum everyone's utility with equal weights" seems to require an independent premise about interpersonal comparability or impartiality that the axioms themselves don't supply. Meanwhile, the ex ante Pareto condition appears to do genuinely contestable work of a different kind: it forces society to evaluate prospects by each person's ex ante expected utility, thereby ruling out ex post fairness concerns (for instance, preferring a fair lottery over a certainty when the outcomes differ across people). I want to know which of these two pressure points — the comparability/cardinalization gap or the ex ante Pareto condition — is where a critic of the utilitarian conclusion should actually push, and whether the impartial-observer version of the theorem dodges the comparability problem or merely relocates it.

Please turn this into a research proposal. Don't include citations or a bibliography — I'll add those myself.
