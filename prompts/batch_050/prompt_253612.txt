I'm co-leading a small reading group that's spent the term on utilitarianism and its critics, and our final meeting is on the demandingness objections. I want to end the session by reading aloud a poem, and the conceit I'd like is the utility monster itself — the creature Nozick invented that gets so much more pleasure out of any good than we do that total utilitarianism seems to require handing everything over to it: our food, our savings, eventually our hours and our choices. I'm less interested in a poem that mocks the monster as obviously absurd than in one that lets it make its case, because the uncomfortable thing about the creature is that it doesn't cheat — it just claims, sincerely or not, to feel more, and the theory has no principled way to say no.

Here's the structural constraint, which is the whole point: I want the poem to enact the distribution problem. Give me a sequence of stanzas spoken by the monster, where each stanza is longer than the last — say starting from a single couplet and growing from there — so that the monster literally takes up more and more of the page the way it takes up more and more of the world's goods, until the poem ends with a form of its own exhaustion: either the final stanza trails off because there's nothing left to ask for, or it contracts back to almost nothing because there's no one left to answer it. Within the stanzas I'd like the monster to deploy the actual moves a philosopher would recognize: the appeal to equal consideration of interests turned against itself, the observation that squeamishness about its appetites is just partiality dressed up as principle, maybe a swipe at the thought that its pleasures could be discounted for being "monstrous." The poem should leave open whether the monster is a reductio of total utilitarianism or merely an especially vivid instance of demandingness we'd face anyway — I'd rather the ending not settle that.

Since it's a creative piece, there's no verdict to grade, but I'd like a short note after the poem flagging where it takes liberties with the philosophical material — for instance, whether it quietly slides between total and average views, or assumes the monster's pleasures are commensurable with ours in a way the literature doesn't take for granted. Say how confident you are that the poem's central conceit tracks the real philosophical pressure point rather than a caricature of it, and single out the one line or move you're least sure a specialist would accept.
