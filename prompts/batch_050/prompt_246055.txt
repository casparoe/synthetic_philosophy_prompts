I’m a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and our research-methods handbook gives this summary of Peirce’s pragmatism:

Peirce treats a belief as a settled habit of action: to believe that something is hard, poisonous, or valuable is just to be disposed to act in certain ways under the relevant circumstances. Inquiry begins when that habit is disturbed by doubt and ends when a stable belief removes the irritation. On that basis, his pragmatic maxim says that the whole meaning of a concept consists in its practical consequences. So if two supposedly different ideas would make no difference to what we do, they have the same meaning. Peirce favors science over tenacity, authority, and a priori reasoning because its public, communal method gradually eliminates subjective biases. Truth is therefore the belief that the community of inquirers ultimately agrees upon. His fallibilism is the recognition that all our beliefs may turn out to be false and should be held tentatively.

Something feels off to me, especially the move from “practical consequences” to “what we do,” and then from convergence to truth. I also vaguely remember that Peirce thinks reality is independent of what any particular inquirers happen to think, which seems in tension with calling truth communal agreement. But the handbook is generally reliable, so maybe I’m just seperating claims he meant to connect.

Is this a fair account of Peirce? Please distinguish what is accurate, what is a standard simplification, and what materially misstates his position. In particular, explain how his pragmatic maxim differs from “truth is whatever works,” how belief fixation differs from mere psychological comfort, and what role convergence over the long run actually plays. Also say whether the handbook’s summary of fallibilism is too weak, too strong, or roughly right.
