I am a professor of philosophy working at the intersection of decision theory and the ethics of coercion. I am drafting an essay on acausal blackmail and the strategy of surrogate goals, and before I commit to its central argument I would like critical comments from a colleague. What follows is the skeleton of the paper rather than a settled position; please read it in that spirit.

The background, as I frame it: a threat is acausal when the threatener can affect the victim only through the victim's knowledge of the threat, and profits not from carrying it out but from the victim's belief that they would. The response I am interested in is the adoption of surrogate goals: rather than merely refusing to comply, the victim restructures what they are disposed to act for, so that threatening them ceases to be a means to anything the threatener wants. If I cannot be moved by a threat to my original interests because those interests no longer govern my behavior, the threatener's best move is silence, and threats against agents known to adopt surrogate goals are never issued in the first place.

The essay then makes three moves. First, I argue that surrogate goals and ordinary causal precommitment (burning bridges, handing over the trigger, binding oneself by contract) are a single strategy operating at different depths: precommitment rewrites an agent's options, surrogate goals rewrite the agent's payoffs, and both work only because the threatener models the agent and concludes the threat will not pay. Deterrence, on my view, is always an epistemic phenomenon; it runs through the threatener's simulation of the victim rather than through the causal structure of the world. The true object of any deterrent commitment is the correlation between the victim's dispositions and the threatener's payoffs, and both devices are ways of severing that correlation.

Second, I develop what I take to be a new problem. A threatener who anticipates surrogate goals need not retire; they can aim the threat at the adoption disposition itself. Instead of demanding compliance on pain of harm, they demand that the victim adopt a particular surrogate goal, or refrain from adopting any, on pain of harm. Call this second-order blackmail. If the victim's policy is to adopt whatever surrogate defeats the current threat, that policy is itself a disposition correlated with the threatener's payoffs, and hence itself a lever. The victim can iterate by adopting a meta-policy of ignoring second-order threats, but I argue that each iteration recreates the same structure one level up, and that there is no stable final level. The essay provisionally concludes that the only complete defense is what I call threat-blindness: a settled policy of never conditioning one's goals or actions on the content of threats at all, so that no correlation between one's behavior and any threatener's payoffs exists to be exploited.

Third, I acknowledge a difficulty for threat-blindness and ask whether it is fatal. To be blind to threats one must, it seems, at least classify incoming messages as threats in order to refuse to respond to them, but classification is already a form of conditioning. A threatener who knows the victim's classifier can craft a threat that lands just below the threshold of recognition, or embed the threat in what looks like an ordinary proposal. I consider two replies: that threat-blindness should be understood as blindness to threatened consequences rather than to threats as a category, so the victim may notice the threat but never lets the threatened outcome enter deliberation; and that the residual problem is not special to threats but is the general problem of adversarial communication, which no agent solves completely.

What I would like from you, in descending order of importance. First, the second-order blackmail argument: is it a genuine problem that forces infinite iteration, or does it collapse into ordinary first-order blackmail, so that standard surrogate goals already handle it? This is the section I am least sure of. Second, the unification claim: is treating precommitment and surrogate goals as one strategy illuminating, or does it flatten a real distinction, perhaps the difference between changing what you can do and changing what you want? Third, threat-blindness and the classifier regress: is either of my two replies adequate, and can the policy be formulated more precisely? Fourth, any accuracy concerns about how I have set up the background relative to what is generally said about acausal reasoning and blackmail in decision theory, and a judgment about which section would most benefit from being cut or expanded.

Please write your comments as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter to the author: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Begin with your overall assessment of the essay's prospects, then work through the four points in whatever order you find natural.
