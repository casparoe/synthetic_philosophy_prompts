In the United States, federal agencies issuing major regulations are generally required to produce a cost-benefit analysis: they assign probabilities to the outcomes a rule might bring about, attach monetary values to those outcomes — including a dollar figure for each statistical life saved — and discount future effects back to the present, so that a regulation is justified only if its expected benefits exceed its expected costs. As a journalist who covers science policy, I want to understand what this machinery quietly assumes. What theory of uncertainty does the practice presuppose — in particular, does it assume that all uncertainty can be represented by precise numerical probabilities, and what happens to the analysis in cases like climate tipping points or novel biotechnologies, where reasonable experts disagree not merely about the numbers but about which probability distributions are even plausible? Could the practice accommodate imprecise probabilities — sets or ranges of distributions rather than single values — while remaining cost-benefit analysis in any recognizable sense? And if we instead built regulation on decision rules designed for deep uncertainty, such as maximin or robust satisficing, what would actually have to change about how rules are evaluated and defended?
