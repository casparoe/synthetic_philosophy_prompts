Ok so here's my situation. I just started as the one-person "data team" at a mid-sized logistics company (grocery delivery routing), and my manager keeps asking me for "objective" numbers on stuff where we have basically no data -- probability a new vendor's trucks show up on time, odds a new route actually saves time, that kind of thing. When I say there's no objective probability without data, she sends me back links about the principle of maximum entropy and "rational priors" and tells me Jaynes settled this decades ago.

So I've now actually read a bit, and I get the basic pitch: given constraints (like a known mean), pick the distribution that maximizes entropy, because anything else smuggles in assumptions you don't have. Fine. What I need is not another retelling of the pitch but a procedure I can actually run, maybe 2-3 times a week, roughly an hour per decision, that answers:

1. When is maxent even applicable? What counts as a legitimate "constraint"? My boss thinks "3 on-time deliveries out of 5 so far" is a constraint. My understanding is that maxent was built for constraints that are genuinely fixed (like a physical theory pinning down average energy), and that sample frequencies are a different beast entirely. Who's right, and what should the procedure do with small samples?

2. How do I set up the possibility space in the first place? This is the part that broke my brain: maximize entropy over "truck late vs on time" and you get one prior; maximize it over "delay <10 min / 10-30 / >30" and you get a different one. Same ignorance, different partition, different answer. Is there a principled way to choose the partition, or is this where the "objectivity" quietly dies? I know Jaynes had transformation-group arguments that solve the classic Bertrand-style cases -- does that machinery even apply to boring business questions like mine, or only to problems with nice symmetries?

3. What do I do when the maxent answer conflicts with rival "objective" proposals like Jeffreys priors or reference priors? Is there a meta-rule for choosing, or do I just pick one and document why?

4. How quickly should the maxent prior stop mattering once real data comes in?

Please give me this as an actual ordered checklist with a couple of test questions per step, then walk it through two concrete cases: one where it produces a clean defensible answer, and one where the honest output is something my boss will definately hate (like "there is no objective prior here, either gather data or admit you're guessing"). Also flag clearly which steps are real decision rules and which steps are secretly "an experienced statistician makes a judgment call and signs their name" -- I suspect several are the latter and I'd rather know upfront which. Casual tone is fine, this isn't going in a whitepaper.
