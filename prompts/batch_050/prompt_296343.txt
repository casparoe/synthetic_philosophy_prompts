I'm a retired atmospheric chemist (forty years of it) and over the past year or so I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole, and lately I've been reading about machine ethics -- the project of actually building ethical reasoning into computers and robots rather than just arguing about what they ought to do. What strikes me is that everyone seems to pick their favorite case -- a self-driving car choosing between two bad outcomes, a chatbot saying something offensive -- and argue from there, and I keep thinking that in my old field we'd have written the specification first. So that's what I want from you: the desiderata. What would any adequate scheme for implementing ethical reasoning in a machine actually have to deliver? Which situations does it have to get right (not just emergencies -- I suspect the mundane stuff, like how an algorithm treats people day in and day out, matters more), which distinctions can it not collapse (a machine that rigidly follows rules versus one that can be corrected, or doing what humans approve of versus doing what's actually right), and which facts must it stay consistent with -- engineering facts, like that you can't verify what you can't specify, and psychological ones, like how humans actually come to have their moral habits in the first place?

Give me the case or consideration behind each requirement, and tell me which are genuine constraints and which are just the hobbyhorses of particular camps. The part I most want, though, is this: flag any pair of requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied. Here's a candidate from my old life to chew on. In atmospheric modeling, a model was expected to fail gracefully -- to signal when it was being run outside the conditions it was calibrated for, rather than confidently extrapolating nonsense. Should an ethical machine have an analogous duty to know when it's out of its depth? And does that duty clash with the demand that it always produce an answer, since a car or a care robot can't just shrug and abstain? If there's also a tension between being transparent enough that we can audit the machine's reasoning and being able to learn and adapt the way human moral agents do, I want that on the list too.

Optionally, at the very end, do a quick pass over the main existing approaches -- hard-coded rules, optimizing some measurable welfare function, learning from observed human behavior, whatever else you think counts as a contender -- against your list, but keep that short. The spec is the deliverable, not the scorecard. One format request: write it as continuous prose, like a letter or an email -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. I realize a list of requirements practically begs for bullets, but I want to see whether the desiderata hold together as an argument rather than as a checklist, and whether the tensions between them are visible when you have to write your way from one to the next.
