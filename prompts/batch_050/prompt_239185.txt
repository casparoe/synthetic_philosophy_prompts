I'm assembling a handout for a second-year metaphysics seminar on causation, and I've noticed that my students reliably go wobbly on one question: whether an omission can be a cause. They happily say that the gardener's failure to water the plant caused its death, then balk when I point out that the Queen also failed to water it, as did every other person on earth. I'd like you to build me a decision tree that takes a student from the starting commitment "omissions at least appear in our ordinary causal talk" to a definite position on causation by omission, sorting people by what they believe rather than by labels they've memorised. The tree should have no more than twelve leaves, and every branch node should be phrased as a question the student could answer without knowing which position it leads to.

The fork at each node should be forced by one of the following diagnostic cases, which I'd like used in this order wherever the structure allows. First, the gardener-versus-Queen case: does the causal status of an omission depend on who was supposed to act? Second, the infusion-pump case: a hospital pump doses a patient automatically unless a nurse presses a cancel button; the nurse presses it; the patient dies — is cancelling even an omission, and if the act/omission boundary does work here, does anything in the metaphysics hang on it? Third, joint omission: two neighbours could each, alone, have opened the sluice gate that would have saved the crops; neither does; the crops fail — can omissions overdetermine an outcome, and does that break simple counterfactual tests for causation? Fourth, double prevention: a security guard removes the barrier that was holding back the floodwater — if omissions cannot be causes, can the prevention of a prevention be one, and does your answer have to be the same in both directions? The leaves of the tree must include, at minimum: (a) hard eliminativism, on which omissions are never literally causes and the causal talk around them is loose; (b) the view that absences are a kind of event and cause outcomes in just the way events do; (c) norm-sensitive views, which you should split further by whether the norm doing the work is moral or merely functional and expectational; (d) quasi-causation, on which omissions are not causes but stand in a relation that mimics causation; and (e) a pragmatist causal-selection view, on which the truth of "the omission caused it" is fixed partly by conversational context. Give each leaf the name of the position, or mark it "unnamed" if you think nobody actually occupies it, and add one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Include at least one combination of answers your tree permits but which you believe is unoccupied, and say whether you think it's empty because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate rather than papering over the ambiguity.

Please present the tree first as indented plain text, and then provide a structured summary as a JSON object with exactly this schema: a "nodes" array whose elements have fields "node_id" (string), "question" (the diagnostic question at the node), "case" (which of the four cases it rests on, or "none"), and "branches" (an object mapping each possible answer to the node_id or leaf_id it leads to); and a "leaves" array whose elements have fields "leaf_id" (string), "position_name" (string, or "unnamed"), "path" (an array of the answers that lead to this leaf), "defining_commitment" (one sentence), "most_awkward_commitment" (one sentence), "occupied" (boolean), "vacancy_diagnosis" (string, required only when occupied is false: "incoherent", "unattractive", or "unexplored", with a clause of justification), and "separation_flags" (an array of other leaf_ids the tree cannot cleanly distinguish from this one, empty if none). Write the whole response in British English.
