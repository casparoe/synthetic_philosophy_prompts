I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I got stuck in the middle of a row between two of my regular sources after a conference dinner last week and still can't settle it. One's a philosopher of physics, the other's a cosmologist. The philosopher argued that special relativity settles it: the future already exists. Her version: two people walking past each other in the street have 'now' slices that diverge by centuries out at the Andromeda galaxy, so events that are still future for one of them are already in the other's present -- and since there's no observer-independent present, past, present and future must all be equally real. The cosmologist pushed back that this is metaphysics freeloading on a coordinate convention: simultaneity is just how you slice spacetime, like lines of latitude, and nobody thinks Greenwich is more real than the other meridians. All relativity shows, he said, is that whether a distant event counts as settled depends on your frame, not that it's sitting there already. She replied that the latitude analogy cheats, because meridians don't decide what's real *now* whereas simultaneity surfaces do, so the analogy just assumes what's in dispute. His last move was that if relativity really forced the block universe, physicists would have to treat indeterministic quantum outcomes as fixed before they happen, and working physicists plainly don't, so the arguement proves too much. Neither would budge and both now want to be quoted as the winner. Go through the exchange move by move: who's right about which specific point, what are both of them missing, and who's more right overall -- I've got to file something defensible.
