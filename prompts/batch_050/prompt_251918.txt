I'm designing a flowchart for a seminar on motivated reasoning that sorts participants into their actual position on self-deception, given a canonical case: a person confronted with strong, freely available evidence that p (a spouse's affair, a terminal diagnosis, a failed investment) who goes on sincerely asserting and acting as though not-p. Starting from the commitment that this case deserves explaining, each branch should be a question the person can answer without knowing the labels: whether genuine self-deception requires the subject to simultaneously believe p and believe not-p; whether it requires an intention or policy of getting oneself to believe not-p (as opposed to merely motivationally biased evidence-gathering); whether the deceptive role must be played by a sub-personal part of the mind rather than the whole agent; and whether sincere but motivationally skewed belief already counts as deception at all. Each leaf should be a named position — intentionalism, the divided-mind or partitioned-self view, Mele-style deflationism (motivation without intention), the view that self-deception is simply incoherent and the cases must be redescribed — plus one or two unnamed leaves where the question-combinations allow a position nobody defends, with a brief note on why that spot is empty (incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored). Phrase each branch as a verdict on the canonical case or on a variant (e.g., one where the subject's avoidance is entirely unconscious), keep the tree to roughly six branch questions, and flag any two leaves the tree cannot in fact separate.
