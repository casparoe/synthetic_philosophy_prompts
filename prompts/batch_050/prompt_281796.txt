So I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm finally writing the piece I've been avoiding for years: why are some countries rich and others poor? My editor wants me to take a position on the institutions-vs-geography-vs-culture fight, and honestly I keep flip-flopping. I want you to help me think, not give me a book report.

Here's the proposition I want your number on, stated as carefully as I can manage: differences in present-day institutions (rule of law, constraints on executive power, secure property rights, that cluster of things) are the dominant cause of the huge current gaps in income per capita across countries, where "dominant" means something like: if you could magically transplant one country's institutions to another while holding its geography and its deep cultural traits fixed, you'd close more of the income gap than you would with any comparably sized transplant of geography or culture. Call the claim INST. Give me your credence in INST as a number.

But I don't want a bare number. My own credence is maybe 0.55, and a colleague whose judgement I usually trust puts it at like 0.15, so I want the decomposition so I can figure out where we actually part company. Here's what I think the question decomposes into, feel free to restructure:

1. First, is this kind of question even answerable at all? We have exactly one run of history. Every regression in this literature leans on instruments (settler mortality, linguistic distance, whatever) whose exclusion restrictions are basically articles of faith, since geography plausibly shaped the institutions directly and culture plausibly shaped both. Whats the probability that the evidence we actually have, or could realistically get, can distinguish these channels rather than just re-expressing our priors?

2. Conditional on the question being answerable: the reversal of fortune evidence. Places that were rich in 1500 (the Aztec and Inca heartlands, Mughal India) are relatively poor now, and the colonial-era empty places got rich. That pattern seems to point away from raw geography. But is it strong enough, given how few data points it really is and how much depends on how you measure urbanization 500 years ago?

3. The interaction problem. If geography caused the institutions (via disease environments, settler mortality, whether Europeans could even survive to build anything), then maybe "institutions vs geography" is like asking whether the spark or the oxygen caused the fire. How much weight do you put on the possibility that INST is not false so much as ill-posed -- that there's no well-defined transplant experiment the claim is even about?

4. Culture. The persistence literature (trust levels tracking centuries-old events, etc.) suggests deep cultural traits move slowly and matter a lot. Probability that, if we could actually measure culture properly, it would eat most of the effect currently attributed to institutions?

Give me a rough probability for each of 1-4, one line of justification each, say how you'd combine them into a credence for INST, and then tell me: which of these sub-questions is my 0.55 vs her 0.15 disagreement most likely living in, and what single piece of evidence or argument would move your own number the most? I'd especially love it if you flag where you think the whole framing I'm using is confused -- I'd rather find out now than in the fact-check stage lol.
