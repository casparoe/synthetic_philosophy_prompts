A colleague lent me a popular book on the science of consciousness, and one chapter characterises Ned Block's access/phenomenal distinction and its bearing on global workspace theory as follows:

"Block's distinction is straightforward enough. Access consciousness is the kind of consciousness that laboratories can measure: a state is access-conscious if the subject can report it and act on it. Phenomenal consciousness is the raw feel of experience — what it is like to see red or taste coffee — and it is the only kind that philosophers of mind really care about. Global workspace theory, developed by Bernard Baars and later championed by Stanislas Dehaene, holds that a mental state becomes conscious when its content is broadcast from a central workspace to the brain's many specialised systems. Block's argument shows that this theory fails. In Sperling's classic experiment, subjects are shown a grid of letters for a fraction of a second: they experience all of the letters vividly, yet can report only a few of them. This proves that phenomenal consciousness outruns access consciousness — we experience more than we can access. But global workspace theory is precisely a theory of access consciousness, of what is available for report, so it cannot explain phenomenal consciousness at all and is therefore not a theory of consciousness in any interesting sense. Block's conclusion, which the experimental literature has since confirmed, is that the neural basis of phenomenal experience lies in local sensory areas rather than in the frontoparietal broadcast networks that Dehaene's work implicates."

The book is by a reputable science writer, and the broad picture matches what little I already knew, so I am inclined to trust it — but something about the confident chain from the Sperling experiment to the verdict on global workspace theory feels too quick, and I cannot say where. Could you audit the summary? In particular:

1. Which parts would Block himself accept as a fair statement of his distinction, which parts are harmless simplifications for a general audience, and which parts get the distinction subtly but consequentially wrong? I am especially unsure whether the definition of access consciousness given here is the one the overflow debate actually turns on.
2. Is the interpretation of the Sperling experiment as settled as the chapter suggests, or are there live responses a global workspace theorist could make that the summary passes over?
3. Grant whatever is accurate in the summary: does the conclusion that global workspace theory is "not a theory of consciousness in any interesting sense" actually follow from the accurate parts, or does that verdict smuggle in something extra — for instance, a premise about what a theory of consciousness must explain that a defender of the theory would simply deny?
4. Finally, how does the closing claim about the neural locus of experience stand relative to the philosophical argument? Is it a consequence of Block's distinction, an independent empirical hypothesis, or something the chapter has presented as more settled than it is?
