Hey, I need help writing the final exam for my upper-level undergrad normative ethics course. One essay question on the demandingness objection to consequentialism, 90 minutes, 40 marks.

The problem with how this question usually goes: students just recite the standard line — consequentialism says you should donate almost everything to effective charities and spend your life on famine relief instead of your own projects, that's absurdly demanding, therefore consequentialism is false, name-drop Williams and integrity, done. They've memorized it. I want a question that can't be answered that way. Specifically I want the question to force them into two places:

(a) Whether demandingness is really an objection to consequentialism in particular, or whether any morality is comparably demanding in a world like ours. Common-sense non-consequentialist morality arguably also demands a huge amount given global poverty — the permissions to walk by aren't obviously as permissive as people assume. So maybe the demandingness is a fact about the world, not a defect of the theory. Students who've understood the debate should see this changes what the objection can even be; students who've memorized it won't know what to do.

(b) What happens when you build the slack back in — agent-centred prerogatives, satisficing versions of consequentialism. I want them to grapple with whether these are principled fixes or ad hoc retreats: a morality that permits you to do less than the best seems to owe a justification of that permission from inside morality itself, and satisficing appears to let you knowingly bring about a worse outcome for no reason the theory can state.

Deliverables: the question itself (phrased as it would appear on the paper, with the mark allocation), a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 40 that names the specific moves an A answer makes that a B answer misses, and the specific mistakes that cap an answer at a C — e.g., treating demandingness as a complaint about personal effort or psychological burden rather than a structural objection to the theory's content.

Two asks about the response itself. First, lead with the exam question itself in the first line — no preamble, no "here's what I've done," no restating my brief. Second, the model answer should actually commit: it should take a definite verdict on whether the objection succeeds and argue for it, because I can't mark against a standard that surveys both sides and shrugs.
