For an upcoming column, I am putting together a short guide to the rules of thumb that scientists and philosophers invoke when deciding whether an animal's behavior justifies attributing thought or feeling to it — the question lurking behind every viral crow, octopus, or dog video. My list so far: (1) Morgan's canon, the rule that one should never explain a piece of behavior by appeal to a higher psychological faculty if a lower one will do the job; (2) the Clever Hans lesson, that apparently intelligent responses may be cued by inadvertent signals from a human handler, so that only tightly controlled experiments count; (3) the continuity argument, the Darwinian point that evolutionary kinship makes it reasonable to take animal minds seriously rather than to withhold them by default; and (4) the warning against what primatologists call anthropodenial, the claim that reflexively denying humanlike capacities to animals is itself a bias and no more scientific than sentimentality. Is there a further principle in actual use that I have missed? It should be a genuinely different consideration — not a restatement, special case, or combination of these four — and I would like to know what it adds that they do not, and whether in practice it tends to license or to restrain attributions of mind to animals. Please write your answer in American English.
