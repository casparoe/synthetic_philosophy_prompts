Since retiring from a career in experimental condensed matter physics, I have been working my way through the literature on artificial intelligence and its long-term risks, and I recently joined a reading group of fellow amateurs with similar interests. Our current text is a popular book on the subject — I will not name it, since my question concerns the ideas rather than the author — and one chapter offers the following summary of two theses I keep encountering in this literature:

'A superintelligent system could be devoted to literally any goal whatsoever, because intelligence and goals are entirely independent of one another: intelligence is simply the capacity to achieve one's objectives, whatever those objectives happen to be, and nothing about becoming smarter pushes a system toward better or more humane ends. This is the orthogonality thesis, and it means that asking what a superintelligence would "want" on its own is a category error — its goals are installed by its designers and could in principle be as arbitrary as maximizing the number of paperclips in the universe. Furthermore, whatever final goal such a system has, we can predict much of its behavior in advance: any sufficiently intelligent agent will resist being shut down, seek to acquire resources, and protect its goals from modification, because these subgoals are useful for virtually any final objective. This is instrumental convergence, and it is a theorem about intelligent agency as such, not a mere empirical conjecture. Taken together, these two results show that a superintelligence is essentially guaranteed to be dangerous no matter what goal we give it, since even a trivial-seeming goal will be pursued by means of power-seeking.'

My own inclination is to find this persuasive, but several members of the group objected that the chapter proves too much, and I would like an accuracy audit before our next meeting. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful proponent of these theses would accept as stated, which parts are overstatements or conflations, and which, if any, are simply wrong. In particular: is the claimed independence of intelligence and goals meant as a thesis about what is logically possible, or as an empirical claim about what systems we should actually expect, and does the popular version blur that distinction? Is "installed by its designers" the right way to think about how a trained system's goals arise? Is instrumental convergence really a theorem, or a claim about default tendencies that admits exceptions and countermeasures? And once the summary is corrected on all these points, how much of its bottom-line conclusion — that danger is essentially guaranteed regardless of the goal we choose — actually survives?
