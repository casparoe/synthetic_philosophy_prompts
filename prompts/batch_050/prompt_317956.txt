At my company we had to choose between two technical platforms for a multi-year infrastructure commitment. Our usual process is a vote by a five-person architecture committee, but this time our CTO proposed instead polling all sixty engineers and going with the majority, defending the idea with the phrase "the wisdom of crowds." When I pushed back, she said the math was settled: bigger groups make better decisions, and she treated my worry -- that nearly all sixty engineers had formed their views from the same two vendor demos and one widely shared internal memo -- as a detail the crowd would wash out.

I suspect "the wisdom of crowds" is doing several different jobs at once in her mouth, and I want to know which one she'd actually need. Please distinguish the precise theses that slogan might be expressing, and for each one say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or outright false. The readings I have in mind would include at least: the claim that averaging many independent estimates cancels idiosyncratic error; the Condorcet-style claim that a majority vote on a yes-or-no question converges on the right answer as the electorate grows, and what that requires about individual competence and independence; the claim that a crowd aggregates dispersed information no single member holds; the claim that cognitive diversity matters more than individual expertise; and the flat claim that adding more voters always helps. For each reading, state the assumptions it needs and what happens when those assumptions fail -- in particular, what majority voting does when the average voter is more often wrong than right, and what correlation among judgments does to the argument.

Then apply this to my situation: which reading could actually support my CTO's proposal given that the engineers share information sources and that platform choice may be a question where non-specialists are systematically misled by polished demos; which readings are irrelevant to a binary vote (as opposed to, say, averaging numerical estimates); and whether anything in the slogan's family of ideas speaks to my side.

Structure your answer in four sections, in this order: The Readings (about 400 words, each thesis stated precisely and labeled), Verdicts (about 250 words, classifying each as trivial, defensible, or false, with the failure modes), Application to Our Vote (about 200 words, identifying which reading my CTO needs and whether she gets it), and What I'd Say Back (about 100 words, the strongest two or three sentences I could actually use in the meeting).
