Construct a thought experiment in which a person is money-pumped even though, at every single moment, their preferences over the options in front of them are complete, transitive, and stable under reflection. The exploitation must work entirely through the *timing* of the offers: the pump operator profits by scheduling proposals so that each trade is one the agent happily accepts at the moment it is offered, yet the sequence of trades returns the agent to their starting position minus money. Constraints: the setting must be ordinary and realistic — a consumer dealing with a retailer, a tenant with a landlord, a subscriber with a service provider — not a laboratory or a casino; the scenario itself should be no longer than one paragraph; and you may not stipulate any irrationality in the agent. In particular, the cross-temporal preference pattern that makes the pump possible must be one that many people exhibit and that is at least arguably rationally permissible (for example, a tendency to weigh immediate outcomes more heavily than delayed ones, or a predictable difference between how one values something when choosing for today and when choosing for next week) — no addictions, compulsions, blackouts, or stipulated inconsistencies within a single moment of choice.

After presenting the scenario, do the following. First, walk through the pump step by step: for each trade, state the agent's preferences at that moment and show that the trade is strictly endorsed by those preferences, so that at no point does the agent act against what they then want. Second, identify precisely which diachronic requirement the agent violates — the case should turn on the relationship between preferences at different times rather than on any defect at a single time — and state that requirement as a general principle. Third, say which feature of the case is doing the work, and confirm that removing it (making the agent's preferences uniform across times) collapses the pump. Fourth, consider the sophisticated version of the agent, who foresees the whole sequence: does foresight alone block the pump, or must the agent also be able to bind their future self, and what does your answer imply about whether diachronic rationality requires mere foresight or actual self-command? Finally, give the strongest defence of the pumped agent: someone who insists that preferences may legitimately change over time will say this case is no different in kind from a person whose tastes genuinely evolve and who pays to reverse earlier purchases. Explain whether this defence succeeds, and what it shows about the relationship between being exploitable and being irrational.
