Good's theorem in Bayesian decision theory establishes that, for an agent who maximizes expected utility and updates by conditionalization, acquiring cost-free information before choosing among acts never reduces expected utility and strictly increases it whenever the information could change which act is best. I want this result stress-tested against a real and remarkably consistent pattern: the response of at-risk individuals to predictive genetic testing for Huntington's disease.

The salient facts, which you may take as stipulated, are these. Huntington's is an autosomal dominant, incurable, and uniformly fatal neurodegenerative disease with onset typically in middle adulthood; the child of an affected parent has a fifty percent chance of carrying the mutation. Since the gene was identified in the early 1990s, a highly accurate predictive test has been available to asymptomatic people at risk. Yet across countries and decades, only a minority of those eligible — often a small minority — choose to be tested, even when testing is free and well publicized. No treatment prevents or materially slows the disease. Contrast hereditary cancer syndromes: people who learn they carry BRCA mutations, where risk-reducing surgery and intensified screening exist, routinely seek the information and act on it.

Work the analysis in both directions. First, the mapping. State the decision the at-risk individual faces in the theorem's terms — acts, states, evidence, utilities — and identify precisely which of its antecedent conditions fail here, if any. Consider at least: whether the information is cost-free once dread and the testing process itself are priced in; whether the assumption that terminal utilities are fixed independently of what the agent believes survives the fact that hope, ignorance, and certainty are themselves objects of preference; whether the assumption of ideal updating survives an agent who reasonably anticipates responding to a positive result with despair rather than calm replanning; and whether, in the absence of any treatment, the result can still feed downstream decisions — reproduction, savings, career, insurance — in a way that gives the theorem purchase.

Second, the single-agent limitation. Good's result concerns one decision-maker, but a positive result leaks information about relatives who may not want it, and it can become visible to insurers and employers. Does the value-of-information argument survive once other agents can condition their behavior on what you learn? Can the so-called right not to know be defended on purely decision-theoretic grounds, or must it rest on something else?

Third, the contrast case. Show that the same theorem straightforwardly vindicates BRCA testing, and use the contrast to isolate the extra premise that separates the two cases.

Finally, reverse the direction of fit: what does the episode show about the theorem? Adjudicate among three readings — that the refusers are irrational; that the theorem is sound but its antecedent rarely holds in medical contexts; that the theorem is sound but simply silent here because knowledge enters the utility function directly. Be disciplined about where the theorem genuinely applies rather than forcing a verdict of rationality or irrationality onto people who decline the test.
