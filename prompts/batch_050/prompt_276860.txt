For the past year I have moderated a reading group that includes, on one side, a convinced Lucas–Penrose anti-mechanist who holds that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show the human mathematical mind cannot be a computing machine, and, on the other, a computationalist who regards that entire line of argument as a subtle equivocation that has been refuted several times over. Their discussions generate more heat than light, mostly because each attributes to the other a position the other disavows: the anti-mechanist hears "minds are machines" as a claim that some particular machine's output exhausts what any human could in principle come to know, while the computationalist hears "humans can see the truth of their own Gödel sentence" as a claim that mathematicians possess a magical faculty of infallible insight. I have asked them to produce a joint statement in the style of an adversarial collaboration, and your task is to draft it for them, in wording both could actually sign.

The statement should begin from what both parties can accept without reservation: what the incompleteness theorems formally establish and about which systems; that human reasoners are finite, fallible, and inconsistent in practice; and that the anti-mechanist argument requires, at some point, a bridge from formal results to claims about human warrant. It should then state the residual disagreement precisely, in two formulations, each written so that its own side recognizes itself in the wording. The cruxes matter most to me. I suspect they include: whether the argument presupposes that a human mathematical community (not merely an individual) is a consistent system and whether we are entitled to assert that; the mechanist's reply that even if I am a machine, I might be unable in principle to know which machine I am, so my failure to prove my own Gödel sentence mirrors the machine's limitation rather than transcending it; whether the relevant comparison is an idealized reasoner or actual mathematicians over historical time; and whether Penrose's further appeal to non-computable physics is load-bearing or an optional add-on. For each crux you identify, specify an argument or case both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each side predicts or concedes about that case. Where part of the dispute turns out to be verbal -- about what "machine," "see," or "prove" is allowed to mean -- the statement should say so explicitly.

Format the response as exactly five numbered items with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the agreed claims, as a compact set of numbered sub-claims; (2) the two opposing formulations of the core disagreement; (3) the list of cruxes, each named and glossed in a sentence or two; (4) for each crux, the argument or case both accept as bearing on it together with each side's recorded prediction; (5) a short accounting of which elements of the original quarrel were verbal and why.
