I am a novelist, and next month I must give the toast at the retirement dinner of Marian, my literary agent of thirty years. The room will hold about eighty people: publishing colleagues who know our history in detail, and Marian's family, who know none of it. I need you to write the speech — roughly five minutes when read aloud — and I want to explain the difficulty you are being asked to solve, because it is the whole assignment.

Twenty-five years ago, Marian discovered that my beloved mentor — a famous novelist, also her client, the man who taught me everything — had quietly plagiarized several passages of his most celebrated book. She did not warn him, and she did not tell me first. She went directly to his publisher and, when the publisher stalled, to a journalist. His career collapsed within the year; he died a few years later, diminished, and I sat beside his hospital bed more than once without telling him I knew who had reported him. I also kept Marian as my agent. She has since made me a better career than I deserved. I have never once spoken publicly about any of this.

Here is what I want the speech to do. It must honor Marian without lying, and it must not expose her family to a story they may not know — but the publishing half of the room will be listening for whether I flinch, and they will notice if the toast could have been written for any agent at all. So the speech must find something true to say that works on both levels at once.

Structurally, I want the toast built around a distinction I have been turning over for years, which I will hand to you raw and ask you to refine: there is loyalty to a person — horizontal loyalty, owed between us as the particular people we are — and there is loyalty to the standards a person is *for* — vertical loyalty, owed to the thing they themselves serve and would claim to want served even at their own expense. My mentor owed his allegiance to literature more than Marian owed him her silence; that, at least, is the strongest version of her defense, and it is also exactly the kind of argument traitors reach for. The speech should take that danger seriously rather than waving it away. It should also turn the framework back on me, because there is a question I cannot escape: whether, by staying with Marian for twenty-five years of comfort and success, I performed a quiet betrayal of my own — one that had no whistle attached, and that nobody will ever be asked to toast.

Concretely: open without a joke (the family can be warmed to later; the first thirty seconds should signal that this will not be a standard retirement toast, without saying why). Include one concrete, warm, *unambiguous* detail of Marian's loyalty to me — something a toast needs in order to be a toast at all — and place it carefully, so that the complicated material around it does not retroactively poison it. The ending should land on gratitude, but it must be gratitude that has visibly survived the middle of the speech, not gratitude that ignores it. Do not use the words "whistleblower" or "betrayal" anywhere in the toast itself; the concepts should be unmistakably present and entirely unnamed, so that Marian's grandchildren hear a speech about loyalty and the editors in the back row hear everything.

Before the toast itself, give me two or three sentences of your reasoning about the single hardest structural choice you made — I want to understand the load-bearing decision, not receive a general summary.
