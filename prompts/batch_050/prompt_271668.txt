I am a political philosopher, and the position I have spent my career defending is a strict cosmopolitanism: I argue that compatriots, as such, have no non-instrumental special claims on one another -- that what looks like patriotic obligation is always either a general duty of justice that happens to fall nearby, or the morally real but derivative obligations created by promises, roles, and shared practices, which any two humans could in principle create. I have also, somewhat awkwardly, just become a citizen myself: I naturalized last year after two decades in this country, and the registrar, who knows my work and finds it amusing, has invited me to give the address at this spring's naturalization ceremony. I said yes before thinking it through.

The audience is about eighty new citizens (some for whom this passport is a rescue, some for whom it is a convenience, and I cannot tell which is which and must not rank them), their children and spouses, two local officials, and a reporter from the regional paper who will certainly notice if I give a seminar instead of a speech. What I cannot do is say the thing this occasion seems to want -- that from today these people owe one another, and are owed, something they owe and are owed by no one else on earth -- because I have argued in print that this is false, and saying it now would be both insincere and a small public humiliation. What I also cannot do is deflate the day, treat the ceremony as empty theater, or smuggle in my whole apparatus.

Please write the address, roughly seven minutes when read aloud. It must find what someone holding my view can say at this occasion that is true, warm, and worthy of it -- it should do real work with the tension rather than avoid it, and it should be clear about what the ceremony does create, given what it cannot create. Deliver it as exactly five numbered sections, each one or two short paragraphs, with no preamble before section 1 and no closing remarks after section 5.
