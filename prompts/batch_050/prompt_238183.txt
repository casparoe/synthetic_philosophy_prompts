I am a novelist, and the following is in the service of a scene I am writing: two of my characters end a long relationship in part because they disagree about what love was supposed to be, and I want their debate to be philosophically sound rather than decorative. To get the dialogue right I attended a writers' retreat and posed the question to two fellow novelists, whose own long-running disagreement I reproduce below as faithfully as I can. I will not tell you which of them I found more convincing; I want the adjudication, not confirmation.

The setting: whether a writer ought to depict love as justified by the beloved's qualities or as independent of them.

The first novelist, whom I will call Martin, opened with this. Love that is not responsive to what the beloved is actually like is not love but fixation. If you ask me why I love my wife and I can say nothing — not her wit, not her patience, not the way she treats strangers — then either I am being coy or something is wrong. We want to be loved for our qualities; a partner who would have loved literally anyone who happened to be nearby does not honor us, he merely uses us as a placeholder. So a breakup scene should show love answering to real features of the person: when the features go, the love's warrant goes too.

The second novelist, Dana, replied that Martin had described not love but a hiring decision. If love is justified by qualities, then it is rationally required to move on whenever someone better turns up — funnier, kinder, more accomplished — and the fact that decent people do not trade up shows that love's attachment outruns any inventory of qualities. What Martin calls warrant is just appraisal, and appraisal is what we do to job candidates and secondhand cars. Love, on her view, is closer to what we do for our children: the person's value for the lover is created by the loving, not discovered by it. A breakup written on Martin's model would be a performance review, and readers would find it chilling.

Martin answered that Dana had confused two questions: what causes love and what could justify it. Of course we do not in fact trade up — psychology, habit, and loyalty see to that — but the question was whether love *should* answer to anything about the beloved, and if the answer is no, then love is as arbitrary as a lottery win, and there is no answer to the dejected lover's question of why this person rather than the next. Besides, he added, there is a middle option Dana had skipped: love may be grounded not in the beloved's timeless qualities but in the particular history of the relationship itself — the shared years, the inside jokes, the weathered crises — which is a reason that both explains attachment and resists trading up, since no rival can offer the same history.

Dana granted the move was clever but said it solved the wrong problem. Grounding love in the relationship's history is circular, she argued: the history counts as a reason only because the love already made it one, and a shared decade with someone you do not love is just a long time. And on her main point she stood firm: any account on which love has grounds at all makes love hostage to the grounds surviving, and that fragility is precisely what lovers promise each other they are exempt from.

Their exchange has gone on for years without resolution, and since my scene depends on it, I would like you to adjudicate it properly. Please proceed move by move: assess each of Martin's and Dana's turns — Martin's opening argument from the wish to be loved for one's qualities; Dana's trading-up objection and her appeal to parental love as the paradigm; Martin's distinction between the causes and the justifiers of love, together with his appeal to relationship-history as grounds; and Dana's circularity charge and her closing point about love's promised exemption from grounds. For each, say whether it succeeds, fails, or succeeds only against a weakened version of the opponent's position, and why. Then give an overall verdict on who is closer to the truth, if either is. Throughout, I ask that you distinguish carefully between what is broadly agreed upon in the philosophical literature on the reasons for love — the appraisal/bestowal debate, the trading-up problem, and related matters — and what remains genuinely contested, and that you not present a contested position as though it were the settled one. My characters deserve arguments that real philosophers would recognize as live, and I need to know which side, if any, is writing from the stronger ground.
