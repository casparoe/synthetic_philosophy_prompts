Analyze the atomic bombings of August 1945 through the lens of just war theory's principle of discrimination and the doctrine of double effect (DDE) — the view that it is impermissible to intend harm to innocents, but sometimes permissible to bring about that harm as a foreseen side effect of a proportionate military action. Crucially, treat Hiroshima (August 6) and Nagasaki (August 9) as two separate acts, not as a single episode, and let the analysis run in both directions: what the theory says about each act, and what the acts reveal about the theory.

Take the following as given facts for the purposes of the analysis. Hiroshima housed the headquarters of the Japanese Second General Army and was a military logistics center, but it was also selected as a target in part because it had been deliberately spared conventional bombing, so that the new weapon's effects could be clearly assessed; the aiming point was a bridge in the city center, not the army headquarters, and the deaths (on the order of 140,000 by the end of 1945) were overwhelmingly civilian. Between the two bombings there was an interval of only three days, no new ultimatum to Japan was issued in that interval, and the Nagasaki mission's primary target was actually the city of Kokura — cloud cover diverted the bomber to Nagasaki, where roughly 70,000 died. Meanwhile, the alternatives on the table included a demonstration detonation on an uninhabited target, a tightening of the naval blockade, awaiting the effect of Soviet entry into the war (which occurred on August 8–9), and modifying the demand of unconditional surrender to preserve the imperial institution — a modification that was in fact granted in the final surrender terms.

Answer the following. First: the standard DDE defense of Hiroshima treats it as an attack on a legitimate military target (the army headquarters) with foreseen but unintended civilian casualties, like the bombing of a munitions factory in a city. Assess whether that defense survives contact with the actual facts — the city-center aiming point, the blast-assessment rationale for target selection, and the stated goal of shocking the Japanese leadership into surrender. Does the intention/foresight distinction have a stable application here, or does it collapse when the civilian destruction is itself the chosen means of producing the desired shock? Whose intention is even at issue when "the intention" belongs to a chain of institutional actors — target committee, president, aircrew?

Second: grant, for the sake of argument, that Hiroshima satisfied discrimination, proportionality, and necessity. Can Nagasaki satisfy them anyway, given the three-day interval, the absence of a new ultimatum, and the fact that the target struck was chosen by weather at the last moment? Does necessity require waiting to learn whether the first bomb sufficed? And is there anything morally significant about the sheer contingency of which city's residents died — or is that a morally arbitrary detail the theory is right to ignore?

Third, run the analysis in reverse. Just war theory's proponents often treat the bombings as a textbook violation that shows the theory has teeth; some critics treat them instead as exposing the theory's weakness — the intention/foresight line, they argue, is too easy to manipulate in deliberation to constrain anything. Which lesson does the episode actually support? Is DDE's distinction administrable in wartime decision-making at all, and if the Hiroshima deliberations can pass it in the form its defenders claim, does that show the distinction is toothless, or merely that the defense is disingenuous?

End with a stated verdict for each bombing separately — justified, unjustified, or (if you defend it) a split verdict with the asymmetry explained — and say what, if anything, the case teaches us about whether DDE should be retained as the operative principle for evaluating strategic bombing.
