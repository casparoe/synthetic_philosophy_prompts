I'm preparing a seminar on the epistemology of financial markets and want to build a session around Adam McKay's film The Big Short, read against the so-called no-trade theorems in economics. For students unfamiliar with the formal side: Aumann's 'agreeing to disagree' result and the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem roughly show that rational agents who share a common prior and whose rationality is common knowledge cannot purely speculate against one another — if someone is willing to sell you a bet at these odds, the very fact of their willingness is information that should make you update until the trade evaporates. Trade can be rationalised only by things like differing priors, non-informational motives (liquidity, hedging, pride), or failures of common knowledge of rationality. The puzzle is that the film is a dramatisation of an enormous speculative trade: Burry, Baum and the others buy credit default swaps on the housing market, and the banks on the other side are delighted to sell, each party privately convinced the counterparty is the fool.

Please read the film through this lens. First, locate the trades in the film within the taxonomy the theorems force on us: are the characters best understood as having private information that hasn't been incorporated into prices (Burry actually reads the mortgage prospectuses nobody else reads), as holding different priors or models from the market consensus, or as exploiting counterparties who aren't rational in the relevant sense (the ratings agencies, the CDO managers, the stripper's five houses)? Which of these the film itself seems to favour, and does it notice that these are distinct explanations with different epistemological morals? Second, consider the scenes where the sceptics meet their counterparties face to face — the meeting with the CDO manager, the conference in Las Vegas, the rating agency visit. In a no-trade world, the seller's eagerness should itself be a reason to hesitate; why isn't it, for these characters, and does the film give a philosophically respectable answer (e.g., that eagerness is explained by perverse incentives rather than superior information, so it isn't evidence about the underlying bet)?

Finally, I'd like you to say where the film resists or complicates the theorems rather than merely illustrating them. The no-trade results assume common knowledge of rationality; the film's emotional arc depends on the heroes suffering years of mark-to-market losses and being treated as mad, which suggests the 'trade' was less a disagreement about facts than a difference in who could afford to wait. Does the film thereby dramatise something the formal models leave out — that being right is not a private state but depends on a margin clerk and an investors' phone line — and if so, what would a philosopher of economics say the film gets right that the theorems don't? Please write your answer in British English.
