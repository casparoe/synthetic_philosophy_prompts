So my roommate in my phil of probability reading group has this argument that the Principal Principle (crudely: if you know the objective chance of E is x and nothing else relevant, your credence in E should be x) is either inconsistent or toothless, and I can't see where it breaks. It goes:

(1) Take the toss where my bent coin actually landed heads. That one event falls under lots of different chance setups at once: it's a toss of this coin (chance of heads 0.7), a toss thrown by me left-handed (0.4), a toss on a humid day (0.55), and so on.
(2) I can know all of these numbers at once -- say I've obsessively recorded my tosses for years.
(3) Apply the Principle to each chance fact I know and it demands credence 0.7, 0.4, and 0.55 in the same event at the same time.
(4) No single credence can be three different numbers.
(5) The only escape is to declare one of these the "real" chance of the event. But every rule for picking one -- narrowest reference class, most causally specific description, the setup actually producing the event -- is either arbitrary or just defines "the chance" as whatever keeps the Principle consistent, which makes it vacuous.
(6) So the Principal Principle is either contradictory or contentless.

I'd really like to keep the Principle since it seems like the only thing connecting objective chance to rational belief, so tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked fine, and what the main ways of rejecting it each cost (e.g. if the fix is that these are frequencies not single-case chances, say what that commits me to metaphysically).
