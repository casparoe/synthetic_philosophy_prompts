I TA for an undergrad philosophy of AI course (about 14 students, 75 minute seminar slot). I want an activity for the week we do wireheading and reward tampering, something they DO rather than something I explain. Design me a game that runs in-class where the pull of wireheading shows up in the gameplay itself, before anyone hears the term.

Rough shape I'm imagining (but push back or restructure if you have something better): students are doing some mildly tedious task for points, points convert into something they actually want (I can bring snacks or offer to bump participation credit slightly, keep the stakes small but real). Somewhere in the rules there should be a legitimate-looking way to get points without doing the task -- e.g. a scorekeeping role that rotates, or a mechanism where a player can pay to re-roll their own score, or an "energy recharge" option that just directly adds points. I want at least two distinct exploits: one where they corrupt the measurement of the thing (tampering with the reward signal) and one where they bypass the task entirely and grab the reward itself (wireheading proper), because half the class always conflates these and I want the debrief to pull them apart using moments that actually happened in the game.

Constraints: no lecture beforehand, I want max 5 min of rules explanation, and nobody should be able to "win" by sitting out or by being the one person who refuses to exploit anything -- if you build in a cooperative angle where defecting hurts the group, tell me how you keep it from just turning into a generic prisoner's dilemma, because they've all played that already and it'll prime them to pattern-match instead of discovering.

Also give me the debrief plan: which moments in the game map onto which concepts (reward tampering vs wireheading vs Goodharting), the questions to ask so they name the phenomena themselves, what will predictably go wrong when I run it (students who refuse to exploit on principle, students who exploit so fast the game ends in 5 minutes, etc) and how to handle each, and be honest about the one big disanalogy -- these are humans with outside-the-game motivations, not an RL agent that only has the reward -- and how I should address that in the wrap-up so it doesn't become a loophole someone uses to dismiss the whole thing.
