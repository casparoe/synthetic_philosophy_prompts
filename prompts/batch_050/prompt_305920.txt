I am a novelist by profession, not a philosopher, but this term I have been helping a close friend who teaches political theory at a small liberal arts college. She has been laid up after a surgery and asked me to take over the grading of her midterm booklets for a seminar on the social contract tradition, on the strength of the years I once spent reading eighteenth-century political writing as background for a historical novel set in Geneva. She left me her rubric and a stack of answers, and I have been working through them conscientiously. One booklet has given me pause, and before I commit a number to it I would like an independent assessment I can set beside my own marginal notes. I am not asking you to confirm any verdict I have already reached; I would rather you graded it fresh and told me exactly what moves the needle in either direction.

The examination question ran as follows. Students were given this passage from Book II, Chapter 3 of Jean-Jacques Rousseau's The Social Contract, in G. D. H. Cole's translation: "There is often a great deal of difference between the will of all and the general will; the latter considers only the common interest, while the former takes private interest into account, and is no more than a sum of particular wills: but take away from these same wills the pluses and minuses that cancel one another, and the general will remains as the sum of the differences." They were then asked: In your own words, explain the distinction Rousseau draws in this passage between the general will and the will of all, and then assess whether, on Rousseau's view, a majority vote of the citizenry reliably expresses the general will. The student's answer, in full, reads: "Rousseau distinguishes the general will, which aims only at the common interest, from the will of all, which is merely the aggregate of everyone's private wishes. His arithmetic metaphor of pluses and minuses canceling is meant to show that when citizens vote, the extreme positions on either side offset one another, so that the general will is essentially the moderate view left standing in the middle once the extremes have canceled each other out. Because this cancellation is built into any fair vote, a majority decision of an informed electorate just is the general will; provided the count is accurate, the majority cannot be mistaken about the common good. This is why Rousseau can say that the general will is 'always right and tends to the public advantage' — he treats the outcome of a properly conducted vote as infallible. It is true that Rousseau worries about factions and partial associations distorting the result, but his remedy is simply that the state should prohibit such associations outright, since any organized group within the state corrupts the deliberation. So on his view majority rule in a well-informed, faction-free society is not merely a convenient decision procedure; it is the very definition of what is right. This also explains his notorious claim that dissenters can be 'forced to be free': if the majority vote is the general will, then the minority has quite literally willed what the majority enacted, whether its members realize it or not."

Please grade this answer out of 20, as if you were the instructor of record, and give me the specific comments that justify the mark. I want a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression: identify each claim or interpretive step in the answer, say whether it is sound, partially sound, or mistaken, and explain why with reference to the passage and to the surrounding argument of the work where relevant. Where the answer goes wrong, I would like to know precisely how it goes wrong — whether it conflates two notions, overstates a claim, or draws an inference the text does not support — and where it is right, I would like that noted too, since partial credit should reflect genuine understanding. A brief summary comment of the sort I could write on the back of the booklet would be a welcome close.
