You are a professional philosopher with expertise in metaethics and moral psychology, and you have been asked to audit a colleague's stated position before they present it at a workshop. Take the task seriously: begin by spending a short paragraph brainstorming where the commitments below might pull against each other — including tensions your colleague probably has not noticed — and only then produce the audit itself. The audit should (i) identify each genuine tension, ranked from most to least severe, stating precisely which commitments conflict and what would have to give; (ii) identify at least one apparent tension that dissolves on inspection, explaining why; and (iii) recommend the single least costly revision that would restore consistency, and say honestly what it costs. Do not treat all tensions as equally bad, and do not recommend wholesale abandonment of the position if a surgical fix exists.

Here is the position to be audited. The colleague writes:

I have been working through what cross-cultural research on moral psychology implies for moral objectivity, and I have landed on the following package of commitments. First, I am a cognitivist: ordinary moral judgments express beliefs that are true or false, and some of them are true. Second, I accept that the cross-cultural variation documented in moral psychology is deep — it is not merely disagreement about nonmoral facts, such as who actually broke a taboo or what the ancestors want, but extends to the weights people assign to harms, fairness, loyalty, and hierarchy themselves. Third, I hold that any given person's moral intuitions are largely a product of the particular culture that formed them; had I been raised in a very different moral community, I would confidently hold intuitions contrary to my current ones. Fourth, despite this, I maintain that some moral truths are knowable by any competent human reasoner regardless of cultural formation — for instance, that inflicting severe suffering on a child merely for amusement is wrong — and I do not think my confidence in such judgments is merely parochial. Fifth, I favor what I call the nutrition model of morality: just as there are objective facts about what nourishes human beings that constrain but underdetermine an adequate diet, there are objective facts about what human cooperation requires that constrain but underdetermine an adequate moral code, so that several genuinely different moral codes — including some that weight loyalty and hierarchy far more heavily than mine does — can all be objectively adequate. Sixth, I hold a strong principle of non-interference: even when we correctly judge another society's practices to be wrong, outsiders are almost never justified in intervening to change them, and the history of moralized imperialism gives us strong reason to treat our reforming impulses with suspicion.

Please tell me whether this package hangs together. I am especially unsure whether the nutrition model buys me genuine objectivity or only the appearance of it, and whether my third and fourth commitments can be jointly sustained — but I would rather you tell me where the real problems are than confirm my own guesses.
