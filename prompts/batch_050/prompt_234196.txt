After forty years as an evolutionary biologist, I have spent my retirement in a philosophy reading group, where we have lately been preoccupied with the question of whether the evolutionary origins of our beliefs undermine their claim to be knowledge. The worry, as I understand it, runs as follows: natural selection shaped our faculties because they promoted survival and reproduction, not because they tracked any truths beyond that, so any belief whose prevalence is fully explained by its fitness value seems epistemically suspect — at least when the belief purports to describe a mind-independent order of things. One member of our group, a moral philosopher by training, has offered a series of verdicts on whether particular beliefs are debunked by their evolutionary etiology:

- That two stones added to two stones make four stones: not debunked.
- That one has special obligations to one's own offspring: debunked.
- That unseen agents watch us and punish concealed wrongdoing: debunked.
- That favors received ought to be reciprocated: debunked.
- That physical objects continue to exist while unobserved: not debunked.
- That mating with close kin is wrong: debunked.

Our next session takes up a harder case: the conviction, held firmly by several of us, that one has genuine obligations to distant strangers and to future generations — obligations that answer neither to kinship, nor to reciprocity, nor to the cohesion of one's own group, and whose content therefore does not appear to have been selected for in any straightforward way; if anything, acting on it looks fitness-reducing. I should like help anticipating how my colleague will treat this case. Please reply as a numbered list of exactly five items, each of one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, addressing in order: (1) the verdict my colleague will most likely reach on the new case, given his record; (2) which of his earlier verdicts is the closest analog, and why; (3) the two or more distinct rules, each consistent with his entire record so far, that diverge on this case, stated precisely; (4) which rule is most plausibly his, with the grounds for preferring it; and (5) one further test case that would discriminate between the two leading rules.
