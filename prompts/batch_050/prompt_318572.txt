I'm TAing an undergrad epistemology course this fall and I want to nail down where I actually stand on testimony before the students start asking questions. Here's my current position set:

(1) I'm a reductionist about testimonial justification: a hearer is only justified in taking someone's word for something if they have positive, non-testimonial reasons to think the speaker is reliable on that topic (track record, credentials, no obvious motive to lie, that sort of thing).

(2) Most of what I know is testimonial -- history, basic science, my own birthday, who my parents are -- and I don't think there's anything epistemically second-rate or shaky about that.

(3) Small children acquire tons of knowledge from testimony long before they're in any position to vet their sources. A four-year-old knows her own name and what a tiger is, full stop, not some downgraded "proto-knowledge."

(4) Testimony transmits knowledge: you can't come to know p on someone's say-so unless someone in the chain actually knew p. Testimony is a conduit, not a factory.

(5) I accept the standard verdict on the creationist-teacher case: the teacher sincerely teaches evolution because it's the curriculum and she does her job well, the students take her word for it, and they end up knowing that humans evolved -- even though she doesn't believe a word of it herself.

My question is whether all five can be held together. Which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it? For the real ones, rank them by how bad they are, say exactly what would have to give, and tell me what the cheapest single revision is -- ideally one that costs me less than giving up the reductionism in (1), which is the commitment I care about most, though I'm open to being told that's exactly the one that has to go. I'd rather you discriminate hard than flag every possible friction point; if two of these are actually fine together, tell me why the apparent problem dissolves. Please write the answer in American English.
