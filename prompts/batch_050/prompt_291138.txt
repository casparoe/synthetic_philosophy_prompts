Next month I am presenting a paper at a two-day workshop in history and philosophy of science, and I want to be prepared for the question period. My thesis is that Imre Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes — taken together with his further claim that rival methodologies of science can themselves be appraised by how much of actual history they succeed in reconstructing as rational — survives the two standard charges that have driven most historians away from it. The first charge is that Lakatosian rational reconstructions are simply history rewritten so that the winners appear to have followed the methodologist's preferred rules. The second is that the distinction between progressive and degenerating problemshifts can only ever be drawn retrospectively, and therefore offers no guidance at the moment when a scientist, referee, or funding body must actually decide whether to stay with a programme or abandon it. Against the first, I argue that the retrospective character of methodological verdicts is a feature rather than a defect, since such verdicts are meant to be revisable as the documentary record grows. Against the second, I argue that a carefully specified notion of novel fact, along the lines Elie Zahar proposed when he shifted the emphasis from temporal novelty to heuristic novelty, makes progressiveness assessable in the near present rather than only after the dust has settled. And against the standing worry that Lakatos's meta-criterion is viciously circular, I argue that the historical episodes used as test cases — the Copernican revolution, the chemical revolution — are fixed by verdicts that are nearly universal across methodological camps, rather than being selected by the very methodology under test.

The audience will be roughly fifteen people. The core is contextualist historians of science: scholars trained to distrust Whiggish retellings, to treat actors' own categories as primary evidence, and familiar with the case-study literature purporting to show that the heroes of rational reconstructions behaved nothing like the reconstructions claim. There will be two sociologists of scientific knowledge of the strong-programme persuasion, for whom the entire project of rationally reconstructing theory choice is the mistake, since in their view the same considerations always underdetermine closure and social negotiation does the real work. There will be three formal philosophers of science, all broadly Bayesian, who will cheerfully grant Lakatos his historical points and then ask what his machinery explains that confirmation theory does not already handle with fewer moving parts. Finally, there is one senior historian of physics who has heard every version of this debate since the 1970s and has a habit of asking, late in the session, the question everyone else was politely circling.

Prepare me. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will actually come, phrased the way these people would phrase them — the historians in their idiom, the sociologists in theirs, the Bayesians in theirs, not in a generic philosopher's. For each objection: what I should concede outright, on the assumption that conceding it early buys credibility for the rest; what I should refuse to concede; my reply, sized to the two minutes a workshop answer realistically allows; and a candid note on where that reply is softest. Rank the objections by how much damage each does to the thesis if I handle it badly. Identify which objection the senior historian of physics is most likely saving up, and why it is him rather than the sociologists who will pose it. Then, separately, give me the question I am hoping nobody asks — the point where my position is genuinely weakest — together with the least-bad answer available to me if it comes.
