I've talked myself into the position that Kierkegaard (well, Johannes de silentio) is basically right in Fear and Trembling: if faith is real at all, it has to involve a "teleological suspension of the ethical" -- Abraham's act can't be justified in ethical terms, where it just is murder, only in religious terms where the single individual stands higher than the universal. Here's the bit that keeps nagging me though, from the opening of the book (Hollander translation, in Selections from the Writings of Kierkegaard):

"Then, faith was a task for a whole life-time because it was held that proficiency in faith was not to be won within a few days or weeks... no one entirely outgrows [the fear and trembling]--except insofar as he succeeds in 'going on' as early as possible."

So my question: what would count against the position? If faith is a lifelong task that nobody ever finishes, then at the moment on Mount Moriah Abraham couldn't have known he was a knight of faith rather than a fanatic or a murderer hearing voices -- and neither could anyone watching. Does that epistemic problem actually wreck the category, or is it the whole point? And more generally: what observations or arguments would show that the teleological suspension is an empty category, e.g. that there's no possible case where a divine command could trump the ethical, or that the view collapses into pure voluntarism where whatever God wills is good by definition? I'm also suspicious the position has been insulated: anytime someone claims a suspension and turns out to be a monster, defenders just say "that wasn't genuine faith," which feels unfalsifiable. Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a sympathetic reader of Kierkegaard would actually conceed.
