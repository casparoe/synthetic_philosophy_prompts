I have no training in philosophy, but I read a lot of popular nonfiction, and I recently picked up a general-audience book on the history of political ideas. Its chapter on Thomas Hobbes, written against the background of the English Civil War, summarizes his argument in Leviathan (1651) like this:

"Hobbes believed that human beings are selfish by nature, always seeking their own advantage. In the state of nature -- his reconstruction of how people actually lived before any governments existed -- this selfishness produces a war of each person against every other, a condition of unceasing violence in which life is miserable and short. Since reason tells everyone that peace is preferable, people rationally come together and hand over all of their rights to an all-powerful sovereign, whose commands they must then obey without exception. Because even the worst government is better than the state of nature, resistance to the sovereign can never be justified, and Hobbes thus emerges as history's great theorist of absolute monarchy."

The chapter then goes on to claim that, on Hobbes's logic, any regime that succeeds in keeping order -- even a modern authoritarian surveillance state -- would have his philosophical endorsement, and that citizens of such a regime would have no legitimate ground for complaint.

Something about this bothers me, but I cannot locate the problem with any precision. Out of curiosity I skimmed a little of Leviathan itself, and two things struck me. First, Hobbes seems to emphasize that people in the state of nature are roughly *equal* -- equally vulnerable, equally capable of harming one another -- and it was not obvious to me how that fits with the book's story that war follows simply from everyone being selfish. Second, I came away unsure whether Hobbes thought the state of nature was a historical period or more of a device for reasoning about what life would lack without a common power, and the book treats the historical reading as settled.

Could you audit the summary for me? Specifically:

1. Which parts would a careful reader of Hobbes accept as accurate, which are understandable simplifications, and which would Hobbes himself object to or explicitly qualify?
2. Does the war in the state of nature, on Hobbes's actual account, really flow from universal selfishness, or is something else doing the work -- and does the equality point I noticed matter here?
3. When people covenant with the sovereign, do they genuinely surrender *everything* and owe obedience without exception, or are there limits built into the contract itself? If there are limits, does the book's "resistance can never be justified" survive?
4. Finally, setting aside what is accurate in the summary: does the chapter's conclusion about modern authoritarianism actually follow from Hobbes's argument, even granting the book its own premises? I would like to know whether the inference fails at the level of the summary or only at the level of the chapter's application of it.

Please go through the quoted passage fairly systematically rather than giving me a general verdict. I am inclined to think Hobbes is being made to sound more extreme than he is, but I am aware this could just be my discomfort with his conclusions rather than any flaw in the book's account.
