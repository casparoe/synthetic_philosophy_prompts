A while back I got into this seventeenth century Ethiopian philosopher, Zera Yacob, who wrote a short treatise called the Hatata (the Inquiry). Quick summary in case he's new to you: he lived through ugly religious conflicts between Ethiopian Orthodox, Catholics, Muslims and others, and if I remember right spent a couple of years hiding out in a cave. His big move was to notice that every tradition claims its teachings come from God, that the traditions contradict each other, that truth is one, and that everyone just happens to be supremely confident in whichever faith they were raised in — so inherited tradition can't settle anything, and you have to test everything by the reason God gave to all of us. Using that test, he concludes their's a wise and good creator God, and he also turns it against various religious rules: for instance he argues that long severe fasting wrecks people's health, and a wise God who made us wouldn't order us to harm ourselves, so rules like that are human invention rather than God's law.

Anyway, I tried to reconstruct his reasoning for myself and I think it proves way too much, and I can't find the step where it jumps the rails. Here it is:

(1) God is perfectly wise and perfectly good.
(2) A perfectly wise and good lawgiver would not give his creatures rules that make their lives worse on balance for no good reason.
(3) The severe fasting rules make the lives of the people who keep them worse on balance (wrecked health, misery) for no good reason.
(4) So the fasting rules are not from God. (from 1-3)
(5) So scripture is a mixed book, part divine and part human invention, and reason is the filter that sorts it. (from 4)
(6) The same test has to apply to creation itself: a perfectly wise and good creator would not build into the world features that make creatures' lives worse on balance for no good reason.
(7) The world is full of exactly those features: disease, childbirth that kills mothers, animals that only stay alive by tearing other animals apart, and so on.
(8) So the world as we find it is not the work of a perfectly wise and good creator. (from 6-7)
(9) So either God isn't perfectly good, or God isn't the creator. Which is not a conclusion Yacob would accept, and it's definately not one I want to accept either.

The frustrating thing is I wanted this argument to stop at (5). I wanted a rational sieve for religious rules, not an argument from evil against the God the whole setup assumes. But every step looks innocent to me — especially (6), which honestly just looks like (2) again with "creator" swapped in for "lawgiver." The two things I most want to keep are (4) and (5), the Yacob-style critique, and (1), the good creator. So: which step do I actually have to reject, why did it look fine when it isn't, and what does rejecting it cost me? And if the honest answer is that I can't keep both the fasting critique and the good creator, say that plainly instead of splitting the difference.
