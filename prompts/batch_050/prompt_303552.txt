My roommate in the stats department hit me with this over beers and I still dont have an answer I'm happy with. (1) A single-case probability claim — like "this 45-year-old smoker has a 12% chance of a heart attack in the next decade" — is only justified if it's grounded in the observed frequency of some reference class the case belongs to. (2) Every case belongs to indefinitely many reference classes: 45-year-olds, smokers, 45-year-old smokers, 45-year-old smokers who drink two coffees a day, men, men with his exact cholesterol reading, and so on forever. (3) These classes have different frequencies, sometimes wildly different, so which class you pick changes the answer. (4) To justify picking one class over the rest, you need a reason that its frequency is the relevant one for this case — otherwise the choice is arbitrary and arbitrary choices aren't justified. (5) But any such reason is itself a judgment about which class tracks the outcome best for cases like this one, and by (1) that judgment needs its own reference class and justification, so you're in a regress. (6) So no single-case probability judgment is ever justified. (7) But actuaries, doctors, and weather forecasters make justified single-case judgments constantly — if they didn't, my roommate's own thesis, which leans on a logistic regression predicting individual loan defaults, would be worthless. (8) So something between (1) and (6) has to give. His move is to reject (1): probability is just subjective degree of belief, so there's nothing to ground and no regress. I feel like that just relocates the problem — the Bayesian still has to set priors, and "whatever you feel like" isn't justification either. Which step do you actually reject, why does it look fine when it isn't (or vice versa), and what does rejecting it cost? And does it matter how you cash out "the right class" — the narrowest class, the class with the most data, the causally relevant class — or do all of those just re-run the regress in (5) one level down?
