I'm a longtime reader of LessWrong, Astral Codex X, and the surrounding ecosystem, which means I've absorbed years of arguments about randomized trials -- the "RCTs are a cargo cult" essays, the replies, the meta-replies -- without ever having to defend a real trial to real people. That changes next month. I have a philosophy PhD and I serve as the outside ethicist on our university hospital's research ethics committee, and I've agreed to present the case for approving a trial I helped design. It's a de-adoption trial: for thirty years our ICU has routinely put every mechanically ventilated patient on acid-suppression drugs to prevent stress-ulcer bleeding, a practice adopted hospital-wide on the basis of older trials showing fewer bleeds on imaging and labs, but never shown to reduce mortality, and carrying a small persistent signal of harm (more pneumonia, more gut infections). Our trial would randomize ventilated patients to continuation versus discontinuation of the prophylaxis. My thesis is that clinical equipoise is comfortably satisfied here -- that the honest state of expert opinion is collective uncertainty, whatever any individual intensivist's gut says -- and, further, that the ethical presumption actually runs in favor of the trial, since the status quo amounts to administering an unvalidated drug to every patient who comes through the door, forever, without consent or data collection. I want to argue that randomization is not the imposition of risk on a safe default; it's the first honest thing we've done with this protocol.

The committee I'm presenting to has five members who matter: the senior intensivist who wrote the original prophylaxis protocol in the 1990s and still believes in it; a surgeon who is generally trial-skeptical and will frame everything as physicians experimenting on the vulnerable; the committee's biostatistician, who will be quietly sympathetic but will test whether I understand my own design; a lay community representative whose questions will be about what we'd tell a frightened family at 2 a.m.; and the hospital's counsel, who cares about consent forms and liability and nothing else. I know the standard distinction between an individual clinician's personal uncertainty and the expert community's collective uncertainty, and I know the intensivist will press exactly there -- that he is not uncertain, so for his patients there is no equipoise, whatever "the community" supposedly believes. I'd rather win that exchange than dodge it. What I don't have is a realistic map of how the whole half hour will go, from people who are not me and haven't read the blogs I've read.

So: prepare me for the question period. I want the objections I'm actually going to get from this specific committee -- not the ones a philosophy journal referee would raise -- each phrased the way that particular member would phrase it, in their vocabulary and from their interests. Order them by a combination of how likely they are and how much damage they'd do to the trial's approval if I handle them badly. For each one, tell me what I should concede up front (there are real concessions to make here, and conceding them early buys credibility), what I should hold firm on, and a reply I can deliver in about two minutes of speaking. Flag where each reply is soft. And give me the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- I have a guess, which is some version of "you're not a physician, so why is your confidence that we're uncertain worth more than his confidence that we're not?" -- but tell me if you think the truly dangerous question is a different one. Format the whole thing as a JSON object with two fields: "objections", an array in your recommended order, where each element has fields "rank", "source" (which committee member), "objection_as_theyd_phrase_it" (a direct quote in their voice), "severity" (high/medium/low), "likelihood" (high/medium/low), "concede" (what to grant immediately), "hold" (the non-negotiable point), "two_minute_reply" (the reply itself, written to be spoken), and "soft_spot" (where that reply is weakest); and "dreaded_question", an object with the same fields minus "rank", plus a boolean field "matches_my_guess".
