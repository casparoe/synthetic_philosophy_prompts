I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size SaaS company, and last quarter our CEO returned from a founders' retreat having converted to Stoicism. Since then, all-hands meetings open with a "premortem meditation," the on-call channel has been renamed #externals, and HR has hinted that the next promotion cycle will incorporate "Stoic principles." During calibration last week, my manager pushed back on my impact write-up by saying, in all seriousness, that the revenue attributable to my project wasn't really mine, and later sent me chapter 6 of Epictetus's Enchiridion, in Elizabeth Carter's translation, as supporting documentation:

"Don't be prideful with any excellence that is not your own. If a horse should be prideful and say, 'I am handsome,' it would be supportable. But when you are prideful, and say, 'I have a handsome horse,' know that you are proud of what is, in fact, only the good of the horse. What, then, is your own? Only your reaction to the appearances of things. Thus, when you behave conformably to nature in reaction to how things appear, you will be proud with reason; for you will take pride in some good of your own."

I have no formal philosophy background, but I recognize a leveling-rubric disaster when I see one, and I'd like to beat HR to the punch. Write the satirical internal document I suspect is coming: the "Impact & Leveling Guide" of a tech company that has operationalized this passage, and the dichotomy of control behind it, as its entire performance philosophy. Use a deadpan corporate wiki/HR voice, roughly 600 words, not counting the passage above. The guide should include: promotion criteria for each engineering level stated purely in terms of one's "reaction to the appearances of things"; instructions for writing a promotion packet that scrupulously avoids claiming credit for any outcome, since shipped features, latency improvements, and revenue are all the good of the horse; a section on incident review in which outage severity is assessed by the composure of the responders rather than the damage done; and a note clarifying that equity grants, being externals, are strictly indifferent, though employees are welcome to decline them as a demonstration of virtue. The satire should exaggerate things the modern Stoic revival genuinely does — the productivity-guru repackaging of the dichotomy of control, the morning-journal-and-meditation-app industrial complex, and the way "focus on what you can control" can conveniently dissolve questions of who deserves credit and who bears blame — rather than inventing absurdities out of whole cloth. Keep the irony legible without winking: a reader should be able to see that the document's logic is the passage's own logic, faithfully extended one step too far.
