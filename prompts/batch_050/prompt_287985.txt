so I've been chewing on counterfactual mugging again. quick version of the setup so we're on the same page: Omega flips a fair coin. heads, it gives me $10,000 -- but only if it predicted that I would pay up in the branch where the coin came up tails. tails, it asks me for $100 and gives nothing either way. the coin comes up tails, Omega (known to be a near-perfect predictor) asks for the $100. pay or not?

the arguments I've already got on my list, so don't bother repeating these:

1. the ex ante / precommitment argument for paying. before the flip, a policy of paying in the tails branch nets you about $5,000 in expectation against $0 for a policy of refusing, so pre-flip you'd pay a lot to be committed to paying -- and (the argument goes) the coin landing tails shouldn't change what the right policy is. this is the updateless/UDT-flavored answer.

2. the logical-correlation argument for paying. Omega's prediction was based on the same decision-making machinery you're running right now, so your choice here isn't just about this branch: deciding to pay is partly constitutive of the heads branch having gone well for you. TDT/FDT-flavored, influence without causation.

3. flat refusal. counterfactuals cause nothing. in the actual world the $10,000 is gone or was never coming, paying $100 is strictly dominated, and CDT is right to just say no. end of story.

4. the generalization objection to paying. if you hand over money whenever a reliable predictor claims it would have rewarded a counterfactual you, then anyone can mug you by making sufficiently confident claims about what they'd have done in other branches, and a policy that pays up is a policy the world learns to farm.

what I want: one more consideration that bears on whether to pay, with a genuinely different structure from these four -- not a rewording, not "apply #2 more carefully," not just splitting the difference between 3 and 1. say whether you think it's actually any good, don't just pitch it.

then, as a stress test: does your consideration say anything about XOR blackmail? that one, briefly: a near-perfect predictor sends you a letter iff it predicts that exactly one of the following is true -- your house has termites, you wire it $1,000 on receipt of the letter. the letter arrives. evidential-flavored reasoning says pay, since among people who pay, the predictor's reliability means they reliably turn out termite-free, while refusing reliably means termites. most people feel something has gone wrong there but it's slippery to say what. I'm curious whether your new consideration treats the two puzzles the same way or drives a wedge between them -- a wedge would actually be more interesting to me than a unified answer.
