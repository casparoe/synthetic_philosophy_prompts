So I run a reading group at our local philosophy society (mixed bunch -- a couple of retired teachers, an engineer, some undergrads, one very committed skeptic) and we've been circling around the question of when testimony about weird stuff deserves to be believed. I realised my own verdicts on cases are a mess and I want to know if there's a principle underneath them before I inflict them on the group.

You're a philosopher of science who also knows the history of psychical research well. Think carefully, and I'd actually like you to brainstorm two or three candidate principles out loud before committing to one -- the process matters to me here, not just the verdict.

Here's the backdrop. Henry Sidgwick, first president of the Society for Psychical Research when it was founded in 1882, took the position -- I'm paraphrasing from memory of his addresses and writings, not quoting, so treat my paraphrase as the text under discussion -- that testimony about phenomena like telepathy and apparitions shouldn't be dismissed just because the phenomena are disreputable or seem impossible on current science, that what matters is the quality and independence of the testimony judged by roughly the ordinary standards of evidence, and that patient investigation could in principle settle the question. He investigated for decades and famously never declared himself convinced. Fine. Now my verdicts:

1. If a friend tells me they saw our mutual acquaintance at the train station, I just believe it. One witness, no corroboration, done.
2. Thousands of people have watched a stage magician apparently levitate someone; I don't believe in levitation for a second, and I don't feel the audience testimony counts for anything at all.
3. The SPR collected hundreds of apparently sincere, independent reports of "crisis apparitions" -- people seeing or sensing a relative at roughly the moment that relative died unexpectedly far away. I feel these deserved serious investigation, though not yet belief.
4. There are centuries-old miracle reports with multiple sworn written attestations, and witchcraft depositions too, signed by magistrates. I dismiss these wholesale. Mass delusion era, basically.
5. One exposed fraud (a medium caught with a cheesecloth ectoplasm rig, say) makes me suspicious of the whole medium scene, permanently.
6. A telepathy experiment replicates in one lab and fails in three others; I take the failures to outweigh the success. But in physics, if a null result came from a less sensitive instrument, I'd discount the null, not the positive. I can't tell if I'm being inconsistent.
7. I accept the consensus of physicists on things I couldn't possibly check, but a consensus of Victorian SPR investigators I'd trust much less, even if they'd been comparably careful.

So: is there a coherent principle of testimony-and-evidence that captures most of these? I suspect "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence" is doing some work but I genuinely don't know what makes a claim extraordinary without just meaning "claims I don't believe," which is circular. Cases 3 and 4 bother me most -- sworn testimony in bulk, and I treat the two bodies completely differently. Case 6 might be the same inconsistency wearing a lab coat.

What I want: the principle (or the two best candidates) stated precisely, with which of my cases motivates each clause. What it verdicts on cases I haven't given you -- your invention, two or three. Then the honest part: which of my verdicts the principle can't save, and whether Sidgwick's position as I've paraphrased it turns out to be an application of my principle, a rival to it, or just the view that my principle has no teeth until someone does the legwork. And does his decades of investigating without concluding count as the principle working as intended or as evidence the principle is broken?
