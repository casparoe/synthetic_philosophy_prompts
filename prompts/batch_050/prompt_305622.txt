I am preparing a graduate seminar on the ethics of aesthetic appreciation, and I want a case analysis I can argue with. Take the case of Paul Gauguin's Tahitian period. The salient facts, which I will stipulate so we are not hostage to biographical detail: Gauguin left France for Tahiti in 1891; once there, he took as his live-in companions local girls in their early-to-mid teens, in a colonial setting where the power asymmetries were enormous; he painted many of his most celebrated canvases in this period, several of them depicting these girls; and the sensuous, idealized presentation of them is not incidental to the paintings' artistic achievement but partly constitutive of it -- the lushness, intimacy, and dreamlike eroticism are what the pictures are praised for. In recent decades, museums mounting Gauguin retrospectives have felt obliged to address this biography in their wall texts and catalogues rather than let the work speak for itself.

Analyze this episode through the debate about whether immoral art can be good art. I want the mapping done carefully in both directions. First, theory to case: take the standard menu of positions -- radical autonomism (aesthetic and moral value are independent dimensions), moderate autonomism, ethicism of the sort that treats an unmerited prescribed response as an aesthetic defect, moderate moralism (moral flaws sometimes, not always, count as artistic flaws), and immoralism (a work's moral defect can itself contribute to its artistic success) -- and say what each predicts about the Gauguin case. In doing so, distinguish three relations the wrongdoing might bear to the work: as biographical cause (no paintings without the exploitation), as subject matter (the girls are depicted), and as endorsed perspective (the paintings invite the viewer to see these girls as available, untouched, exotic). My suspicion is that the standard positions were built for works whose immorality lies in the third relation -- propaganda, works that endorse cruelty -- and that the Gauguin case is uncomfortable precisely because the wrongdoing sits mostly in the first two. Test that suspicion: does ethicism's merited-response machinery even get a grip on the sensuous responses these paintings prescribe, or is there a morally unobjectionable aesthetic response available that the pictures fully merit?

Second, case to theory: what does the episode show about the positions? Consider in particular the curatorial practice of appending biographical context -- does that practice presuppose that the moral facts bear on artistic value, or is it consistent with a purely autonomist view on which the wall text serves moral rather than artistic purposes? And does the case support the immoralist's claim that the defect feeds the achievement, or does it rather show that the achievement and the defect merely share a common cause, which no theory of artistic value needs to care about?

I do not want a survey that ends by saying each view captures part of the truth. Commit: state which position handles the Gauguin case best, say whether on balance the moral facts about their production diminish the artistic value of these paintings, and give the reasons that settle it for you -- including an honest account of the strongest consideration against your verdict and why it does not, in the end, move you.
