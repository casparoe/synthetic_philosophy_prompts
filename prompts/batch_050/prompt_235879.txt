A colleague of mine has a favorite argument he deploys whenever our department deadlocks over policy: moral compromise between people with different value systems is just like trade between countries with different endowments. Each side gives up what it values less and receives what it values more, so compromise creates surplus out of nothing, and refusing to compromise is simply moral protectionism — leaving gains on the table out of squeamishness. He uses this to press me to trade concessions on things I consider genuinely wrong in exchange for wins on things I consider more important, and to treat my reluctance as irrational. Audit the analogy for me. Set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the goods, the prices, the comparative advantage, the enforceable contract, and the surplus itself? Then tell me where it breaks: whether a normative conviction behaves like a preference or endowment, whether there is any common currency in which the "gains" are measured when the two sides rank outcomes by different values, what prevents defection when there is no external enforcer, and whether repeatedly trading on your commitments changes what those commitments are. Distinguish the disanalogies that actually threaten his conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic, say whether the protectionism charge survives once those are on the table, and if the trade picture fails, suggest a better analogy for what moral compromise between incommensurable value systems is actually doing.
