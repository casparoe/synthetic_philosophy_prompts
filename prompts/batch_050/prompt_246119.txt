I serve on the staff of a standing national commission on democratic reform. The commission issues recommendations on proposals to expand, restrict, or reweight political participation, and it prizes consistency with its own prior rulings. Its published record to date consists of six verdicts. First, it endorsed lowering the voting age from eighteen to sixteen for all elections. Second, it rejected a proposal to let parents cast an additional proxy ballot on behalf of each child under sixteen. Third, it endorsed extending the franchise in municipal elections to non-citizens after three years of legal residence. Fourth, it rejected extending the franchise in national elections to those same residents. Fifth, it rejected mandatory voting backed by a modest fine, even with a formal option to abstain on the ballot. Sixth, it endorsed restoring the vote to citizens currently serving prison sentences.

A new proposal has now been referred to us: age-weighted voting. Under it, every citizen's ballot would be multiplied by a factor that declines with age -- for instance, ballots cast by voters under thirty would count one and a half times, those cast by voters over seventy would count three-quarters of a vote -- on the grounds that younger cohorts must live longest with the consequences of present decisions about climate policy, public debt, and pension design, yet are routinely outvoted on exactly those questions.

I want to know what the commission should recommend if its overriding aim is to remain consistent with its own record. Please do the following. First, identify at least two general principles, each compatible with all six prior verdicts, that yield opposite recommendations on age-weighted voting, and state each principle's verdict on the new proposal along with its reasoning. Second, say which of the six earlier rulings is the closest analog to the new proposal and which way it cuts. Third, give your best guess as to which principle the commission is implicitly running, with reasons drawn from the pattern of the record rather than from the merits of age-weighting itself. Fourth, if you conclude that the record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say so plainly, and state what principle the commission would be committing itself to under each possible recommendation. Finally, describe one further reform proposal -- not age-weighted voting and not a minor variant of any of the six settled cases -- whose resolution would most cleanly discriminate between the two principles you identified.
