Two colleagues of mine in a political theory workshop have been arguing for weeks about whether liberal states have a right to exclude would-be immigrants, and last week they asked me to settle it. I could not, so I am handing it to you. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Ingrid opened with an analogy. States, she said, are associations, and freedom of association includes the freedom not to associate. A couple may exclude anyone they like from their wedding; a church may set conditions of membership; a self-governing people inherits the same right at scale. A political community that cannot decide who joins it is not really self-governing at all, since decisions about membership are the most fundamental decisions a collectivity makes about itself.

(2) Leo replied that the analogy collapses at precisely the load-bearing point. Weddings and churches are voluntary; states are not. Nobody chooses their fellow citizens, membership is assigned coercively at birth, and so there is no exercise of associational freedom for the state's right to exclude to protect. What closed borders actually guard, he said, is an inherited privilege: where you are born determines your life prospects more than almost anything else about you, and defending that accident with fences is closer to feudalism than to freedom of association. He added that border controls restrict insiders as well — the citizen who wants to hire, house, or marry a foreigner is told she may not.

(3) Ingrid conceded that states are not voluntary but denied that this matters. Families are not voluntary either, she said, yet no one thinks outsiders may force their way into a household's common life; non-voluntary associations can still possess rights of exclusion. She then shifted ground: even if the pure associational argument fails, there is a functional one. The redistributive institutions of a wealthy democracy rest on solidarity and mutual trust among members, and a community that cannot control the pace and scale of admission cannot sustain those institutions. On this version, the right to exclude is not about the pleasures of company but about the preconditions of collective self-government.

(4) Leo's reply came in two parts. First, the family analogy proves too little: at most it protects intimate association, not control over a labor market and a territory shared by millions, and there is no principled route from one to the other without abandoning the associational framing entirely. Second, the functional argument is an empirical bet dressed up as a right: whether realistic levels of immigration would in fact erode solidarity or welfare institutions is unresolved, and if they would not, Ingrid is defending a modest policy preference with an absolute-sounding entitlement. Either the associational claim does the work and scale is irrelevant, or scale does the work and it was never about association.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: identify where each speaker's argument is strong, where it rests on a weak analogy or an undefended empirical premise, and say which moves you take to succeed and which to fail. Then give an overall verdict — who is more nearly right about whether states have a right to exclude, and what that right, if it exists, actually amounts to — and identify the most important consideration that both speakers are ignoring.

One request concerning calibration: in doing all of this, distinguish clearly between points on which philosophers who work on immigration ethics broadly converge and points that remain genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus view. Where your verdict turns on an empirical matter that philosophy alone cannot settle — such as the actual effects of immigration on social trust or redistributive institutions — say so explicitly rather than resolving the question by assumption.
