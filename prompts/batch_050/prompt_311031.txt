So I work at a mid-sized company and something about how we hand out praise has been bugging me for a while, and when I complained about it to a friend who did an ethics minor she said congrats, you've stumbled into the philosophy of supererogation, which I had to go look up. Here's the situation. Every quarter the company gives an award to someone who did something beyond their job description -- last one went to a guy who spent three weekends rebuilding a client's database after a crash that wasn't his fault, and the announcement made a big deal of how he went above and beyond and nobody would have blamed him for just going home. Fine. But at the same company, inside the same performance-review system, people get dinged in reveiws as "not a team player" or "lacks ownership" for declining exactly that category of thing -- weekend work nobody assigned them, mentoring juniors in other teams, quietly absorbing an understaffed project's slack. And my manager once told me, sincerely I think, that nobody is required to do any of this stuff, it's just what gets noticed.

What I want to understand is what view of duty this whole setup presupposes, if it's even one view. The award side seems committed to there being a genuine line between what you owe and the good stuff beyond that, and to praise only making sense on the far side of the line -- nobody gets a plaque for showing up on time. The review side seems committed to the line not mattering much, since declining the beyond-duty stuff costs you real money at raise time. Is there any coherent theory of obligation on which both halves make sense -- say, the line is real but where you land relative to it can still legitimately factor into your overall evaluation without the extra becoming required -- or does the practice only make sense on a deflationary view where there's no stable line at all and "supererogatory" is just rhetoric employers find convenient? Reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories, if the halves really presuppose different ones), tell me whether the company is being straightforwardly incoherent or whether there's a subtle position that licenses the combination, and say what would concretely have to change in the practice if we took seriously the idea that going beyond duty is genuinely optional -- do the awards have to go, do the penalties, or something less obvious? I'm also curious how much of this is actually underdetermined: maybe several rival views of duty and praise would all license exactly this arrangement, and the tension I feel is an artifact of assuming the practice has a theory at all rather than just incentives.
