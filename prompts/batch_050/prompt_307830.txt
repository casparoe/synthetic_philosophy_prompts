I am taking a seminar on self-reference, computation, and formal systems, and we have just spent two sessions on typogenetics, the toy genetics-style formalism in which a "strand" is a string of the four letters A, C, G, T, and the strand is read off in triplets that specify one or more "typoenzymes" — little machines whose available operations include cutting, deleting, shifting, and copying stretches of any strand they attach to, including the very strand that encoded them. The seminar has split into two camps, and I would like to force them to stop talking past each other by making them co-author something. The first camp, the isomorphists, hold that typogenetics genuinely instantiates the core of Gödelian self-reference: because one and the same string serves both as a description (the code that determines which enzymes exist) and as manipulable material (the substrate those enzymes act on), and because an enzyme's binding preferences are themselves a function of the letters of the strand that encodes it, the system lets a string "get a hold of itself" in essentially the way a Gödel sentence does, and a self-reproducing strand is therefore a true formal quine rather than a metaphor. The second camp, the deflationists, grant every fact about the mechanics but deny the moral: they say the strand means nothing on its own, that the translation from triplets to operations and the notion of "copying" are fixed entirely from outside by the formalism's conventions, that the typoenzyme does all the interesting work while the strand is merely copied and never interpreted by anything within the system, and that calling this self-reference confuses a physically instantiated mapping with genuine syntactic self-description. Their recurring flashpoint is whether a self-reproducing strand — if one can even be constructed — amounts to the strand reading itself or merely to the strand being read, twice over, by machinery whose rules it does not encode.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. It should contain, first, a numbered section of agreed claims about the typogenetics formalism itself and about what the Gödel analogy requires, phrased so that neither side has to concede anything substantive to sign them and none are vacuous; second, a table of the residual disagreements, each stated in wording that the side it characterizes would itself accept, with a column flagging any disagreement both camps concede is purely verbal, and why; third, a list of the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp — which I expect to include at least whether externally fixed interpretation rules are compatible with genuine self-reference, whether the dual code-and-substrate role is sufficient for the analogy or only necessary, and whether constructibility of a self-reproducing strand bears on the philosophical question at all. For each crux, specify the argument, construction, or analogy both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts that consideration will show and what it would take for that camp to update. Keep the entire statement under 800 words.
