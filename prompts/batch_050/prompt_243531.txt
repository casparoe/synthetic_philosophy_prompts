I have taught the philosophy of religion section of my Wittgenstein seminar for the better part of a decade, and I have come to suspect that one debate in the secondary literature is going nowhere. Ever since Kai Nielsen coined the term "Wittgensteinian fideism," commentators have been arguing about whether Wittgenstein's later remarks on religious belief -- in the lectures and scattered remarks, where belief is treated as a form of life and a matter of pictures, passion, and commitment rather than hypothesis -- amount to an objectionable insulation of religion from rational criticism. Roughly three positions recur. First, the fideism charge: by treating religious language-games as autonomous and criticism as a category error, Wittgenstein renders faith immune to evidence and argument, and this is a defect, not an insight. Second, the grammatical defense: Wittgenstein is describing how religious utterances actually function, not legislating that they be exempt from criticism, so the charge confuses description with prescription. Third, the expressivist reading: religious "belief" was never propositional assent in the first place, so the whole dispute about evidence rests on a misdescription of the phenomenon. Every year my students reproduce all three, and every year the exchange ends where it began.

Acting as a philosopher with expertise in Wittgenstein and in philosophy of religion, think carefully about this impasse before answering, and then give me a diagnosis rather than another round of the debate. Is the disagreement fundamentally exegetical (what Wittgenstein actually held), phenomenological (what religious belief actually is), or normative (what may legitimately count as criticizing a form of life)? Which positions are answering different questions under one label? I want a committed verdict: identify which of these you take the disagreement really to be, say which of the three positions, if any, survives your diagnosis, and state what would count as progress -- a distinction to be drawn, an exegetical result, an observation about religious practice -- or argue that no progress should be expected, and why. Do not merely lay out the options.
