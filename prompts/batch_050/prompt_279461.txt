In policy discussions of autonomous weapon systems, I keep encountering the same analogy from those who see little cause for alarm: delegating a targeting decision to an autonomous system is no different in kind from a commander delegating to a subordinate. A commander cannot personally verify every strike; she issues intent, constraints, and rules of engagement, and the subordinate exercises judgment within them — and we call that meaningful human control. The conclusion drawn is that a human operator who sets a system's objectives, boundaries, and abort conditions thereby exercises meaningful human control, and that demands for stronger control requirements misunderstand how military command has always worked.

Audit this analogy rather than taking a side on the underlying policy question. Lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the commander's intent, the subordinate's judgment, the rules of engagement, the possibility of refusal, and the accountability chain if something goes wrong. Then identify where the mapping breaks, distinguishing the disanalogies that matter for the intended use from the ones that don't — I expect candidates include a subordinate's ability to comprehend the reasons behind an order rather than merely its parameters, to refuse or escalate when the situation outruns what the order anticipated, to be held responsible and to respond to that prospect, and to be trained into shared norms that fill gaps orders leave open. Consider also whether the analogy's defenders would accept its own logic in reverse: if we would not say a commander exercised meaningful control over an order given to a subordinate she knew would execute it mechanically regardless of circumstances, does that admission undercut the analogy or merely relocate the disagreement?

State your verdict on whether the relaxed conclusion — meaningful human control is already achieved by objective-setting and constraint-issuing — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if a better analogy exists for what human-machine delegation in targeting actually resembles, offer it. Finally, say what would change your mind: which consideration or argument, if established, would lead you to a different verdict, and where in your own reasoning the analysis is most likely to fail.
