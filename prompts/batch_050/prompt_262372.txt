I spent forty years as an experimental condensed matter physicist and only started reading philosphy after I retired, so forgive me if this is a well-known puzzle with a name and a literature. A guy at my reading group showed me a thought experiment and I built an argument out of it that seems to prove way too much, and I can't find the loose screw.

The setup first. A being with a flawless track record of predicting people flips a fair coin and tells you the deal: if it comes up heads, it checks its prediction of what you would have done in the tails case -- if you'd have handed over $100, it gives you $10,000, if not, nothing. If it comes up tails, it asks you for $100. The coin comes up tails. The being holds out its hand. Do you pay?

My reading group guy says obviously not, and here's his argument, which I buy every step of until the end:

(1) A rational choice is just one that leaves you better off than the alternatives given how things actually stand. You can't change what's already settled, so what's already settled is irrelevant to what you should do now.

(2) The coin already landed. Either the $10,000 was never on the table for me or it was, and nothing I do with my wallet now causes anything about that flip.

(3) So paying is just lighting $100 on fire. Don't pay.

(4) Notice the reasoning never used anything about the being or the coin. It only used the fact that the other side of the deal is already fixed when I decide.

(5) So consider: my neighbor drives me to the airport at 5am because I promised him $40 and he trusted me to pay afterwards. We're not close, he's moving abroad next week, I'll never see him again, and he'd never be able to badmouth me to anyone I know. When we arrive, the favor is done, fixed, in the past. Paying him doesn't cause the ride. By (1)-(4), I shouldn't pay.

(6) But that's just a dressed-up way of saying stiff everyone once they've done their part, as long as they can't retaliate. Which is absurd, and also, if everyone reasoned like that, nobody would get rides to the airport at all.

(7) So something in (1)-(4) has to give.

The thing is, (1) still looks to me like plain common sense -- it's basically how we thought about causation in the lab, you can't fix what's fixed. If I have to give up one step I'd rather it not be (1). So: which step actually goes wrong? Why did it look fine? And if rejecting it commits me to handing over the $100 to the coin-flipper, or to the neighbor, or both, I'd like to know what else I'm signing up for. Also, does the fix say anything about the version where the being is only right about people 90% of the time?
