I'm a high school student in an after-school philosophy seminar, and we've spent the last two weeks on the bootstrapping problem and easy knowledge, and I keep going back and forth on it. The case that stuck with me is this: my school gave us access to an AI study app that answers questions and also displays a self-reported accuracy figure (it says 94%). Suppose the only way I can check whether the app's answers are right is by asking the app itself, and every time I do, it confirms its own answers were correct. Intuitively, compiling a long "track record" this way shouldn't teach me anything new about whether the app is reliable. But some of the epistemologists we read seem to argue that this kind of reasoning is exactly as good as the source's own deliverances, and that the intuition against it just smuggles in a requirement that you must already know your source is reliable before it can justify anything — which would threaten huge parts of ordinary knowledge, including my belief that my own senses work. I brought up the classic case of a driver checking her fuel gauge by looking at the gauge, and my teacher said this is one of those problems where your verdict depends on deep commitments about the structure of justification rather than on the details of the case. I want to know where that verdict actually lands.

So here is my request, and please take it seriously as a professional epistemologist rather than as someone summarizing the debate: give me your actual credence in the following proposition — a rational agent who begins with no independent evidence about a source S's reliability can come to be epistemically justified in believing that S is reliable, purely through repeated successful use of S as assessed by S itself. I want a number, not "it depends," and I want the decomposition behind it. Please break your credence into the sub-questions it rides on and give each a rough probability with a line of justification: for example, the probability that justification genuinely requires antecedent warrant for a source's reliability (as opposed to that being a fallacious demand), the conditional probability that bootstrapping yields real justification rather than mere coherence given that no such antecedent warrant is required, the probability that bootstrapping and easy knowledge stand or fall together so that accepting one forces accepting the other, and any other term you think belongs in the product. Then show how the terms combine into your final number, and identify which single term the final credence is most sensitive to.

I'd rather have a hedged, self-critical number with its weak points marked than a confident one that hides them.
