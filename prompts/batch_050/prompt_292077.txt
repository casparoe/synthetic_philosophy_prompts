I am a novelist preparing a book about mind uploading, and I want to commission a short story from you that I can study as a model of how to dramatize this material without lecturing. Please write the story in a polished literary register, roughly 1,800 to 2,500 words, and build it around the scenario below, which I have devised for the book and which I believe poses a genuinely hard problem rather than a familiar one reheated.

The scenario: some decades from now, gradual replacement is the standard route to uploading. A patient's neurons are substituted, one at a time, with synthetic counterparts that replicate each cell's functional behavior; the work is done in nightly sessions over about eighteen months, and the patient experiences nothing — no gap, no flicker, no change in the texture of thought. By statute and by medical consensus, the person who completes the procedure is the same person who began it. There is, however, a retention clause in the regulations: because early substitutes occasionally failed, the extracted biological neurons must be preserved, alive, in culture, for ten years as warranty stock, after which they are to be destroyed.

My protagonist is a woman nine years past the completion of her replacement. She learns, through a clerical disclosure, that her warranty stock was never scheduled for destruction — and, worse, that a private institute of "continuity fundamentalists" (people who hold that only the original biological tissue is ever the person, and that gradual replacement is a slow execution) has acquired such stock and developed a technique for reconstituting it into a complete, functioning brain. Hers has been reconstituted. It has woken up. Because each neuron carried only what had been encoded in it up to the night of its extraction, the reconstituted brain's memories end partway through the eighteen months of her procedure: it remembers going to sleep for roughly its two-hundredth session, and nothing after. It believes it is her. In a strict biological sense, it has a claim. A court will now decide whether it is a person with rights in her estate, her name, her marriage — and she has been asked to testify. She can also, under the retention clause, simply sign the destruction order.

I want the philosophical problem to carry the plot: her decisions, and the confrontation itself (if she agrees to meet the reconstituted brain), should be where the thinking happens, not in any expository dialogue. No character should deliver a tidy lecture on personal identity, and the story should not settle the underlying question with confidence — but it must take a discernible emotional position on what it is like to be her. Please do not resolve it with a twist that dissolves the problem (it was all a dream, the institute is fraudulently faking the brain, and so on); I want the hard version faced squarely.

One request about candor: if my scenario as specified cannot sustain the story I've asked for — if it smuggles in a conceptual confusion about identity or consciousness, commits me to a view that quietly undermines the tension I'm relying on, or contains an internal contradiction I haven't noticed — say so plainly rather than writing politely around the flaw. I would rather have the objection than a story built on sand.

After the story, please append a JSON object with exactly these fields: "title" (string); "approx_word_count" (integer); "view_of_identity_the_story_leans_on" (string — one or two sentences naming the position on personal identity the story's emotional resolution implicitly favors, if any); "contested_moves" (array of strings — each place where the story takes a side on a genuinely contested philosophical question rather than leaving it open); "premise_problems" (string — your plain-spoken answer to my candor request above, or "none" if you find the scenario coherent as given).
