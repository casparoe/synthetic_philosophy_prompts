I am a novelist at work on a book set during a military occupation, and I have run aground on a philosophical question that I need to settle before I can finish my protagonist's central scene. My character is ordered by an occupying officer to commit a small but genuine act of betrayal — denouncing a neighbor's son — on the explicit understanding that if she complies, her own children will be spared, and if she refuses, they will be shot. I want to portray her choice honestly, neither excusing it too cheaply nor condemning her in a way that ignores the coercion, and I keep finding that my own judgment wavers depending on how I frame the scene.

While brooding over this I came across a passage in Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics (Book III, in the Ross translation) that seems to be about precisely my character's situation, yet I find it genuinely hard to parse. Aristotle writes: "But with regard to the things that are done from fear of greater evils or for some noble object (e.g. if a tyrant were to order one to do something base, having one's parents and children in his power, and if one did the action they were to be saved, but otherwise would be put to death), it may be debated whether such actions are involuntary or voluntary." He then concludes that such actions are "mixed, but are more like voluntary actions," and adds that "some acts, perhaps, we cannot be forced to do, but ought rather to face death after the most fearful sufferings," while for other acts people are not praised but pardoned when the pressure is more than human nature can withstand.

Here is what I cannot untangle. First, what work is the voluntary/involuntary distinction doing for him here? I would have thought the urgent question about my character is whether she does wrong, not whether she acts voluntarily, yet Aristotle treats the classification as if it settles something important — is he assuming that voluntary simply means blameworthy, or is something subtler going on? Second, his verdicts seem to pull in two directions: he appears to say there is a fact of the matter about what she should do (some things must be refused even at the cost of death), yet his talk of actions that deserve pardon rather than praise, and of how difficult it is to determine what should be chosen at what cost, suggests he thinks such cases may have no clean right answer at all. Does this passage commit him to the view that there is always a correct choice available in extremity, however terrible, or does it leave room for genuine dilemmas — situations in which whatever the agent does, she does something wrong? Third, I would like to know what follows for my character in particular: on the most defensible reading of the passage, is her compliance something she can be blamed for, something she should be pardoned for, or possibly even the right thing to do, and does Aristotle's framework give me any principled way to decide where my invented denunciation falls among those three?

Please explain the passage patiently, as you would to an intelligent reader with no philosophical training, and answer my three questions in the order I have raised them, saying plainly where the text is itself ambiguous rather than forcing a tidy reading onto it. I would like your response as continuous prose throughout — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — written the way one would write a careful letter.
