Got into a dumb argument with my roommate last week after I paid forty bucks to one of those commitment apps that donates your money to a cause you hate if you miss your deadline. Her position was that paying money because you've predicted your own failure is incoherent with actually having a choice about it, and I've been chewing on it alot since. Here's roughly where I've landed on the whole cluster of questions around predicting your own choices, and I want to know whether the package is coherent or whether I'm trying to have it both ways:

(1) While I'm genuinely deliberating between options, I can't put a probability on what I'll end up picking. Deliberating just is treating the matter as open, and if I catch myself thinking "I'm 80% likely to go with the fries," that means the deliberation was already over and I was just going through the motions.
(2) At the same time, I'm a pretty good predictor of my own future behavior, and I'd be irrational not to use those predictions. I know future-me will procrastinate on grading, will pick the fries over the salad, will hit snooze. Planning around these predictions is just good sense -- it's why I book the library room and don't keep cookies in the apartment.
(3) Commitment devices are rational, and they're rational precisely because the future choice is still open. If what I'd do were already settled, binding myself would be pointless. The app works because the deadline-night decision is genuinely up for grabs and the app changes its incentives.
(4) I'm responsible for my choices in a way I'm not responsible for my heartbeat, and part of what that means is that a free choice can't be known in advance, even by me. If anyone, including me, could know beforehand what I'd choose, it wouldn't really be up to me.
(5) When I do end up ordering the fries exactly as predicted, it still seems to me that I really did deliberate at the restaurant and really could have ordered the salad. The prediction didn't settle anything; it was just a good guess about what my deliberation would produce.

So: which of these actually collide and which only look like they do? For each genuine collision, tell me what would have to give and how painful that is, and rank them by seriousness -- I suspect at least one apparent tension here dissolves once you're careful about a distinction, and I'd rather be told which one than have everything flagged as equally bad. And if there's a single edit to the list that fixes the most while giving up the least, name it and tell me what I'd be conceding. Please answer in American English.
