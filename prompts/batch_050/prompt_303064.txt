Last weekend I spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about how moral norms actually change, and I want help thinking through the practical upshot of the disagreement. She is a historian who holds what I gather is a fairly orthodox view among scholars: that the great moral revolutions, above all British abolition, were driven primarily by economic interests and structural change, with moral argument serving largely as after-the-fact rationalization (she kept invoking the Williams thesis about capitalism and slavery). I found myself defending something closer to the account I read in Kwame Anthony Appiah's work on moral revolutions, where the decisive variable is honor: dueling in England and footbinding in China did not end because the arguments against them improved, since the arguments were old and well known for generations, but because the practice came to be seen as dishonorable, often through a fairly rapid collapse in the social standing of those who persisted. My friend countered that Appiah's own case of abolition fits her story at least as well as mine, and that honor is usually just the packaging in which interests get delivered. We ended in a stalemate about the history, but it struck me afterward that the disagreement has concrete implications I have never drawn out.

Here is why it is not academic for me. I volunteer on the strategy team of an organization trying to shift public norms around the consumption of industrially farmed meat, and we are perpetually deciding how to allocate limited effort among three approaches: making and refining the moral arguments, pressing the interest-based case (health, cost, climate exposure for businesses), and what I will call honor work, meaning attempts to make factory-farmed eating carry a whiff of social embarrassment among the audiences our members care about, through public commitments, institutional defaults, and visible defections by admired figures. Please describe the most important implications of the honor-code account of moral revolutions, taken seriously as a rival to interest-based and argument-based explanations, for how a campaign like ours should allocate effort among those three approaches. I want the implications to be genuinely in the weeds: what the historical cases suggest about sequence and timing (does honor collapse come early or late in a revolution, and what has to be in place first), about the difference between making a practice look low-status and making abstention look high-status, about the risks of honor strategies backfiring into defensiveness and counter-identity among the targeted group, and about where the analogy between ending dueling or footbinding and changing rich-world eating habits plausibly breaks down, given that meat eating, unlike dueling, has no obvious equivalent of a code that practitioners themselves privately resent.

Please answer in American English. I would also like you to be candid about the epistemic status of what you tell me: indicate how confident you are in each of the main implications you draw, and single out explicitly the single claim in your answer you are least sure of, explaining whether the uncertainty comes from the historical evidence, from the extrapolation to my case, or from something else.
