The extended mind thesis, associated with philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers, holds that objects outside the skull can literally be part of a person's cognitive processes if they play the right functional role. Their well-known thought experiment contrasts Inga, who recalls a museum's address from biological memory, with Otto, an Alzheimer's patient who reliably retrieves the same address from a notebook he carries everywhere. On the thesis, Otto's standing belief that the museum is on 53rd Street is stored in the notebook just as Inga's is stored in her brain, provided the notebook meets conditions roughly like these: it is in constant use and reliably available, its contents are directly and easily accessible, its entries are automatically endorsed on retrieval rather than double-checked, and the information was consciously endorsed when first written down. Critics press the "cognitive bloat" worry: if reliable coupling is sufficient, the mind threatens to leak into phones, filing cabinets, and other people.

With that background, write a parody claims-adjustment letter, roughly 600 to 700 words, from a fictional insurer (invent the firm, the claim number, and the adjuster) denying a policyholder's claim. The claimant, an earnest amateur philosopher, has filed under his policy's "cognitive injury and memory impairment" rider after his pocket notebook was stolen, arguing on extended-mind grounds that the theft constitutes a partial memory loss and therefore a compensable injury, and itemizing damages: the addresses, appointments, and half-formed ideas that were, on his view, literally parts of him.

The humor must come from the philosophy, handled accurately. The adjuster takes the extended mind thesis entirely seriously -- the letter notes that the firm retains two philosophers of mind on retainer and is prepared to concede the thesis for the sake of argument -- and instead denies the claim on a technicality internal to the thesis itself. For instance, the adjuster might find on the evidence that the notebook fails one of the coupling conditions: the claimant admits under questioning that he sometimes left it at home for days, or that in recent months he had taken to verifying its entries against his phone, which defeats automatic endorsement. The notebook therefore never achieved the status of memory, and the loss is an ordinary property claim, reimbursable at the replacement cost of one ruled notebook, $4.99 plus applicable tax. The letter may gesture in passing at the bloat problem with bureaucratic dread -- a referenced internal memo on the firm's exposure should smartphones ever satisfy the criteria -- but the denial itself must rest on the conditions, not on any dismissal of the view.

Keep the tone deadpan and courteous throughout. A specialist in philosophy of mind should laugh because the letter applies the criteria correctly; a reader new to the debate should come away genuinely understanding why a notebook's location and habits of use could matter to the boundaries of a mind. Do not let the adjuster sneer at the claimant. The joke is precisely that the insurer is the one party treating his philosophy with full professional respect.

After the letter, add a brief out-of-character note of 100 to 150 words stating: which reading of the thesis the letter assumes (for example, on the status of dispositional versus occurrent belief), where it knowingly exaggerates or simplifies, and the single point on which you are least confident the satire is fair to the position as its defenders actually hold it.
