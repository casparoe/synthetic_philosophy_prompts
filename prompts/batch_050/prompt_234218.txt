In my early modern philosophy seminar last week we reached Berkeley's idealism, and whenever someone pressed the obvious objection — surely the tree in the empty quad still exists when nobody is looking at it — the professor waved it away with Berkeley's slogan, "to be is to be perceived," as though that settled the matter. She then invoked what she called the master argument: try to conceive of a tree existing unperceived, and you will fail, because any tree you conceive is one you are conceiving, and hence is present to a mind. I left class unsure what the slogan is even claiming, and suspicious that it is doing more work than it is entitled to.

Lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that "to be is to be perceived" could be expressing. I can already see a few candidates: a trivial observation about what happens when we think of something (that we are, in fact, thinking of it); a conceptual claim that "unperceived object" is a contradiction on the order of "unmarried husband"; a full-blooded metaphysical claim that nothing can exist mind-independently; and perhaps a deflationary claim about how the word "exists" gets its meaning. There may be readings I am missing. For each one, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or straightforwardly false — and, crucially, which readings the master argument would actually establish if it worked, and which it does not touch at all. Then commit to a verdict: name the single reading you find most defensible as philosophy, state flatly whether the master argument succeeds on that reading or commits the conflation my classmates kept suspecting (between "I cannot conceive of a thing as unconceived" and "no thing can exist unconceived"), and defend that verdict rather than leaving me to choose among the options. Keep the entire reply under 500 words.
