Two policy philosophers have agreed to submit the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration on pure time preference. One is a Temporal Neutralist: after controlling for mortality risk, uncertainty, economic growth, changing needs, and diminishing marginal resources, the bare fact that a benefit or harm occurs later provides no reason to count it for less. The other is a Moderate Temporal Partialist: a limited preference for nearer benefits can be normatively defensible even when all those factors are held fixed, at least within an individual life and perhaps in some collective decisions.

Draft the statement they could both sign concerning a government evaluating an investment whose main benefits would begin 150 years from now. The statement must contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. Include, at minimum, the distinction between pure time preference and discounting based on uncertainty, expected wealth, opportunity costs, preference change, or declining marginal utility; the difference between an individual discounting her own future experiences and a society discounting the experiences of not-yet-born people; and the consequences of each view for long-horizon policy.

2. A section stating their remaining disagreements exactly. Formulate each position so that its defender would accept the wording, and avoid reducing either side to a caricature about caring nothing for the future or nothing for present people.

3. A crux table. Each row should identify a genuine crux, explain why settling it would change at least one side’s position, describe a case or argument both regard as relevant, and record in advance what each side predicts or concludes about that case. Consider at least the following:
   - Two identical populations receive the same benefit at different times, with survival, wealth, uncertainty, and needs held fixed.
   - A person consistently prefers one unit of pleasure now to a slightly larger but otherwise identical unit in twenty years, solely because the latter is later.
   - A public project benefits only people who do not yet exist, while its costs fall on presently existing people.
   - Citizens strongly discount the far future even after understanding that doing so is pure time preference rather than uncertainty or expected growth.

4. A diagnosis of which disagreements are substantive and which are verbal. In particular, determine whether the parties are using preference, reason, and social welfare in the same way.

5. An agreed decision procedure for the 150-year investment despite the unresolved dispute: for example, parallel calculations under several discounting rules, sensitivity thresholds, or conditions under which one rule would be decisive. Do not present this practical compromise as settling the philosophical issue.

Conclude with the strongest claim both parties can endorse and the smallest possible reformulation of the disagreement that neither can yet accept.
