I am a data engineer with twelve years' experience designing event-sourced systems and bitemporal databases. My working vocabulary is append-only event logs, current-state projections, valid time versus transaction time, as-of queries, retroactive corrections and compaction; my philosophical training consists of a reading group that has recently reached the philosophy of time. In J. M. E. McTaggart's 1908 paper "The Unreality of Time" (Mind), we came across the following passage, which the group could not agree on how to read:

"It would, I suppose, be universally admitted that time involves change. A particular thing, indeed, may exist unchanged through any amount of time. But when we ask what we mean by saying that there were different moments of time, or a certain duration of time, through which the thing was the same, we find that we mean that it remained the same while other things were changing. A universe in which nothing whatever changed (including the thoughts of the conscious beings in it) would be a timeless universe."

I want you to do three things.

First, translate. Restate the dispute between presentism, the growing-block view and eternalism — together with McTaggart's distinction between the A series and the B series and the argument of the passage above — in the vocabulary of temporal data systems, as faithfully as you can. I expect the mapping to run through something like destructive-update current-state tables, append-only logs and fully populated bitemporal stores, but do not adopt my guesses uncritically: if one of the positions has no clean architectural counterpart, say so rather than forcing one.

Secondly, give me the residue report. For each element of the debate, state whether it translates cleanly, translates only with distortion, or has no expression at all in my vocabulary. Three points in particular need addressing. Does the passage's claim that time essentially involves change have a genuine counterpart in a bitemporal store, where every recorded fact is immutable and change shows up only as succession in transaction time? Does the ontological force of the dispute — the question of what exists — survive translation, or does it collapse into the much thinner question of what a system stores and can return to a query? And does the present have any counterpart beyond an as-of timestamp supplied by whoever issues the query?

Thirdly, a verdict, and I want you to commit to one. Does the residue show that my engineering vocabulary is missing something real — that there is a fact of the matter about existence or tensedness which no data model can represent — or does it show that the metaphysical dispute is, at least in part, less substantive than it appears, tracking nothing that even our best systems fail to track? Give reasons for the side you take, and flag any point in your own translation where you were tempted to smuggle in an assumption (for instance, treating what exists as what is in the store) and call it translation.

Please write in British English and in a committed register: definite judgements with reasons at each stage, rather than a survey of options for me to choose between. Around 1,500 words; the quoted passage does not count towards that.
