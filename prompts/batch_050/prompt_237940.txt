I teach a philosophy of mind seminar, and my students have spent two weeks arguing past each other about an Aunt Hillary-style ant colony. I want you to draft the joint statement they could all sign after a serious adversarial collaboration.

Use this stipulated case rather than relying on any published version. A colony of roughly 500,000 short-lived ants forages, allocates labor, repairs nest damage, responds differently to repeated intruders, and preserves some effects of past disturbances in its trail network. Individual ants use only local cues: pheromones, antennal contact, simple behavioral rules, and a very limited memory. No ant surveys the colony or knows its overall plan, and there is no hidden queen-controller issuing symbolic instructions. Researchers can nevertheless predict some colony behavior more compactly using colony-level states such as food priority, threat level, and repair goal than by tracking every ant separately.

The two camps are:

1. The Colony Functionalists. They think mentality depends on the right functional organization, not on neurons, a single organism, or a centralized control room. If colony-level states carry information, interact with one another, persist through ant turnover, and flexibly control behavior, then the colony can literally have colony-level beliefs, desires, intentions, or memories. They are not required to claim that it is phenomenally conscious.

2. The Many-Minds Skeptics. They think the colony is an organized population of tiny agents, plus a useful high-level description invented by observers. On their view, a mind requires enough reciprocal integration and unity for there to be one continuing subject of cognition and action, not merely a system whose behavior can be summarized as if it had goals. They should not deny distributed cognition in principle or claim that every mind needs a homunculus.

Write the actual document, not a report about what such a document would say. It should include:

- A short preamble defining colony-level competence, colony-level agency, subjecthood, and phenomenal consciousness separately, since confusing these has caused half the seminar’s problems.
- Eight to twelve numbered agreed claims. They should be substantive enough that each camp gives something up. For example, both might agree that predictive usefulness alone does not establish mentality, but also that spatial distribution and lack of a center do not by themselves rule it out.
- A disagreement ledger with four columns: the exact issue, the formulation the Functionalists would sign, the formulation the Skeptics would sign, and whether the disagreement is empirical, conceptual, or partly verbal.
- At least four cruxes. Include causal integration versus observer-relative description, the basis of a colony’s boundary and unity, persistence through turnover or splitting, and the relation between cognition and phenomenal consciousness.
- For every crux, describe one test or thought experiment both sides accept as relevant and record each side’s prediction in advance. The available manipulations are selective disruption of communication channels, a bandwidth ladder created by adding increasingly capable relay ants, replacement of ants with behaviorally matched microrobots, temporary splitting and reconnection of the colony, and interventions on colony-level variables while leaving the ants’ local rules unchanged.
- For the consciousness crux, do not pretend that successful task performance settles the issue. State what the behavioral evidence could and could not show.
- A final section listing claims both camps agree not to make, followed by the smallest possible statement of their remaining disagreement.

Keep the Functionalists from inferring a mind merely because the macro-description compresses behavior, and keep the Skeptics from defining a mind so biologically that they simply win by stipulation. If part of the dispute turns out to be about words, identify the offending term and propose a more precise replacement.
