So here's my situation. I'm a retired chemist, spent fourty years on polymer kinetics, and in retirement I've fallen down the philosophy-of-social-science rabbit hole. Recently I got into an argument at my reading group about whether "Millennial" or "Gen Z" actually pick out anything real, or whether they're just marketing labels we slapped on arbitrary birth-year ranges.

In the course of that argument someone mentioned the "age-period-cohort problem" from demography, and I looked it up, and I gather the gist is: any difference you observe between, say, 30-year-olds today and 30-year-olds in 1990 could in principle be due to (a) something about being 30, (b) something about living through the present era, or (c) something about having been born in a particular stretch of years -- and apparently there's a mathematical reason you can't fully tease these apart no matter how much data you have, something about the three variables being linearly entangled so that any pattern in the data is consistent with infinitely many decompositions.

Here's what I'd like explained, at a gut level rather than with formal proofs, since my statistics is fifty years out of date:

1. Why exactly does this identification problem arise? I want the intuition for why more data doesn't fix it -- in my old field, underdetermination usually went away once you got enough measurements, so I find this genuinely puzzling.

2. Does this underdetermination actually bear on the philosophical question, or is it a red herring? I can imagine two views. On one, if we can't even in principle separate "cohort effects" from age and period effects, then "generations" fail some basic reality test -- a real kind should make a difference you could at least in principle isolate. On the other view, generations might be real for a completely different reason than demographers assume: the labels themselves do causal work. Once a society sorts people by birth year (policy eligibility cutoffs, marketing, everyone believing "Millennials are like X"), those beliefs and institutions feed back and mold the cohort into something genuinely cluster-like, so the kind is real but self-manufactured rather than discovered. Call these the "no isolate, no kind" view and the "self-fulfilling kind" view.

So: explain the identification problem, then explain which of these two views it actually supports (if either), and whether there's a coherent middle position where a generation is real but its boundaries are still arbitrary -- real inland, fuzzy at the coastline, so to speak.

Keep it informal, and assume I know basic stats but nothing of sociology. End your whole reply with a single last line starting "Bottom line:" that gives your one-sentence answer to whether generations are real kinds.
