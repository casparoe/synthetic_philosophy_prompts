Government cost-benefit analysis and government "nudge" programs seem to me to rest on opposite assumptions, and I want to understand what, if anything, holds them together. On the one hand, when regulators decide whether a safety rule is worth its cost, they value the benefits by measuring people's preferences -- willingness-to-pay surveys, wage premiums for risky jobs -- and they count those preferences more or less at face value, as data to be aggregated. On the other hand, many of the same governments run behavioral policy units whose entire premise is that people's choices are riddled with error: they redesign pension enrollment defaults, calorie labels, and organ-donor forms precisely because what people actually choose is treated as a mistake to be engineered around. So in one room, revealed and stated preferences are the gold standard of what people want; in the next room, the same preferences are symptoms of present bias, inertia, and framing effects. I have no academic training in this, but it seems to me that the combined practice needs some principled way of telling a genuine preference (an input that policy should respect) from a bias (a defect that policy should correct), and I can't see what it is. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. Specifically: reconstruct the implicit theory the combined practice presupposes -- what test, if any, it assumes for sorting preferences from biases (stability? informedness? what the person would choose under ideal conditions? something about welfare versus choice?). Then say whether the practice actually requires that theory or is merely compatible with it -- could someone who thought all apparent biases are just unusual preferences (a steep discount rate, say, rather than a self-control failure) still coherently run both programs? And consider the harder case: if preferences are partly constructed by the choice architecture itself, so that there is no pre-existing "true want" for a debiasing procedure to recover, does the preference/bias distinction collapse into a policy decision dressed up as a discovery -- and if so, what would both practices have to change to be honest about that?
