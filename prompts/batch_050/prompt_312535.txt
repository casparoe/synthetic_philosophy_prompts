Sanity check before I present this at our formal epistemology reading group — I want to know if my package on logical uncertainty actually hangs together. Here's what I currently hold:

(1) The whole justification for having probabilistic credences at all is Dutch-book-style: a set of credences is defective if it exposes you to sure loss, and coherence is the standard credences have to meet.
(2) It's rational for me, right now, to assign something like 0.9 to P ≠ NP and 0.5 to the claim that the 10^100th digit of pi is even — even though each of those is either a logical truth or a logical falsehood, and I know that.
(3) Logical induction (the MIRI-style framework where credences are prices in a computable market over mathematical statements) is the right account of how bounded agents should handle this, and I'm fine with the fact that its prices are only guaranteed coherent in the limit, not at any finite time.
(4) A norm of rationality has to be satisfiable by a computable process, otherwise it's not a norm for agents like me — "ought" here implies "can, in principle, compute."
(5) In the long run I should converge to certainty on any checkable mathematical claim; being permanently uncertain about something a proof could settle would be a defect in teh view.

Tell me which of these genuinely conflict, which only look like they conflict, and rank the real tensions by how bad they are. For each real one, say what the cheapest revision is and what it costs me elsewhere (e.g., if I weaken (1), what happens to my justification for being a probabilist about ordinary one-shot events?). And if some pair looks bad but dissolves once you're careful about what limit guarantees actually say, show the dissolution rather than just asserting it. Please write the answer in American English.
