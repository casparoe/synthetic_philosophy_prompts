Write a dialogue between two friends, Maya and Jonas, who've each committed to donating $20,000 to charity and are stuck on whether any of it should go to insect welfare (e.g. better conditions on insect farms) instead of something like chicken welfare.

Jonas argues the scale case: even if the chance that insects are sentient is really low, say 1%, there are so many insects that the expected moral weight crushes everything else, so being a responsible donor means paying up for that tiny probability.

Maya pushes back with a specific objection she calls the subdivision problem: expected-value reasoning needs individuated candidates, but nature doesn't come pre-individuated. Is a beehive one candidate or 50,000? If you individuate finely enough, bacteria and soil mites outnumber insects by just as much as insects outnumber chickens, and Jonas doesn't actually want to donate to bacteria. So the scale argument only works if you sneak in some principled cutoff for what counts as one candidate -- and that cutoff is doing basically all the work the probability estimate pretended to do.

Jonas should have a real answer to this (maybe something about how fast sentience-probability plausibly drops off relative to how fast the numbers grow), and Maya should have a real counter to that. Neither of them is a strawman, both are smart, they respond to what the other actually said rather then talking past each other. By the end, at least one of them should have budged on something concrete, even if they dont fully agree on where the money goes.
