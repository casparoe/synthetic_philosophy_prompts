I chair the committee of an allotment association founded in the 1880s, and we are currently divided over whether to replace our rulebook wholesale. Someone mentioned "Chesterton's fence" to me — the idea that you should not remove an old rule until you understand why it exists — and this led me to read Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790), which I am working through slowly, having no background in philosophy or political theory.

Early on, Burke distances himself from two London clubs sympathetic to the French Revolution, describing their members as departing from "the firm but cautious and deliberate spirit which produced the one, and which presides in the other" — the one being the English constitution, the other the Revolution of 1688. He also writes, "I reckon myself among the most forward in my zeal for maintaining that constitution and those principles in their utmost purity and vigor."

Here is what I take this to mean, and where I would like to be checked. Burke's objection to the French seems, on this passage, to be procedural: they were hasty, intemperate, lacking the "cautious and deliberate" manner. So I have concluded that the Burkean test for any change — in a state or in an allotment association — is about how the change is made: slowly, carefully, after consultation. On that reading, a revolution conducted by patient annual statute over thirty years, each step minuted and calmly debated, would pass Burke's test even if it ended by dissolving everything the French revolutionaries dissolved. Am I right that he would have had no grounds for complaint in that case? And for my own committee: does the fence principle get discharged simply by our going slowly and keeping good records of our deliberations, or is something more required?
