Two friends of mine, Dana and Marcus, got into an argument after a talk at our local philosophy society about Berkeley's so-called master argument — the passage where he challenges his reader to conceive of a sensible object, say a tree, existing while unconceived by anyone, and claims the attempt defeats itself because in conceiving the tree you thereby conceive it. Dana's position is that this is a bare equivocation and nothing more: of course you cannot conceive of an unconceived thing, just as you cannot describe an undescribed thing, but that shows only a trivial fact about acts of conceiving, not that trees cannot exist unperceived — the failure lies in the performance, not in the possibility. Marcus replied that this standard dismissal underestimates the argument: Berkeley's point, he said, is not that the exercise is awkward but that the phrase "unperceived sensible object" is contentless, because everything we mean by a tree — its color, shape, texture, size — is drawn from perception, so subtracting all perceiving leaves not a hidden tree but nothing thinkable at all. Dana answered that this conflates the object conceived with the act of conceiving it: a photograph of a supposedly unphotographed mountain is self-defeating, but the mountain survives the failed photograph untouched, and likewise the tree is not constituted by my thinking of it. Marcus countered that the photograph analogy begs the question, since it assumes the object retains its features once the representation is stripped away, whereas Berkeley's whole claim is that sensible qualities cannot be abstracted from being sensed — you cannot peel a color off its being seen the way you can peel a mountain off its being photographed. Dana then retreated to primary qualities: perhaps color is perception-soaked, but shape and size are geometrical and can be thought of mind-independently. Marcus answered that Berkeley has a reply there too, in his critique of abstract ideas — we have no idea of extension that is not some determinate, perceivable extension, so the allegedly mind-independent residue is an illusion of abstraction. Dana's final move was concessive: even granting all of that, the inconceivability of unperceived existence shows at most that we cannot conceive it, not that it is impossible, unless Marcus adds a bridge principle licensing inference from inconceivability to impossibility, and that principle needs independent defense. Marcus's final move was that for sensible things — as opposed to, say, numbers — conceivability by some mind is the only notion of possibility available to us, so "possible yet inconceivable to every mind" is itself a phrase no one can attach content to. They asked me to referee and I found I couldn't. Please adjudicate: who is right about the equivocation charge, about whether the photograph analogy clarifies or begs the question, about whether the retreat to primary qualities saves the realist, and about who bears the burden on the conceivability-to-possibility bridge? Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important — about the argument's proper target or its limits — that both of them are missing.
