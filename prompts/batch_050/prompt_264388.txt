I don’t have any academic background, but I’ve been turning over the mental causation problem and keep landing on a view I half-invented. Suppose every bodily movement has a complete physical cause: neurons fire, chemicals move, muscles contract, and there is no gap where an extra mental force needs to squeeze in. My idea is that a mental state can still be a cause because it belongs to a different “ledger.” The physical ledger records the immediate pushes. The mental ledger records the pattern that made this whole sequence happen rather than one of the alternatives. Pain doesn’t add a second shove to the neurons; it is the organized condition under which those neurons make me withdraw, avoid the stove later, tell someone about it, and so on.

My provisional position is: mental states are genuinely causal when changing the mental pattern would systematically change the bodily pattern, even though every individual bodily event is already physically accounted for. On this view, mind and brain are not competing causes, and the causal exclusion argument is treating different levels of explanation as if they were two billiard balls fighting over the same collision.

What would count against this? Please map the view’s exposure rather than just repeating that physics is causally closed. Which possible cases, arguments, or experimental results would show that my two-ledger picture is really only a useful redescription and not causation? Would it be fatal if holding the exact physical process fixed while changing only the mental state is impossible? Or does that merely show that mental states are physically realized? What if mental-level explanations are almost always less predictive than neural ones—does that demote them to summaries, or can something be causal without being the most detailed predictor?

I’d especially like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely embarrassing. For each one, say whether someone sympathetic to my position would honestly accept it as a threat, or whether they would claim it depends on too narrow a picture of causation. Also tell me what this view predicts that a straightforward “the experience is just along for the ride” view does not. If there is no real difference in predictions, is my position defnitely untestable, or can two views still differ conceptually without being rivals in the laboratory?
