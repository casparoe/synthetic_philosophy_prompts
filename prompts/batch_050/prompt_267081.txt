I serve on a volunteer citizen advisory panel for a mid-sized American city that fields requests to remove, rename, relocate, or add interpretive context to statues, street names, and named public buildings. We meet six times a year and typically handle eight to twelve cases; our recommendations go to the city council, which has followed them about four times out of five, so the stakes feel real. I have no academic training in ethics or political theory -- my thinking on this comes from newspaper debates, public hearings, and long arguments with two other panel members -- but I keep circling one principle I find compelling: that a statue in a central square and a photograph in a museum do different things, that the first is an act of ongoing public endorsement by the community in the present while the second is a record of the past, and that the key question in each case is therefore not "was this person good or bad" but "what does it say about us, now, that this name or figure still occupies this place." I am sure this principle is not original to me, and I suspect others have developed it more carefully than I can.

What I want is for you to turn that principle into a working procedure our panel could actually follow: an ordered sequence of tests or questions we apply to every case the same way, before we open the floor to public comment, so that the process stops being a referendum on whichever side shows up to the meeting in greater numbers. The procedure should specify, in order: how we establish what the marker was originally intended to communicate and how much weight that original intent should get against the marker's present-day meaning; how we determine what a monument or name communicates to residents now, and whose testimony counts as evidence of that; which features of a case (the person's connection to the city, whether the thing being honored is the same as the thing that was wrong, the difference between honoring someone's whole life and honoring a specific act, the distinction between a marker erected to intimidate and one erected to mourn) belong in the analysis and which are distractions; what the menu of options is -- leave as is, add interpretive context, relocate to a museum or cemetery, rename, remove -- and what thresholds move a case from one option to the next; and how to handle situations where we cannot get the facts we need. Wherever a step is really a judgment call dressed up as a checklist item, mark it explicitly as a judgment call rather than pretending the procedure settles it.

Please apply the finished procedure to three hypothetical cases, showing the step-by-step working: first, a bronze memorial to Confederate soldiers on the courthouse lawn, installed some fifty years after the war, during a period when segregationist laws were being consolidated locally; second, an elementary school named after an admired founding-era statesman who enslaved people, where the name dates to the school's founding and the current neighborhood is majority Black; third, a street named sixty years ago for a beloved local mayor under whom the city prospered but who used his office to block integrated housing, with the renaming request coming from residents of that street who would bear the administrative cost and hassle of the change. I lean personally toward removal in most disputes, so include at least one case where the procedure returns a verdict I will not like, and be explicit about which step produced it. Close by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do -- the classes of disagreement it will not resolve and where the panel will still just have to vote its conscience.
