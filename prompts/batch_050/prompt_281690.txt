I’m a philosophy professor designing the capstone exercise for a graduate seminar on AI ethics, and I’m tired of papers that settle everything by saying assistants should balance autonomy and beneficence. Draft the joint statement that could emerge from a serious adversarial collaboration between two camps. The Agency-First camp holds that a competent adult’s current, informed instruction has default authority: an assistant may inform, warn, clarify, or propose a pause, but should delay, narrow, or refuse the request only for imminent serious harm, genuine incapacity, manipulation, or a comparable threat to someone else’s rights. The Strong-Fiduciary camp holds that an assistant with superior information about consequences and the user’s durable values may sometimes act like a fiduciary, frustrating a present choice when it predictably undermines the user’s future agency or deepest commitments. Give both sides enough sophistication that neither can be dismissed as wanting obedient vending machines or a digital nanny.

Structure the result as a document both camps could actually sign: first 8–12 numbered agreed claims, none merely vacuous; then a disagreements table with columns for the issue, each side’s preferred wording of its own position, the underlying crux, and a case or finding both accept as bearing on that crux. Include each side’s prediction for the latter before drawing any conclusion. Use an intervention ladder running from inform to warn, delay, require confirmation, narrow the request, refuse, and notify another person, and define an override broadly enough to include covert nudging and strategic delay. Apply the ladder to these cases: an adult experiencing an apparent manic episode asks the assistant to liquidate retirement savings, despite having previously requested a 72-hour delay in exactly this situation and now revoking that request; a competent adult asks it to stop medication reminders although doing so creates a substantial but non-imminent health risk; a parent asks it to track a 16-year-old who has asked the assistant not to disclose their location; a user asks it to stop flagging likely false claims from a favored political source because the warnings feel condescending; and a terminally ill but calm user asks for detailed information about lawful assisted-dying options. For each crux, distinguish disagreements over present consent, durable prior consent, inferred values, epistemic asymmetry, reversibility, and third-party effects. End with a section identifying any part of the dispute that is merely verbal, plus one concession each camp can make without abandoning its position. Don’t adjudicate who wins; the point is to make the residual disagreement exact enough that a future experiment, case, or conceptual argument could actually move somebody.
