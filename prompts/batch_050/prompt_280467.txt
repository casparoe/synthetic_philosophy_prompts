Big family argument over Christmas and I want a word for something. My uncle, who drove drunk regulary in his twenties and once totalled his car (nobody hurt, thankfully), has been sober for fifteen years and now absolutely lays into my cousin for getting a DUI last month. Everything he said was true, he doesn't do the thing anymore, and still the whole table had this visceral "YOU of all people" reaction. Same thing with a guy at my work who used to pad his expense reports, got quietly disciplined years ago, made it right, and last week reported a new hire for doing exactly that -- and somehow his reporting her felt *off* to everyone even though the new hire genuinely did it. A third case: an ex-friend of mine who never repays small loans himself (still doesn't!) keeps tweeting angrily about people who stiff their friends. The last one feels like plain hypocrisy, fine, but the first two feel different because the person has actually stopped. Does the thing that's wrong with the reformed person's blaming have its own name, or is it just hypocrisy with a time delay? I want the carving, not just a label: what exactly is it, how does it differ from straight hypocrisy on the one hand and from the "this is none of your business" case on the other, and where are the edges -- e.g. does it matter if he only stopped because he got caught, or if he never apologized for the old stuff, or if the person he blames doesn't know about his past?

The reason I'm asking is that my uncle defended himself with Romans 2, which he clearly hadn't read carefully, because it says: "Therefore thou art inexcusable, O man, whosoever thou art that judgest: for wherein thou judgest another, thou condemnest thyself; for thou that judgest doest the same things." Here's what's bugging me: Paul says "doest," present tense, the person judging is *currently* doing the same things. So does the mechanism Paul is pointing at -- the way judging someone else convicts you by your own standard -- even apply to someone who has genuinely quit? It seems like the self-condemnation bit might actually get *stronger* for the reformed person (their own judgment condemns their past self, which is maybe fine and even admirable?) while the "who are you to talk" reaction stays put, which would mean the two things come apart. I'd like your reading of the passage and whether it supports my uncle, my cousin, or neither.

One request: when you answer, please separate what's actually agreed on by philosophers who work on blame and moral criticism from what's genuinely up for grabs between camps. I've noticed that some people talk as if it's just obvious that a guilty person can't blame anyone, and others talk as if it's obvious that true blame is fine from anybody, and I suspect the real state of play is messier than either. Don't present one side's view as the settled consensus if it isn't. And if it turns out there's no established name for the reformed-wrongdoer case specifically, go ahead and coin one, with a definition tight enough that I could tell whether new cases fall inside or outside it.
