I'm a TA for an undergrad epistemology course and I need a fresh Gettier case for the midterm, one the students can't just pattern-match from the textbook (they've all seen the stopped clock, the sheep in the field, the fake barns, the ten coins, a hundred times). So construct one to these specs: (1) the protagonist's belief is true and well-justified but clearly not knowledge; (2) nothing in her reasoning runs through a false premise -- every belief she actually forms on the way to the conclusion is true, so the cheap "no false lemmas" fix can't save her as a knower; (3) the setting is completely ordinary, an office or a kitchen or a bus commute, no clocks, no barns, no lottery tickets, no disguised anything; and here's the constraint I care about most, (4) the bad luck that undermines her justification and the good luck that makes her belief true must share a common cause -- a single event or decision in the story has to be responsible for both, rather than the usual setup where two independent coincidences collide. That last one is because I want the students to see that Gettier luck isn't always just the world being doubly weird.

After you give the scenario (keep it to a paragraph or so), do the bookkeeping for me: state the belief, the justification, and exactly where the epistemic luck enters; walk through why each of the four constraints is satisfied, not just assert that they are; then tell me what a defeasibility theorist and a safety theorist would each say about the case and whether they agree on the verdict. Finally, identify the one structural feature doing all the work and tell me what happens if you remove it -- does it collapse back into an ordinary case of knowledge, or into a standard two-coincidence Gettier case? If you cheat by stipulating the verdict instead of generating it from the setup, my students will smell it immediatly, so don't.
