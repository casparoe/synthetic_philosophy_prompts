Draft the joint statement that could emerge from a serious adversarial collaboration between two camps in the theory of legal proof, working on the problem of naked statistical evidence. Camp one, the probabilists, holds that legal proof just is a matter of posterior probability: if the evidence available to the fact-finder makes the defendant's liability sufficiently probable -- above the preponderance threshold in a civil case, beyond reasonable doubt in a criminal one -- then a finding against the defendant is justified, and it makes no difference in principle whether that probability rests on eyewitness testimony, forensic traces, or base rates alone. Camp two, the particularists, holds that bare statistical evidence is defective as a basis for legal proof even when it is highly reliable: proof must be tied to the particular case by evidence that is, in some sense to be specified, about this defendant and this event, and a conviction or judgment resting on base rates alone would be illegitimate no matter how strong the numbers.

Use the two standard cases as shared fixtures, and treat them as common ground that both sides must explain rather than as trump cards for either: the Blue Bus case, in which a plaintiff is run off the road by an unidentified bus and the defendant company operates a stipulated large majority of the buses on that route; and the gatecrasher case, in which a stipulated large majority of attendees at an event entered without paying and a randomly selected attendee is sued for the price of admission on no other evidence.

The statement should contain: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims -- for instance, about what the relevant probability thresholds are taken to be, about the fact that the two cases feel different from paradigm eyewitness cases, about the unreliability of actual eyewitness testimony relative to intuition, and about any real-world practices (such as the market-share liability doctrine or the treatment of DNA match statistics) that both sides accept as data points; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated in wording its own side would sign, with no formulation that smuggles in the other side's premises -- pay special attention to whether the particularist's operative notion (individualized evidence, sensitivity, normic support, a knowledge norm on verdicts, or something else) can be stated without begging the question against the probabilist; (3) a cruxes section identifying the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp -- likely candidates include whether legal proof aims at knowledge rather than justified belief, whether the asymmetric treatment of statistical and testimonial evidence is defensible once error rates are equated, and whether the legitimacy worry is really about the evidence or about what a verdict expresses toward the defendant; and (4) for each crux, a stipulated case, argument, or empirical finding that both sides agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction about it recorded in advance -- for example, how each side handles a case where the statistical evidence is far more reliable than any testimony ever is, or where the only evidence against an identified defendant is a base rate and the only evidence against an unidentified one is an eyewitness.

Where part of the dispute turns out to be verbal -- where the two camps use proof or evidence differently and would agree on the verdicts once the vocabularies are disentangled -- the statement should say so explicitly and mark which disagreements survive that cleaning-up. Close with a short section each camp writes for itself: the strongest reason it might be wrong.
