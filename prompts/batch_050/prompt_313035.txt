I'm a PhD student in philosophy (my own work is in philosophy of science rather than epistemology), and I put together the following argument for a reading group on epistemic circularity. The conclusion strikes me as absurd, but each step looked defensible when I wrote it, and I can no longer tell where the trouble is. I'd like a diagnosis.

(1) It is epistemically illegitimate to use a source of belief to establish the reliability of that very source: circular justification justifies nothing.
(2) Any check on a basic faculty must employ that faculty. I cannot verify my vision except by looking, nor validate my reasoning except by reasoning, nor confirm my memory except by remembering (or by perception, whose own check would require the same). So basic faculties admit only circular validation.
(3) From (1) and (2): no basic faculty can be non-circularly validated, and since circular validation is worthless, beliefs produced by basic faculties are unjustified -- unless (1) is false.
(4) Global skepticism is unacceptable, so (1) must be false: some bootstrapping, using a source to vouch for itself, must be epistemically permissible.
(5) But if bootstrapping is permissible at all, nothing in the rejection of (1) confines it to basic faculties. A driver could learn that her fuel gauge is reliable by repeatedly noting that the gauge reads F when the tank is full and E when it is empty -- where each judgment about the tank's state is itself taken from the gauge. A mystic could establish that his crystal ball is reliable by asking the ball whether it is reliable and receiving a yes. Both would thereby come to know their sources are reliable.
(6) The results in (5) are absurd; that is knowledge come by too easily.
(7) So (4) is false, (1) stands, and skepticism about basic faculties follows after all. Perception is, epistemically, no better off than the crystal ball.

The conclusion in (7) seems to prove too much -- it would undermine not just common sense but the empirical science I rely on in my own research. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Identify the step or steps a careful epistemologist would reject, explain why each bad step looked innocent to me, and tell me what the live alternatives cost. For what it's worth, I'm most reluctant to surrender (1), which seems like a near-platitude about circularity, and most reluctant to accept either global skepticism or the mystic's knowledge; but I take it I cannot keep all three, and I want to know which is cheapest to abandon and why. If it matters, note that some reliabilists I know simply accept the gauge case in (5) -- I'd like to understand whether that's a coherent bullet to bite or whether it concedes more than they realize.
