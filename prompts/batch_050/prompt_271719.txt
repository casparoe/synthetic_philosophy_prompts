I teach a seminar on the ethics of blame, and I want to pressure-test my own intuitions about standing by finding out whether they reduce to any coherent principle. My verdicts: (a) A man who routinely cheats on his wife, and who berates a colleague over his affair, plainly lacks standing. (b) A woman twenty years sober who sharply rebukes her still-drinking brother-in-law has standing. (c) An executive who quietly voted for the cost-cutting policy she now publicly denounces lacks standing, though her fault here is complicity and concealment rather than hypocrisy strictly so called. (d) My childless neighbor may permissibly criticize my harsh parenting, but the same rebuke costs less and lands better coming from my sister, who has raised children of her own. (e) The recovered alcoholic who still relapses once or twice a year, and rebukes the brother-in-law during a sober stretch, strikes me as retaining standing, though I admit this sits uneasily with (a), since the difference seems merely statistical and temporal rather than principled. Is there a single account of standing that delivers all five verdicts -- perhaps distinguishing differential fault, concealment, and relational proximity as independent defeaters -- or must one of them go? If a verdict must be sacrificed, tell me which one and why. Then state what the resulting account predicts for a case I have not listed: a sentencing judge who privately commits, but has never been caught for, the very offense before the court. Please state your principle precisely, and keep the whole reply under 400 words.
