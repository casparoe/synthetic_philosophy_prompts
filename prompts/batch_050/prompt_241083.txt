After a career as an experimental chemist, I have become interested in Otto Neurath’s ambition that carefully standardized pictures could communicate practical information across linguistic boundaries. Please construct a thought experiment that puts this ambition under pressure rather than merely illustrating its advantages.

Devise a realistic scenario involving a hospital, refugee camp, or international disaster-relief center that adopts an invented pictorial language called MERIDIAN. The system must follow three Neurathian design principles: use simplified and standardized human and object figures; make meanings independent of any spoken or written natural language; and represent larger quantities by repeating identical symbols rather than by enlarging one symbol.

The scenario must satisfy these constraints:

1. Present it in no more than one paragraph.
2. Include people from at least three language communities who must make a consequential decision from the same display.
3. Make MERIDIAN genuinely successful at eliminating the ordinary problems of translation and at allowing rapid comparison across cases.
4. Engineer the case so that this very standardization produces a serious error that a less elegant hybrid system—one using pictograms together with selected words, numerals, or units—would probably prevent.
5. The error must not result from an incorrectly drawn symbol, inadequate training, careless reading, or an obvious violation of MERIDIAN’s rules. It should arise from the categories, quantities, or distinctions that the pictorial system has chosen to encode.
6. Do not make MERIDIAN’s designers foolish or negligent; their choices should be defensible in light of the information available when the system was created.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it works. Explain the relevant Neurathian commitments concerning standardization, cross-linguistic accessibility, reduction to salient features, and the claimed practical neutrality of well-designed pictures. Then show precisely how those commitments generate the erroneous interpretation rather than merely making it psychologically possible. Distinguish the failure from three more familiar problems: arbitrary symbols, cultural differences in interpreting images, and simple ignorance of a code.

Finally, identify the single feature of the case doing most of the philosophical work, explain what would happen if it were removed, and give the strongest Neurathian response. In particular, consider whether Neurath should say that every system of representation must suppress some distinctions, so the case reveals an unavoidable trade-off rather than a distinctive defect of picture languages. I intend to use the case with a mixed audience of scientists and beginning philosophy students, so please make the technical details concrete without presupposing knowledge of semiotics.
