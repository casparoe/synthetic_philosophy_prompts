I am preparing a graduate seminar on the epistemology of logic, and before we assess any positive account of the justification of deduction — rule-circular justifications, primitivism about modus ponens, inferentialist treatments of the connectives, sceptical quietism — I want the seminar to agree on what such an account is supposed to accomplish in the first place. Write the desiderata: a specification of what any adequate philosophical account of the justification of deductive inference must deliver.

The central test case is the regress dramatised in Lewis Carroll's dialogue in Mind (1895): Achilles takes the Tortoise to accept the premises of a modus ponens, and also the conditional linking them to the conclusion, yet the Tortoise withholds the conclusion itself; and when the missing principle is written down as an additional premise, the difficulty simply reappears at the next step. Your list should state, with the motivating consideration in each case, which phenomena an account must explain, which distinctions it must respect, and which cases it must handle. Among the candidates I want treated explicitly: whether it must explain why the Tortoise is making a mistake rather than merely reasoning differently; whether it must avoid employing, within the justification itself, the very rule being justified; whether it must distinguish justifying a particular inference from justifying a general rule of inference; whether it must leave room for the intelligibility of logical revision (the intuitionist who rejects excluded middle, the relevant logician who rejects ex falso quodlibet) without collapsing into anything goes; and whether it must account for the normative force of deduction — why one ought to believe what follows from what one believes.

I also want the list tested against two cases of my own construction, which I have not seen treated in the literature. First, the Chained Tortoise: she applies modus ponens readily to any single step but refuses to accept conclusions reached by chaining more than a few dozen steps, on the grounds that each further application is a fresh act of faith rather than something secured by the first. Second, the Deontic Tortoise: she grants that the conclusion follows from premises she accepts, but denies that this gives her any reason to believe it — for her, validity is a classification of argument-forms, not a source of obligation. An adequate account should have something determinate to say about each, and your desiderata should make clear why saying something about them is a requirement rather than an optional extra.

Mark which desiderata are non-negotiable for any account and which you suspect are the prejudices of a particular camp. Flag any subset that appears jointly unsatisfiable — in particular, whether a strict non-circularity requirement can coexist with the requirement that a justification take the form of a piece of reasoning — since if the list itself harbours an inconsistency, that is the result I most care about. Identify the single desideratum that excludes the largest number of existing accounts. Only at the end, and briefly, indicate how the main contenders fare against the finished list; the criteria and their motivating cases are the deliverable, not the scorecard. Please write in British English throughout.
