You know Lewis Carroll's dialogue where the Tortoise makes Achilles keep adding the rule of inference itself as another premise, so the conclusion never quite follows no matter how many premises get piled on. Take that as shared background, no need to recap it at length.

Two camps have grown out of that dialogue. Camp One, call them the Grounders: they think the Tortoise's demand is perfectly legitimate at every step, so the real lesson is that modus ponens itself needs justifying, and since any inferential justification would be circular, deduction has to bottom out in something non-inferential -- a rational insight, a brute disposition to infer, something like that. Camp Two, call them the Deflationists: they think the Tortoise just conflates reasoning *according to* a rule with reasoning *from* a premise, so there's nothing to justify and the whole regress is a red herring that evaporates once you keep the rule/premise distinction straight.

Now here's a wrinkle I want them to deal with. Imagine a third character, the Sloth. Unlike the Tortoise, the Sloth never asks for modus ponens as a premise -- he happily reasons according to it. But before each application he insists on double-checking that *this particular instance* is licensed, and the checking is itself a little inference, which then needs its own check, and so on. The regress moves from the content (what's written among the premises) to the act (the individual applications). Grounders tend to say the Sloth proves their point, since the demand for justification just reappears at the level of use. Deflationists tend to say the Sloth proves theirs, since nobody ever needed the rule as a premise and the "checks" are obviously optional extras. 

Draft the joint statement these two could actually sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. Specifically:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims -- what both accept the original dialogue does and doesn't show, whether both agree a rule-circular justification of modus ponens is at least circular, etc. Nothing vacuous, nothing slanted toward one camp.
2. A disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated precisely, with the wording of each side's position phrased so that side would accept it as a fair statement of its own view.
3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one camp. For each crux, give a case or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, with each camp's prediction written down in advance. Candidates you might consider: whether someone could coherently accept the premises *and* the rule-as-premise while withholding the conclusion; what Prior's tonk connective shows about whether inference rules need justification at all; and of course the Sloth.
4. For each camp, at least one thing they admit would count against their own view.
5. If any part of the dispute turns out to be merely verbal, name that part and explain why it's verbal rather than substantive.

Keep it to a page or two, and don't let either camp come off as the author's favorite.
