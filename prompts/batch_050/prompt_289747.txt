In my graduate seminar on the philosophy of the social sciences, a student offered the following argument, which purports to show not merely that Malthusian predictions of resource-driven collapse are unscientific but that the defect infects all long-run prediction. I reproduce it numbered, since I cannot locate the step I wish to reject.

(1) A prediction is falsifiable only if some possible course of events would count decisively against it.
(2) Whenever a Malthusian prediction fails to materialize by its deadline, defenders respond that the underlying mechanism — population growth outrunning the growth of subsistence — is still operating and that the collapse has merely been postponed; every non-occurrence is reinterpreted as "not yet."
(3) A prediction whose every failure can be reinterpreted as deferral is one against which no possible course of events counts decisively.
(4) Therefore Malthusian collapse predictions are unfalsifiable, and a theory resting on them is not empirical science.
(5) But deferral of precisely this form is available for any long-run prediction: the heat death of the universe, the exhaustion of any finite resource, the decay of a given radioactive atom, the proposition that a fair coin tossed indefinitely will eventually land heads.
(6) Therefore none of these claims is falsifiable either, and thermodynamics, resource economics, and nuclear physics fail the demarcation criterion along with Malthus.
(7) Since (6) is absurd, something in (1)–(3) must give way.

Diagnose the argument. If it is invalid, identify the defective step, explain why it appeared sound, and state what the repair costs. If it is valid, identify which premise is least expensive to surrender; I should prefer to retain (1), since some demarcation criterion seems indispensable, but tell me what I am paying for it. In either case, address two further questions. First, is there a principled distinction between deferral supported by independently testable auxiliary hypotheses and deferral that merely insulates a theory from evidence, such that (4) might be retained while (6) is blocked? Second, is "right but early" a coherent epistemic status for a theory at all, or only a euphemism for "wrong so far"? At most 700 words, in prose organized by numbered points.
