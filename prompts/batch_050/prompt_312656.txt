A friend and I keep arguing about when it's rational to flat-out believe something rather than just assign it a high credence. Here's how she's ruled on cases so far:

- One-million-ticket fair lottery, exactly one winner: believing "ticket #581,204 will lose" is NOT rational outright belief, however tiny the chance of being wrong.
- An eyewitness who's been right about 90% of the time in comparable conditions says the getaway car was green: believing the car was green IS rational.
- A cold-hit DNA case where the only evidence against the defendant is a database match with a random-match probability of 1 in 50 million: believing he's guilty is NOT rational.
- A historian who has carefully checked each of the 300 claims in her new book: it IS rational for her to believe each individual claim, and also rational for her to believe at least one of them is false (she's caught errors in every previous book). I never asked her about believing the conjunction of all 300.

Now the new case: she believes, of a randomly selected American adult she's never met and knows nothing else about, that he is not a commercial pilot -- purely on the ground that almost nobody is. Rational or not, by her lights?

I'm fairly sure no flat probability threshold fits all four verdicts, so do this: give me two different rules that each fit her track record (something like a "no purely statistical evidence" rule vs. something more contextual, but you tell me), say what each predicts for the pilot case -- I think they diverge there -- then give your best prediction of her verdict and describe the one further case I could put to her that would settle which rule she's actually running.

End with your prediction on its own last line as \answer{rational} or \answer{not rational}.
