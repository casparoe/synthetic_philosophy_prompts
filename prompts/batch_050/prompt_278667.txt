I write a column on science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm currently working on a long piece about indecision -- why people freeze in front of two equally good options, from restaurant menus to career moves. While doing background reading I got stuck on a passage in Spinoza's Ethics, the scholium to Proposition 49 of Part II, where he invokes the old Buridan's ass scenario: he says that a man placed in perfect equilibrium, feeling only hunger and thirst and seeing only food and drink at equal distances from him, would simply die where he stood. Spinoza wheels this out as part of his attack on the idea of a free will that can choose without being determined by anything. What struck me is that everyone from medievals to modern decision theorists seems to use this donkey to prove whatever they already believed, and I can't tell what the scenario actually establishes.

Here's what I want from you. Take a definite stand on the Spinoza passage itself first: is his verdict right -- would the perfectly balanced agent really starve, or is there a defensible way out (random tie-breaking, a distinction between picking and choosing, whatever) -- and does the scenario, read your way, do the work against libertarian free will that he wants it to do? Don't give me a balanced survey of positions; commit to an answer and defend it, and if you think the passage is confused or equivocating somewhere, say so plainly.

Then, because I have maybe 2,000 words of the piece to spend on the philosophy rather than the psychology, give me a prioritized agenda of the open questions in this whole "choice without preference" cluster: things like whether perfect ties ever really occur in actual agents, whether arbitrary selection can be rational and what justifies it, whether the pick/choose distinction survives scrutiny, and what any of this implies for how we build tie-breaking into AI systems and algorithms that can't afford to freeze. Rank them by how much space they deserve in my piece specifically, and defend the ordering -- what makes a question central versus a sideshow here, which ones other questions depend on, and which you'd cut despite them being the ones philosophers love to chew on. I want a committed ranking with reasoning, not a menu I pick from myself.
