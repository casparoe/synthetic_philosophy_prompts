I'm preparing a position statement for a graduate reading group on formal epistemology, and before I circulate it I'd like an outside check on whether my commitments actually cohere. We've spent the semester on the principle of indifference, starting from Bertrand's paradox (the random-chord construction, where equally plausible ways of counting "random" chords yield different probabilities that a chord exceeds the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle), and I found that the discussion pushed me into a set of views I each find attractive but which I've never stress-tested as a package. My statement will be discussed by people who know this material well, so I'd rather find the weak joints now than in the seminar room.

Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) The principle of indifference is a genuine rule of rationality, not just a heuristic: when a finite set of outcomes is related by a real symmetry, as with a fair coin or a well-shuffled deck, rationality requires a uniform distribution, and anyone who assigns 0.9 to heads on a symmetric coin without evidence is making an epistemic mistake, full stop. (2) Bertrand's paradox shows that the principle simply cannot be extended to continuous magnitudes: because there is no privileged parametrization of, say, the unknown length of a cord or the unknown probability of an event, any uniform assignment over a continuous quantity is an arbitrary artifact of how the problem was described, and so I reject all continuous applications of indifference-style reasoning as ill-posed. (3) I am a subjective Bayesian in the strict sense: credences are constrained only by coherence with the probability axioms, no prior is rationally mandatory over any other, and asking which prior nature "wants" is a category mistake. (4) When an agent has testable constraints (e.g., a known mean), the maximum-entropy method is the objectively correct way to convert those constraints into a probability distribution, over continuous variables included, which is why I take it to underwrite the legitimate use of priors in statistical mechanics.

My request: audit this set for joint tenability. For each pair of commitments that genuinely pulls apart, explain the conflict precisely, rank the conflicts by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each one say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me elsewhere in the set. Equally, if some pair that looks tense actually isn't — if two of these can be held together cleanly once the notions are made precise — say so and show why, because I don't want to abandon a commitment over a merely verbal conflict. Finally, if there is a single revision that resolves the most tension at once, identify it, but don't assume in advance that I know where the problem lies or that I've written the set with a preferred escape route in mind.
