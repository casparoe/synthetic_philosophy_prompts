I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working up a piece on the everyday morality of promises — not the contract-law stuff, just the ordinary social practice. I've been interviewing behavioral economists and moral philosophers, and the transcripts keep circling the same puzzle. So I want to road-test an argument on you before I put anything in print. Here are the bits of the practice as I've been describing them to sources, and I think they're all pretty uncontroversial as sociology: people treat a promise as binding even when breaking it would produce slightly more good all around (I promised my sister I'd help her move, but I could do more good donating that Saturday to a charity drive); a bare statement of intention — "yeah, I'll probably be at your party" — binds much less than the words "I promise I'll be there," even in cases where the listener's expectations end up identical; promises extracted under duress, or made while drunk, are widely treated as not counting at all; and yet the promisee can release the promisor with a wave of the hand ("forget it, you're off the hook"), and the obligation just evaporates. Oh, and most people I've talked to feel that promises to the dead still bind — executors, last wishes, that sort of thing — even though nobody's expectations could possibly be disappointed anymore.

Here's my question, and I want the full philosophical workup: what theory of why promises bind does this bundle of practices actually presuppose? Like, does the practice only make sense on the view that the binding force comes from the expectations a promise deliberately invites (the Scanlon-ish line, if I'm using the name right), or from a social convention we all benefit from, or from some act of the promisor's will — handing over authority over your future self, or something? I'm especially interested in the tension I think I've spotted: the expectation-based story seems to explain the duress and drunk cases nicely (no genuine voluntary undertaking, no binding), but it seems to get the intention-vs-promise case wrong, since expectations there are equal, and it really struggles with promises to the dead. Meanwhile a pure will-based or convention-based story might handle those but has to say something awkward about why duress voids things. So tell me: does the actual practice presuppose one theory and merely sit comfortably with the others, or is it underdetermined — compatible with several rival accounts? If it secretly mixes commitments from incompatible theories, say which parts conflict. And then, as a sort of stress test: if we rebuilt the practice wholesale on the expectation view — so that anything that reliably creates an expectation binds like a promise, and bindingness scales with the strength of the reliance — which of the everyday features above would have to go, and would what remained still deserve the name promising?
