Last weekend, over a long dinner that grew progressively less friendly, a friend and I fell into an argument that began when I described an instrumental adagio as genuinely heartbreaking. She flatly denied that any sequence of sounds, lacking a mind, could be heartbreaking in anything but a loose metaphorical sense; I insisted that the sadness is in the music itself, not merely projected onto it. Three hours later we had circled through whether hearing sad music requires feeling sad oneself, why anyone would choose to listen to it if it does, and what a composer contributes beyond arranging notes, and we parted without either of us giving an inch. I host a small monthly listening-and-philosophy salon, and for our next meeting I want to turn this dispute into something more productive than another shouting match.

Write me a sorting quiz that will tell each guest which theory of musical emotion they actually hold. Eight questions, each built around a concrete case or scenario -- a wordless instrumental piece, a performance of sad music at a celebration, a listener who recognizes a piece as joyful while remaining unmoved, and so on -- with two to four answer options each. The options must be phrased in plain language describing what the person would say about the case, never using the names of theories, so that guests are sorted by their intuitions rather than by labels they already favor. At least one question must confront the paradox of enjoying sad music, and at least one must separate merely recognizing an emotion in music from feeling it.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto these positions: resemblance or contour theories (music is sad because its movement and sound resemble sad expression), arousal theories (music is sad in virtue of reliably making listeners sad), persona theories (we hear the music as an imagined someone expressing emotion), expression theories (the music expresses the composer's or performer's emotional life), and eliminativist or associationist views (the emotion talk is metaphor, convention, or learned association and nothing more). Allow for a hybrid outcome when a pattern fits no single position. Where two positions give the same answers to every question, say so explicitly and note which additional case would be needed to split them, rather than pretending the quiz can distinguish them.
