You're a philosophy professor who specializes in aesthetics and has spent way too many hours thinking about video games. Think carefully before you start writing, and maybe do a quick mental brainstorm of the strongest arguments on each side first.

Here's the setup. Two philosophers of art, Maya and Tomas, both agree that games can be art, but they disagree about where the artistry lives:

- Maya defends what she calls the "possibility space" view: the designer is the artist, and the artwork is the structured space of choices the designer built. Playing a game is like walking through a sculpture garden — the player *experiences* the art, and a great player appreciates it more deeply, but the player doesn't create it. On her view, the player's agency matters aesthetically only insofar as it lets you *notice* what the designer put there.

- Tomas defends what he calls the "co-performance" view: a game is more like a musical instrument than a composition, and the player is a genuine co-creator of each playthrough, which is itself the aesthetic event. On his view, a playthrough can be graceful, cowardly, witty, or tragic in ways no designer scripted, and those properties belong (partly) to the player.

Now write a dialogue between them (roughly 500-800 words) in which they hash out their disagreement using these two test cases, which they keep circling back to:

1. Save-scumming: a player of a narrative game makes a choice that gets a beloved character killed, feels awful about it, and reloads an earlier save to undo it. Tomas thinks this reveals something about what players actually value in their agency; Maya thinks it shows the player is treating the game as a product to be consumed rather than a work to be received. Let them actually argue it out.

2. Speedrunning: a speedrunner beats a solemn, carefully paced story game in 11 minutes using glitches the designers never intended, skipping essentially everything the designers wrote. Who, if anyone, has engaged with the artwork here? Has the speedrunner created a *new* work on top of it, appreciated the old one, or ignored it entirely?

Requirements: both of them should be sharp and charitable — no strawmen, nobody wins cheaply. They should respond to each other's actual points, concede things when they're forced to, and ideally each should have to revise or qualify their view at least once by the end. It doesn't have to end in agreement, but it should end somewhere more precise than where it started. Casual tone is fine, they're colleagues chatting over coffee.
