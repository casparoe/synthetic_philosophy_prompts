I’m helping set the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on blame and moral standing. This has become annoyingly personal lately: I criticised a colleague for missing a project deadline, then remembered I’d done basically the same thing to another team last year and never properly apologised. They said I had no business blaming them, which felt both a bit convenient and uncomfortably plausible.

Write one essay question, answerable in 75 minutes, built around that sort of case. The question should make students distinguish three claims that are often collapsed together: that my criticism may still be true, that I may be a hypocrite, and that my hypocrisy may remove or weaken my standing to blame. Include a short scenario with three characters rather than my exact situation: the blamer has committed a comparable wrong, one person affected by the earlier wrong is present, and the current wrongdoer tries to use the blamer’s history to avoid answering the criticism.

To stop students just reciting a standard hypocrisy lecture, put these two invented notions into the scenario and require them to be analysed rather than treated as established theory:

- proportional self-indictment: a blamer keeps full standing only if they explicitly acknowledge that the principle they are applying also condemns their own relevantly similar conduct
- blame escrow: a blamer whose own past wrong remains unacknowledged can issue only a provisional criticism, held “in escrow” until they acknowledge it and take some proportionate step towards repair

The question should push students on whether standing is binary or comes in degrees, whether only victims of the earlier wrong can object to the blamer’s standing, and whether dismissing blame because of hypocrisy risks giving wrongdoers an incentive to hunt for dirt on critics rather than address what they did.

After the question, provide a model answer of roughly 900–1,200 words and a markscheme out of 30. The markscheme should identify six must-hit points, allocate marks to each, and add one sentence on the characteristic mistake weaker candidates make at that point. At least two of those mistakes should be things like treating hypocrisy as evidence that the criticism itself is false, or assuming that any unrepented past wrong automatically disqualifies every future criticism. Don’t require citations or a bibliography; students should be able to do well through careful argument alone.
