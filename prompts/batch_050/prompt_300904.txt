So at work we have this internal thing where teams write little bidding agents for a repeated auction platform, and last week it spilled into an actual argument between two coworkers, Maya and Devon, that nobody could settle. Background: in the final round of the last tournament, two agents faced off in what was basically a one-shot prisoner's dilemma (bid cooperatively and split the contract value, or undercut and take it all, with the usual payoff structure where mutual undercutting leaves both worse off than mutual cooperation). Maya's agent and Devon's agent happened to be near-identical code, since they'd both built off the same internal library. Maya cooperated. Devon defected, and his agent happened to face a *different* cooperative bot, so he cleaned up, and Maya thinks his reasoning was wrong even though it worked out.

Maya's argument: if you know the agent across from you runs the same decision procedure as you, then whatever you conclude, it concludes. So the live options aren't four, they're two -- both cooperate or both defect -- and between those, cooperate wins. She said defecting against a copy of yourself is like trying to outsmart your own mirror. She name-dropped Hofstadter's old superrationality idea and said this is the one case where it's not even exotic, because the symmetry is literal, not metaphorical -- same weights, same code, same inputs.

Devon's response: the payoffs are what they are, and defection dominates. Whatever the other agent does, you do better defecting. He said Maya is confusing correlation with control -- yeah, your reasoning and its reasoning will match, but your output doesn't *cause* its output, any more than two thermometers showing the same temperature cause each other. And he pointed out that in the actual tournament the agents *weren't* perfectly identical anyway -- different config files, slightly different tie-breaking -- so even the correlation she wants isn't guaranteed, and a strategy that needs perfect symmetry to be rational is a strategy that loses money in practice.

Maya's counter: the dominance argument assumes your choice is independent of the other agent's choice, and that's exactly what's false when the other agent is running your procedure. She said if your reasoning is sound, it's sound for it too, so "I defect while it cooperates" was never actually on the table -- it's an outcome you can write down in a payoff matrix but can't actually reach by reasoning correctly. She conceded the config-file point but called it an engineering detail, not a philosophy point.

Devon's last word: even granting the symmetry, "the outcomes I can reach by reasoning correctly" is doing all the work -- he can reason correctly and defect; nothing about correct reasoning forces cooperation unless you build the symmetry-thing in as a premise, which he says is just assuming your conclusion. And he said the proof is empirical: he won, she lost.
