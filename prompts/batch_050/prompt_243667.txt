My friend who took one philosophy of emotions course explained the "judgment theory" of emotion to me like this: 'The idea, which goes back to the Stoics and got revived by people like Robert Solomon, is that an emotion just IS a judgment. Being afraid of the dog is nothing over and above judging that the dog is dangerous; being angry at your brother is judging that he wronged you. The view has a fatal flaw though: you can be terrified of a spider while judging perfectly well that it's harmless, so emotions can't be judgments, case closed. Plus judgments are things we arrive at by deliberating, so the theory also implies you could just talk yourself out of being angry on the spot, which nobody can do.'

He says this with total confidence, but I want to know what to actually believe here. Which parts of his summary are accurate, which are unfair to the judgment theorist, and does the spider case really refute the view the way he says? I'm inclined to think he's right that it's a knock-down, but maybe that's too quick. Also if the view survives his objections, what's the strongest version of it -- what does it do better than the idea that emotions are just bodily feelings?
