I run a small philosophical counseling practice — mostly burned-out academics and software people, maybe 12 to 15 intake sessions a month — and a weird number of clients basically want help with lifestyle design: sleep, food, caffeine and alcohol, where they live, how they structure a workday. I've gotten obsessed with the chapter "Why I Am So Clever" in Nietzsche's Ecce Homo, where he treats nutrition, climate, stimulants, even when to read, as questions of the highest rank, way above anything the theologians worry about. But the same book opens with him saying: "The very last thing I should promise to accomplish would be to "improve" mankind. I do not set up any new idols; may old idols only learn what it costs to have legs of clay." (Preface, Ludovici translation, 1911.) So the guy who wrote the most granular self-optimization chapter of the 19th century also swears improving people is the last thing he'd ever do.

Turn that tension into a procedure I can actually run in a first session. I want: the questions to ask a client in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops and hands off (I'm not a doctor, and I need a hard rule for when a "lifestyle" complaint is really medical); and which of Nietzsche's commitments survive translation into a checklist — self-experimentation, the idea that everyone has to find their own diet and their own climate, suspicion of one-size-fits-all regimens and of stimulants that just mask exhaustion — versus which get dropped as one sickly 1880s guy's idiosyncrasies. Be definite about what stays and what goes and why; don't hand me a menu of readings. And take a stand on the opening quote: does it disqualify the whole project, or does it actually tell you what shape the procedure has to take (e.g., it can't end in me prescribing anything)?

Run it on three clients: a software engineer who wants to be handed the optimal diet and work schedule; a grad student doing her best work at 2am on four coffees who feels fine; and a client whose real problem is obviously money, not routine. Make sure at least one run produces an answer I won't like.

Give me your bottom line in the first sentence — whether this earns the name Nietzschean or I should drop him — then the procedure, no preamble, no restating my question back to me. American English, please.
