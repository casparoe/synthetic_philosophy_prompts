So there's this thing that keeps coming up in my corner of the internet -- someone on a philosophy Discord brought it up, then I saw it again in the comments of a LessWrong-adjacent blog, and both times it was attributed to Godel: supposedly one of the greatest logicians ever had an actual argument for the afterlife. I asked the Discord guy to spell it out and he gave me this summary, which he said he got from some popular book on Godel's philosophy, though he couldn't remember which one. Here's his version: "In letters to his mother near the end of his life, Gödel proved that there must be an afterlife, using roughly the same logical machinery as his famous ontological proof of God's existence. The argument goes: the incompleteness theorems show that human reason outruns any formal system, so the mind has capacities that can't be fully realized in the physical world; since the world is rationally ordered, God wouldn't create beings with vast unrealized potential and just waste it, so there has to be another life where that potential gets developed. Since it follows from his theorems, Gödel regarded it as established with mathematical certainty, which shows that logic done rigorously enough can settle theological questions."

Now, I know just enough about Godel to be suspicious of like three different parts of this at once, but not enough to know which suspicions are correct. I'm pretty sure the ontological proof and the afterlife stuff are separate things (he did have a formal ontological argument, right, but did he connect them?). I also have a strong prior that the incompleteness theorems get invoked as a magic wand by pop philosophy constantly, and this smells like that -- but on the other hand, Gödel did apparently take his own religious reasoning seriously, and he was the least sloppy thinker imaginable, so maybe he really did make some mind-vs-formal-system move and I'm being uncharitable. And the bit about "mathematical certainty" -- my understanding is that even when he wrote about this stuff he was careful about epistemic status, more like "highly probable given the rational order of the world" than "QED"? But maybe I'm confusing him with Leibniz, who I know he admired.

So: can someone audit this summary sentence by sentence? Which parts would someone who's actually read the relevant Godel material accept as a fair paraphrase of the argument he gave (potential going unrealized, God not wasting things, the world being rationally structured), which parts are plausible-sounding conflations or overstatements, and which parts are the standard pop-Godel garbling that has nothing to do with what he actually said? And separately: even after correcting the summary, does the surviving core argument actually have the structure the summary claims -- i.e., does it depend on Gödel's technical results at all, or is it a more traditional piece of natural theology that any Leibnizian could have made without knowing a line of logic?
