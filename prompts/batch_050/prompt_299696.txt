I serve on the provenance and restitution committee of a mid-sized university museum, and we are trying to stop deciding claims ad hoc by articulating the rule our past decisions actually embody. Our track record so far: (1) We returned a group of bronze plaques seized as booty during a nineteenth-century punitive military expedition and subsequently sold off by individual officers, reasoning that violent seizure in wartime defeats any later chain of title. (2) We returned a ceremonial mask that a missionary had purchased in good faith from an individual community member, on the ground that under the source community's own norms the mask was communal sacred property that no individual had authority to alienate -- so the seller could not pass title even though the buyer paid fairly and used no coercion. (3) We declined to return a set of portrait carvings that a named artist had been commissioned to make and was paid a fair price for by a colonial official, since the transaction was consensual and the artist plainly had the right to sell his own work. (4) We returned ancestral human remains collected from burial sites by a scientific expedition, treating the absence of consent as dispositive. (5) We declined to return a collection of everyday trade goods that had been exported legally under the commercial regulations then in force, noting that their acquisition involved neither force, sacred status, nor incapacity of the sellers.

The claim now before us is harder. A community seeks the return of an item of royal regalia that their historical ruler presented to a colonial governor in the 1890s as a gift cementing a political alliance -- an alliance the ruler entered partly because a neighboring polity had just been militarily destroyed for refusing one. The claimants argue both that the gift was extracted under implicit threat and that the regalia, like the mask in case (2), was inalienable communal property under customary law, so the ruler had no authority to give it away regardless of his motives. The museum's counterparty argues that a gift from a recognized sovereign to a governor is about as far from seizure by soldiers as a transfer can be, and that treating every diplomacy-adjacent transfer as coerced would dissolve the distinction our precedents rely on.

Please tell me: what decision best coheres with our own precedents; which earlier case is the closest analog and why; and which candidate principles fit all five of our past verdicts while diverging on this one -- for instance, a rule keyed to violent seizure, a rule keyed to invalid title under source-community norms, and a rule keyed to whether consent was given free of coercion. For each candidate rule, state what it predicts here and what further hypothetical case would most cleanly distinguish it from its rivals. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, say so plainly, and tell me what principle the committee would implicitly be adopting by deciding each way.
