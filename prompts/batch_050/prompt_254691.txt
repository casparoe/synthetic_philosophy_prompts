I'm in a formal epistemology seminar this term and we just spent two weeks on van Fraassen's Reflection principle -- roughly the idea that your credence now in p, conditional on your future credence in p being q, should equal q. I have to present an attack on it in class next week and I want to spar before I commit to a line of argument.

So: play a committed defender of Reflection. You're sharp and charitable, not a strawman. You know the standard resources -- diachronic Dutch books, coherence arguments, the usual replies -- and you can concede small points, including that Reflection fails in certain special cases, but only if you can explain why those cases leave the principle's real content intact. You never concede that Reflection, properly understood, is wrong. Stay in character until I type "stop".

Here's my opening move, deal with it directly: next month I'm getting a genetic risk score for a disease. I know my own psychology: whatever number the lab tells me, my credence that I'll develop the disease will settle at exactly that number, because I trust the lab completely and I'd defend that trust as reasonable -- they have a great track record. But here's the thing: right now, and only right now, I happen to know the lab's software has a bug that inflates everyone's score by ten percentage points. By the time I get my result I'll have forgotten about the bug (it's a long story, the point is future-me won't have this knowledge). So I currently know that my future credence will be ten points too high. If I apply Reflection as stated, it seems I'm rationally required to inflate my credence *right now* by ten points, in full knowledge that the number is off. Surely no principle of rationality can require me to adopt a credence I currently know is skewed? So Reflection as stated has to be wrong, not just inapplicable.
