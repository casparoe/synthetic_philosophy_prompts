I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I recently wrote a short explainer on the "unilateralist's curse" — the idea, developed by Nick Bostrom and colleagues, that when many independent agents can each single-handedly trigger an outcome affecting everyone, the outcome tends to occur even if most agents judge it unwise, because each agent estimates the situation with some error and the most optimistic estimate wins. Ever since filing that piece I keep seeing my own profession through its lens, and I'd like help thinking it through properly.

Please describe the most important implications of the unilateralist's curse for how science journalism actually works. Some of the situations I have in mind: any one outlet can break an embargo on a paper and everyone else then has to publish immediately or be scooped; any one reporter can write up a flashy preprint that careful peers think is junk, after which the claim is "out there" and other outlets feel forced to cover it; any one publication can name a researcher accused of misconduct before an investigation concludes; any one outlet can publish vivid operational details of a dual-use experiment (say, making a pathogen more transmissible, or a step-by-step account of a model's dangerous capability) on the grounds that the information is already public somewhere. In each case, my worry is that every individual editor can reason sincerely and in good faith — my coverage is responsible, my sourcing is sound, my framing is careful — while the collective outcome is determined by whoever has the lowest threshold for publishing.

In your answer, I'd like you to go beyond merely restating the abstract model. Which structural features of science journalism (competition for attention, the embargo system, the norm that "the public has a right to know," the fact that silence by one outlet buys nothing if a rival publishes) map onto which features of the curse? Where does the analogy break down — for instance, is a story being covered really a shared harm in the way the model assumes? And what mitigation mechanisms does the logic of the curse itself suggest (coordination norms like embargoes, professional codes, self-binding editorial policies), including their known failure modes? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single most consequential implication — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
