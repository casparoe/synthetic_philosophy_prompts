I'm in a political theory reading group (mostly grad students, one very opinionated local activist) and we've been circling the sortition debate for an entire semester without anything changing. One side keeps arguing that randomly selected citizen assemblies would fix what elections broke: no more career politicians optimizing for re-election, no donor capture, descriptively representative bodies that actually look like the public, and deliberation among people who have nothing to gain personally from the outcome. The other side concedes almost all of that and then says: exactly -- people with nothing to gain and nothing to lose electorally are people nobody can hold accountable, and accountability through the ballot is the whole point of democracy, plus voters at least get to *consent* to their rulers, which a lottery never gives you. What strikes me is that both sides seem to agree on basically every empirical claim (yes, professional politicians are a self-selecting class; yes, assemblies of ordinary people can handle complex policy given time and good information; yes, accountability mechanisms are weak either way) and yet nobody has moved an inch, and the arguments keep getting restated at higher volume rather than engaging.

My tentative diagnosis, which I want tested rather than confirmed, is that this isn't an empirical disagreement at all but a clash between two things called "democracy": one side treats it as a method for producing good, representative decisions, the other treats it as a relationship of authorization between rulers and ruled -- and since those are different questions wearing the same name, no amount of evidence about how real citizen assemblies behave (Ireland, British Columbia, the French climate convention, etc.) can settle it. But maybe I'm wrong and there IS a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work here, like a factual dispute about whether sortition bodies would be captured by staff and experts instead of donors, which would just replace one principal-agent problem with another.
