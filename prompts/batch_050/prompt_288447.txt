Exam question:

In the Preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy (1859), Marx writes:

"In studying such transformations it is always necessary to distinguish between the material transformation of the economic conditions of production, which can be determined with the precision of natural science, and the legal, political, religious, artistic or philosophic – in short, ideological forms in which men become conscious of this conflict and fight it out. Just as one does not judge an individual by what he thinks about himself, so one cannot judge such a period of transformation by its consciousness, but, on the contrary, this consciousness must be explained from the contradictions of material life, from the conflict existing between the social forces of production and the relations of production."

Does this passage commit Marx to the view that the economic base explains everything else in society? In your answer, distinguish what the passage actually says from the stronger thesis sometimes attributed to it, and consider whether the passage leaves any room for law, politics, or ideas to matter causally.

Student's answer:

This passage does not commit Marx to the crude view that the economic base explains everything else, though it comes close. His central distinction is between two kinds of transformation: the material transformation of the economic conditions of production, which he says can be studied with the precision of natural science, and the ideological forms — law, politics, religion, art, philosophy — in which people become conscious of this conflict and fight it out. The base, that is a society's technology and stock of productive resources, is where the real action happens; the ideological superstructure is just where it gets processed.

Two features of the passage soften the determinism. First, Marx says people fight the conflict out in ideological forms, which suggests politics, law, and philosophy are the actual arena of class struggle rather than a mere reflection of it, so they must have some causal role. Second, Marx himself elsewhere qualifies the doctrine, insisting that the economy is determinative only in the last instance, which implies ideological factors exercise genuine influence within limits the economy sets.

Still, the analogy with the individual is telling: just as we do not judge a person by what he thinks of himself, we cannot judge a revolutionary period by its own self-understanding. This shows Marx held that actors in such periods are systematically mistaken about their own motives — the doctrine later Marxists called false consciousness. Since people's ideas about why they act are unreliable, the content of law, religion, and philosophy in these periods cannot be taken at face value but must be explained from the contradictions of material life.

So the verdict is: not everything is explained by the base, but everything that matters is. Ideological forms are where conflicts are lived and fought, yet their content is fixed by material contradictions, and the participants' own understanding of what they are doing is no guide to the real explanation.
