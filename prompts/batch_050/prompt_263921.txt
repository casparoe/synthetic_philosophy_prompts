I'm preparing a seminar on the philosophy of translation, and I want to pair Borges's Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote with Hofstadter's worry about machine translation: whether a system that produces excellent translations by statistical means, without anything we would recognize as understanding, thereby understands. Menard laboriously reproduces passages of the Quixote word for word, yet the story's narrator insists his text, though identical to Cervantes's, is richer and almost infinitely more ambiguous because of who produced it, how, and in what century. Read the story as a thought experiment about output versus process: if two texts are textually identical, can one be an achievement of understanding and the other not, and what does that imply for the case where the two producers are a human translator and a statistical model? Take a definite position rather than surveying the options: argue either that the story vindicates a process view (understanding resides in how a text is produced, so mere output-matching can never amount to understanding, however good the translations) or that it actually parodies that view (the narrator's raptures over Menard's invisible differences are a send-up of intentionalism, and identical outputs are identical achievements). Where the story resists your reading, say so plainly. Close with what, on your reading, the fiction contributes to the machine-translation debate that the standard arguments do not.
