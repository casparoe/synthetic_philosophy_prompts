My father was recently diagnosed with early-stage Alzheimer's, which is how I ended up refereeing an argument between two friends, Mara and Jon, about Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity.

Mara opened: if the self just is the story a brain spins about itself, then once my father can no longer spin it, my siblings and I can keep it going for him -- telling his stories back to him, reminding him who he is, acting toward him as the person the story describes. On Dennett's view there is no deeper self underneath the narrative to lose, so a collectively maintained narrative keeps him present in the only sense anyone was ever present.

Jon replied: the gravity metaphor requires the story to be generated by, and to organize, the system itself. A narrative authored entirely by others is a biography, not a self -- it exerts no pull on what he does and predicts nothing. What persists is a beloved character in someone else's story, not a person.

Mara answered that all selves are largely co-authored and confabulated anyway: split-brain patients invent reasons for actions they didn't choose, healthy people misremember their own motives, and much of anyone's self-story is supplied by others. So the difference between my father and the rest of us is one of degree, and a difference of degree cannot mark the death of a self.

Jon's final move: degree is exactly what the metaphor is about. A story counts as a center of gravity only insofar as it pulls on the system's future trajectory; confabulation in a functioning brain still guides behavior, whereas the family's narration guides nothing of his. Once the pull drops to zero, the trajectory no longer belongs to that center.

Who is right about what? Please adjudicate move by move: say what each of the four moves gets right or wrong, whether Mara's degree-versus-kind point survives Jon's answer, and what, if anything, both are missing about the view itself. At most 600 words.
