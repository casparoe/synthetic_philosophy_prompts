I teach an upper-level undergraduate course on Marx and political economy, and a colleague has proposed the following summary for our course reader: “Marx’s theory of machinery rests on a simple asymmetry. Only living labor creates new value, whereas machines merely transfer to commodities the value already embodied in them. Thus, although each capitalist has an incentive to automate in order to reduce costs, an economy that replaces workers with machines progressively destroys the source of profit. Marx therefore concluded that full automation is unsustainable under capitalism: the drive to mechanize lowers the rate of profit, intensifies crises, and maintains a reserve army whose competition keeps wages down. Machinery would be beneficial only after capitalism, when it could shorten necessary labor and produce material abundance rather than private profit.”

Is this a fair presentation? Please separate the parts that are textually and conceptually well supported from those that are reasonable pedagogical simplifications, and from any that a careful defender of Marx would regard as conflations or distortions. In particular, distinguish claims about value from claims about use-values or material wealth; the incentive facing an individual capitalist from the dynamics Marx attributes to the system as a whole; and Marx’s account of machinery’s effects under historically existing capitalism from a claim that machinery must always worsen workers’ position. I am also unsure whether “only living labor creates value” entails that automation directly lowers aggregate profit, or whether additional premises about competition, realization, productivity, and the composition of capital are required.

Finally, assess the summary’s conclusion that capitalism cannot sustain full automation. Does that conclusion follow from Marx’s own distinctions, or is it one later reconstruction among several? Please be explicit about which interpretive points are comparatively settled among knowledgeable readers and which remain genuinely contested, rather than presenting a disputed reconstruction as the consensus. I want a conceptual audit, not a bibliography or a general overview of Marxism.
