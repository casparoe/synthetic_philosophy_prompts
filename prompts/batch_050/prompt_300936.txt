I am plotting a novel around a builder and a shoemaker who cannot agree on what a house is worth in shoes, and I keep returning to Aristotle’s claim that money makes just exchange between unlike goods possible. The following argument seems to turn that claim against itself:

1. Exchange is just only when it realizes proportional reciprocity: the parties receive values proportionate to what they give.
2. Proportion requires a common measure.
3. A house and shoes serve different needs; no natural magnitude intrinsic to them makes one house equal to some number of pairs of shoes.
4. Money’s measuring power rests on convention and public confidence, not on a natural magnitude shared by houses and shoes.
5. A changeable convention cannot transform naturally incommensurable things into genuinely commensurable ones.
6. Therefore, a monetary price can create the appearance of reciprocity but cannot make an exchange of unlike goods genuinely just.
7. If that is right, Aristotle’s account proves too much: it condemns virtually all trade rather than explaining how exchange binds a political community together.

Which step should be rejected, and why did it appear plausible? In answering, distinguish equality of usefulness, equality of need, equality of price, and practical sufficiency for cooperation; then explain what each possible rejection costs Aristotle’s picture of exchange.
