I chair the exhibits committee of the Port Anselm Maritime Museum, a small institution in a fishing town of about six thousand. Next spring marks the centenary of the great gale of 1924, and we are producing a permanent exhibit about it; the text must go to the fabricator in six weeks. The exhibit centers, as nearly everything in this town does, on Elias Marr, the lighthouse keeper who, in the story every local child learns, rowed out alone at the height of the storm and brought the seven-man crew of the fishing boat Alder safely to shore. The town celebrates Marr Day every October, the elementary school bears his name, and his great-granddaughter, a former mayor, is among our most generous donors, though she does not sit on the committee.

Last year a graduate assistant digitizing county holdings turned up three documents that complicate the story: the harbormaster's telegraph log, an insurance inquiry into the Alder's cargo, and the regional maritime board's annual return. All three indicate that the rescue itself was carried out by the lifeboat crew from Dunross, eleven miles up the coast, and that Marr's part, though genuine, was supporting: he sighted the Alder's distress flare, telegraphed the Dunross station, and kept his lamp burning past dawn to guide the lifeboat in. The Dunross historical society, which holds crew logs naming its men, has written a courteous letter asking that the new exhibit credit them. No one believes anyone lied; the Marr version first appears in print only in 1938, in a town-history pamphlet, and our consulting historian takes it for a textbook case of honest consolidation, a real supporting role magnified over decades of retelling. Several descendants of the rescued men live in Port Anselm and grew up with the Marr version themselves.

The committee is split three ways, and I must circulate a decision memo before our vote. One faction holds that the museum's first duty is to the record, and that a plaque we now know to be false is a small lie with our institutional name on it. A second holds that the story is itself part of what the museum exists to preserve, that no one is harmed by it, and that correcting it would gratuitously wound elderly residents and insult a family that has given the town much. A third, led by a retired philosophy teacher, rejects the framing altogether: collective memory and history, she argues, are different enterprises answering different questions — one tells a community who it is, the other establishes what happened — and a museum can display the legend as legend, with the record beside it, so that neither has to give way. Please draft the memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action for the exhibit text (revising it to match the record, retaining the legend, the dual presentation, or some further option you specify), give the reasoning, engage honestly the strongest consideration favoring each option you reject, and state whether your rationale extends to Marr Day and the school name, since the town council will ask. It would also help if the memo articulated a general principle the board could apply to other contested items in the collection, as this will not be the last.
