I’m a novelist working on a book about a fictional care-home company, Alder Gate, whose residents are harmed through a long chain of ordinary decisions rather than one villainous act. Head office introduces a staffing-efficiency programme, local managers learn to game the safety reports, the board notices worrying figures but treats them as accounting noise, and nobody quite decides to let the harm happen. Afterwards everyone says the company knew and the company failed, although several individuals can honestly say they never knew the whole picture. By the time of the public inquiry, half the staff have changed and Alder Gate has been sold to another group.

I want the moral architecture of the novel to be philosophically respectable, but I only have room for three or four pressure points. So: among the open questions about corporate moral agency, which should I prioritise sorting out, and why? I’m especially interested in whehter a company can literally believe or intend something beyond what its members believe and intend; how distributed information can amount to culpable ignorance; what kind of internal decision structure would make blame attributable to the organisation rather than merely to its employees; how responsibility should be divided between the corporation and particular people; and whether corporate blame can survive staff turnover, restructuring, or a change of ownership.

Please give me a defended ranking, not a general survey. Fix the criteria explicitly: how many of the other issues depend on the question, how much difference it would make to real practices such as regulation or punishment, how tractable it is, and how much dramatic and moral clarity it would give the novel. Also flag any commonly treated as central that I could safely sideline, and explain what would change your ranking. No bibliography or citations needed.
