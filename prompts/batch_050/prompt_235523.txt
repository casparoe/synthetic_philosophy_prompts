I share a studio with two type designers, Mara and Deniz, and last week they had a forty-minute argument I keep turning over. Mara has been drawing an experimental typeface for a literary journal's masthead, pushing each glyph toward the edge of legibility, and the flashpoint was her letter 'a': a single open spiral with no enclosed counter and no stem. In the word "masthead" everyone in the studio read it as 'a' immediately, and Mara took that as vindication. Deniz said that proved nothing except that readers are good at repairing damaged words. His position: a letter is a shape that can be recognized as that letter in isolation, and if you showed her spiral to someone alone, nobody would say 'a' -- a coffee stain filling the gap in "c_t" isn't an 'a' either, it's just an accident the sentence rescues. Mara replied that nothing is ever read in isolation; fluent reading is holistic, which is why we can parse mutilated text, children's handwriting, and deliberately distorted CAPTCHAs. Worse for him, she said, geometry can't be the essence of a letter anyway: a Fraktur 'a' and a Helvetica 'a' share almost no shape, and what makes both of them 'a's is the role they play -- the slot they occupy in the alphabet, the spirit of the letter, not its body. Her spiral plays that role inside the word, so it's an 'a'.

Deniz answered that the role criterion proves too much: in a substitution cipher where a smiley face encodes 'a', the smiley is a symbol for an 'a', not an 'a', and Mara's view can't say why. Typefaces differ, he granted, but their 'a's preserve an abstract skeleton -- an enclosed counter, an aperture -- and her spiral has neither; variation has a floor, and below the floor you're designing codes, not letters. Mara's counter was the one that stuck with me. She said the "skeleton" is just whatever a historical community of readers happens to be used to: scripts that look like pure noise to us were transparent to their contemporaries, so Deniz's isolation test really means "unrecognizable to people like us, right now," which makes his essentialism a disguised conservatism. And she pointed out that the Latin alphabet itself tolerates two quite different skeletons for the same letter -- the single-storey and double-storey lowercase 'a', not to mention 'g' -- so he can't even state the allegedly essential features without the alphabet refuting him. Deniz's last move was that this shows letterhood is disjunctive rather than essential: a short list of permissible skeletons, still shape-based and not role-based, and the smiley fails every disjunct. Mara said a disjunction with no principled membership rule is just a museum of habits, and round it went.

I genuinely can't tell who got the better of this, and I stayed out of it on the day, so I'm asking you to adjudicate. Go through the exchange move by move rather than declaring a winner overall: Is Deniz's isolation test doing real work, or does Mara's holism point defeat it? Does the cipher case actually refute the role criterion, or only embarrass it? Does the two-storey 'a' objection damage Deniz's essentialism as much as Mara thinks, and is his retreat to a disjunction of skeletons a stable position or a concession dressed up as one? Finally, is there something both of them are missing -- a distinction or a third option that would dissolve at least part of the dispute? I'd rather have a careful verdict on each skirmish than a summary judgment.
