Next term I'm teaching the philosophy of biology week on a mixed third-year module -- about half philosophy students, half biology students who got drafted in via a science-and-values pathway -- and I have one ninety-minute lecture to get across the difference between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function, with a seminar after. I don't want you to explain the two accounts to me; I know them. I want the plan for how to teach them, in order, to this particular room.

Specifically: what has to come first and why. My instinct is to open with the contrast between what a part does and what its function is before saying anything about either theory, but maybe the normativity point (that things can malfunction, that a diseased kidney still has the function it isn't performing) has to land before the contrast even makes sense -- tell me the actual dependency structure, not just an ordering. Then the carrying example: I refuse to use the heart, both cohorts have had it since GCSE and they'll pattern-match instead of thinking. Give me one example that can carry the whole lecture, and tell me what it has to have and what it must not have -- I'm thinking it needs an obvious effect-that-isn't-a-function, a live malfunction case, and crucially a variant where the two theories deliver different verdicts, since that variant is what I'll build the seminar around. If the example you're drawn to is the first-ever instance of a novel trait, or some swampman-style duplicate, say why that does more work than a vestigial organ would.

Then the misconception map, which is the bit I most care about because this room will generate them in stereo. The biologists will arrive thinking the function talk in their textbooks is already the etiological view and that the causal-role account is just philosophers refusing to do science, and later they'll swing to thinking etiological means adaptationist Panglossian just-so stories. The philosophers will assume the whole dispute is about what the word "function" means and so is merely verbal, and at least one of them will definately claim causal-role accounts can't even state that a malfunctioning organ is malfunctioning. For each misconception (plus whichever ones I've failed to predict), tell me where in the sequence it will form and exactly where to head it off -- before the theory it latches onto is even named, or after it's on the board, and why.

Also: one check question for the last ten minutes that would show the distinction has actually landed and isn't just vocabulary they've memorised (a novel case they haven't seen, presumably, where getting the right answer requires running both analyses), and what to leave out entirely at this level -- I'm inclined to skip propensity and modal accounts, organisational/self-maintenance views, and the Millikan-Neander internal disagreements, but tell me if cutting any of those leaves a hole the students will fall into in the seminar. British English in the answer please.
