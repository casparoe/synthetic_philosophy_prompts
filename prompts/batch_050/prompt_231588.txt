I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm preparing a long feature on what explains the behavior of ordinary perpetrators in atrocities. My editors keep framing the piece as a settled question -- people do terrible things because of the situations they're put in -- but the historians and social psychologists I've interviewed keep circling back to an old, unresolved fight: the dispute between Christopher Browning's Ordinary Men (1992) and Daniel Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners (1996). Both books lean heavily on the same evidence base, the interrogation records of Reserve Police Battalion 101, a unit of middle-aged, working-class Hamburg reservists who shot or deported tens of thousands of Polish Jews in 1942-43, and whose commander famously offered the men the chance to step out of the killing. Browning's reading is broadly situational: conformity, deference to authority, careerism, and group cohesion did the work, and most of the men were no more antisemitic than their peers elsewhere. Goldhagen's reading is broadly cultural-dispositional: a specifically German eliminationist antisemitism made the killing intelligible and even welcome to the perpetrators, so the battalion's behavior reveals something about German political culture rather than about human nature in general. Rather than write another piece declaring one side the winner, I want to include, as a sidebar, the kind of document the two camps might have produced if they had ever run a proper adversarial collaboration.

Draft that document for me: the joint statement of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between a Browning-style situationalist and a Goldhagen-style culturalist, both of whom accept the same archival record. Structure it as (1) a numbered list of agreed claims -- statements about the battalion and about perpetrator behavior generally that both camps can sign without reservation, which I suspect will include things like the fact that opting out was permitted and rarely taken, that no one was severely punished for refusing, and that both situational pressure and antisemitism were present in some degree; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual dispute is stated in wording each side would accept as a fair description of its own position, with explicit flags where a disagreement turns out to be partly verbal (for instance, over what it means to call the perpetrators ordinary, or what would count as a cause being sufficient); and (3) a cruxes section identifying the two or three sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one camp to substantially revise its view.

For each crux, I want a concrete test or body of evidence that both sides agree in advance would bear on it, with each side's prediction registered beforehand. Here is the one genuinely new element I'd like built in, since it lets both camps commit to predictions rather than relitigate old footnotes: suppose a research consortium has just digitized and cross-linked the complete personnel files, judicial interrogation transcripts, and private correspondence for all German Order Police battalions deployed in the East, plus comparable records for the non-German auxiliaries who worked alongside them, so that for the first time we can compare, at the level of individual men, pre-service backgrounds, expressed attitudes, volunteered versus assigned killing, refusal rates, and behavior under identical orders across units of different composition. For each crux, state what pattern in this dataset the situationalist predicts and what pattern the culturalist predicts -- for example, whether men with measured low pre-war antisemitism killed at the same rates as measured high ones, whether refusal clustered by friendship circles or by ideological profile, and whether the non-German auxiliaries behaved as the Germans did under similar orders. Close the statement with a short section, signed by both parties, on what this dispute implies for how journalists like me should write about perpetrators in contemporary atrocities, and note explicitly any point on which the two sides agree that popular accounts -- including, possibly, my magazine's editorial framing -- get the lessons wrong.
