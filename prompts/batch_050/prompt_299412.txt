At my school's debate club last week, two members, Ana and Ben, had an argument that went well beyond the actual topic they were supposed to be preparing for, and it ended unresolved. I'd like a ruling on it.

The background: the two of them had researched a motion and reached opposite conclusions. Ana was confident the motion was true, Ben that it was false. Then this exchange happened.

(1) Ana said that, since Ben is roughly as intelligent and informed as she is, and since they had read the same sources, learning that he disagreed with her should make her substantially less confident -- ideally she should move toward something like a fifty-fifty stance. Treating a peer's judgment as evidence, she said, is just what intellectual honesty requires.

(2) Ben replied that this position defeats itself. The claim that you should split the difference with disagreeing peers is itself controversial among philosophers and smart people generally, so by its own standard Ana should only half-believe it -- and someone who only half-believes a rule has no business demanding that others follow it fully.

(3) Ana answered that the objection proves too much, because on Ben's logic nobody could ever rationally hold any contested philosophical view at all, including Ben's own view that you should stick to your guns. Every philosophical position would cancel itself out, which can't be right, so disagreement can't be a reason to abandon a position wholesale -- at most it's a reason to hold it less confidently, and her original point was only about confidence.

(4) Ben then changed tack. He said there's a crucial difference between checking a long arithmetic problem together and arguing about politics or ethics. If Ben adds up a restaurant bill and Ana gets a different total, he said, he'd recheck his work, because arithmetic errors are common and independent of the person. But in the debate case, he knows the particular reasons that moved him -- a specific study he found convincing, a distinction he noticed -- and Ana can't see inside his reasoning, so her disagreement tells him almost nothing about whether those reasons are good. Steady confidence is therefore rational whenever you've actually done the thinking yourself.

(5) Ana's final move was to accuse him of a double standard: he treats his own reasoning as transparent and trustworthy while treating hers as a black box, but from his own perspective, her reasoning is exactly what his is from hers. If he wouldn't accept her saying "trust me, I did the thinking," he can't rest his own confidence on having done the thinking either.

Could you go through the five moves in order and say who is right at each step, and why? In particular, I'd like to know: whether Ben's self-defeat objection in (2) actually works or whether Ana's reply in (3) defuses it; whether the arithmetic-versus-politics distinction in (4) does the work Ben needs it to do; and whether Ana's symmetry argument in (5) is as decisive as she seemed to think. Finally, tell me who is more right overall, and whether both of them are missing some consideration that would have moved the discussion forward.
