The rationality of voting has been argued about for over sixty years, and as far as I can tell the positions are exactly where they started. The setup is familiar: a single vote is almost never pivotal, so the instrumental payoff of voting, computed as the probability of decisiveness times the stakes, comes out below the trivial costs of showing up; hence the claim that widespread turnout is a standing anomaly for instrumental rationality. The standard responses are well rehearsed. One camp adds a non-instrumental term to the calculus — civic duty, expressive satisfaction, the act's symbolic value — and declares the anomaly dissolved. A second camp tries to rehabilitate the instrumental term itself, arguing that pivot probabilities in close elections are not as negligible as the naive calculation suggests, especially under winner-take-all rules where the effective stakes are the value of the whole policy bundle. A third camp reaches for universalization: the relevant question is not what my one vote does but what happens if everyone in my position abstains. The skeptics reply, in turn, that the expressive term is unfalsifiable and can equally 'explain' staying home, that even generous pivot estimates leave the expected payoff under the cost of the trip in the overwhelming majority of elections, and that universalization arguments smuggle in a different theory of rational action rather than repairing the old one. Meanwhile, the empirical record sits awkwardly with everyone: turnout stays high, yet it moves with closeness, registration costs, and weather in ways that look very much like the calculus working at the aggregate level even while it supposedly fails at the individual one.

My working hypothesis is that the debate never resolves because it is two debates wearing one label: an explanatory question about what drives turnout levels and their fluctuations, where cost-benefit reasoning arguably performs well, and a justificatory question about whether the individual act of voting makes sense, where it arguably does not — with the residual heat coming from a further, rarely stated disagreement about whether rational choice theory is in the business of prediction or prescription, plus a purely verbal dispute about what 'rational' commits us to. But I hold this loosely. What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict: engage with what each side actually says, and tell me which parts of this disagreement are substantive, which are verbal, and which are cases of two questions being answered under a single heading. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress on each part — a formal result, a distinction that both sides would have to accept, an empirical finding, an agreed scope condition — or, if you think no progress should be expected on some part, argue for that instead.
