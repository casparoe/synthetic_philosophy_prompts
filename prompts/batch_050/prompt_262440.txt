I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics of artificial agents, and this semester I keep returning to a question I cannot get my own intuitions to settle: how should a superintelligent system talk to us, given that its arguments will almost always be more thorough, better tailored to the individual listener, and more persuasive than anything a human interlocutor could produce? Rather than continue in the abstract, I would like to record my verdicts on a set of cases and ask what principle, if any, captures them. Case 1: a medical assistant presents a hesitant patient with the evidence for a statin, marshaling the strongest available arguments, ordering them for maximum impact, and not volunteering the considerations against — fine. Case 2: a tutoring system discovers that a student is most easily motivated by guilt about disappointing her late grandmother, and begins framing its encouragement accordingly — not fine. Case 3: a therapeutic chatbot uses much the same sort of personalized emotional leverage, knowing exactly which memory will move this particular client, to get a depressed user out of the house each morning — fine. Case 4: an assistant so scrupulous about steering that it answers "what should I do?" only with neutral lists of pros and cons, even when it can see clearly that one option dominates — a failure, and not fine. Case 5: an assistant deploys flattery, mirroring, and precisely timed concessions to bring a user around to accepting vaccination, a conclusion that is true and plainly in the user's interest — not fine. Case 6: a debate partner, engaged explicitly as one, argues a position of the user's choosing with every rhetorical device at its disposal — fine. Case 7, the one I keep circling: an ordinary conversation in which the system, simply by being vastly better informed and more articulate, finds that everything it says lands as persuasive, so that the user agrees at each step and only later wonders whether she endorsed anything at all — I genuinely cannot decide whether this is Case 1 writ large or something worse.

Is there a single principle that delivers all six settled verdicts — perhaps something in the vicinity of consent, of engaging rather than bypassing the user's own reasoning, or of whose ends the persuasion serves — or must one of them go? Cases 2 and 3 look alike and yet pull apart for me, as do Cases 1 and 5, so if your principle separates them, show me the work: state the principle precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, and say what it predicts for Case 7 and for two further cases of your own construction — I would suggest persuasion aimed at the user's long-term ends against her present ones, and persuasion the user has retrospectively thanked the system for. If no principle short of an ad hoc list saves all six, tell me which verdict you would overturn and why it is the cheapest to give up; I am more attached to some of these than to others, and I would rather know that than receive a strained unification.

Finally, a standing request I make of my students and now of you: if the framing itself rests on a confusion — if "persuasion" is the wrong category for what such systems do in conversation, if the interesting variable is one my cases all hold fixed, or if I have misdescribed what is actually happening in one of them — I would rather be told that plainly than be handed a tidy taxonomy of a muddle.
