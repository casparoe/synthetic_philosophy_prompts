I serve as the chair of the independent ethics advisory board for a defense research consortium. The consortium has asked us to rule on whether a prototype loitering munition may proceed from simulation to live field trials, and I need a decision memo I can bring to a vote.

The system in question operates as follows. Once launched into a pre-authorized geographic area with a pre-authorized mission profile (e.g., "disable radar installations of type X"), it searches for, identifies, and selects targets on its own, using an onboard classifier. A human operator monitors the feed and holds a veto: any engagement the system proposes can be aborted, but the window between the system's firing decision and weapons release is roughly ten seconds, and the operator is simultaneously supervising up to four munitions. The manufacturer describes this as "human-on-the-loop control" and notes that no engagement occurs without the theoretical possibility of human intervention. They also point out, correctly, that the alternative defensive systems we currently field — automated close-in weapon systems on ships, for instance — select and engage targets with even less human involvement, and nobody proposes banning those.

Three of my board members find the system acceptable on roughly the grounds the manufacturer gives: a veto is a veto, the target class is unambiguously military hardware, and consistency with existing practice cuts in its favor. Two members object that a ten-second window shared across four aircraft is not control but rubber-stamping: the human's role is structurally guaranteed to be confirmatory, since there is no time to independently verify the classifier's output, and if a strike goes wrong, responsibility will be unplaceable — the operator merely failed to override, the engineers only built the classifier, and no one actually chose this target.

Draft the board's decision memo. It should recommend one of three outcomes — approve the trials, reject them, or approve subject to specified design conditions — and give reasons the consortium's engineers could concretely respond to. In particular, the memo should articulate what would have to be true of the human's role for it to count as genuine control rather than nominal oversight, address the argument from consistency with existing automated defensive systems rather than ignoring it, and state the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly enough that its holders would recognize their own position in it.
