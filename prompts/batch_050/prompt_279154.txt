At a family gathering last month, my brother-in-law finally explained how he lost most of his savings over two years of options trading, and his defense has been sitting with me ever since: he insisted that every trade he placed had a positive expected return, and having checked a few of his examples, I believe he was right about that much. I could not articulate, on the spot, why being right about that was compatible with ending up nearly broke. I run a monthly personal-finance workshop at our public library — about twenty adults, ninety minutes, very mixed comfort with arithmetic, at least a third of the regulars actively trading on their phones — and I want to build my next session around the answer I failed to give him.

Design the session as a game, not a lecture. The core mechanic I have in mind: each participant starts with an identical pile of tokens; every round, each person stakes their entire pile on a coin flip, where heads increases the stake by half and tails costs two-fifths of it. The arithmetic I want participants to trip over is that a single round of this game has positive expected value (the stake grows by five percent on average) while anyone who plays repeatedly watches their pile shrink by about five percent per round along a typical trajectory — so that over a couple of dozen rounds the large majority of the room ends up worse off than when they started, even as the average pile in the room keeps climbing. If you adjust these parameters, keep both halves of that sentence true and show the arithmetic.

The session needs: complete rules and a materials list; a payoff scheme using tokens rather than money; a mechanism for displaying the room's average pile round by round, so that each participant can watch it rise while their own pile falls; facilitator roles if the logistics require them; and a timeline for the ninety minutes. The discovery has to belong to the participants — no lecture up front, and none of the vocabulary (expected value, ensemble average, time average, ergodicity) until the debrief. I would also like a second phase in which players may voluntarily band together and split each round's winnings and losses, staged so the room can see what pooling does to individual trajectories; specify how to handle holdouts and unequal piles.

Then give me the debrief: which moments in the session map onto the distinction between an average across many parallel players and an average across one player's successive rounds; what the second phase suggests about why insurance and diversification exist; and the two or three sentences I can actually say to my brother-in-law at the next family gathering. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I am already worried about the one lucky participant who ends the night rich and concludes the game is fine, about players who want to stake only part of their pile, and about arithmetic friction in a numeracy-mixed room — and how to handle each. Finally, be candid about which features of real trading losses the game cannot reproduce, and what I will have to supply through testimony rather than mechanics.
