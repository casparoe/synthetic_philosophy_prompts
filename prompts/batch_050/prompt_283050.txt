I am a university student currently enrolled in an upper-division course on the metaphysics of personal and object identity, and I have been drafting a short essay on the Ship of Theseus. Before I submit it, I would like substantive critical comments. My instructor values originality, so I have tried to develop an argument I have not encountered in our readings, and I want to know where it goes wrong. Please comment on the three points I list at the end, and feel free to raise other objections as well. I am not asking for citations or further readings, just philosophical feedback.

Here is the essay draft:

Title: The Order of Repairs

The classical puzzle asks us to consider a ship whose planks are gradually replaced until none of the original timber remains, while the discarded planks are collected and assembled into a second ship of identical design. Standard responses appeal to spatiotemporal continuity of form (favoring the repaired ship) or to continuity of matter (favoring the reconstructed ship). I believe both responses misidentify what the puzzle is actually testing, and I will argue that the identity of the repaired ship is secured not by continuity of form or matter but by the order in which its repairs occurred.

Consider a variation I call the Ledger Case. Suppose two dockmasters each oversee the restoration of a decaying vessel. Dockmaster A replaces one plank every week for thirty years, working around the clock in a single harbor. Dockmaster B, in a different harbor, dismantles a copy of the same vessel entirely on a single afternoon, then rebuilds it the next morning from a stockpile of new planks cut to the same specifications. At the end of the process, both ships stand in roughly the same relation to their originals: wholly new matter, preserved design. Yet our judgments diverge sharply. Nearly everyone says A's ship survived; nearly everyone hesitates about B's. But if spatiotemporal continuity of form were doing the real work, B's ship should qualify, since the gap was only a day and the form was preserved on paper throughout. And if matter were doing the real work, A's ship should fail just as B's does.

My proposal is that what distinguishes the cases is that A's repairs were serial and overlapping: at every moment, the new plank was integrated into a structure composed mostly of planks that had already been integrated earlier. Each replacement inherited its status as "part of the ship" from parts that had previously inherited that status, forming a chain of successive accreditations. B's reconstruction has no such chain, because the integration events were simultaneous rather than successive. Identity through material change, on my view, is nothing over and above a chain of accreditation events in which each new part is ratified by a structure that already counts as the object. Call this the Accreditation Theory.

The theory has a welcome consequence: it explains why the reconstructed ship of Theseus, built from the old planks, cannot reclaim the name. Its planks were de-accredited one by one as they were discarded, and the later act of reassembly is a new founding, not a continuation. It also handles the case of a ship frozen in a glacier for a century and then thawed: no replacements occurred, so no accreditations were needed, and the ship survives because the chain was never required to run.

I acknowledge one apparent difficulty: if accreditation chains matter, then it seems a ship could be slowly teleported, plank by plank, to a new harbor and remain the same ship, provided each plank is installed before the next is removed. I am inclined to accept this consequence, since I take the puzzle to show that location was never essential to vessel identity in the first place.

End of draft.

The three points I would most appreciate comments on:

1. Is the central inference in the Ledger Case sound? Specifically, does the divergence in our judgments about the two dockmasters really show that neither form-continuity nor matter-continuity explains identity, or might a defender of either standard view absorb the case?

2. Does the Accreditation Theory simply relocate the puzzle? A critic might say that whether a new plank "counts as part of the ship" at the moment of installation is exactly the question the theory was supposed to answer, so the account is circular. Is there a way to state the notion of accreditation that avoids this, or should I concede the circularity and reframe the theory as merely descriptive?

3. The final paragraph bites a bullet about plank-by-plank teleportation. Is this bullet as costly as I assume, or does accepting it actually undermine the intuition I relied on in the Ledger Case, since that intuition seemed to depend on the ship remaining in one harbor?

Thank you in advance for detailed and candid feedback.
