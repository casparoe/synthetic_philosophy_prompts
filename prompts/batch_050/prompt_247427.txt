So I'm on the ethics committee of a 40-person co-living co-op (long story) and we're writing the spec for an assistant AI that will help run the place -- scheduling the shared kitchen, mediating noise complaints, proposing house rules, drafting the agenda for house meetings. The big fight is: whose values should the thing actually serve when the house disagrees, which is always?

While procrastinating I reread the dedication of Mill's On Liberty, where he writes about Harriet Taylor that her "exalted sense of truth and right was my strongest incitement, and whose approbation was my chief reward". So here is Mill, the great democratiser of whose-opinion-counts, flatly saying that one particular person's approval mattered more to him than everyone else's combined. That threw me a bit.

Here are the five candidates we're considering for the AI's "value source". Rank them best to worst, and also give each one a weight between 0 and 1 (they should sum to 1) for how much it should count in the final spec:

1. Aggregate current preferences -- whatever the majority of residents report wanting right now, weighed equally.
2. Liberal neutrality -- the AI refuses to take sides on anyone's conception of the good life and only enforces what everyone could accept (no harm, keeping promises, shared-resource fairness); on everything else it stays silent or forces a vote.
3. Deference to the house's most reflective member(s) -- the two or three people whose judgment has held up best over time get outsized say, the way Mill treated Harriet's.
4. Laundered preferences -- serve the values residents *would* endorse if they were calm, informed, and had actually heard each other's arguments, even if that diverges from what they currently say.
5. Revealed preferences -- infer values from what residents actually do (who shows up to clean, what food gets eaten) rather than from what they say in meetings.

For each: one or two sentences on why it lands where it does. Then the harder part: does the Mill dedication show that option 3 is secretly what even believers in option 1 do -- or does it show something different, like that "whose approbation I want" and "whose values should rule a collective" are two separate questions that I keep smushing together? And if you think 2 and 4 are in genuine tension (neutrality says don't correct people, laundering says correct them), tell me which one you'd sacrifice first for a house AI specifically, as opposed to a state. No need to be diplomatic about it, I want an actual ordering not a "it depends" essay.
