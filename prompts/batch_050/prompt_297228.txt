I teach a graduate seminar in practical ethics that enrolls students from philosophy, public policy, and the sciences, and next week I am introducing the unilateralist's curse: the structural phenomenon in which a group of well-intentioned agents, each of whom can unilaterally bring about some irreversible action (say, publishing sensitive research, releasing a technology, making a binding commitment on the group's behalf), will tend to see the action taken even when most or all of them, had they known what the others knew, would have judged it unwise. The mechanism is that the action goes forward whenever even one agent's private assessment favors it, so the outcome is effectively selected by the most optimistic estimate in the room, and the more agents with unilateral power, the more likely someone draws an optimistic estimate by honest error alone. My students reliably find this fascinating but reliably misunderstand it, so rather than explain the idea to me, plan how I should explain it in a single ninety-minute session.

Specifically: set out the order in which the components must be introduced and the dependency relations among them (my own sense is that a selection-effect intuition of the winner's-curse sort must precede the claim about unilateral authority, and that both must precede the claim that the curse afflicts fully rational, fully honest agents, but tell me whether you think that ordering is right); identify the one carrying example to build the session around and say what features it must have (irreversibility, dispersed decision authority, honest variance in judgment) and what it must avoid (a villain, a coordination failure due to secrecy alone, payoffs that make the action privately profitable); map the misconceptions I should expect — I anticipate at least these: that the curse is just individual overconfidence and vanishes once agents are debiased, that it is simply a collective action problem of the familiar kind, that better communication among the agents dissolves it entirely, and that adding more decision-makers is protective — and indicate precisely where in the sequence each should be defused rather than allowed to harden; and give me a closing exercise or question that would show the idea has landed rather than merely been admired, ideally one that tests whether students can generate the structure in a new domain rather than recognize it in mine.

Throughout, indicate how confident you are in each part of the plan, and single out the one point — whether in the ordering, the example, or the misconception map — where you are least sure of yourself and why.
