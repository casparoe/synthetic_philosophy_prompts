I'm a high school student, and I've been trying to sort out when it's reasonable to just take someone's word for something. I read a short article that described two broad views: one says believing testimony is justified by default unless you have a specific reason to doubt it (the "anti-reductionist" view), and the other says you need some positive evidence that the speaker is competent and honest before their word gives you a reason to believe them (the "reductionist" view). Before I pick a side, I want to figure out what I actually believe, so here are my gut verdicts on some cases.

1. A classmate tells me our history teacher moved the test to Thursday, saying he heard it from the teacher directly. I believe him without checking. That seems fine.
2. A stranger in a city I'm visiting tells me the bus stop is two blocks north. I take his word for it. Fine.
3. A classmate who once lied to get me in trouble tells me the test was moved. I don't believe him until I confirm. Fine to doubt.
4. My math teacher tells me a proof in our textbook is valid. I can't follow the proof myself, but I believe her. Fine.
5. Three different people at school tell me, supposedly independently, that a student got suspended. I believe it. Fine.
6. My friend's cousin, whom I've never met, tells my friend, who tells me, that the concert is sold out. I believe it. Fine.
7. My dentist gives me stock tips. I don't put any weight on them. Fine to discount.
8. A screenshot circulates in a group chat quoting something a teacher allegedly said. I don't trust it. Fine to doubt.
9. When I ask an AI chatbot a history question, I mostly believe what it says, but I feel like I shouldn't treat it the same way I treat a person's word -- and I can't articulate why.

Here's what bothers me: cases 5 and 6 seem to pull against each other. In 5 I trust three people repeating the same claim, but in 6 I trust a single chain of two intermediaries, and in 8 I reject a chain that looks structurally similar to 6. Also, case 1 looks like a chain too (teacher to classmate to me), and I'm fine with it. So either my confidence tracks something other than chain length and number of sources, or one of these verdicts has to go.

My questions: Is there a single principle that captures verdicts 1 through 8 -- ideally one precise enough to say, for each case, exactly which feature does the work (independence of sources, incentives to lie, verifiability of the subject matter, expertise, the possibility of garbled transmission, or something else)? Does that principle land me closer to the reductionist or the anti-reductionist view, or does it force a hybrid? Which of my verdicts, if any, does the best simple principle have to overturn, and is the price worth paying? Finally, what does the resulting principle say about case 9 -- is chatbot output testimony at all, and if not, on what footing am I trusting or distrusting it?
