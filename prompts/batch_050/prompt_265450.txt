Having worked through the standard Bayesian treatment of the value of information, I have arrived at the following package of commitments, and I would like to know whether it is internally coherent:

1. Rational credences satisfy the probability axioms and are updated by conditionalization; rational action consists in maximizing expected utility.
2. Good's theorem is sound: cost-free information that might bear on a pending decision has non-negative expected value, and a rational agent therefore never refuses it.
3. It can nonetheless be rational to pay in order not to learn something. The standard cases are the patient who declines a predictive genetic test for an incurable condition because the knowledge would poison her remaining healthy years, and the reader who avoids spoilers in advance of a novel he intends to read.
4. In evaluating prospective choices, I may assume that my future self will conditionalize on whatever has been learned and will choose optimally at the moment of decision.
5. The value of information is wholly instrumental: knowing has no value or disvalue in itself, but only in virtue of its bearing on decisions.
6. Information that could not change any decision available to me has no value for me at all.

Is this set jointly tenable? Please identify which commitments genuinely pull against one another and which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection; order the genuine tensions by severity; and for each, state what would have to be surrendered and what that surrender would cost. I am not attached to any particular member of the list, so I would also like a verdict on which commitment is cheapest to abandon, and why.
