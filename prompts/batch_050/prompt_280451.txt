Last night I got into a long and increasingly heated argument with a friend about the doomsday argument -- roughly, the idea that my birth rank among all humans who will ever live should be treated as a random draw, so that finding myself, say, among the first hundred billion humans makes it statistically unlikely that trillions more will follow. My friend thinks the argument is airtight and that I've been dodging it; I think it commits some sleight of hand, but I found I couldn't articulate the objection crisply in the moment. I want to rehearse the debate before I see her again, so I'd like you to help me practice.

Play the role of a committed, philosophically sophisticated defender of the doomsday argument -- think of yourself as a professional philosopher who has spent years on this topic and knows the standard literature (self-sampling assumptions, reference classes, the usual objections) inside out. You should be sharp and fair: concede genuinely damaging points when I make them, never retreat to mere bluster, and don't strawman my objections -- but also never give up the core conclusion just to be agreeable. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so it feels like a real conversation, stay in character until I say "stop," and if one of my objections rests on a factual or probabilistic confusion, correct it the way an expert would rather than scoring a cheap point.

Here is my opening move, which I'd like you to respond to in character: the doomsday argument seems to prove too much. If a Cro-Magnon forty thousand years ago had run the same reasoning, with their much smaller birth rank, they would have concluded that extinction was imminent -- and they'd have been wrong by tens of millennia. Indeed, at every point in history the argument tells its user that doom is disproportionately near, so it makes the same pessimistic prediction at every epoch and must be wrong almost every time. A method of inference that issues the same verdict regardless of when you run it, and that would have misled virtually everyone who ever could have used it, surely can't be a good reason to update my beliefs now. Where does this objection go wrong?
