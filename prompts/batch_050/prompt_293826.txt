I'm preparing a position statement for a graduate seminar on moral conflict, and before I defend it I would like a consistency audit. Here is where I have landed. (1) Genuine moral dilemmas are impossible: there are no situations in which an agent acts wrongly no matter which available option they take; in every predicament, at least one course of action is permissible. (2) 'Ought' implies 'can': nobody is obliged to do what is beyond their power. (3) Obligations agglomerate: if I am obliged to do A and obliged to do B, then I am obliged to do both. (4) Obligations sometimes genuinely conflict: there are cases in which I am obliged to assist one person and obliged to keep a commitment to another, I cannot do both, and neither obligation is merely apparent or already defeated. (5) In such cases, whichever way I choose, I owe the person I failed an apology and often amends, and guilt is the fitting response on my part — although I have not acted wrongly. (6) Blame is appropriate only in response to wrongdoing.

Please examine this set and tell me whether it hangs together. Which pairs or clusters of commitments are genuinely in tension, and how severe is each? Which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and why? For each genuine tension, identify the least costly revision available to me and say what it costs. Finally, if I am permitted to change only one commitment, tell me which single revision would restore the most consistency to the set, and what I would lose by making it. Please write your answer in British English.
