I've been thinking about Dutch book arguments and I want to figure out what I actually think they show, so here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases -- tell me what principle, if any, unifies them.

1. Alice has credence 0.7 that it rains tomorrow and 0.7 that it doesn't. A bookie offers her bets at prices matching her credences and she accepts. She's guaranteed to lose 40 cents no matter what. Verdict: her credences are irrational, full stop, and the Dutch book is what shows it.

2. Bob has the exact same credences as Alice but he hates risk and never bets at any odds, so no bookie can ever actually get money off him. Verdict: his credences are still irrational, but here's the thing -- I don't think the Dutch book arguement gets to claim credit for showing it, since by construction nothing bad can happen to him. Something else must be wrong with his beliefs.

3. Carol assigns credence 0.5 to some big mathematical conjecture that is in fact a theorem, provable from axioms she accepts. Verdict: not irrational, or at least not irrational in the way Alice is. Nobody who can't prove the theorem could actually book her, and "you should have already seen the proof" feels like a different kind of criticism than "your beliefs guarantee you a loss."

4. Dana announces today that tomorrow she's going to raise her credence in rain to 0.9 regardless of what she observes overnight -- she's just decided. A bookie can exploit this with a bet today plus a bet tomorrow. Verdict: irrational, and here the book does the work again.

5. Eve's credences violate no synchronic rule, but she updates by a rule that isn't conditionalization: on learning E she moves to credence in H of (old credence in H&E) + 0.05, for no reason. Verdict: irrational, and I think the diachronic Dutch book shows it, but I feel weird about this because she never accepts a bet she expects to lose at the time she accepts it.

6. Frank's credences sum to 1.2 across a partition, same as Alice in spirit, but the only bookie in town is a casino that takes a cut on every bet, so no combination of bets guarantees Frank a loss. Verdict: his credences are just as irrational as Alice's. The exploitability seems beside the point.

So, what do I actually think? Is the criterion something like "credences must be probabilities, period" with Dutch books as a diagnostic, or is exploitability itself doing the normative work? Cases 2 and 6 seem to push hard against the exploitability reading, but then I owe an account of what's wrong with Bob and Frank that doesn't just smuggle the betting story back in. And 3 vs 4 and 5 makes me wonder whether diachronic cases are really about coherence at all. Give me the best principle (or two candidates), tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and say what it predicts about a case I haven't listed: someone whose credences are finitely but not countably additive.
