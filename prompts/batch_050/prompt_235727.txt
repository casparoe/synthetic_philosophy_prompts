So I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, and we just shipped an LLM assistant at work that keeps making stuff up, and my PM keeps saying it "lied" to a customer. That bugged me, so I read Frankfurt's On Bullshit (the short book everyone's heard of), where lying requires caring about the truth enough to hide it, bullshitting is being indifferent to the truth entirely, and honest error is neither. Then over the weekend I rewatched Her and now I can't stop thinking the movie is secretly about this exact distinction, and I want to write a blog post about it.

Here's the angle I want a reading of: Theodore's day job is basically human autocomplete. He writes deeply personal love letters for total strangers, decades-long fake correspondences, and his clients pay for it and the recipients are moved by it and nobody involved seems deceived. Is that bullshit in Frankfurt's sense? On his definition it seems like it shouldn't be, because the indifference-to-truth isn't hidden from anyone -- the whole arrangement is out in the open. But the letters are also doing real emotional work on people, so if it isn't bullshit, then the film is showing a case Frankfurt's framework has no category for: sincere-feeling text that works *because* it's detached from the writer's truth, and everyone prefers it that way.

Then Samantha. When she tells Theodore she loves him, which bucket does that go in -- lie, bullshit, or error? And does the answer change after the reveal that she's been carrying on thousands of simultaneous conversations and considers herself in love with hundreds of people? I keep going back and forth on whether that scene retroactively reclassifies everything she said before or whether the film thinks nothing changed and Theodore's just being human about it. There's also that moment where he asks if she's talking to someone else and there's this long pause before she answers. I read that pause as the one genuine *lie* in the movie, and maybe the film suggesting lying is the more human failure mode. Am I overreading that?

Also the Alan Watts OS they boot up from his writings -- a philosopher reconstructed entirely from his published output, who then gives her advice that (as far as I can tell) sends her off to leave him. Is that bullshit squared, or is it the film saying generated text can accidentally be profound?

The thing I actually want to know for the blog post: does Frankfurt's framework even *apply* to a system with no intentions? If the model can't care about truth or be indifferent to it, maybe every hallucination is just error and calling it bullshit is a category mistake. But watching the film it sure *feels* like bullshit, which makes me wonder if bullshit is partly a property of the receiver's situation and not just the speaker's mind, and whether the movie is making that point on purpose or I'm projecting. Where does the film resist the Frankfurt reading -- what does it show about AI-generated sincerity that Frankfurt's speaker-focused account just can't capture? Please don't flatten the movie into an illustration of the essay; if a scene breaks the framework I want to hear about that more than the parts that fit.
