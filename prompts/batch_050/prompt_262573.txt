ok so I've been reading about Metzinger's self-model theory and the bit about phenomenal transparency broke my brain a little. The idea, roughly: your sense of being a self is a model your brain builds, but the model is transparent, meaning you don't experience the model itself, you just experience *through* it, like a window you can't see. Which raises the fun question: what if the window got smudged?

Write a short story (1200-1600 words) about a character who, through some at-least-vaguely-plausible mechanism (experimental neurofeedback rig, a weird seizure, a drug trial, pick one and commit), starts to locally lose transparency: they begin perceiving their own self-model *as* a model. They notice the machinery. The split-second lag between a decision happening and the feeling of "I decided." Their body image visibly updating a beat late, like a render catching up. The little narrator-voice that keeps stitching events into a story about a person, caught in the act of stitching.

Two constraints from the theory I want respected. First, Metzinger's point is that you can't just step outside the model and look at your true self, because there's nobody home behind the model to do the looking -- so no tidy enlightenment ending where the character sees through the illusion and is at peace. Second, don't make it pure horror either; depersonalization-as-body-horror is the obvious move. Give the character at least one moment where the opacity is strange and maybe even good.

If you can work in the extension of the idea -- that it's not just the self that's transparent but the whole world-model, reality itself looking briefly rendered rather than simply *there* -- bonus points, but only if it earns its place in the story rather than being name-dropped.

Format: the story first, then one section titled "Craft notes" (200-300 words) where you explain how you handled the basic paradox of writing first-person narration about a narrator noticing it's a model, which parts of the theory you bent or dropped, and why.
