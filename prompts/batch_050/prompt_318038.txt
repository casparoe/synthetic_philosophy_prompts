For an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the metaphysics of social kinds (16 students, one 75-minute meeting, no advance reading assigned), design an in-class game that makes participants feel, rather than merely nod along to, a specific and easily missed feature of institutional facts: a socially constructed kind can be sustained by nothing at all except collective acceptance, and yet be entirely beyond the power of any individual participant's acceptance or rejection. Money is my target kind. My students already parrot the standard classroom point that money is just paper whose value rests on convention; they say it without feeling anything. What I want them to run into bodily is the harder point that follows from it: if the money is nothing but our shared treating-it-as-money, then any one of them should be able to destroy it by simply refusing to go along -- and yet when someone in the room attempts exactly that, nothing happens, while a single sentence from the right person ends the currency outright. The rules and incentives should be arranged so that this asymmetry happens to them before anyone names it: no lecture up front, and the phrase social construction should not be uttered until the debrief.

Please give me: complete rules, roles, and materials, including how tokens first acquire value, what the real but small stakes are (no money, nothing grade-related), how an opt-out or renunciation attempt arises (scripted role or naturally emerging, your choice, but justify it), the mechanism by which a designated office-holder's declaration creates or destroys the kind, and whether and how counterfeiting is possible and policed; a phase-by-phase timeline fitting the 75 minutes; a debrief plan mapping particular moments in the game onto the ideas at stake (declaration, the self-referential structure of collective acceptance, the asymmetry of whose acceptance counts, and the continuing maintenance work a community must do to keep a kind in existence); a frank prediction of the two or three things most likely to go wrong the first time I run it, with fixes; and an honest note on which features of real institutional kinds the game cannot reproduce -- I am thinking especially of involuntariness (my students can leave the room; actual subjects of a monetary order cannot) and of historical sedimentation (everyone present will remember the founding moment, which is true of no real currency).
