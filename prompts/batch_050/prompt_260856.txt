I am a retired materials scientist — I spent the better part of four decades characterizing ceramics and alloys, which perhaps explains why I have lately become absorbed in the philosophical problem of material constitution. The puzzle, as I understand it, begins innocently: a sculptor forms a lump of clay into a statue. The statue and the lump coincide exactly, atom for atom, yet seem to differ in their persistence conditions — flatten the lump into a pancake and the statue is destroyed while the lump of clay carries on; subject the statue to slow erosion and matters seem less clear. From this one case a whole menagerie of positions has grown, and I confess I cannot reliably tell them apart or see which commitments force which.

Please construct for me a decision tree that takes a scientifically literate but philosophically untrained reader from the initial puzzle to a named position on material constitution. I have in mind six to eight diagnostic questions, each branching at most three ways, and each phrased as a concrete case or a plain claim that a reader can answer without knowing any of the labels — for instance, by asking how many things sit on the pedestal, whether two objects could be made of precisely the same matter at the same time, whether anything is lost when the statue is crushed that would not be lost if a mere heap were crushed, whether an object can survive the gradual replacement of every one of its atoms, or whether questions of sameness always have an absolute answer or only an answer relative to a way of counting. The leaves should include at least: the coincidence or constitution view (two objects, one material basis); four-dimensionalism with temporal parts; the eliminative view denying that statues exist over and above their matter; mereological essentialism; the view that only one object was ever there and our sortals merely mislead us about which; relative identity; and the deflationary or conventionalist view that the whole dispute is verbal.

Three further stipulations. First, order the questions so that the dispute's logic drives the tree rather than a checklist of famous names: I want to see which combinations of answers are available and which are not. Second, where two leaves cannot be distinguished by any question my intended reader could actually answer, say so explicitly rather than inventing a spurious discriminator; likewise, mark any combination of answers that no one defends and give your best diagnosis of whether it is empty because incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Third, my real interest is in whether anything in this debate turns on facts a scientist could in principle investigate — so where a branch concerns whether the disagreement is factual or merely about how we choose to speak, make that the operative question rather than burying it.

Finally, a request about your own epistemic posture: please indicate how confident you are, overall, that the tree faithfully sorts the actual positions in the literature, and single out the one placement or branching decision you are least sure of, with a sentence on what would settle it.
