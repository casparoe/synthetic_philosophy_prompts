I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or biblical studies, but I recently fell down a rabbit hole reading about the so-called criteria of authenticity that historians have used to sift the sayings and deeds attributed to Jesus of Nazareth in the gospels. Three of them, roughly: (1) multiple attestation — material that shows up in plausibly independent sources is more likely to be historical; (2) embarrassment — material that would have been awkward for the early Christian communities is unlikely to have been invented by them; (3) dissimilarity — material that fits neither the Judaism of the period nor the later church is unlikely to have been retrojected onto Jesus.

What struck me is that I instinctively model these criteria as features in a classifier trained on noisy, correlated data. Each criterion has some false-positive and false-negative rate; none of them is a proof procedure; and the independence assumption behind multiple attestation is exactly the kind of thing I'd flag in a code review, since the synoptic gospels appear to be literarily interdependent rather than independent witnesses. At the same time, in my own work, converging weak signals often do justify confident conclusions — that's basically what integration testing and anomaly detection rely on.

Write an essay assessing whether criteria of this kind can ever settle who the historical Jesus was. Engage seriously with the classifier analogy: say where it illuminates the epistemic situation and where it distorts it. Weigh the main considerations on both sides — for example, that the criteria seem to deliver probabilistic verdicts on isolated units of tradition rather than a portrait of a whole person; that what counts as embarrassing depends on a community we know largely through the very texts being evaluated; that dissimilarity, applied consistently, would make Jesus neither Jewish nor Christian; that some scholars have proposed abandoning authenticity-criteria altogether in favor of asking how memories of Jesus were transmitted and reshaped — against the counter-consideration that historiography never deals in certainty, that no alternative method has emerged that isn't arguably the criteria in disguise, and that demanding more from these tools than we demand of evidence in courtrooms or in debugging may be an impossible standard. Reach a verdict and justify it. A qualified verdict is acceptable (for instance, that the criteria can settle some questions but structurally cannot settle others), but don't end on a shrug.
