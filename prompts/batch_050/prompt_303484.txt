My friend Dan and I have an ongoing argument about how we each donate money. Every month we both set aside a few hundred dollars to give away. I give mine to charities with a solid track record — distributing malaria nets, funding treatments for preventable diseases, that kind of thing. Dan gives his to a small research outfit that claims to be working on preventing a hypothetical catastrophe that hasn't happened yet and may never happen, but which, if it did, would affect an absolutely staggering number of people (including people who don't exist yet).

Here's his reasoning, and I can't seem to find the flaw in it. He says: suppose the chance that his group's work actually prevents anything is tiny — he's happy to grant it's like one in a hundred thousand, maybe worse. But the number of beings who would be affected, spread over the entire future, is so mind-bogglingly huge that if you multiply it out, his donation beats mine on expected impact by an absurd margin. He admits freely he has no decent evidence the group is accomplishing anything, and probably never will. He says that's irrelevant to the math.

When I push him on where this ends, he bites every bullet. He says that in principle he'd take any gamble, no matter how vanishingly unlikely the payoff, as long as the payoff is big enough to compensate — and that if someone walked up to him on the street and offered him a one-in-a-trillion shot at some astronomically wonderful outcome in exchange for his life savings, the "rational" answer would depend only on the numbers, not on how fishy the offer smells.

Something about this feels deeply wrong to me, but whenever I try to say why, I end up just... restating that the probabilities are low, which he already grants. Am I missing something? Is there a principled way to explain what goes wrong with his style of reasoning — some line you can draw that doesn't just amount to refusing to take small probabilities seriously — or am I the irrational one here and he's just more consistent than me?
