I serve on the admissions committee of a regional registry of historic watercraft, and we have an application that has split us down the middle. I would like a recommendation that is answerable to our own record rather than to first principles, so here is that record.

Our past decisions:

1. A pilot cutter in continuous service for sixty years, during which every plank, spar, and fitting was replaced at least once -- but always piecemeal, a few components per season, with the vessel never out of the water for longer than a winter. Kept on the register.

2. A steam launch that was fully dismantled so its boiler could be replaced; the parts spent three years in storage and were then reassembled, with everything original except the boiler and its piping. Kept on the register.

3. A trawler that sank at its moorings; the proposed "restoration" amounted to building a new hull to the original lines and transferring over the engine, the wheel, and the nameplate. Removed from the register.

4. A barge whose hull was ninety percent original fabric but which had been permanently converted into a floating café -- interior gutted, incapable of moving under its own power, never to travel again. Removed from the register over vigorous dissent.

5. A lifeboat that spent twenty years as a static museum exhibit and was then returned to sailing condition using roughly seventy percent of its original timbers, the rotted remainder replaced with new material. Kept on the register.

The new application is in fact two applications, each claiming to be the one historic fishing smack launched in 1908. Claimant A is the vessel that has remained in the water and in use since that year, maintained in the ordinary piecemeal way, so that only about fifteen percent of its current material dates from the original build. Claimant B is a boat assembled last year by a collector who spent decades buying the original components as they were removed from A; roughly seventy percent of B's material is original fabric from the smack, but those parts sat in sheds, changed hands, and were only recently joined together, and B has no continuous history of service. Our charter allows exactly one entry per historic vessel.

Please tell me: which claimant should get the entry if we are to be consistent with our own precedents; which two of the earlier decisions are doing the most work on each side and why, taken together, they still underdetermine this case; what standing rule we would effectively be adopting with each possible verdict; and which hypothetical case -- had it come to us years ago and been decided -- would have done the most to settle this one in advance. If you think some of our past decisions cannot in fact be reconciled under any single rule, I would rather hear that than receive a forced consistency.
