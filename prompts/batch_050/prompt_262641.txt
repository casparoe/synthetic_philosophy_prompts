I'm a journalist working on a piece about how the history of science gets told to the public, and I keep bumping into the complaint that most popular science history is Whig history -- the past narrated as a march toward what we now know is true, with the losers cast as obstacles rather than as people with their own reasonable picture.

Here's what I want from you. Take these five episodes, all staples of popular retellings: (a) Galileo's telescopic observations and his clash with the Church, (b) the reception of Darwin's Origin in the decades after 1859, (c) Wegener's continental drift being ridiculed and then vindicated, (d) Lavoisier's oxygen theory displacing phlogiston, and (e) Newtonian mechanics overthrowing Aristotelian physics. Rank them from most Whiggish to least Whiggish *as standardly told*, using this test I came up with: how badly would the story fall apart if you had to tell it without knowing the ending -- without being allowed to use who turned out to be right as a source of drama or moral? A story that still works on those terms is less Whiggish; one that collapses into a bunch of confusing people being difficult is more.

A few ground rules so this is actually useful to me. First, commit: give me a definite ordering, not "it depends on the telling" -- take the most common pop-science telling you can think of for each and judge that. Second, tell me in each case what specifically survives or dies under my test (e.g., I've heard Galileo's opponents had actual empirical worries about stellar parallax -- does that kind of thing save the story?). Third, if you think one of the five is usually told in a way that's *less* Whiggish than its reputation suggests, or one is a stealth offender that nobody flags, say so and let that drive the ranking.

Format: exactly five numbered items, one per episode in your ranked order, each just a sentence or two of justification. No intro, no conclusion, no "it depends" preamble -- I can add the throat-clearing myself.
