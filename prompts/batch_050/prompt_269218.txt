Construct a thought experiment in which a single, clearly competent decision-maker is ambiguity-averse in one choice and ambiguity-seeking in another, where the two choices arise in the same situation, on the same day, and concern uncertainties of the same kind and magnitude. The pattern must look like her settled judgment rather than a slip: a reasonable reader should find both preferences at least initially sympathetic. I intend to use the case in a paper arguing that "ambiguity attitude" is not a stable trait of agents but a label we paste onto choices driven by other features of the situation, so the case only counts if the reversal genuinely falls out of the scenario rather than being stipulated.

Constraints on the scenario:

- Ordinary life only: a hiring decision, a medical choice, a route to the airport. No urns, no balls, no casinos, no Ellsberg-style compound lotteries, and no monetary payoffs at all.
- The two choices must be matched on everything you can plausibly match: stakes, time horizon, the agent's information, and the absence of known probabilities. In particular, the reversal must not be attributable to the well-worn effects I want to set aside: small versus large stakes, low versus moderate probability of the good outcome, gains versus losses, or a difference in the agent's perceived competence with respect to the two uncertainties. Isolate a different variable, and hold these fixed.
- Do not build the verdicts in by fiat. The preferences should emerge from how the situation is described, not from a sentence announcing that she prefers the ambiguous option.
- One to two paragraphs for the scenario itself.

After the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specifications:

1. State the two preferences precisely and identify, in each choice, exactly where the missing probabilistic information lies and why the agent knows it is missing.
2. Run the case through three standard models: maxmin expected utility, the smooth ambiguity model, and the probabilistic-sophistication account. For each, either give the representation that captures the pattern -- the set of priors, the second-order distribution and ambiguity function, or the relevant violation -- or show that no such representation exists and explain why. No gesturing.
3. Name the single feature of the case that generates the reversal, and say what happens to the preferences if that feature is removed: does the case collapse into uniform aversion, uniform seeking, or indifference?
4. Steelman the strongest objection available to a defender of the trait view: that the agent is not really ambiguity-seeking in the second choice but is tracking something else the ambiguous option happens to offer -- a sense of control, a better story to tell afterwards, an opportunity to learn. Then adjudicate: does this objection dissolve the case, or merely relabel the very phenomenon the case was built to exhibit?
5. Close with two or three sentences on what the case shows about whether attitudes toward ambiguity are attitudes toward ambiguity at all, and what a proponent of a stable ambiguity-attitude parameter would have to concede to accommodate your scenario.
