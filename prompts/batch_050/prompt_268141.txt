I am a novelist, formally trained in nothing beyond my craft, currently drafting a book narrated by a concert pianist who develops focal dystonia and gradually loses the reliable use of his right hand. To get the phenomenology of this right, I have been reading a popular book on the embodied mind, and it devotes a chapter to Merleau-Ponty's Phenomenology of Perception. The chapter summarizes his view like this:

"Merleau-Ponty's core claim is that the body, not the mind, is the true subject of perception. Long before we form thoughts or judgments about the world, the body already understands it: reaching for a glass, catching a ball, weaving through a crowd require no deliberation, because the body possesses what he calls motor intentionality, a built-in, pre-reflective know-how that orients us toward things. Perception, on this view, is something the nervous system carries out automatically, largely below the threshold of awareness. And since every perceiver's body is different, there is no single objective world standing behind our various perspectives; there is only the world as it shows up for this or that embodied subject. In this he anticipated today's embodied-cognition research, which treats most of cognition as an unconscious autopilot."

The chapter then draws a lesson for writers: since experience is bodily and pre-reflective, representing a character's inner verbal thought is a falsification of how experience actually unfolds, and serious fiction should confine itself to gesture, posture, and sensation. As you might guess, I have professional reasons to resist that conclusion, but I do not trust my motives here, and I cannot tell where the summary itself goes wrong.
