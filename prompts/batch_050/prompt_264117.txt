I help moderate a mid-sized online discussion community in the lineage of the rationalist blogs. Roughly thirty items a week get flagged as manipulative — posts, comments, and occasionally off-site behavior by members — and my co-moderators and I have never had a shared standard for what the word means. Some flags are obvious (sockpuppets astroturfing a product). Most are not. We get charity fundraisers written by people with professional marketing skill, members recruiting friends into expensive self-development programs, emotionally devastating personal essays that end in a donation link, and outreach posts by our own community's most careful writers that nonetheless use framing devices any persuasion professional would recognize. Right now every ruling is a gut call, decisions are inconsistent across moderators, and our appeals process devolves into a debate about what manipulation even is.

Write a decision procedure we can actually run: the questions a moderator should ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which failures are disqualifying versus merely aggravating, and where the procedure terminates in remove or allow versus escalating to the full moderator panel. Constraints: a single moderator should be able to complete a run in about fifteen minutes without contacting the author, and nothing in the procedure may require reading the author's mind — every test must key on features of the communication and its context that a third party can verify from the outside.

The conceptual work I care about is the translation. The philosophical literature on manipulation offers several candidate marks of the distinction between manipulation and legitimate persuasion — bypassing or subverting the target's rational agency rather than engaging it, covertness of the influence, pressure versus trickery, indifference or hostility to the target's interests, deliberate exploitation of known biases — and these do not all draw the line in the same place. Decide which distinctions survive the move from seminar room to moderation queue and which can be collapsed or dropped, and be explicit about what is lost in each simplification. Mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated, and say honestly what kinds of disagreement will survive any procedure at all.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) the factually accurate but emotionally engineered charity appeal; (2) the self-development recruitment relying on social proof and an expiring discount; (3) a case of your choosing where the procedure returns an answer I will not like — plausibly something our community currently tolerates, or something it currently condemns. Close with a short list of what the procedure cannot settle.
