I TA for an undergraduate philosophy of mind seminar, and last week a discussion of the color phi phenomenon turned into a dispute I couldn't settle, so I'd like help locating exactly where the disagreement lives. The setup, as I presented it: a subject is shown two stationary spots flashed briefly in sequence at different locations -- say, a red spot on the left, then a green spot on the right -- and reports seeing a single spot that moves from left to right and changes color partway along its path. The puzzle is that the color change is experienced as occurring at a point along the trajectory *before* the green spot has appeared, even though the brain seemingly could not have known the second spot's color until it was presented. In the terminology Dennett introduced for cases like this, there are two candidate explanations: a Stalinesque model, on which the brain delays and pre-edits the percept -- fabricating the intermediate frames, including the mid-path color switch -- before the content ever reaches consciousness, so the show is fixed in advance; and an Orwellian model, on which the subject actually experiences the red spot sitting still (or moving without changing color) for a moment, and the second flash then triggers an immediate revision of memory, so the subject sincerely but falsely *remembers* having seen the color change en route.

My student insisted that exactly one of these models must be true as a matter of fact -- that there is a real, in-principle discoverable answer to whether the editing happens before experience or after it, even if no experiment we can currently run could distinguish them, and even if subjects' introspective reports come out identically either way. I argued the opposite: that once the timescale of the relevant processing drops below roughly a few hundred milliseconds, the distinction between revising content on its way into consciousness and revising the record of consciousness an instant later loses any truthmaker, because there is no single moment at which a percept becomes conscious and no canonical "finished draft" relative to which pre- or post-editing could be defined. My credence that there is a genuine fact of the matter here -- something that makes one model true and the other false, not merely a difference in how we describe the same neural events -- is about 0.15; hers is about 0.9.

Please give me your own credence that the Stalinesque/Orwellian distinction corresponds to a real difference in cases like color phi, and, more importantly, derive it in the open. Decompose the question into the sub-claims it depends on: whether consciousness has a determinate arrival time for individual contents at sub-second resolution; whether there is a principled difference between a content never being experienced and being experienced-but-instantly-misremembered; whether any conceivable evidence -- neural, behavioral, or introspective -- could in principle discriminate between the models, and whether in-principle undecidability would itself settle the metaphysical question or merely the epistemic one; and whether the apparent distinction presupposes something like a single theater where contents are presented. Assign a rough probability to each sub-claim, show how they combine into your overall number, and tell me which sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to -- that is, which one, if you changed your mind about it, would move your answer the most. Finally, given that my student and I are at 0.9 and 0.15, tell me which sub-question you think our disagreement most likely lives in, and what argument or finding would move a reasonable person at either extreme.
