I work as a software engineer, and a conversation with colleagues about universal basic income left me stuck on something about the welfare system we actually have, as opposed to the one UBI advocates want. Consider the following asymmetry, which I believe holds in most Western countries, including here in the UK. If you lose your job and claim unemployment benefits, the support is conditional: you must demonstrate that you are actively seeking work, attend appointments, and accept suitable job offers, and your payments can be sanctioned or withdrawn if you don't. The system's whole design treats the money as something you must continually qualify for through effort. But if you receive income without working in other ways — dividends from inherited shares, rent from a property you happened to buy decades ago, interest on savings — nobody asks you to demonstrate anything. The money simply arrives, unconditionally, and the state taxes it rather than interrogating you. So the same society runs two regimes: unconditional non-work income for those who hold capital, and heavily conditional non-work income for those who don't.

I have no formal philosophy background, so I'm likely missing the standard vocabulary for this. What I'd like to know is: what theory of desert, reciprocity, or the moral status of work does this asymmetry presuppose? Is there a coherent principle under which income from owning things requires no justification while income-replacing support for the unemployed requires constant proof of effort — for example, some view about property entitlements being already justified while transfers need separate warrant, or about contribution being owed only by those with nothing to contribute but labour? Or is the asymmetry not one principle but two different theories bolted together, held in place by history rather than coherence? I'd also like to know which rival views the current arrangement rules out, and conversely which views it is merely consistent with — I suspect the practice underdetermines the theory more than its defenders admit.

Then run it in the other direction: if a society adopted a genuine UBI, paid to everyone without conditions, what would that presuppose that the current system denies? Is UBI just the unconditional regime of capital income extended to everyone, or does it rest on a stronger claim, such as that a share of social wealth is owed to each person as such rather than earned? And what would a UBI advocate have to concede or explain about why conditionality feels intuitively right to so many people — is there a defensible core to the reciprocity intuition that a well-designed system should accommodate rather than dismiss?
