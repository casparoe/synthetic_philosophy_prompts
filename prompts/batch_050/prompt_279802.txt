I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I design resource-allocation logic for emergency-dispatch systems, and I've fallen down the rabbit hole of John Taurek's 1977 paper arguing that the numbers shouldn't count when you must choose between saving one person and saving five. I've been collecting the standard objections to his position, and so far I have: (1) the fairness objection to his coin-flip proposal -- giving the lone person a 50% chance effectively erases the claims of the five, as if four of them didn't exist; (2) the extension objection -- nothing in Taurek's reasoning stops at five, so it seems to license saving one person over a thousand, or a million, which most people can't accept; (3) the standpoint argument -- saving the five sacrifices no one, since each of the five has exactly as much at stake as the one, so aggregation here isn't trading anyone off against anyone else; and (4) the contractualist objection that each person's complaint against being left to die must be weighed individually, and when the complaints are equal in severity, the greater number of them wins. Can you give me an objection to Taurek that is not on this list and not a restatement, special case, or combination of these four? Explain how it differs from them, and then tell me honestly whether you think it holds up or whether Taurek has a ready reply.
