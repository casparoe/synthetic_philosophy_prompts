Over the past year I've been reporting a long feature on the ethics of randomized trials — the adaptive platform trials that became prominent during the COVID-19 pandemic, emergency-medicine studies that enroll unconscious patients under deferred-consent rules, and hospital systems that now randomize quality-improvement interventions much the way tech companies A/B test product features. Interviewing trialists, bioethicists, and IRB members has left me with a working set of convictions, and before I commit them to print I want to know whether they actually cohere as a package. They are: (1) A randomized trial is ethically permissible only when clinical equipoise obtains — genuine, honest disagreement within the expert medical community about the comparative merits of the trial arms; where the community has quietly converged on a preferred treatment, randomizing patients anyway is a kind of exploitation, whatever the consent forms say. (2) What ultimately matters ethically is a trial's risk-benefit profile for participants relative to the value of the knowledge it will produce; if the burdens imposed are minor and the question is important, a trial can be justified on that basis — the epistemic state of the expert community is useful evidence about risk, not a freestanding moral condition in its own right. (3) A physician may in good conscience enroll her own patient in a trial even when she personally leans toward one arm, provided the expert community is genuinely divided; her duty of personal care is discharged by honesty about her leanings and by declining to randomize where her judgment is settled. (4) Placebo controls can be permissible even where an effective standard treatment already exists, so long as withholding it poses no risk of serious or irreversible harm and participants give informed consent. (5) Deferred or waived consent is legitimate in emergency research comparing treatments already in standard use, since the realistic alternative to the trial is uncontrolled variation in practice, not informed individual choice.

Some of these commitments come from the people I interviewed, some from my own reactions to particular cases, and I have never systematically checked them against one another. What I'd like from you is an audit, not a survey of the literature. Which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension — where the motivation underlying one, taken seriously, undermines another — and which pairs merely appear to conflict but dissolve on careful analysis? Please rank any genuine tensions by severity, and for each one state precisely what would have to be revised or abandoned to restore consistency and what that revision would cost the overall position. I'm not asking which of these views is correct in the abstract; I'm asking whether this particular set can be defended as a package, and if not, what the cheapest coherent version of it looks like.
