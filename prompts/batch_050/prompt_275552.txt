Next month I'm presenting the central chapter of my dissertation at my department's philosophy of mind colloquium, and the Q&A is the part I'm worried about. The chapter defends phenomenal epiphenomenalism — roughly, the view that phenomenal properties are caused by physical goings-on in the brain but themselves cause nothing — against what I take to be its most serious internal problem: the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If our experiences have no causal influence on anything, then it seems they cannot cause our beliefs and reports about them, in which case my judgment that I am now seeing red is caused entirely by non-phenomenal states, and it's hard to see why that judgment should count as knowledge, or why we should trust any of the introspective data that motivated the view in the first place. My reply, in outline, is that the objection conflates two things: a judgment can fail to be caused by an experience and still be justified, because the judgment and the experience share a common physical cause — the same neural state that produces the phenomenal redness also produces my disposition to judge that I'm seeing red — so the reliability of introspection is guaranteed by the underlying causal architecture rather than by any causal arrow from experience to judgment. I argue that demanding more than this (a genuinely phenomenal contribution to the etiology of the judgment) builds the causal efficacy of consciousness into the very standard of justification, and so begs the question against the epiphenomenalist.

The audience matters for how this goes. The department is mostly mainstream philosophers of mind of a broadly physicalist or functionalist bent — people whose instinct will be that my view is crazy but that the paradox is the *wrong* objection, since on their view introspective judgments don't need phenomenal causes either. There's also one sympathetic property dualist who works on consciousness and knows the literature on phenomenal concepts and the explanatory gap better than I do, a metaphysician who will immediately connect the causal structure of my reply to the exclusion problem and overdetermination worries, and a philosopher of science who tends to ask whether a view is doing any explanatory work or is just an idle wheel bolted onto a complete physical story. My dualist colleague worries me most, because he'll grant me the premise that phenomenal properties are causally inert and then press on whether common-cause reliability is *enough* for knowledge — he may accept my architecture and deny it delivers justification, which is the version of the objection I have the least to say about.
