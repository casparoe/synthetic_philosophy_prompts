I am a novelist finishing a book whose two central characters — a wine critic with phenomenological leanings and a cognitive scientist of an eliminativist bent — have spent four hundred pages arguing about whether first-person reports of experience track anything real. Their dispute crystallizes around a case I have adapted from Dennett's coffee-taster intuition pumps in "Quining Qualia": the critic insists that when a vintage he once loved began to seem thin and over-acidic to him, the taste itself changed; the scientist counters that only his standards, memories, and reactions shifted, and that there is no fact of the matter that could settle which description is right. For the final chapter I do not want another dialogue. I want the document the two of them would produce after a genuine, good-faith adversarial collaboration — the kind of text they could both sign and then shelve their quarrel as precisely located rather than resolved.

Please draft that joint statement. It should contain:

1. A numbered list of claims both parties can accept. I expect these to include points about what introspection can and cannot establish (e.g., that it reliably reveals one's current dispositions to judge and react, but not whether the phenomenal character of an experience has changed as opposed to one's standards for describing it); about the difference between a change in preference, a change in discriminatory capacity, and an alleged change in the experience itself; and about what both sides concede the science of perception could and could not show.

2. A table of residual disagreements, each phrased in wording the relevant side would itself accept — no strawmen, since my readers will include philosophers who will check. One row, I suspect, will concern whether "the way the wine tastes to me now" picks out anything over and above a cluster of reactive dispositions; another, whether the impossibility of verifying a quale change from the third person shows there is no fact of the matter or merely that the fact is private.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. For each crux, specify a case, experiment, or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each side predicts — for instance, what each would say about a subject whose discriminatory abilities are tested blind before and after a supposed shift in taste, and whether either side's view is actually falsifiable.

If, in drafting, you find that some portion of their dispute is merely verbal, mark it as such and explain why — that discovery would itself be a fine ending for the book.
