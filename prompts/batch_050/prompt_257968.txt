I co-host a tiny philosophy podcast (two mics, no budget, the whole thing) and next month we're interviewing an epistemologist who defends pragmatic encroachment — roughly the view that whether you know something can depend on your practical situation and not just on your evidence. He's already published the standard defense: bank-style cases, a knowledge-action principle, his view as the sensible middle way between contextualism and boring old purism.

I don't want questions that let him re-run the greatest hits. Draft 8-10 interview questions, and for each one give me a follow-up keyed to the answer he'll most probably give, so i'm not caught flat-footed when he gives it. The set should cover at least:

1. So-called abominable conjunctions — the view seems to license saying things like "I know the bank is open Saturday, but given what's riding on it I shouldn't just rely on that." When he says these are odd-sounding but true, or conversationally inappropriate rather than false, what's the follow-up that keeps the pressure on instead of letting "odd but true" end the exchange?

2. Whose stakes, and when — push on cases where the subject doesn't know the stakes are high: a nurse checks a dosage, everything looks routine, but unknown to her the patient has a rare condition that makes the normal dose lethal. Did she know the dose was safe when she administered it? If he says the subject's *actual* practical situation is what matters whether or not she knows about it, ask about the version where the high stakes only come into existence later — a clerk correctly judges a form is in order and files it, and that night a rule change makes the filing hugely consequential. Did she retroactively stop knowing? And if instead he says stakes only count once the subject is aware of them, press on whether that smuggles purism back in through the awareness condition.

3. The double-checker — a surgeon is as confident as anyone ever is that its the left kidney, and checks the chart anyway. If you may treat p as a premise in deliberation only when you know p, then either she doesn't know (she verified it a dozen times pre-op) or the mandatory check is irrational. He'll reach for some reply about institutional norms or asymmetric costs — what's the follow-up that stops that from being a deflection?

4. Why knowledge and not just justification — the moderate position says practical factors encroach on when you're warranted in *acting*, or on justification, while knowledge itself stays pure. Why does he need the stronger claim, and doesn't the stronger claim get stuck with the weirdest consequence, knowledge evaporating while you're asleep because the stakes rose overnight?

5. Third-person attributions — sometimes the attributor's stakes are sky-high while the subject's are nil: I'm deciding whether to bet the company on my assistant's calm judgment that the shipment went out. Contextualists have a tidy story here. He has to say the assistant simply knows, since her situation is relaxed, even though it'd be crazy for me to call it knowledge from where I sit. Does he bite that bullet, and if so, why isn't that just a reason to become a contextualist?

6. Machines and institutions — if stakes encroach on knowledge, what about a triage algorithm that flags a scan as clear, where the stakes are borne by patients it knows nothing about? Does the view even make sense applied to institutional or artificial knowers, and if it does, whose practical situation counts — the hospital's, the patient's, the programmer's? As far as I know he's never written on this, so his answer should be interesting either way.

7. End with what would change his mind: an intuition, a case, an experimental result? Or is the view flexible enough to absorb anything, in which case what exactly is it claiming?

He'll be quick and will have heard all the obvious objections, so no softballs and nothing that just asks him to restate the position. Order the questions so they build — start where he's comfortable, end where he isn't. And phrase them the way i'd actually say them into a mic, not like a journal article.
