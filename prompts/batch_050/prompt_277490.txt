My alignment reading group has been stuck for months on AI safety via debate, and I can't tell whether we're actually disagreeing or just talking past each other.

The setup is the usual rough one: two AI debaters argue opposite answers to a question, and a weaker judge — maybe a human, maybe a less capable model — decides which answer is better supported. The hope is that a debater arguing for the truth can expose the other side's mistakes, so the judge gets a more reliable answer than they could reach alone.

The fight is about obfuscated arguments. By that I mean arguments where each individual step sounds plausible, but the conclusion is false because of some subtle interaction: an ambiguous term changes meaning halfway through, a bunch of approximately true assumptions compound, a statistical claim is applied outside the domain where it was established, or the argument buries the one bad inference under thirty boring valid ones. Imagine a debate over whether some complicated agent-training setup is safe. The dishonest debater gives a 40-step argument where every step seems locally reasonable, while the honest debater has to reconstruct the whole tree before finding that step 17 quietly assumes away the dangerous case.

One faction says this is a fatal problem for debate. Their point is that the judge doesn't need to be fooled by an obviously bad argument; they only need to be unable to distinguish a deep, subtle flaw from a genuinely valid argument. If the dishonest debater is much better at argument construction than the judge is at verification, more rounds of debate might make things worse because it gives them more room to build elaborate traps.

The other faction says this assumes the honest debater is passive. In a good debate protocol, they say, the honest side can demand definitions, force the other side to formalize each inference, isolate contested premises, present counterexamples, and recursively cross-examine exactly those steps. So obfuscation isn't a special refutation; it's just the normal thing adversarial cross-examination is meant to handle. If it fails in current tests, that shows we need better protocols or judges, not that debate has a fundamental ceiling.

The first group replies that formalization and cross-examination only move the problem: the judge still has to decide whether a proposed counterexample is relevant, whether two formalizations mean the same thing, and when a premise has been adequately supported. The second group says the first is smuggling in an impossible standard — if no amount of local checkability counts, then debate has been defined as doomed from the start.

My hypothesis is that this is really a disagreement about what debate is supposed to accomplish: producing arguments a judge can independently verify, versus creating an adversarial search process where the truthful side usually wins. But I'm not confident.

Diagnose the disagreement rather than declaring one side the winner. Is it substantive or partly verbal? Is there a hidden empirical disagreement about judges, a hidden formal claim about adversarial arguments, or a deeper disagreement about what reliability means? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a toy experiment, a clearer protocol, a formal result, a distinction between kinds of judges, or something else? Please be concrete and don't just give a survey of debate as an AI-safety proposal.
