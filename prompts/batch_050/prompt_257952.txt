I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about tranquilism -- the view that the only thing of intrinsic value is the absence of craving and suffering, so that pleasure and joy are at best ways of getting rid of discomfort. A colleague of mine genuinely holds it, and I've been polling her on cases to figure out what she actually believes. So far she says: a two-hour meditation sit with zero desire and zero discomfort is genuinely good; the euphoria of winning a championship adds nothing beyond relieving the tension of wanting to win; a morphine drip producing total contentment is fine but no better than dreamless sleep; and the life of a cheerful, satisfied person with lots of fulfilled desires is not in itself better than never having existed. Now here's the case I want to put to her: a brain implant that permanently eliminates all craving and suffering but leaves its user having vivid, intense positive experiences -- they notice and relish beauty, they laugh, they feel waves of bliss, they just never want anything or hurt. What do you predict she'll say about it? I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in the rule she's apparently been running all along, which other rules fit her four verdicts equally well, where those rules diverge on the implant case, and which further case would best settle which rule she's really using.
