A friend of mine who keeps bees and reads a lot of entomology made an argument over dinner last week that I haven't been able to shake. We were talking about whether there's anything ethically troubling about destroying a wasp nest or an ant colony, and he said that the whole framing of the question is wrong, because a colony of social insects isn't a community of many animals the way a herd of deer is -- it's a single organism, a superorganism. In his telling, the workers are like somatic cells: they don't reproduce, they share essentially the same genome, they cooperate seamlessly because their interests are perfectly aligned by kinship. The queen is like the germ line, the only part that passes genes forward. Swarming is the colony's way of reproducing, pheromones are its nervous and hormonal systems, and the nest itself is something like a body wall. His conclusion: squashing a forager ant is morally on a par with a person shedding a skin cell, and what deserves moral consideration, conservation effort, and maybe even rights, is the colony as a whole -- the individual workers drop out of the picture entirely.

I'd like a careful audit of this analogy and of the conclusions it's being made to carry. First, lay out the mapping explicitly -- what in the colony corresponds to cells, organs, the germ line, signaling systems, reproduction, death -- and then work through where the mapping breaks. I'm aware, at least dimly, that real ant and bee colonies are messier than my friend's picture: workers of some species can lay eggs, there's conflict within colonies over sex ratios and over whose sons get raised, relatedness among nestmates varies a lot across species, nobody is centrally in charge, and colonies can fuse, split, or execute their own queens. Which of these disanalogies actually damage the analogy for the purposes he was using it, and which are just pedantic? Second, there's a prior question I want taken seriously rather than waved at: whether superorganism is merely a suggestive metaphor or whether a colony can literally qualify as an individual organism. Presumably that depends on what criteria make something a biological individual -- physiological integration, aligned fitness interests, a bottlenecked life cycle, suppression of internal competition -- so tell me which criteria colonies genuinely meet and on which the verdict is unclear. Third, and this is the part I care most about, suppose the analogy holds even in its strongest form and a colony is a genuine organism: do his moral conclusions actually follow? Does a worker's status as something like a cell really strip it of independent moral significance, or is he smuggling an assumption about parts and wholes into the biology? Finally, if the superorganism picture is the wrong tool for his conclusion, is there a better way of putting whatever insight it contains?

Throughout, please separate what is well established in the biology and philosophy here from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on it, and don't present a contested position as settled consensus. Please write the answer in American English.
