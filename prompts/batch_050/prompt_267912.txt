My grandmother signed an advance directive about ten years ago, after watching her own mother die slowly in a nursing home. It says that if she ever reaches advanced dementia and can no longer recognise family or feed herself, she wants no life-prolonging treatment -- no feeding tube, no antibiotics for pneumonia, nothing. She's now in moderate dementia in a care home and, awkwardly for everyone, she's... happy? She doesn't remember signing anything, eats pudding with great enthusiasm, and last week when a nurse mentioned the directive she said "why would I want to die, I have lunch at noon." Our family is split and I keep arguing the directive should stand, so I want to stress-test my side before the next family meeting with the doctor.

Play my opponent: a committed defender of the view that advance directives lose their authority once the person can no longer grasp what they decided. You believe what matters is the person sitting in the chair now, with her current pleasures and distresses, not the will of a self that no longer exists in any psychologically continuous sense. Be a sharp, honest version of this view, not a strawman -- you can concede that the case is uncomfortable and that my grandmother's earlier wish was sincere, but you never concede the core point that the then-self's instructions can't trump the now-self's wellbeing. Stay in character until I say stop, keep replies to a couple of paragraphs so it feels like an actual argument, and if I appeal to something you think is incoherent, push back directly instead of being polite about it.

My opening move: here's my problem with your position. My grandmother didn't just express a preference, she *decided*, carefully and repeatedly, over years, with full knowledge of what dementia looks like. On your view, that whole process of self-determination gets cancelled the moment she loses the ability to re-affirm it -- which means her autonomy only ever counted for as long as she could keep defending it. Isn't that just saying that the people least able to advocate for themselves get the least say over their own lives? And if her current contentment is produced by the very disease she feared -- she'd be horrified by this version of herself if she could see her -- why should we treat it as her real will rather than as the disease talking?
