**Draft: The Spoon-Feeding Problem in Turing's Learning Machines**

In the closing section of his 1950 Mind paper, Turing suggests that instead of trying to simulate an adult mind directly, we should build a 'child machine' and educate it. The proposal is usually read as optimistic: teaching is faster than evolution, and a well-organised course of reward and punishment could turn a suitably constructed machine into a thinking adult. I want to argue that, on Turing's own terms, the proposal is nearly empty. The education he describes cannot do the work he needs it to do.

Turing is clear that schooling a child machine involves more than rewarding right answers and punishing wrong ones. A teacher who can only communicate through such a channel transmits very little information, and Turing himself notes that mere rule-following — what I shall call compliance — is not enough. Something further is required: the machine must be able to strike out on its own, to attempt what it has not been told to attempt. Call this initiative. Turing grants that initiative is essential and grants that punishment-and-reward training cannot supply it. He seems to think it must somehow arise from the machine's construction.

Here is the spoon-feeding problem. If initiative cannot be taught, only built in, then everything that matters about the finished thinker is fixed before the first lesson. Education merely polishes a mind whose intellectual character was determined by the original wiring. The much-quoted analogy with educating human children collapses, because the whole point of human education is that it shapes character rather than merely revealing it.

Turing's unpublished 1948 report for the National Physical Laboratory makes the difficulty worse. There he describes 'unorganised machines', randomly connected networks that he compares to an infant's cortex and proposes to organise through training. But a randomly connected network, trained by reward and punishment, is precisely a system in which initiative has nowhere to come from. The 1948 construction story and the 1950 education story do not fit together: the first makes everything depend on initial randomness, the second makes everything depend on the teacher. My conclusion is that Turing's educational programme is either vacuous (if initiative is built in, education is decoration) or impossible (if it is not, no amount of punishment will produce it).

One might object that human children face the same dilemma, so it cannot be a problem. I think this misses the point: Turing chose the education analogy precisely to avoid such dilemmas, and it fails to avoid this one.
