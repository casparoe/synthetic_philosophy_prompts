I direct a small philosophy-of-mind research group that has just been awarded three years of funding for two postdocs, and I must now decide how to divide our effort among the live problems in the debate over the cognitive penetrability of perception. The candidate projects are these four:

First, the demarcation problem: developing criteria that would distinguish genuine top-down effects on perceptual processing itself from effects on attention, response bias, or post-perceptual judgment and report — including the meta-question of whether any behavioral or neuroimaging paradigm could in principle settle this, or whether the confound between perception and post-perceptual processing is methodologically ineradicable.

Second, the architectural question: what remains of the thesis that perception is informationally encapsulated once strong modularity is conceded to be false — that is, what a weak or graded impenetrability thesis would have to look like to be simultaneously true, non-trivial, and empirically distinguishable from its negation.

Third, the epistemological upshot: whether, if perception is penetrated by beliefs and desires, perceptual experience can still confer justification on belief, and in particular whether there is a principled line between epistemically benign penetration (the expert radiologist who literally sees the tumor the novice misses) and epistemically pernicious penetration (wishful seeing, racialized threat perception), or whether the two collapse into one phenomenon with different moral aftertastes.

Fourth, the applied stakes: whether findings about penetration should actually change institutional practice regarding eyewitness testimony, medical diagnosis, forensic examination, and similar domains where we currently treat trained perception as neutral evidence.

Rank these four projects for our group and defend the ranking against explicit criteria: how much other work in the area depends on resolving the question, its tractability within three years, how neglected it is relative to its importance, and whether it is answerable by philosophical argument, by empirical work, or at all. State which criterion you are weighting most heavily and justify that weighting. Then tell me which two projects you would fund if the budget were halved tomorrow, and identify the single experimental or theoretical result that, if it arrived in the next three years, would most substantially reorder your ranking. I do not want a survey of the literature or a list of who has said what; I want a defended allocation of scarce research time, with the reasoning exposed enough that my co-investigators can attack it.
