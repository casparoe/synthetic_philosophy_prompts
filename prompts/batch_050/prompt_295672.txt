Having retired from a career in experimental physics, I have spent the past two years working my way through philosophy of science, and I have become fixated on what I take to be an underappreciated connection between two literatures that rarely speak to one another: Hume's problem of induction and Wolpert's no-free-lunch theorems from statistical learning theory. My claim is that the no-free-lunch results amount to a formal restatement of the problem of induction rather than a solution to it: they show that, averaged over all possible data-generating processes, no learning algorithm outperforms any other, and therefore that any preference for one method over another must rest on substantive, unjustified-in-advance assumptions about which processes nature actually draws from. I further claim that the practical success of modern machine learning does not circumvent this but merely relocates the inductive assumption into the distribution of real-world data, so that practitioners who regard their methods as assumption-free are simply Hume's unreflective reasoners with better hardware.

I am to present this argument at an interdisciplinary reading group hosted by my old department. The audience will comprise a dozen machine learning doctoral students who know the learning-theory literature well and regard philosophy with polite suspicion, three philosophers of science sympathetic to formal epistemology, and one senior statistician of broadly Bayesian sympathies. I would like a question-and-answer preparation sheet, not a defence of my thesis. Set out the objections I should actually expect, in roughly the order they will arise, phrased as these particular listeners would phrase them -- the doctoral students will press me on whether the uniform averaging over target functions is a caricature that no practitioner need accept, the statistician on whether a well-chosen prior escapes the averaging, and the philosophers on whether I have merely dressed up Goodman in new notation. For each objection, indicate how damaging it would be if left unanswered, give me a reply I can deliver in two minutes, and mark candidly where that reply is weakest. Please also tell me which concession I ought to volunteer at the outset to disarm the most predictable attack, and identify the single question I am privately hoping nobody asks, together with my best answer to it.

The entire sheet should run to no more than 800 words.
