A dispute has broken out in my neighborhood association's group chat, and I would like it settled.

The association runs an annual raffle with an unusual structure. Each of our sixty households may submit as many entries as it likes, at no cost. After the deadline, one entry is drawn at random, and the household behind it wins a cash prize. The catch is that the prize is not fixed: the association puts up a lump sum of $6,000, which is divided by the total number of entries received. If sixty entries come in, the winner gets $100; if six thousand come in, the winner gets $1.

One neighbor argues that the obviously sensible policy is for each household to submit exactly one entry and to trust the others to do the same. Her reasoning: we are all in identical situations, working from identical, public rules, and each of us knows that everyone else knows this. Whatever reasoning leads her to submit one entry will lead every other reasonable household to submit one entry, so we each end up with a one-in-sixty chance at $100. Flooding the pool, she says, defeats itself: universalized, it shrinks the prize toward zero, whereas universal restraint leaves everyone strictly better off. She is not proposing an agreement or a pact — there is no enforcement — only that we are all similar enough reasoners that she can treat her own deliberation as a preview of everyone else's.

A second neighbor calls this fantasy. Nothing connects his submission to anyone else's; the other households will do whatever they will do regardless of what he does, and the correlation she is counting on exists only in her imagination. Moreover, he points out, restraint is most attractive exactly when it is most exploitable: if fifty-nine households submit one entry each and he submits fifty-nine, he wins with probability exactly one-half, for a prize of about $50.85 — an expected payoff of over $25, against the $1.67 that universal restraint yields. And if everyone floods the pool, submitting one entry is no better than submitting many. Either way, he concludes, extra entries beat one.

I am genuinely torn, and I want more than a verdict. First: whose reasoning is sound, and does the disagreement turn on an empirical premise — that similar reasoners really do produce similar outputs — or on what rationality demands in the first place? Second: given that no household can communicate or bind itself, what should a household that wants the best outcome actually submit, and does the answer change if the raffle repeats annually with the same participants? Third: if this situation is structurally identical to some better-known problem, identify it, and say whether that identification changes your verdict. Finally, if the association wants the raffle to remain meaningful, what is the minimal rule change that would achieve it?
