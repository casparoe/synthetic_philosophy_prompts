So here's where I've landed after a lot of back and forth: I think Nozick's experience machine thought experiment is genuinely decisive against hedonism about well-being, and more broadly against any view where wellbeing supervenes entirely on the subject's experiential states. I don't just mean it gives us one intuition among others -- I mean that if the machine works at all (and I think the standard objections fail), then any theory that says a life spent plugged in, with experiences engineered to be as pleasant and as subjectively rich as you like, could be as good as or better than a comparable real life is simply refuted. And I further hold that the usual escape routes don't work: not the status quo bias objection (De Brigard's reversed version still gives the anti-plugging intuition, roughly), not the complaint that the machine is underdescribed (fill in the details however you like -- perfect simulation, indistinguishable from inside -- the intuition survives), not the claim that it only tests our intuitions about meaningful lives rather than prudential value (I'll bite that bullet: a life of pure engineered bliss is prudentially deficient, not just less meaningful).

So here's my question: what would count against this position? I'm not asking for the standard battery of objections rehearsed one more time, though you can draw on them. I'm asking what would actually have to be true, or what would have to turn out to be the case, for me to have to give it up. Some things I'd want you to consider, as candidates for genuine defeaters: (a) Suppose careful experimental work on intuitions showed that anti-plugging reactions are driven by features that are clearly normatively irrelevant -- say, the framing of the machine as involuntary, or its sci-fi creepiness, or fear of being duped by other people -- and that a properly cleaned-up scenario loses the intuition entirely. Does that refute me, or does it just show people's untutored judgments are noisy while mine, the reflective one, stands? Where's the line between debunking and debunking-the-debunking here? (b) Suppose it turns out the intuition does real work only against crude hedonism but is fully explained away by a slightly fancier experiential theory -- say, one that counts false beliefs as experientially costly, or one that builds the value of veridical perception into experience itself. Does that leave my position standing against the original target but conceding the generalization? (c) What if the intuition conflicts with things I'm otherwise committed to -- like the idea that wellbeing is what it makes sense to want for someone for their own sake, including people who'd clearly be better off plugged in on any experiential measure (irreversible suffering, locked-in states)? Am I committed to an uncomfortable verdict there, and is that a defeater or just a cost? (d) And the meta-question: could my position be unfalsifiable in a way that should worry me? If every disconfirming intuition poll gets dismissed as noisy, and every rival theory gets reclassified as not-really-experiential, then what, if anything, am I risking?

Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether someone who shares my position would concede it counts against them or would brush it off. I'd also like to know which single piece of evidence or argument you'd actually expect to move someone like me, if any.
