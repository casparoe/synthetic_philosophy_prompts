I'm the medical director of a college health service, and roughly once a week I get a request that sits right on the line between treatment and self-improvement: stimulants for a student who doesn't meet ADHD criteria but swears he can't focus, propranolol for a violinist whose hands shake at auditions, a referral question about growth hormone for a healthy but very short 17-year-old, a student who's unhappy (not clinically depressed, we screened) asking about ketamine. We have no policy. Right now it's basically my gut call each time, which means two students with the same request can get different answers depending on the day, and I can't defend that if anyone asks.

Write me a decision procedure I can actually run inside a 30-minute appointment, with a resident doing the intake and me signing off. I want: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, the points where the procedure just stops with a no, and the points where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is about values rather than medical facts. Wherever philosophy or bioethics has a usable distinction for this -- disease vs. normal variation vs. enhancement, treating deficits vs. raising people above baseline, "leveling up" someone at the low end of the normal range vs. pushing past it -- translate it into something a resident can apply without having read any of it, and tell me which distinctions survive the simplification and which I should just drop.

The middle cases are what kill me: someone at the 5th percentile of normal attention, someone at the 60th. The procedure has to say something about both, not just the easy ends. Also flag which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where the whole thing will misfire no matter how careful I am.

Then run it on the stimulant case, the propranolol case, and the growth hormone one, and tell me which of the three verdicts I'll find most uncomfortable -- because if the procedure never bites, it's useless.
