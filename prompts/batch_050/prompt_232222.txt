I'm a retired condensed matter physicist (40 years of it, mostly magnetism plus a stint doing neutron scattering) and since hanging up my boots I've gotten weirdly into philosophy of probability. Here's the thing. I spent my whole career using maximum entropy without ever worrying about it — you maximize S subject to fixed energy and particle number, out pops the canonical ensemble, done, it works. Now I find out there's a whole philosophical fight about whether that trick is epistemically special. The objective Bayesians (Jaynes and his crowd) say the maximum entropy principle hands you uniquely rational priors: if all you know is a set of constraints, the maxent distribution is the only honest assignment, and anything else smuggles in information you don't actually have. The critics say there are no rational priors, period — maxent answers depend on how you carve up the space of possibilities (the Bertrand paradox stuff), on which constraints you decide to count as "knowledge," and the whole idea of an ignorance-based prior is confused.

What set me off: I was reading Laplace's A Philosophical Essay on Probabilities (the old Truscott and Emory translation) and hit this near the start of chapter 2:

"All events, even those which on account of their insignificance do not seem to follow the great laws of nature, are a result of it just as necessarily as the revolutions of the sun. In ignorance of the ties which unite such events to the entire system of the universe, they have been made to depend upon final causes or upon hazard, according as they occur and are repeated with regularity, or appear without regard to order; but these imaginary causes have gradually receded with the widening bounds of knowledge and disappear entirely before sound philosophy, which sees in them only the expression of our ignorance of the true causes."
