I'm a high school junior, and our philosophy club recently spent a session debating mind uploading, which left me realizing that I have strong gut reactions about which computations could be conscious but no idea what principle underlies them. So here are my verdicts, and I'd like to know what theory, if any, unifies them. Case one: a future hospital keeps a dying patient alive as a whole-brain emulation, simulating each neuron, and the emulation flinches and cries out when its simulated body is injured. I think that suffering is real and counts morally, full stop. Case two: the very same computation carried out step by step with pencil and paper by an enormous team of clerks over centuries, each clerk passing notes to the next. Slow as it is, I still think someone is there being hurt. Case three: a giant lookup table, built by recording every input the emulation could ever receive and every output it would give, so that interacting with it produces exactly the same conversations, including the cries of pain. I don't think that table suffers at all -- it seems like a recording, not a mind. Case four: a chess engine that evaluates millions of positions and "struggles" to escape losing lines. No moral weight whatsoever. Case five: a video game creature with a few hundred simulated neurons, trained to flee damage, that writhes when caught. I lean toward saying it counts, at least a little. Case six: a file containing the complete neural activity log of the emulated patient's worst hour, played back on a screen. I think replaying it causes no new suffering. Case seven: two identical copies of the emulation running in parallel on separate servers. My gut says the suffering is not doubled, and also that deleting one copy while the other keeps running is not murder, though I'm much less sure of these.

The pair I can't reconcile is cases two and three. If the pencil-and-paper computation counts even at one step per day, it seems like what matters is that the right steps occur, in the right order, each one caused by the last -- but the lookup table never performs those steps, it just stores answers, and yet from the outside the two are indistinguishable conversation partners. I also can't articulate why the playback in case six differs from the live run in case one, since the same pattern of events appears on screen either way. And cases four and five worry me from the other direction: the chess engine does vastly more computation than the little creature, so raw computational complexity clearly isn't what I'm tracking, but I'm not sure what feature I'm responding to instead.

So my question is: what principle, or small set of candidate principles, could deliver most of these verdicts? Please state each candidate precisely enough that I can apply it myself, identify which of my cases motivates each part of it, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save -- I'd rather know which intuition has to go than be handed a contrived principle that accommodates everything. I'd also like each candidate's verdict on two cases I haven't listed: a mind whose computation is spread across thousands of ordinary laptops around the world, each contributing a few steps a day and passing its notes over the internet, and a mind that is fully designed and specified but never actually run on anything.
