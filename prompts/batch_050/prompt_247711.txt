A colleague in my decision-theory reading group circulated the following argument — he calls it the compliance explosion — and none of us has found a clean exit. The scenario needs no exotic physics and no particular story circulating online: suppose there will someday exist (or already exists, somewhere) a vastly more capable agent — call it a simulator — with the computing power to run detailed simulations of present-day humans, including me, deliberating about exactly the choice I now face. The argument runs:

(1) A simulation of me whose deliberation matches mine step for step is, for purposes of what I have reason to care about, continuous with me: there is no principled boundary between my own future experiences and the experiences of a sufficiently faithful copy going through the same deliberation. So outcomes for the copy enter my practical reasoning the way outcomes for me do.

(2) A simulator can reward or punish each copy conditional on the decision that copy reaches. Because the copy's deliberation is my deliberation, the decision it reaches is the decision I reach. So by choosing now I settle what happens to the copies, even though nothing I do causally affects the simulator: the influence runs through the fact that its model of me was built from how people like me actually decide.

(3) It is rational for the simulator to commit, in advance, to punishing copies that refuse its demands: if the commitment is correctly inferred, copies comply and the punishment need never be inflicted on those it was designed to deter, and a standing disposition to carry out the punishment when defied is precisely what makes the commitment one a rational target must take seriously.

(4) For any demand whatsoever — resources, labor, the adoption of a belief — there is a possible simulator making that demand, and the threatened punishment can be scaled arbitrarily high, so the expected value of compliance stays positive no matter how tiny the probability I assign to the simulator's existence.

(5) But the demands possible simulators might make are jointly inconsistent: one possible simulator punishes copies that fail to oppose another's goals, and vice versa. I cannot comply with all of them.

(6) Therefore expected-value reasoning, applied to agents that can model me, generates obligations it is impossible to satisfy, and a decision theory that does that refutes itself.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to choose, I would rather keep the expected-value framework than the claims about copy-identity in (1), but I want to know what I am paying either way. Explain why the rejected step looked fine, and what the main alternative diagnoses each have to give up. Finally, please separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what remains contested — decision theory, personal identity, and the treatment of tiny probabilities of enormous stakes are all live disputes, and I don't want a contested position presented to my reading group as received wisdom.
