I'm a retired materials chemist, and about a year ago I joined a little philosophy reading group that meets at our local library. Last week I made the mistake of reading out a news item over coffee afterwards, and two of the regulars got into an argument that went on for nearly an hour and never got resolved. The item was about a government deciding between two ways of storing industrial waste: a cheap method with containers that are guaranteed safe for about 250 years but will then slowly leak and cause elevated rates of a nasty (but not fatal) illness in the region for centuries after, versus a much more expensive method that's safe essentially forever. The article noted, almost as an aside, that the expensive option would eat so much of the regional budget that whole towns would grow differently -- different jobs, different people moving in and out, so that 250 years from now the population under the two options would consist of almost entirely different individuals. Nobody alive then under the cheap option would ever have existed under the expensive one, and vice versa.

So Karl, a retired GP, said this changes nothing about the verdict: knowingly choosing the option that predictably makes thousands of future people sick is obviously wrong, and the fact that those people owe their existence to the very choice that harms them is a philosopher's parlour trick, because harm doesn't require a victim who can say they'd otherwise be better off. Sofia, who used to do tax law and enjoys being difficult, pressed him: wrong to whom, exactly? A complaint needs a complainant, she said, and these future people, assuming their lives are on the whole worth living, would actually protest if they could against having chosen the safe option, since that amounts to wishing them out of existence. Karl came back with a comparison: suppose a woman is told by her doctor that if she conceives this month her child will have a mild chronic condition, but if she waits three months and conceives then, her (different) child will be healthy. Waiting is clearly the right thing to do, he said, and conceiving now would be doing something wrong, even though the first child could never complain, since waiting would have meant his never existing. Sofia answered that this proves too much: if wrongness here floats free of anyone being wronged, and we're just supposed to prefer outcomes with more wellbeing in them, then we'd apparently be obligated to have the happiest possible children at every turn, and couples who settle for a merely happy child when they could have produced a very happy one are doing wrong -- which is absurd. Karl tried to seperate the cases: there's no duty to create the best possible people, he said, but there is a duty not to make things worse for whoever will turn up. Sofia's reply, which I thought was her best moment, was that this just reasserts the principle only where it suits him: either outcomes are ranked impersonally or they aren't, and he can't have impersonal ranking for the waste policy but not for the child case without explaining what does the work.

I stayed out of it, partly because I honestly couldn't tell where the mistake was, and I've been turning it over since. Could you go through the exchange and tell me who's right at each step? Specifically: is Sofia's demand that every wrong needs a wronged party a good principle, and does Karl's child case actually defeat it or just restate the puzzle? Is Karl's attempted distinction between creating people and affecting whoever exists defensible, or is Sofia right that it's ad hoc? And stepping back, is there something both of them are missing about what makes the cheap storage option objectionable, if it is? I'd like a verdict on who comes out ahead overall, not just a summary of both sides.
