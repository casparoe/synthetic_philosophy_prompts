I am a novelist, and I am currently plotting a book whose central engine is a forced moral compromise: two characters with genuinely different value systems -- one a rights-focused civil libertarian, the other a communitarian who thinks in terms of collective flourishing -- must cooperate to govern a small institution, and the drama comes from the deals they strike. I have read enough to know that philosophers sometimes discuss moral compromise in terms of gains from trade, by analogy with economic exchange between parties with different preferences, and I have also seen the claim that some compromises are corrupting rather than mutually profitable. Before I start drafting, I want to understand what I am entitled to demand of any theory in this area, so that my characters' bargaining rings true rather than reading like two economists haggling over prices dressed up as ethics.

Act as a professional moral philosopher with expertise in value theory and political compromise. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming, in rough note form, the phenomena that any account of compromise between value systems would have to cover; only then organize your findings into a polished specification.

Specifically, write the desiderata a theory of moral compromise must meet to count as adequate for my purpose -- that is, a theory good enough to structure credible moral conflict in fiction, where readers must be able to feel both the pull of the deal and what each party loses in making it. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it. I want, at minimum, your view on the following, but do not limit yourself to them: which sorts of cases the theory must get right (logrolling across unrelated issues, splitting the difference on a single issue, compromising with an opponent one takes to be morally wrong); which distinctions it must not collapse (for instance, between a compromise that merely licenses wrongdoing by one's own lights and one that produces genuine moral gain, or between compromising on means and compromising on ends); and which results from neighboring fields -- the structure of preferences, the idea of value incomparability -- it cannot simply contradict.

Then mark which desiderata you regard as non-negotiable and which are the preferences of a particular camp. If any subset of the desiderata strikes you as jointly unsatisfiable -- say, if the demand that compromises be genuinely gainful conflicts with the demand that agents retain moral integrity -- say so explicitly, since an impossible specification would itself be a useful discovery for my plot. Finally, indicate which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the popular pictures (that compromise is impossible between truly different values, that it is simple aggregation, that it is always a moral loss), but keep the scoring brief: the criteria are what I need, since I will be stress-testing my characters against them, not against the literature.
