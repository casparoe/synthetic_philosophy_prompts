I write a magazine column on science and public affairs, and I keep running into the same shape of dispute across very different beats: gain-of-function research, autonomous vehicles, geoengineering trials, vaccine mandates, AI model deployment, even fireworks bans. Someone imposes a risk on others without asking them, and the argument immediately collapses into shouting about the particular technology. What I want, before I cover the next one, is the general machinery. Write the desiderata a theory of permissible risk imposition without consent would have to meet to be adequate for evaluating real policy disputes. Specifically:

Give me the requirements list, with the case or consideration motivating each. At minimum, tell me which of the following distinctions a serious theory must preserve and which it can safely collapse: the difference between imposing a risk and merely failing to protect someone from a background risk; the difference between an activity that harms no one and an activity that exposes everyone to a small chance of catastrophe; the difference between risks borne by the very people who benefit and risks where the beneficiaries and the risk-bearers are disjoint populations (much of my pandemic-lab coverage turns on exactly this); and the difference between ex ante justification (the risk was reasonable to impose) and ex post luck (nothing bad happened). Flag any of these that are more contested than the literature admits.

I also want the theory's position on several pressure points, stated as requirements: whether there is any de minimis threshold below which consent is not required, and what stops that threshold from being gamed by splitting one large risk into many small impositions; whether expected-harm arithmetic is ever the right tool or whether the distribution across persons matters independently of the total; whether compensation after the fact can retroactively justify an imposition, or whether that confuses redress with permission; whether future people, who cannot consent by construction, make every long-horizon risk imposition a special case or whether the theory should handle them with its general machinery; and whether the fact that everyone imposes comparable risks on everyone else (driving being the canonical case) licenses an imposition that would be impermissible from a single party.

Mark which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which look like the signature commitments of particular camps (contractualists, expected-utility theorists, rights theorists), and identify any subset of the desiderata you believe cannot be jointly satisfied -- if the list itself contains a tension, that is the part of the column I most want to get right. Conclude by naming the single requirement that disqualifies the most positions people actually defend in public debate, and explain why those positions survive anyway. A table for the requirements is acceptable; prose for the analysis is not optional.
