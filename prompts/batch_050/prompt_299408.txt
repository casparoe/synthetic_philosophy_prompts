I am a retired biochemist who has, in recent years, been reading historical sociology with the habits of mind I acquired in the laboratory, and I should like help with a puzzle about Weber's Protestant ethic thesis. As I understand it, Weber proposed that the Calvinist emphasis on disciplined work, frugality, and worldly calling caused the emergence of modern capitalism. My instinct is to treat this as an ordinary causal hypothesis and ask how it has fared empirically — and on that score it seems to me the verdict has long since come in. The historically Protestant parts of Europe industrialized first and, on the whole, remain wealthier than the Catholic south; the same pattern repeats across the Atlantic, where the Protestant-majority United States outperformed Catholic-majority Latin America. When a variable predicts outcomes across so many independent cases, for over a century, I do not understand why historians and economists continue to treat the thesis as controversial, or why they keep reaching for rival explanations in terms of institutions, geography, or colonial extraction. In my former field, a hypothesis that survived this much testing would simply be promoted to textbook fact, as the germ theory of disease was. So my first question is: given that the cross-country data have confirmed Weber, on what grounds is the thesis still disputed rather than taught as settled?

And a second question follows from the first. If a particular religious culture is the decisive cause of economic development, then the policy conclusion seems inescapable, if impolite: development aid directed at infrastructure or governance is wasted effort unless it is accompanied by conversion of the underlying culture, or at least of the population's attitude toward work and saving. I take it nobody in development economics is willing to say this. Is that merely a diplomatic silence, or is there some principled reason why a confirmed cultural cause of capitalism would fail to license a cultural remedy?
