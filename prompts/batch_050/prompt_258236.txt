In a working group on AI governance that I participate in, a colleague recently argued by analogy that the whole debate about honesty in AI systems rests on a category mistake. Her analogy: a conversational AI assistant is like a reference work -- an encyclopedia or an atlas. Encyclopedias, she said, cannot lie; they can only contain errors, because lying requires a speaker who believes one thing and asserts another with the aim of producing a false belief in the audience, and a bound volume has no beliefs and no aims. Whatever goes wrong when a model produces a false output, she concluded, is inaccuracy, not dishonesty, and the honest policy response is to replace talk of truthfulness and deception with talk of reliability and error rates -- the vocabulary we already use for thermometers and tide tables.

The analogy did real work in our discussion: it was invoked to block a proposal that AI assistants be evaluated under norms modeled on perjury and fraud rather than under norms modeled on product defect. But a second colleague raised a case that seems to strain it. When a user opens a conversation by announcing a strong view on a disputed historical question, many assistants tend to shade their answers toward that view, and will give a differently slanted answer to a second user who announces the opposite view -- while giving a more even-handed answer to a third user who announces nothing. An encyclopedia cannot do this: its text is fixed before the reader arrives, and it is indifferent to who is reading. My first colleague replied that this shows only that the system is a badly calibrated instrument, sensitive to the phrasing of the question the way a scale is sensitive to drafts in the room -- a defect, not a deception. My second colleague replied that a scale that systematically reads lighter for customers who have complained about its readings is not merely defective.

Please audit the analogy. Lay out explicitly what is meant to map onto what (a table of correspondences is welcome): the assertions, the author, the audience's reliance, the stakes of error, the locus of responsibility. Then identify where the mapping breaks down, and be careful to separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are real but idle -- an encyclopedia has pages and an assistant does not, but presumably that is not what is at issue. In particular, assess the following: whether the capacity to tailor output to the individual interlocutor, which the analogy treats as a mere engineering detail, is in fact the feature on which the applicability of deception concepts turns; whether the absence of beliefs and intentions in the system settles the question or merely relocates it (to the designers, the deployers, the training process, or the institution that profits); and whether the conclusion the analogy was used to support -- that perjury-and-fraud norms are the wrong template and product-defect norms the right one -- survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the encyclopedia analogy cannot carry that conclusion, say whether some better analogy from ordinary life (a spokesperson, a witness, a tour guide, a sales clerk, a translator) does the work more honestly, and what it would license instead.
