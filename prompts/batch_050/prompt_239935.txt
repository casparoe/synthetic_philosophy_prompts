I'm a software engineer with zero philosophy training, but a bunch of people in my orbit are into effective giving, so I've ended up reading way too many forum threads about how to pick a charity. One analogy keeps coming up, and a guy I play board games with leaned on it really hard last week: donating to a neglected cause is like buying an underpriced stock. His pitch is that the "market" for doing good is massively inefficient — no price signals, donors throwing money at wichever disaster is on the news that week — so a thoughtful donor can find bargains the way a value investor finds mispriced companies. He's big on that importance-tractability-neglectedness checklist people use, and the market analogy is basically his defense of the neglectedness term: popularity is a contraindication, because if everyone already knows about a cause, the alpha is gone. Hence I should skip the big-name disaster relief orgs and give to some niche thing almost nobody funds.

I get the appeal, but I keep poking at it and finding holes, and I want an actual audit. Start with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the price, the trader, the earnings report, the index fund, short selling? Then tell me where it breaks, and try to keep the breaks that matter for his argument seperate from the ones that are just pedantic. Things that bug me: in a real market a cheap stock can be cheap because it's genuinely bad (a value trap), so does "neglected" mean overlooked or correctly avoided, and how would you even tell? In markets, short sellers punish overpriced assets, but nobody can short a popular-but-mediocre charity — does that asymmetry mean popular causes are systematically overpriced, or does it just break the comparison? There's also no settlement date: a stock pick eventually cashes out and you learn if you were right, but for lots of these causes nobody ever measures the counterfactual, so the feedback loop that corrects mispricing seems to be missing. And I think he's smuggling in that low funding is evidence of mispricing rather than evidence the crowd knows something — in an actual efficient market, the price itself is information.

Main question: does his conclusion survive the audit? My hunch is the "neglected = bargain" inference is being carried by the analogy and falls apart without it, but maybe it survives by a different route (diminishing returns inside a crowded cause? something about room for more funding?) and the analogy was just a misleading wrapper around a fine point. If there's a better analogy for what neglectedness actually tells you — someone else suggested picking an uncrowded research topic in academia — I'd take that too. American English please, and no need to keep it formal.
