So my roommate and I got into it over Mozi last week and he cornered me with an arguement I can't get out of. Context: in "Universal Love III" (the third of the 兼愛 chapters of the Mozi; I'm using W. P. Mei's translation), Mozi first says:

"If we should classify one by one all those who hate others and injure others, should we find them to be universal in love or partial? Of course we should say they are partial. Now, since partiality against one another is the cause of the major calamities in the empire, then partiality is wrong."

and then, when he explains why universal care would actually fix things:

"Now, when every one regards the states of others as he regards his own, who would attack the others' states? Others are regarded like self. When every one regards the capitals of others as he regards his own, who would seize the others' capitals? Others are regarded like self. When every one regards the houses of others as he regards his own, who would disturb the others' houses? Others are regarded like self."

My roommate's reconstruction:

(1) Mozi's test for whether a standard of care is right is purely downstream: adopt it, see if calamities or benefits follow.
(2) The mechanism in the quoted passage is: nobody attacks what they regard as their own. So universal care works by extending the circle of "one's own" to everything.
(3) But then the argument only ever condemns injuring others for the sake of one's own. It says nothing about how much positive care anyone owes anyone.
(4) A Confucian graded love -- intense for family, thinning out to strangers -- combined with a standing rule against profiting at another's expense would pass Mozi's test just as well: the calamities he lists (states attacking states, houses disturbing houses) all involve injury-for-gain, which that rule already forbids.
(5) So Mozi's argument establishes at most a duty of non-injury, not 兼愛, impartial care. Everything beyond non-injury is surplus the argument never earns.
(6) Worse: if "regards the states of others as he regards his own" is doing the work, it proves too much in the other direction. I govern, rearrange, take risks with, and sometimes neglect what is genuinely mine. So regarding your state as mine could license me to run your state the way I run mine -- benevolent annexation, not peace.
(7) So the passage either proves too little (mere non-aggression, which the Confucian gets for free) or too much (a license to absorb other people's affairs as your own). Either way the flagship argument for impartial care fails.

I hate this conclusion and I think it's wrong, because the passage obviously felt like a real argument when I read it. But I can't locate the bad step. My best guess is something slippery happens around (2) or (6) with what "regards as his own" means, but saying "that's an equivocation" isn't the same as showing it.

Tell me: which step do you reject? Why did it look fine? And what does rejecting it cost -- e.g., if you fix (2) by building in a psychology of what causes aggression, does Mozi's argument survive revenge, honor, and status motives, or only greed? Also, if it turns out the Confucian really can accept the repaired version, is that a refutation of Mozi or just a sign that the two were never as far apart as the rhetoric suggests? I'd like to keep (1) if at all possible -- the consequentialist test seems like the least controversial thing in the chapter -- so bonus points if the diagnosis doesn't force me to drop it.
