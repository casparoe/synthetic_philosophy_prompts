Stoics ancient and modern explain the dichotomy of control with the image of the archer: the archer should do everything in his power to aim well, but whether the arrow hits depends on wind and chance, so he should make his goal the shooting well, not the hitting. Modern popularizers of Stoicism use the same picture to argue that we should set goals only over things entirely up to us -- our efforts and judgments -- and treat outcomes like promotion, health, and other people's opinions as matters of indifference. Audit the analogy: lay out the correspondence table (what plays the role of the aiming, the wind, the target, the hit), identify where the mapping breaks (for instance, the archer is plainly permitted to prefer hitting, and good aiming is itself partly a matter of adjusting to feedback from misses), say what the image quietly imports that agency in life lacks, and assess whether the strong conclusion -- outcomes are wholly outside the scope of rational concern -- survives the disanalogies or was smuggled in by the picture. Give your response as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the correspondence table as item one, the two most consequential disanalogies as items two and three, the verdict on the strong conclusion as item four, and a better analogy or refined statement of the dichotomy as item five.
