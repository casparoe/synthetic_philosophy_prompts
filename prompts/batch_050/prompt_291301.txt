A colleague of mine works on the foundations of welfare economics, and over several years of conversation she has given me a consistent-seeming set of verdicts about when one person's good can be weighed against another's. I want to predict her view on a new case, and more importantly to understand what rule she is actually running.

Her verdicts, as I have recorded them:

1. She accepts that losing both legs is a greater loss than losing a fingertip, and that a health system may treat the first patient's misfortune as the larger one without apology.

2. She accepts that transferring a fixed sum from a billionaire to a destitute family increases aggregate welfare, even though no one can measure by how much, and even though the billionaire sincerely insists the loss stings terribly.

3. She refuses to say which of two strangers enjoyed a concert more, and refuses to let a second ticket be allocated on the basis of their answers to a detailed questionnaire about their enthusiasm. There is, she says, no usable fact of the matter.

4. She accepts that, other things equal, saving the twenty-five-year-old rather than the eighty-year-old in triage is justified partly because more life is gained for the person saved.

5. She refuses the claim that a severely depressed person's life contains less welfare than a contented person's life, at least in a case where both sincerely report equal life satisfaction. She says settling that would require knowing what each life is like from the inside, which no one can do.

Now the new case, which is about to become practical for her because she sits on a public health board. A preventive program is justified, in the proposal before the board, partly by the claim that it stops children being born with a congenital condition that leaves them with a permanently lower level of welfare than other children at the same level of material resources. The evidence offered is entirely third-personal: functional limitations, behavioral measures, sleep and pain markers, and the condition precludes reliable self-report, so nobody is asking the board to trust or distrust anyone's questionnaire. The claim is not about the size of a change or a gain; it is a comparison of where two lives sit.

What will she say? I can see at least two reconstructions of her record that pull apart here. On one, her principle is evidential: comparisons are legitimate when grounded in publicly observable facts about function, resources, and life, and illegitimate when they rest on unverifiable inner report -- which would license the board's claim. On another, her principle is structural: comparisons of changes, losses, and gains are legitimate, but comparisons of the overall levels at which different lives sit are not -- which would forbid it. Perhaps she is running some third rule I haven't seen. Please identify the candidate rules that fit all five verdicts, say what each predicts about the board's proposal, give me your best prediction with reasons, and describe the single further case -- a verdict I could elicit from her -- that would most efficiently discriminate among the remaining candidates.
