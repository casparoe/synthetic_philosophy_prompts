ok so I'm on the board of a small memorial museum and we've landed in the exact fight I hoped we'd avoid. We have limited floor space and budget, and we're covering several mass atrocities -- the transatlantic slave trade, the Holocaust, the Holodomor, the Khmer Rouge genocide, a couple others. A consultant has pitched us a "gravity index": a composite score built from death toll (as a share of the population at risk), duration, cruelty of methods, and documented intergenerational effects, and suggested we size the exhibits proportionaly to the scores. Half the board thinks this is just what responsible curation requires -- you can't allocate anything without comparing, and refusing to rank is itself a covert ranking (equal space IS a verdict). The other half thinks the index is obscene: it turns the dead into units, pretends incommensurable suffering is commensurable, and will inevitably launder the consultants' own biases into fake precision.

Write me a dialogue between two board members, one defending the index, one against it. Neither should be a fool: the index-defender has to actually grapple with the objection that a number on a wall does something different to visitors than a name or a face, and the anti-ranking one has to answer the point that "no comparisons" is impossible once you have one building and finite money -- somebody's suffering is getting ten square meters either way, so the question is only whether the criterion is explicit or hidden. They should respond to what the other actually says, not give parallel speeches.

And commit to something. I don't want the dialogue to end with them agreeing to disagree or with a mushy "both have a point." Have the argument genuinely go somewhere -- one of them concedes something real, or they land on a specific concrete decision for the museum (adopt the index, scrap it, or some definite third option that isn't just splitting the difference) -- and make it clear which position you think actually survives the exchange, with the reasons doing the work rather than the verdict coming out of nowhere.
