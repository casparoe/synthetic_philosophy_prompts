So I made a mistake and volunteered to give a 20-minute talk at my company's internal "ethics in tech" meetup next month. I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background at all, and this started because I was arguing with some friends about moderating open source projects and I fell down a rabbit hole on this sociological framework about moral cultures: honor cultures, where your reputation is everything and you respond to slights yourself because backing down costs you status; dignity cultures, where everyone's worth is treated as inherent, insults aren't worth reacting to, and serious conflicts go to official third parties like courts or HR; and victimhood cultures, where people appeal publicly to third parties by emphasizing their victim status to rally support. My friends basically all assumed dignity culture is obviously the most morally advanced of the three and honor culture is just macho leftovers. I'm taking the spicy position in my talk that they're wrong, at least in our world: in pseudonymous, distributed online communities (open source, Discord servers, whatever), third-party enforcement is weak or nonexistent, so something like honor norms actually does load-bearing work — maintainers' public reputations function kind of like escrow in a contract, and communities that try to run purely on "just report it to the code of conduct committee" collapse into either flame wars or callout dynamics. So my thesis is that a partial, deliberate revival of honor-culture norms can be morally *superior* to pure dignity culture in contexts with weak institutions, and that treating dignity culture as the default enlightened answer is a mistake engineers should stop importing into community design.

Here's who I'm presenting to: about 40 people, mostly software engineers of the skeptical-but-not-well-read-in-sociology type, a few designers, one UX researcher who used to be an academic sociologist (this person scares me the most), two HR folks, and a handful of coworkers who are pretty activist and will hear "honor culture" and immediately think toxic masculinity and dueling. Prepare my Q&A defense: the objections I'll actually get from *this* audience, not from professional philosophers, phrased the way these people would actually put them (the HR person will not talk like the sociologist), ordered roughly by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do to my thesis. For each one give me a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, plus a note on where that reply is soft.
