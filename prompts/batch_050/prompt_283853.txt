Next month I'm giving the toast at the wedding of my two closest friends from graduate school, who met — I promise this is true — in a seminar on G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica, arguing about his chapter on the ideal. She went on to write a dissertation attacking Moore's method of isolation; he defends a broadly Moorean picture of intrinsic value to this day. The reception will be split roughly evenly between their philosophy colleagues, who know the book cold and will notice every slip, and relatives who have never heard of Moore and simply want to hear that these two people love each other.

I want the toast, about five minutes read aloud, to be built seriously out of Moore's two great goods rather than merely decorated with them: the couple chose each other partly over these ideas, so the speech's architecture should be Moorean. Moore holds that the value of a whole need not equal the sum of the values of its parts, and I want that principle of organic unities to be what lets me say, precisely and without gushing, why this marriage is something more than two already-excellent people happening to share an address. I also want the guests to have genuine work in the toast: on Moore's view, beauty that no one ever beholds is worth very little compared with beauty consciously appreciated, so the friends and family in the room are not spectators at a performance of this good but part of what makes the occasion valuable.

Two constraints. First, the philosophers present will pounce on anything a careful reader of Principia Ethica would flag: do not call Moore a hedonist (he thinks the pleasures of affection and aesthetic enjoyment involve cognition of their objects and are the greatest goods partly for that reason), and do not have me praise the couple in the abstract, since Moore thinks affection is most valuable when directed at qualities that are genuinely admirable and accompanied by true belief — the compliments should be specific, true, and earned, not ceremonial flattery. Second, because she built her career objecting to the method of isolation, the toast cannot treat Moore as settled authority; please work her dissent in affectionately, so that the story of two people falling in love while disagreeing about whether value can be isolated lands as an in-joke for the philosophers and still reads as charming to everyone else. One good joke about the attempt to define love running aground on Moore's claim that the basic value-concept is simple and indefinable, like a color, would be very welcome.
