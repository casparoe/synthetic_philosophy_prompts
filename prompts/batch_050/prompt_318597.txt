ok so for a reading group on trolley problems + the doctrine of double effect I have to write down my positions in advance so everyone can poke holes, and I'd rather find the holes first. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. The DDE is basically right: intending harm (as an end or a means) is forbidden in a way that merely foreseeing the same harm isn't, at least when the harm is proportionate. The bombing contrast is my paradigm -- terror bombing is impermissible, tactical bombing with the same number of foreseen civilian deaths can be permissible.

2. Plain switch case: you may turn the trolley away from the five toward the one.

3. Footbridge: you may not push the guy. And I don't think this is about the squicky up-close-and-personal aspect -- if you could drop him onto the track by pressing a button that opens a trapdoor under him, thats equally wrong.

4. Transplant: the surgeon obviously may not carve up the one healthy patient to save the five.

5. What explains 2-4 is the means principle: footbridge and transplant are wrong because the one is used as a means to saving the five, while in switch the one's death is just a side effect of redirecting the threat. I take it the means principle and the DDE are basically the same insight stated two ways -- intending/using the person is out, foreseen side effects can be in.

6. The loop variant (the side track loops back around toward the five, and the only reason the trolley stops short of them is that the one person on the side track is heavy enough to halt it): you may still turn. Whether the track happens to connect back around is morally irrelevant geography; it's just switch with extra track.

7. I'm not a consequentialist, I don't think rightness reduces to outcomes -- but numbers still count. In the scarce drug case (one patient needs the whole dose, five patients each need a fifth of it) you give it to the five, and in rescue cases generally you save the greater number, other things equal.

8. I also think the doing/allowing distinction is real: killing is harder to justify than letting die, other things equal.
