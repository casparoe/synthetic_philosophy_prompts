Last night a friend and I argued past midnight about whether the brain is a computer, and about whether running the right computation, on whatever substrate, suffices for a mind. Midway through I began testing her with cases, and she gave four verdicts I wrote down:

(i) A patient with a neurodegenerative disease has his neurons replaced one at a time with synthetic units that reproduce each neuron's input-output behavior exactly, until nothing biological remains. She held that he is conscious throughout, and that the finished, fully synthetic brain is as minded as the original.

(ii) An English speaker who knows no Chinese is sealed in a room with an enormous rulebook correlating incoming Chinese symbols with outgoing ones; by following it mechanically he produces fluent written Chinese conversation. She held that nothing in the scenario -- not the man, not the room, not the man-plus-book considered as a system -- understands Chinese.

(iii) A machine stores an astronomically large but finite table mapping every possible conversational history onto a prerecorded response, and so passes any spoken examination. She held that it is not minded, whatever its performance.

(iv) A warehouse-sized device built from water pipes and valves is engineered so that its switching states undergo, transition for transition, the same counterfactual-supporting pattern as a laptop running a chess engine. She held that it genuinely computes chess moves -- that a person playing against it is playing against the machine.

We then reached the case we could not settle. A billion volunteers, each briefed with one simple rule and equipped with a radio, are each assigned the role of a single neuron in the connectome of a particular brain -- say, hers -- during one conscious afternoon. For an hour they relay signals to one another exactly as those neurons did, so that the crowd instantiates, person by person and message by message, the same causal transition structure her brain instantiated that afternoon. Is there, during that hour, a second instance of her mind -- a mind belonging to the crowd, over and above the volunteers' own minds?

Please do the following. First, identify the candidate principles consistent with all four of her earlier verdicts, and show where at least two such principles diverge on the crowd case. Second, predict her verdict, giving reasons drawn from the pattern of her commitments, and name which earlier verdict is the closest analog to the crowd case. Third, if her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so plainly and describe the further thought experiment whose verdict would force a choice between the competing principles.
