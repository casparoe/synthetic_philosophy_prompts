Last night a friend and I argued for hours about the color phi phenomenon, and we want a particular kind of arbiter. The setup: a red spot is flashed briefly on one side of a screen, then a green spot on the other side, and subjects report seeing a single spot move across the screen, changing from red to green around the midpoint — even though the second spot had not yet appeared when the first was extinguished, so the brain could not have known its color or location in advance. My friend defends what Dennett calls the Orwellian reading: the subject really does experience the first spot, and memory is rewritten afterward so that the motion-and-color-change narrative overwrites the record. I defend the Stalinesque reading: nothing is experienced until the brain has registered both flashes and staged the whole fabricated sequence, like evidence prepared in advance of a show trial.

We both know that Dennett and Kinsbourne, in Time and the Observer: the Where and When of Consciousness in the Brain (1992), argue that once the relevant events are compressed into a short enough window, there is no longer a fact of the matter as to which of us is right — that the distinction between revising content before it becomes conscious and revising it after outruns anything in the subject's perspective or in any possible evidence, and should be dissolved rather than adjudicated. Neither of us is yet willing to concede that.
