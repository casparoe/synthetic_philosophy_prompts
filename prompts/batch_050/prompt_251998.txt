I keep thinking about political lying and I can't figure out my own position, so here are my gut verdicts on some cases -- tell me what principle unifies them, if anything does. (Arendt's essay on lying in politics is floating around in the back of my head but I don't think she answers my actual question.)

1. A mayor running for reelection announces crime is down 15% using a massaged stat she knows is misleading. Not fine, easy one.
2. A diplomat publicly denies that back-channel talks with an enemy state are happening, because admitting it would blow up the talks and maybe the ceasefire. Fine, I think?
3. A president conceals a serious but manageable illness from the public while in office. Fine, or at least defensible.
4. During a bank panic, a finance minister goes on TV and swears the major banks are solvent when she knows at least one isn't, because the truth would trigger the very collapse she's trying to prevent. I go back and forth on this one, and the fact that I can't just call it case 1 all over again bugs me.
5. Officials internally cook optimistic numbers about an unwinnable war and come to half-believe their own reports. Somehow this feels worse than case 1, even though less directly deceptive.
6. A whistleblower leaks real documents proving the government lied in case 1 -- but the leaker also lies to his own colleagues to get access. The leak was right, the lying to get it still feels wrong.

Here's my problem: 2, 3, and 4 all look like "lying to prevent a worse outcome," same as 1 arguably is, but my verdicts split. And 5 seems bad for reasons that have nothing to do with the public being deceived. Is there a principle that captures most of this -- something about who the lie protects, or about self-deception vs. deception, or about democratic accountability -- and which of my verdicts does the cleanest version force me to give up? Also tell me what it says about a case I haven't listed, like a campaign lying about a candidate's crowd sizes purely for morale.
