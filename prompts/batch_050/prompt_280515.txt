I'm a high school senior writing my final civics paper on political obligation, and I've run into a problem with the standard debate as I understand it. Every major account of why we ought to obey the law — consent, fair play, natural duty, gratitude, associative ties — seems designed with adults in mind: people who could vote, chose to stay, and accepted benefits they were free to refuse. None of that describes me. I have never consented to anything, I cannot vote, and the main benefit I've received, my education, was legally compulsory, so it's unclear whether I "accepted" it in any sense that could ground a duty. Yet minors are punished for truancy, taxed on part-time wages without representation, and disciplined by schools for joining walkouts, so for us this is not an abstract question.

What I'd like from you is a defended ranking of the open questions in this area that matter most specifically for the case of minors, which is where I want my paper to make its small contribution. I'm currently torn between candidates such as: whether benefits one was legally forced to receive can generate fair-play obligations; whether the inability to vote dissolves political obligation for minors or merely weakens it; whether the classic condition that civil disobedients accept the consequences of their lawbreaking still makes sense when, for a teenager, those consequences are parental discipline and a school record rather than criminal punishment; and whether political obligation arrives all at once at eighteen or accrues gradually, and which picture is more coherent. For each question in your ranking, I want the reasoning, not just the placement: why it is upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is for a one-term paper rather than a dissertation, and how much its answer would actually change what a minor ought to do, since a question can be philosophically deep and practically idle. If one of my four candidates strikes you as overrated by those criteria, or if a question I've missed deserves to displace one, say so as part of the ranking.

Please give your answer as exactly five numbered items — your ranked questions — each in one or two sentences that defend its position, with no introduction and no conclusion.
