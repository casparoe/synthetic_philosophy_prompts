I'm preparing a graduate seminar on Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life, and I want a handout case that will break my students of a lazy reading they've fallen into: that monumental history is the inspiring mode, antiquarian history the stodgy preservative one, and critical history the dangerous one. Acting as a specialist in nineteenth-century German philosophy who is also an experienced designer of philosophical thought experiments, please construct a thought experiment that reverses the usual assignment of benefits and harms: a case in which the antiquarian relationship to the past -- the reverent preservation and cultivation of what is old -- becomes the genuinely life-furthering, even subversive force in the story, while the monumental mode -- the celebration of past greatness as a model for action -- does the damage Nietzsche more typically attributes to antiquarian excess, weighing down and mummifying the present. Constraints: the setting must be realistic and contemporary (a city, a profession, an artistic tradition, an institution -- not a fantasy kingdom); the scenario itself must be statable in one paragraph; no character may be simply mistaken about the historical facts, so the reversal must arise from the structure of the situation rather than from anyone's ignorance or propaganda; and the case must operate within Nietzsche's own criterion -- history is to be judged by whether it serves life -- rather than rejecting that criterion as misguided.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specifications: walk through how each of the three modes functions in the case and why monumental and antiquarian history deliver their reversed verdicts; explain where critical history fits and why the case does not merely collapse into critical history wearing an antiquarian costume (this is the most obvious objection, so take it seriously); identify which single feature of the scenario is load-bearing for the reversal and what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed; and finally give the strongest counterattack available to a reader who wants to defend Nietzsche's original ordering against your case. Think carefully and brainstorm several candidate settings internally before committing to the one that most cleanly produces the reversal. Present your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items -- (1) the scenario, (2) the analysis of monumental history's harm, (3) the analysis of antiquarian history's benefit, (4) the treatment of critical history and the collapse objection, and (5) the load-bearing feature, the removal test, and the counterattack -- each item two to four sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
