Teaching my intro course again this fall and I ran my usual sorites demo -- slides of a sand pile, remove one grain per slide, show of hands after each: still a heap? Something keeps happening that I don't think has a name, or if it does I don't know it, and I've started wondering whether it's philosophically important or just a classroom artifact.

Run the demo downward (start with a clear heap, remove grains): the class keeps voting "heap" well past piles that, shown cold on the first slide, they'd all call "not a heap." Run it upward (three grains, add one at a time): they keep voting "not a heap" past piles they'd call heaps if shown cold. Fine, so far that's old news, and I suspect the direction-dependence bit on its own might even have a name in the psych literature somewhere.

Here's the part I can't find anywhere. Next term I told them in advance. Explained the whole thing, showed last year's data, told them exactly where the drift would happen, and asked them to pre-commit: "write down, right now, the grain count where you'll switch your vote." Then I ran the demo. The drift happened anyway, and -- this is the weird part -- it overrode their own pre-commitments. People sailed straight past the number they'd written down forty minutes earlier, and afterward most couldn't say why. It wasn't forgetting; we had the cards. They'd say things like "I know I wrote 200, but at 200 it still looked like a heap, what was I supposed to say?"

So the phenomenon: a judgment pattern in a sorites series that (a) depends on the direction/history of presentation, (b) survives full advance knowledge of itself, and (c) overrides the subject's own stated policy, all without the subject experiencing any single step as an error.

Is there an established term for this? I've checked the obvious neighbors. It's not just the sorites paradox itself -- the paradox is about the argument; this is about what people do and apparently can't stop doing. It's not quite the "forced march" thing either, since nobody's forcing anything and they answer happily at every step. And the standard solutions -- epistemicism, supervaluationism, degree theories -- all seem silent on why knowing about the effect in advance doesn't immunize you, which strikes me as potentially embarassing for some of them (if vague predicates have an unknowable sharp boundary, why can't subjects just use their own pre-committed one? they literally put it there!).

If there's a name, I want it plus a precise statement of how the named thing differs from what I described. If there isn't, coin one and define it, and then tell me: which standard solution does this put the most pressure on, and could you design a version of the demo where it wouldn't occur?
