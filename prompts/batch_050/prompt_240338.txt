The exam question (worth 25 points; students had 30 minutes and had read the article in advance):

In "Should the Numbers Count?", John Taurek describes a case in which you have a supply of a drug sufficient to save either five people on one island or one person, David, on another; the drug cannot be divided and you cannot reach both islands. (a) Reconstruct Taurek's argument for the conclusion that you are not morally required to save the five. (b) Explain why Taurek thinks that flipping a coin is a fair way of deciding, and what, on his view, David is and is not in a position to complain about. (c) Does Taurek's position commit him to the claim that the number of people at stake is never morally relevant in such choices? Briefly defend your answer.

The student's answer:

(a) Taurek's argument begins from the thought that losses to different people do not add up into a single, bigger loss. If the five die, no one undergoes a loss five times as great as the loss David undergoes if he dies; each of the six loses his life, and that is the worst thing that can happen to each of them. Because the death of the five is not worse for anyone than the death of David is for David, Taurek concludes that the outcome in which the five die is not worse than the outcome in which David dies, and hence that there is no obligation to bring about the latter rather than the former. Fairness therefore requires giving David an equal chance — flipping a coin — and anyone who simply saves the five without doing so treats David as counting for less than the others and thereby acts wrongly.

(b) David is in a position to complain if I simply hand the drug to the five as a matter of course, since that procedure gives him no chance at all and so fails to respect the equal significance of his life. He is not in a position to complain about the coin flip itself, since under that procedure he receives the same 50 percent chance of surviving that each of the five receives; if the coin comes down against him he has been treated fairly even though the result is bad for him. Taurek is careful to note that things would be different if I stood in some special relation to David — if he were my friend or my child, say — in which case I could permissibly save him outright.

(c) Yes. The non-additivity of losses holds however many people are involved — a million deaths is still not a loss that anyone experiences as a million times worse than one death — so Taurek is committed to giving the one an equal chance against any number, and to the claim that the number of people at stake is never morally relevant. Parfit's well-known reply in "Innumerate Ethics" accepts this framework but argues that fairness instead requires a weighted lottery: each person should receive an equal individual chance, so that the five collectively receive a five-sixths chance, and this is the most that fairness can demand.
