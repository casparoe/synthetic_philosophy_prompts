I recently joined the infrastructure committee of my coastal city's council, and our first major project is evaluating a storm-surge barrier engineered to protect the city for at least 150 years. When the consultants ran the cost-benefit analysis, the entire recommendation flipped on a single input: the discount rate. At roughly 5 percent the barrier fails the test decisively; at roughly 1.5 percent it passes easily, because nearly all of its benefits arrive after 2080. That experience sent me to the most famous real-world instance of this fight: the dispute over the 2006 Stern Review on the economics of climate change. As I understand the episode, Stern's team worked within the standard Ramsey framework, decomposing the discount rate into a pure rate of time preference, a term for how much extra consumption matters to richer future people, and expected consumption growth; they set pure time preference at about 0.1 percent per year (justified only by a small annual probability of human extinction), which yielded an overall discount rate near 1.4 percent and a recommendation for aggressive, immediate emissions cuts. William Nordhaus objected that this was far below the rates people reveal in actual markets, and his own modeling, with pure time preference around 1.5 percent and parameters calibrated to observed saving and returns, produced a rate of roughly 5.5 percent and a much more gradual policy ramp. Critics such as Partha Dasgupta argued the real problem was elsewhere in Stern's parameters, and others, notably Martin Weitzman, argued that deep uncertainty about catastrophic outcomes matters more than any of these point estimates.

I'd like you to analyze this episode as a case study in the theory of pure time preference, playing the role of a philosopher of economics with expertise in intergenerational ethics and welfare economics. Think carefully about the mapping before committing to conclusions. Specifically: first, reconstruct what each side's chosen pure rate of time preference actually commits them to normatively, including the awkward implications of each (Stern's near-zero rate famously implies enormous sacrifices for future generations in other contexts; Nordhaus's market-matching approach seems to let the consumption habits of the present dictatorially settle the claims of the future). Second, determine how much of the substantive disagreement is genuinely about pure time preference and how much is smuggled into the other parameters — whether the dispute everyone describes as ethical is partly a technical dispute about the inequality-aversion term and growth assumptions. Third, assess what the episode shows in the other direction: does it vindicate the prescriptive position that discounting welfare merely because it comes later is ethically groundless, or does it instead reveal that pure time preference does less real work than advertised, since Stern-like conclusions can be reached through uncertainty, declining discount rates, or catastrophe aversion without taking any stand on whether later welfare counts for less? Finally, say plainly where the theory is silent — for instance, on non-marginal damages, on whether future people being richer settles anything, and on what the framework cannot tell my committee.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — a one-sentence verdict on what the Stern–Nordhaus dispute actually demonstrates about pure time preference — and then develop the reasoning. No preamble and no restating of the question.
