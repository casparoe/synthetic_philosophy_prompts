He's ML-adjacent and his line was that Goodhart's law is basicly a triviality: of course a proxy degrades once you optimise against it hard enough, and the whole field just needs to engineer better proxies. My line was that nobody has a theory of this at all — we have a slogan plus a zoo of anecdotes (Soviet nail quotas, cobra bounties, grade inflation, RL agents that learn to pause the game so they can never lose) and no account of when and why a proxy breaks rather than holds. He said fine, then describe what such a theory would even have to do before you go round complaining nobody's built one. Touché, and now I'm stuck, so I'm outsourcing.

So: write the desiderata for a theory of when measurement goes wrong under optimisation pressure — good enough, say, to tell a regulator or an ML team whether a metric they currently rely on is safe to keep using, not just good enough for a blog post. What cases must it get right? I particularly want it to handle the failures AND the non-failures: pilots put enormous selection pressure on altimeters and altimeters stay trustworthy, while exam results collapse as indicators the moment schools get ranked on them, and any theory that only explains the dramatic failures is just curve-fitting the anecdotes. What distinctions must it respect — is there a real difference between the measure drifting away from the goal, the optimiser learning to exploit the measure, and the measurement process itself corrupting the goal? Must it explain why piling on more metrics sometimes helps and sometimes just hands the optimiser extra degrees of freedom? Must it cover self-inflicted cases (someone who ends up living for their step count) as well as externally imposed ones, or is that a nice-to-have? For each desideratum give the case or consideration motivating it, mark which you'd call non-negotiable versus the preference of one camp (statisticians, causal modellers, the AI safety lot), and flag any subset you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable — my hunch is that "works uniformly across humans, institutions and algorithms" fights with "says something actionable rather than merely taxonomic", but I could well be wrong.
