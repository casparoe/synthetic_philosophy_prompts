ok so i'm leading a session in our ethics reading group on moral worth and the whole "acting for the right reasons" debate, and before i get shredded i want to check that the view i've landed on is even internally consistent. roughly here's what i think:

1. No moral credit for accidentally or purely self-interestedly right action. The shopkeeper who gives correct change only because honesty is good for business does the right thing, and it has zero moral worth. Worth tracks why you acted, not what you did.

2. It's the motive the agent actually acted on that matters, full stop. What someone *would* do in other circumstances — wether they'd still help if their sympathy dried up, say — is at most evidence about their actual motive, never part of what makes the action worthy in itself.

3. Moral worth comes from being moved by the features that actually make the action right — the right-making considerations themselves have to be what moves you, not just the abstract thought "this is the right thing to do."

4. Huck Finn helping Jim escape has real moral worth, even though Huck believes his duty is to turn Jim in and feels terrible about himself the whole time. He's responding to Jim as a person, and that's enough.

5. The cold misanthrope who helps someone in need purely because it's his duty, while feeling nothing for the person at all, does something with genuine moral worth — arguably *more* worth than the warm sympathetic helper.

6. Overdetermination doesn't dilute: if someone helps both from duty and from sympathy, the worth is intact.

7. Since worth is about the agent's relation to their reasons, two people can perform the exact same outward act and differ completely in moral worth just because of why they did it.

which of these are actually in tension and which only look like they are? i honestly can't tell which ones are pulling against each other, so don't just tell me the set feels Kantian-ish or whatever. rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each one say specifically what has to give and what it costs to give it up. and if a pair looks contradictory but isn't, say why it dissolves.

give me the audit in prose first, then a summary table with these columns: "commitments involved" (the numbers), "genuine or apparent", "severity" (high/medium/low), "cheapest fix" (which commitment gets revised or dropped and how), and "cost of the fix" (what you lose by making that move).
