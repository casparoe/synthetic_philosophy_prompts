So my friend and I got into it for like two hours last night (it started over whether a goalie "caused" a goal by not diving, don't ask) about whether doing nothing can ever be a cause of anything. His line is that only actual events cause stuff -- absences are just the world not happening -- and I kept throwing cases at him until we both lost track of which side we were on. So let me just dump my gut verdicts on the cases we argued about and see if there's a principle hiding in them, because right now I suspect I'm contradicting myself.

Case 1: my roommate goes away for a week, makes me promise to water her plant, I completely forget, it dies of thirst. Obvious to me: my failure caused its death, and I owe her a plant. Case 2: during that same week, roughly every other human on earth also failed to water that plant -- including some guy in Lisbon who has never heard of it -- and I absolutely refuse to say their failures caused its death. Case 3: a doctor has the antidote on the shelf, the patient is dying of the sting, the doctor just watches the clock and does nothing. Caused the death, full stop, feels no different from poisoning. Case 4: I could donate $50 to a charity that would very likely save a stranger's life, I don't. My friend says this is structurally identical to the doctor and I'm a cause of that death if the doctor is a cause of his patient's. I hate this conclusion and also can't say what breaks the analogy -- "proximity" feels like cheating. Case 5: my alarm doesn't go off (I forgot to set it), I oversleep, I miss my flight, the flight crashes. I find myself saying "my forgetting saved my life" -- i.e. my omission caused me to survive -- and I can't tell if that's a real causal claim or just a manner of speaking. Case 6 (the one that wrecked us): same as case 1, except it turns out the plant had root fungus and would have died even if I'd watered it perfectly. Counterfactually my forgetting changed nothing. And yet I still feel like I killed it -- if I'd confessed to her I'd say "I killed your plant," not "I coincidentally neglected it." Friend says I have to give this one up if I believe in any counterfactual-flavored account. Case 7: two lifeguards at a beach, one on shift, one off shift napping in the chair next to him; a swimmer drowns in front of both. The on-duty guard's doing-nothing caused the death; the napping one's didn't. But the only difference between them is a duty roster, and it feels bizarre that a piece of paper in the office determines what's a cause out in the water.

So: is there one principle that delivers all seven of these, or do I have to drop some? I want the principle stated precisely enough that I can check it -- if it leans on norms or expectations ("the failure of what normally happens"), explain how that handles case 2 and case 7 without just re-describing my intuitions back at me. If it leans on counterfactuals, tell me honestly that case 6 has to go (and maybe case 5?) and why that's fine. And then apply whatever you land on to two cases I haven't thought about: a software safety system that doesn't trigger because a sensor was never installed, and a city mayor who doesn't order an evacuation before a hurricane. Also, if the honest answer is that my verdicts in cases 3 and 4 genuinely stand or fall together, just tell me that -- my friend would love it.
