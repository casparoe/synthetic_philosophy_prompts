You are a professional decision theorist with deep expertise in Newcomb-style problems and in the differences among the major families of decision theory — causal, evidential, and the more recent logical or policy-based approaches. I would like you to think this through carefully, and I ask that you begin by brainstorming the candidate theories before committing to any prediction.

A friend of mine has a settled track record of verdicts on decision problems, and I want to predict how she will judge a new case she has not yet considered. Her earlier verdicts are these. First, on the standard Newcomb problem with a highly reliable predictor, she one-boxes without hesitation; her rationale is that the people who walk away rich are the one-boxers. Second, in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against an exact copy of herself, where she is certain the copy is reasoning identically, she cooperates; she says that whatever she ends up doing, the copy is doing it too, so the real question is which joint outcome she is selecting. Third, in a Newcomb variant where the being filling the boxes is known to decide purely by coin flip, so that the contents of the big box bear no relation to her choice, she takes both boxes, on the grounds that in this version nothing about her choice has any bearing on what is in the box. Fourth, in a one-shot anonymous prisoner's dilemma against a stranger whose reasoning has no connection to hers, she defects, saying there is nothing linking the two decisions in that case.

Here is the new case. The setup is Newcomb's, except that the boxes are made of glass and the prediction has already been made. She can see with her own eyes that the big box is empty. She must now choose between taking only the big box, which she knows contains nothing, and taking both boxes, which nets her the thousand dollars in the small one. Predict her verdict, and defend the prediction. In doing so, please identify which decision-theoretic rules are jointly consistent with all four of her earlier verdicts — I suspect more than one survives — and state what each surviving rule predicts about the transparent empty-box case, since at least two of them should diverge there. Give me your best guess as to which rule she is implicitly running, with your reasons for preferring it, and say whether her stated rationales in the earlier cases support that guess or cut against it. Finally, describe one further case, not among the ones above, whose verdict would discriminate between whichever rules remain live after the transparent-box case is decided.

Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
