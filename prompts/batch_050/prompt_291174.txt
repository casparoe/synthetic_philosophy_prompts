Write a short story related to the "afterlife conjecture" — philosopher Samuel Scheffler's idea (from his book Death and the Afterlife) that most of what we care about secretly depends on believing that humanity will carry on after we personally die. His claim, roughly, is that if we knew the species would end shortly after our own deaths, huge swaths of human activity — curing diseases, preserving artworks, scientific research with long horizons, even conservation — would lose their point, even though our own lives wouldn't be cut short at all.

Here's the setup I want you to use: astronomers announce that a sterilizing gamma-ray burst will reach Earth in about forty years. Nobody dies early, cities aren't destroyed, everyone alive gets to live out a more or less natural lifespan — but conception becomes impossible everywhere after the wave hits, so the youngest humans ever are already alive. (If you'd rather do it as "everyone wakes up sterile one morning," that works too, pick whichever you prefer.)

You are an award-winning short story writer who has also read your share of philosophy, so think carefully before writing. The story should dramatize the distinction the conjecture draws: some projects collapse without a future humanity, while others arguably don't (friendship, a good meal, music, a walk). But please don't have characters lecture each other about this — show it through what people quietly stop doing, keep doing, or suddenly start doing. Also include at least one character whose reaction complicates the conjecture: someone whose committments survive the news intact, or who turns out to have never been motivated by posterity in the first place. That tension is the interesting part.

Before writing the story, spend one short paragraph brainstorming: list three everyday activities that plausibly depend on humanity having a future and three that don't, and say which one from each list you'll weave into the plot. Then write the story itself, roughly 800–1200 words. No moral at the end, and please avoid making the last line a tidy summary of the theme — trust the reader to get it.
