I facilitate a small moral-philosophy discussion group (mixed backgrounds, no one with formal training beyond an intro course), and we've arrived at the question of whether, and how, the numbers should count in rescue choices. Our last session dissolved because several members insisted they all held the same view -- "obviously you save the five" -- until we started varying the details of the cases (what if the one is your spouse? what if the five would each lose a hand rather than die? what if helping the larger group is only likely to work?), at which point the supposed consensus fragmented and nobody could articulate the principle they'd actually been applying. I'd like to turn this mess into a decision-tree handout for next session: a flowchart that starts from a neutral first choice and walks each member, through their own answers to concrete scenarios, to a named position in this debate. Aim for six to eight decision points and roughly five to seven leaves.

Each decision point must be a concrete rescue scenario with two or three answer options, phrased so that a member can answer without knowing any theory vocabulary -- no question should ask anything like "do you believe in aggregation?" The leaves must include at least: straightforward aggregation (always save the greater number, stakes permitting); the Taurek-style view on which, when you cannot save everyone, each person gets an equal chance, so you might flip a coin between one and five; a proportional or weighted-lottery view that gives each person a chance but weights by numbers; a view on which individual claims are weighed by their strength and numbers enter only to break ties between the strongest competing claims; and a view on which special relationships legitimately let the chooser save the one over the five. The tree must route members through at least these scenarios, chosen so the positions genuinely come apart on them: one stranger versus five strangers; the chooser's close friend or partner versus five strangers; one death versus several serious but non-fatal harms (this one should expose whether someone aggregates within a life, across lives, or not at all); and a case with uncertainty, where one option saves one person for sure and the other has, say, a fifty percent chance of saving six. If your six-to-eight questions cannot cleanly separate two of the required leaves with the given scenarios, say so explicitly and explain what further case would be needed, rather than pretending the tree works.

Before drawing the tree itself, please first lay out the logical space it will be built from: list the diagnostic questions, the combinations of answers they permit, and then locate the actual positions within that space. Note any unoccupied combinations and briefly diagnose each -- is that region empty because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? End each leaf with a two-or-three sentence description of the position in plain language that a member who landed there would recognize as their own, plus one sentence on the standard pressure point for that position, so the handout doubles as a map of where the real disagreements are. Please don't include any readings or citations; I want the handout to be fully self-contained.
