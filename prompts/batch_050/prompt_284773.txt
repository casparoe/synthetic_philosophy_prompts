For the interview segment of the science-and-ideas magazine where I work as a staff writer, I am preparing a conversation with a metaphysician who has spent her career defending David Lewis's counterpart theory against the more familiar Kripkean picture of transworld identity, on which a possibility for a person is always a possibility for that very individual rather than for a merely similar stand-in located in some other possible world. My last outing in this territory, an interview with a physicist sympathetic to the many-worlds interpretation, drew reader letters complaining that I had let the guest deliver his sales pitch uninterrupted, so this time my editor wants a conversation that presses the position exactly where specialists press it, while remaining intelligible to an audience that has never opened a metaphysics journal.

Draft seven to nine questions, each accompanied by a brief note on the answer I should expect and a follow-up keyed to that answer. I want at least one question on the objection that counterpart theory gets the subject matter of modal talk wrong — that when we say a losing candidate might have won, we take ourselves to be talking about that candidate, not about a qualitative duplicate in a remote world — with a follow-up testing whether first-personal modal emotions like regret and relief survive her reply. At least one should take up Lewis's argument from accidental intrinsics against transworld identity and push her to justify the asymmetry she inherits: persons persist through time by having temporal parts, but nothing persists across worlds, even though the underlying puzzle about intrinsic change looks structurally identical in the two cases. At least one should probe the consequence that essences become context-dependent once the counterpart relation is a matter of contextually salient similarity — whether she takes that to be a discovery about modality or a reductio of the framework. And one should ask why the analysis should not simply be rerun with abstract ersatz worlds in place of Lewis's concrete ones, given that the ontology is what most readers will balk at. The closing question may be looser — what, if anything, hangs on the dispute outside metaphysics — but the rest should make her earn the view rather than recite it, and none of them should presuppose that my readers already know the literature's vocabulary.
