I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm finishing the closing essay of a book on coordination failures — the Moloch pattern, multipolar traps, races to the bottom across the attention economy, AI development, sport doping, and so on. My editor's note on the draft says the chapters don't add up to a coherent worldview, so before I revise I want an audit of the positions the essay actually commits me to. As I currently hold them:

1. Structural diagnosis. These dynamics are genuine multipolar traps: each actor's move is locally rational and the aggregate outcome is collectively ruinous. The right level of explanation is the trap itself, not the character of the people caught in it. Part of the concept's force is that no villain is required — moralizing at individuals misses the mechanism.

2. Individual accountability. Nonetheless, particular actors deserve real blame: the executive who shipped the engagement-maximizing feed knowing what it would do, the lab that compressed its safety testing to beat a rival's launch date. "The system made me do it" should not exculpate, and journalism that refuses to name names is shirking.

3. Escape requires binding coordination. Traps like these are escaped only through enforceable agreements at a level above the competitors — treaty, statute, a referee with teeth. Unilateral restraint by one player merely cedes ground to the least scrupulous rival; the pause accomplishes nothing except changing who wins.

4. Unilateral restraint is worth praising. When a lab slows down or a government declines to compete on some dimension, something real has been achieved — feasibility demonstrated, a norm seeded, time bought — and such moves should be publicly encouraged rather than dismissed as theater.

5. Competition is not the enemy. Markets operating under well-designed rules are the best large-scale coordination technology we have. Moloch is what happens when the rules let actors externalize costs onto bystanders, not what happens when people compete as such.

6. My own work matters. Journalism that explains these dynamics to a broad general readership contributes to solving them; it isn't mere description, and it isn't therapy for the writer.

Here is what I'd like from you. First, identify which pairs among these six genuinely conflict and which only appear to. My editor flagged (1) against (2), and I'm honestly not sure whether she's right — if that tension dissolves on inspection, show me exactly what dissolves it, because it shapes the whole book. Second, for each genuine tension, rate its severity and specify the least costly revision that resolves it; I'd rather weaken a claim than delete one, so tell me what the minimal weakening is and what rhetorical or explanatory force it forfeits. Third, rank the genuine tensions rather than treating them as equally serious, and tell me which single revision, if I could make only one, would buy the most consistency for the least cost. Finally, check (6) against the rest: I have a professional interest in believing it, which makes me distrust my own assessment of whether it follows.
