Next semester I'm taking over a colleague's unit in a second-year philosophy of science course, and the session I've been handed is on Peirce: the pragmatic maxim from How to Make Our Ideas Clear, sitting between The Fixation of Belief's four methods and the fallibilism that runs through his later work. The students will have read the two Popular Science Monthly papers, or will claim to have. I've taught James's pragmatism before and found it easy to land; Peirce I've always half-taught as a footnote to James, and this time I want to do it properly, in a single eighty-minute seminar with discussion. Don't explain Peirce to me -- I know the material well enough; what I want is a plan for the explanation itself.

Specifically: work out the dependency structure. My instinct is to start with the doubt-belief psychology from Fixation, because the maxim reads like a dry semantic rule unless the students first see inquiry as the struggle to escape genuine doubt, but I'm not sure whether the four methods should come before or after the maxim, and whether fallibilism is a destination or something that has to be seeded early. Tell me what has to be in place before the maxim makes sense as anything other than verificationism. Then give me the one example to carry the session: Peirce's own hardness-of-the-diamond case is famous, but I suspect it invites exactly the wrong reading -- that meaning is a laboratory test -- so either defend it or propose a better one, saying what the carrying example must have and must lack. I'd rather build the session around one case the students can argue about than survey several.

Finally, map the misconceptions I should expect, in roughly the order they'll surface, and tell me where in the sequence to head each one off rather than correct it after the fact. The ones I anticipate: that the maxim makes meaning a matter of usefulness or practical payoff (the James conflation, which some of them will arrive with from other courses); that conceivable practical effects means actual experiments, turning Peirce into a proto-positivist; that fallibilism is just skepticism with better PR; and that the method of science in Fixation is being recommended on grounds of authority or success rather than on something more internal to inquiry. For each, name the point in the plan where a well-placed question or distinction can prevent it from forming. Close with one check I can run in the last ten minutes -- a question or a case to put to the room -- that would show they've grasped the maxim as Peirce meant it, plus a note on what I should openly defer (the categories, the semiotics, truth as the ideal limit of inquiry) and how to defer it without making the day's material look like a teaser for the real thing.
