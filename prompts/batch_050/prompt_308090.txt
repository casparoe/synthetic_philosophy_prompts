i cover science and ideas for a magazine and i'm working on a column about the sunk cost fallacy, and i keep hitting a wall on the follow-up question that never gets answered cleanly: is honoring sunk costs *always* irrational, or does the standard econ advice overreach?

here's where i've gotten on my own, tell me if i'm missing something. the textbook line is that sunk costs are bygones and only prospective costs and benefits should enter the decision. but there are a few defenses floating around that i find genuinely hard to dismiss:

1. the commitment-device defense: someone who knows they're prone to quitting projects the moment they get tedious might rationally adopt a blanket rule like "i finish what i start," and from the outside that rule looks exactly like honoring sunk costs. if you fully internalize "ignore sunk costs," do you lose the ability to stick with anything hard? this smells related to hyperbolic discounting and George Ainslie's stuff about personal rules as willpower, but maybe i'm misreading him.

2. the self-signaling defense: the money and time are gone, sure, but the fact that past-you invested them is still *evidence* -- about how much past-you believed in the project, about what kind of person you turn out to be when things drag on. is treating your own past choices as informative really irrational, or is it only irrational when the past choice carried no information?

3. the reputation version: a firm or government that abandons a half-built project gets read as flaky by investors, voters, allies, and that reputation is a live, forward-looking cost. in that case "we've come too far to stop" isn't about the money at all, is it?

so what i want explained, with an actual verdict: when, if ever, is honoring sunk costs the rational move -- and in those cases, is it genuinely honoring sunk costs, or is it just responding to non-sunk considerations wearing a sunk-cost costume? and on the flip side: is there a real phenomenon of people *over*-correcting, bailing on things too early because they've been trained to flinch at the fallacy, and if so is that a bias in its own right or just healthy updating?

pick a definite position and argue for it -- i don't want a survey of views where i have to decide for myself. lead with your verdict in the very first sentence, no preamble, no restating my question. and after the main argument, flag which of my three defenses you think is weakest and why it fails.
