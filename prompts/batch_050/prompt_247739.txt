I've been arguing with my family about who ought to get a vote in our country's elections, and I've noticed my own instincts don't seem to add up to anything consistent. Here are my verdicts on the cases that came up, stated as plainly as I can.

My daughter is sixteen, works weekends, pays income tax on her wages, and follows politics more closely than I do. I think she should be allowed to vote. Her best friend, also sixteen, is indifferent to politics and badly informed; I still think she should vote, and I would reject any test of knowledge or civic literacy as a condition of the franchise. Our neighbor has lived here twelve years as a permanent resident without taking citizenship; she runs a business on the high street and pays every tax I pay. I think she should vote in municipal elections, but I balk at giving her a say in national ones -- that feels like it belongs to citizens. My cousin emigrated thirty years ago, has never returned, pays no taxes here, and follows our politics as a hobby from abroad; I don't think she should receive a ballot, though as a citizen she currently does. Yet her daughter, born abroad, never once resident here, a citizen only by descent -- I feel she should keep her vote. Citizenship is citizenship. I also think a man with a profound cognitive disability, who cannot say what an election is but brightens when his support worker reads him the party leaflets, must keep his vote, with whatever help he needs to mark the ballot. But a bright twelve-year-old who could pass the citizenship exam tomorrow? No. Some threshold has to hold even against demonstrated competence. Finally, I would not fine anyone for failing to vote -- compelled voting sits wrong with me -- though I consider it perfectly proper that our courts fine people who skip jury service.

Is there a principle -- or a small set of them -- that captures all this about who belongs to the electorate? If so, state it precisely enough that I can see which of my verdicts motivates each clause, and show me what it says about cases I haven't considered: prisoners serving sentences, dual citizens who would vote in two countries' elections, the owner of a holiday home across the river whose zoning is decided by a council he can't elect, soldiers deployed overseas, and the residents of a territory our parliament is debating whether to annex. If no principle saves every verdict, tell me which of mine is the weakest and why, rather than contorting the theory to keep it.

One more thing. I'd like to know what would change your mind. Which consideration or argument, if it held up, would force a different principle on you -- and where is your reconstruction of my verdicts most likely to go wrong?
