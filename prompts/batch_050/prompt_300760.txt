I recently began working in patient communications at a clinical genetics service, and I keep running into the same analogy from every direction. Counselors tell patients that DNA is the body's instruction manual or code, that a pathogenic variant is a spelling error in that manual, and -- the conclusion this is used to license -- that genetic testing is essentially proofreading: find the typo, and you have located the cause and, in principle, the fix. Meanwhile, some philosophers of biology whose work I've been skimming argue that calling DNA a code is actively misleading. I need to know whether the analogy can bear the weight our clinic is putting on it.

Please audit it. First, lay out the correspondence table explicitly: in a genuine code or instruction manual, what plays the role of the alphabet, the lexicon, the syntax, the semantics, the author, the reader, and the distinction between a misprint and an ambiguous but grammatical sentence -- and what, if anything, actually occupies each of those roles in the genome and the cell? Second, identify where the mapping breaks down: I'm aware of candidate disanalogies such as the absence of an author, the context-dependence of gene expression, the fact that the machinery that reads the code is itself encoded in it, and the many-to-many relation between sequences and traits -- but I want to know which of these are real breaks and which are superficial.

Third, and this is the part that matters to me practically: separate the disanalogies that undermine the clinic's intended use of the analogy (reassuring patients that a variant is a discrete, locatable, potentially correctable error) from the ones that are irrelevant to that use. Does the proofreading conclusion survive once the important disanalogies are on the table, or is the analogy smuggling in a determinism the biology doesn't support? Finally, if a better analogy exists for talking to patients -- one that preserves what's genuinely code-like while avoiding the manual's false implications -- propose it and explain what it buys us.
