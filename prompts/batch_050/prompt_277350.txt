Next month I'm giving a talk in an evolutionary biology department's colloqium series (they let in the occasional philosopher, lucky me). My thesis: Popper was basically right the first time. His old claim that Darwinism is a "metaphysical research programme" rather than a falsifiable scientific theory still has real bite for a lot of the ways natural selection actually gets invoked, and his later recantation in the late 70s answered a weaker version of the worry than the one he'd originally raised. Specifically I want to argue that whenever fitness gets defined in terms of expected reproductive success, you can test selection hypotheses, but when fitness gets read off survival after the fact you're back in tautology territory, and biologists slide between these more than they admit.

The audience: mostly working evolutionary biologists, no philosophers in the front rows except two philosophers of biology who will definately know the recantation and the whole tautology literature, and one of them has told me by email she thinks Popper's original worry was "dead on arrival." The biologists will come at this as scientists who feel their field is being called unscientific, not as Popper scholars.

Prep me for the Q&A. I want the eight or so objections I'll actually get from THIS audience (not from philosophy conferences, and not the dumb "evolution is just a theory" stuff), roughly in the order they'll come, phrased the way an evolutionary biologist would actually phrase them in a seminar room. For each: a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft or where I'd have to concede ground, and whether to concede up front or hold. Include the obvious ones (Lenski's long-term E. coli lines, antibiotic resistance, Darwin's finches as retrodiction vs prediction) but also at least two I probably haven't thought of. And tell me which question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks, and what I'd say if they do.

American English please.
