I have just started as a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course in philosophy of technology, and this week I am grading midterm essays for the first time. Before I commit to marks, I want to calibrate my judgment against someone more experienced.

Before judging it, think carefully through what a full-credit answer would need to establish. Essay question (30 points): In his essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?", Langdon Winner distinguishes two ways in which artifacts can be said to have politics. (a) Explain both, giving one of Winner's own examples of each. (b) Consider the following case: the automatic soap dispensers installed throughout an office building dispense soap reliably for light-skinned hands but frequently fail to detect darker-skinned hands, because their infrared sensors were calibrated and tested on a narrow range of skin tones. Does this artifact have politics in either of Winner's senses? Defend a verdict and respond to one objection.

Student's answer:

Winner distinguishes two ways in which artifacts can be political. The first is that a particular technical arrangement can settle a political question within a particular community. His famous example is the low bridges over the parkways on Long Island: Robert Moses had them built too low for buses, which kept poor and Black New Yorkers, who depended on public transit, from reaching Jones Beach. Winner acknowledges that the story about Moses's intentions has been disputed, but holds that the arrangement embodies a social order either way. The mechanized tomato harvester belongs to this first category as well: introduced into California agriculture, it was economical only for large growers and so helped concentrate tomato production in few hands, reshaping rural life. The second way is that some technologies are inherently political: they require, or are strongly compatible with, a particular pattern of authority. Nuclear power is Winner's central example, since its dangers demand centralized, stringently controlled management.

The soap dispensers are a case of the second, inherently political kind. No attendant decides anything; the artifact itself performs the exclusion, automatically and invisibly, so the politics lies in the technology rather than in anyone's use of it. And since infrared sensing simply does not register darker skin, the dispensers cannot be redesigned to be neutral: they inherently favor some users over others. One might object that this is mere thoughtless design rather than politics, but Winner's point is precisely that politics can be embodied in artifacts without conscious intent.

Winner's overall view is a form of technological determinism: once an artifact exists, its political effects are fixed by its design and can no longer be renegotiated, which is why he thinks we must examine technologies before they are built — afterwards the die is cast.
