Two people in my reading group have been going back and forth about self-deception for months and its gone nowhere, so I want you to write the document they should have produced by now: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between them.

Quick sketch of the two camps. The intentionalist thinks genuine self-deception is structurally like deceiving someone else: the agent has to at some level believe (or suspect) the truth, and then intentionally do something to end up believing the opposite. That's why she spends her time on the two classic paradoxes -- the static one (how can one person simultaneously believe p and not-p?) and the dynamic one (how can you carry out an intention to deceive yourself when executing it seems to require knowing about the very deception you're trying to fall for?). She usually answers with some kind of mental partitioning or by weakening "intention." The deflationist thinks all of that is overengineering: self-deception is just motivationally biased belief formation -- desire-influenced evidence gathering, selective attention, asymmetric standards of proof ("must it be true?" for what you want, "can it be true?" for what you don't) -- no intention to deceive, no contradictory beliefs, and the paradoxes only show up if you take the interpersonal-deception analogy too literally.

The document should have: (1) a numbered list of claims both can sign -- e.g., about what the everyday phenomena actually look like, about wishful thinking without any conflicting belief being common, about the analogy to interpersonal deception being imperfect somewhere; (2) a disagreements section where each remaining dispute is stated in wording that side would accept for itself; and (3) the cruxes, with tests. For the cruxes I suspect include: whether there are any real cases that need simultaneous contradictory belief or whether every apparent case dissolves under scrutiny; whether deflationism can actually distinguish self-deception from plain old bias, wishful thinking, or being mistaken (if it can't, is that a bug or a feature?); and whether the intentionalist's partitioning moves are empirically respectable or just renaming the mystery.

To force them to actually engage, make both sides give verdicts with reasoning on these two cases, in advance and in writing:

Case A (the diarist): someone keeps a private prediction diary about whether their failing project will succeed. Every entry confidently predicts success, but the same person consistently books their flights, insurances, and backup plans as if failure is likely. Intentionalist and deflationist: what does each say is going on, and does either view get embarrassed here?

Case B (open-eyed selective exposure): someone says to a friend, in full awareness, "I only read news sources that support my candidate, because reading the other side would just upset me and I'd stop supporting him." Six months later they sincerely report being highly confident the candidate is great. Is this self-deception? Each side has to answer, and if their view gives a counterintuitive verdict they have to own it rather than patch silently.

Finally, if part of their dispute turns out to be verbal -- about what we're willing to *call* self-deception rather than about what happens in anyone's head -- say which part and why.
