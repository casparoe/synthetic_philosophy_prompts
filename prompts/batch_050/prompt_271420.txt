I am a novelist, currently drafting a book about a people who lost a war a century ago and have spent the intervening generations turning that defeat into the very center of their national self-understanding. In the margin of one chapter, my editor has written: remember that history is written by the victors — your survivors would never have been allowed to shape the memory of the war at all. The line is so familiar that I nodded at it at first. On reflection, though, I cannot say precisely what it claims, and my instinct as someone who reads a great deal of history is that the defeated often write with the most fervor of anyone: the American South's Lost Cause, the Serbian cult of the Kosovo myth, and the memory of Masada all look like histories authored, and kept fiercely alive, by the losing side.

Please take the slogan apart for me. Distinguish the several precise theses it could be expressing — for instance, a thesis about who controls archives and physical records after a war, a thesis about whose interpretation comes to dominate schooling and public memory, a thesis about which side is remembered as righteous, and perhaps a deeper and more cynical thesis about what victory does to truth itself. For each reading, say whether it is a near-platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something closer to false, and assess it against cases where the defeated demonstrably did author the dominant memory. Attend also to the ambiguities in the slogan's key terms: what "writing" history amounts to (composition, selection, canonization), and what "history" is in the first place (the events, the documentary record, or the collective memory).

Finally, tell me which reading my editor must have had in mind for the marginal note to count as sound advice, and whether, on that reading, the note is in fact sound. If you conclude the slogan should simply be retired, propose what a more defensible replacement would say.
