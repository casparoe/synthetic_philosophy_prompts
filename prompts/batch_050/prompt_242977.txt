I'm a graduate student in philosophy of science writing the final literature chapter of my dissertation, and I need to stress-test a commitment before my defense. For years I was a default subjective Bayesian: priors are personal, rationality only demands coherence and conditionalization. Over the past year, mostly through working on problems in statistical mechanics and indifference-based reasoning, I've converted to objective Bayesianism in its maximum-entropy form. My current position is roughly this: for a given total evidential situation, there is a uniquely rational (or nearly unique) prior, obtained by maximizing entropy subject to the constraints the evidence imposes; when two rational agents with the same evidence disagree, at least one of them has made an error, and apparent underdetermination of priors usually reflects sloppy specification of the evidential situation rather than genuine freedom. I use this view in my own work, so I'm not a neutral referee.

Here's what I want from you. Acting as an expert philosopher of probability who is familiar with both the technical machinery and the dialectic, do a genuinely hostile audit of my position. Don't just give me the Bertrand-paradox problem about parameterization-dependence of the principle of indifference -- I know that one and have the usual transformation-group response rehearsed. I want the full map of the view's exposure: the formal results and known tensions it has to survive (for instance, whether maximum-entropy updating can be reconciled with ordinary Bayesian conditionalization, and what happens when it can't), the cases it allegedly can't describe, the places where the choice of constraint or reference measure does hidden normative work, and the question of whether retreating from "unique rational prior" to "rationally permissible range of priors" saves the view only by collapsing it into a slightly fussy version of the subjectivism I abandoned. For each potential defeater, tell me whether a committed objective Bayesian would concede it as genuinely damaging or dismiss it as answerable, and rank the whole set from fatal to merely awkward. I also want the comparative dimension: what does my view actually predict or deliver that sophisticated subjective Bayesianism doesn't, since if the answer is "nothing," the whole position may be idle.

Finally, the meta-question that worries me most: has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability, such that every counterexample gets absorbed by redescribing the evidential situation or the constraint set? If so, is that a defect for a view in normative epistemology, or is some insulation from refutation expected here? Please brainstorm freely before organizing your answer, think carefully and step by step about which objections have real teeth versus which only appear to, and write your response in American English.
