I am a novelist, several chapters into a book whose central conceit has put me in debt to a philosophical claim I don't understand well enough to defend. My narrator perceives much of her own mental life as what I have been calling wordless knowing: episodes of thought that arrive fully formed but with no verbal or imagistic clothing, which the prose then has to translate after the fact, always at a loss. A friend with training in philosophy of mind read the manuscript and told me the conceit quietly presupposes the falsity of the language of thought hypothesis — the view, associated with Jerry Fodor, that thinking takes place in an inner languagelike medium with a combinatorial syntax, and that this is not a claim about English or any public language but about the format of cognition itself. I do not need my novel to be scientifically correct, but I refuse to build a book on a premise the informed consensus regards as dead, and I want to know what I've staked.

So treat the language of thought hypothesis as the position under examination, and respond as a philosopher of mind who works on cognitive architecture and knows the empirical literature, not as a humanities scholar gesturing at it. Think carefully, and before giving me your verdict, make a first pass that simply enumerates every candidate defeater you can think of — introspective, behavioral, computational, neuroscientific — then prune to the ones that actually bite. The map I want has four features. First, what would have to be true for the hypothesis to be wrong: which findings or arguments would genuinely refute it, and which would merely embarrass it or force a retreat to a weaker version. Second, whether the position has been insulated by qualifications — retreating from all thought to some thought, or from conscious thought to subpersonal computation — such that it now risks nothing, and if so, whether that is a vice or just what maturing theories do. Third, what its own defenders would concede as counting against it, not only what connectionists and other critics say. Fourth, the question I most need answered: does the phenomenon my novel dramatizes — unsymbolized thought, of the sort reported in descriptive experience sampling studies and familiar to me from my own working life as a writer — actually bear on the hypothesis at all, or does the hypothesis make no prediction about what introspection can access, in which case my friend's remark was a category error?

For the record, I would personally find a computational result most persuasive — some cognitive capacity that provably cannot be captured by a compositional symbol system — because introspective reports, mine included, strike me as too slippery to rest a theory on; if you think that judgment is itself mistaken, say so. And if the framing of this request rests on a confusion — if, for instance, the hypothesis and my novel's conceit are simply orthogonal — I would far rather be told that plainly than have you play along and answer the question as posed.
