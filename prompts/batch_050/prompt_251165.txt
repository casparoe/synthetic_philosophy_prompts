I teach a first-year seminar on ethics and public life at a small college in California — eighteen students, one ninety-minute meeting. Last fall my street failed, for the third consecutive year, to fund a shared firebreak: every household agreed it was necessary, the cost per household was trivial next to the risk, and we still could not collect the pledges. Two months later a fire two canyons over forced evacuations. Since then I have been preoccupied with the argument associated with the philosophers Ingmar Persson and Julian Savulescu: that human moral motivation evolved for small-group problems and has not kept pace with the harms our technology now lets us cause, and that if education and democratic institutions cannot close that gap in time, we should seriously investigate biomedical means of strengthening moral motivation itself.

I do not want to lecture my students on this. I want them to arrive at the temptation on their own, and then recoil from it on their own terms, before they ever hear the phrase moral bioenhancement. Design an activity that does this. Specifically:

1. A first phase in which the group faces a collective problem where everyone knows what joint action is needed, knows that everyone else knows it, and predictably fails to deliver it anyway. The failure has to be real, with a small genuine stake that does not involve money changing hands — something they can actually lose. Give the full rules, materials, communication constraints, and payoff structure.

2. A second phase in which an enhancement analog becomes available: some mechanism, individually taken or collectively imposed, that reliably makes people act on what they already know they should do. The group must decide whether and how to use it, under decision rules you specify, and the decision procedure should itself exhibit the very problem the mechanism is meant to cure — the people voting on whether to bind themselves are the same people who just failed to act. Lay out the exact sequence of phases with timings, and what the facilitator may and may not say at each stage.

3. A closing debrief that connects particular moments of the session to the parts of the argument: the gap between knowing and being motivated; the claim that moral education and liberal institutions may simply work too slowly; the objection that mandatory enhancement sacrifices freedom; the objection that it changes the person rather than the choice; and the question of who gets to decide. For each objection, tell me which moment in the activity is engineered to make it land.

4. Where this design will break the first time I run it — students colluding around the intended failure, the vote passing too easily because the stakes are tokens, one sharp student dismissing the parallel to actual drugs — and what to do about each.

5. A candid note on which feature of the actual proposal the activity cannot reproduce. I suspect the involuntariness and irreversibility of a real biomedical intervention is the big one: my students will be bound by a rule they voted on, not by altered dispositions they never chose. Tell me whether that gap is fatal to the exercise, and what I should say about it in the room.
