The art world runs on attribution, and what bugs me is how brutally the consequences follow from it. Take a painting that's hung in a museum for fifty years, loved by visitors. Then some panel of experts decides it's actually by a forger or just "studio of" instead of the master. The object doesn't change one molecule -- same canvas, same brushstrokes, looks identical. But the market price craters by like 98%, the museum pulls it from the wall into storage, and meanwhile a perfect copy painted last year by a conservator, which is arguably *cleaner* and easier to see, has no value at all and would never get hung. Some jurisdictions even let foundations destroy works they declare fake. So here's my question: what theory of artistic or aesthetic value does this whole practice presuppose? It seems to assume that value lives not in what you experience looking at the thing but in the object's causal history -- who actually painted it, what it connects to. Is that right? Or could you defend the practice on purely aesthetic grounds (maybe knowing the provenance changes what you see)? Try to reconstruct the implicit theory, and then press on whether the practice actually *needs* it -- maybe the price collapse is just about scarcity and investment and says nothing about aesthetic value proper, and maybe several rival theories would license the exact same behavior. Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined? And finally: if you fully embraced the rival, experience-based view, what would have to change concretely -- would museums really be obliged to hang good copies, and would anything be lost if they did?
