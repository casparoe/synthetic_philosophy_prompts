Okay so through a weird chain of events (long story, it started at an ACX meetup) I've ended up on the ethics advisory board of a defense robotics company, and we vote next week on a new system. Our past calls, as best I remember them:

1. Approved a ship defense system that detects incoming anti-ship missiles and shoots them down fully autonomously. A human sets the engagement envelope before deployment and can switch the thing off, but nobody approves individual interceptions, because the whole engagement takes a couple seconds.
2. Rejected an autonomous border sentry that identifies armed people crossing a marked line and fires unless a supervising human vetoes within 30 seconds.
3. Approved a targeting-recommendation AI that ranks possible targets and flags proportionality concerns, where a human officer reviews and personally approves each strike, usually spending a few minutes per decision.
4. Rejected a loitering munition that circles a 50 km² area for up to two hours and attacks anything its classifier tags as a military vehicle, with no abort channel after launch. Part of the problem: in testing it tagged civilian trucks as military vehicles something like 8% of the time.
5. Approved an autonomous drone swarm doing electronic warfare only -- picks its own jamming targets, no kinetic effects, everything reversible.

Now the new proposal: a loitering system launched across a border (so, offensive) that hunts enemy *uncrewed* ground vehicles -- supply robots, drone carriers, that kind of thing. The classifier is supposedly very good (sub-0.1% confusion between crewed and uncrewed vehicles, plus a separate thermal/occupancy check that makes it hold fire if there might be a person inside). A human supervisor watches a feed of each proposed engagement and has 30 seconds to veto; no veto, it fires. Tempo is only a few engagements per hour, so unlike case 2 the supervisor isn't exactly scrambling... though we're all familiar with the automation bias literature, or at least with the pop-sci version of it that everyone cites.

So: if we want to be consistent with our own track record, what should we do? Which earlier case is the closest analog -- case 2 (same veto structure) or cases 1 and 5 (same "targets are machines, not people" profile)? If the precedents genuinely underdetermine this one, spell out the different principles we could have been running all along that fit cases 1-5 equally well but diverge here, and tell me what principle we'd implicitly be committing to with each possible verdict. And what's the future case that would most cleanly reveal which principle we were actually following?

Partly I'm asking because I suspect our past votes weren't driven by one coherent rule at all, and I'd like to know whether I'm pattern-matching to vibes before I have to defend my vote in front of people who definately know more about this than me.
