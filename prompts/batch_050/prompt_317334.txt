I've been going back and forth on which digital systems, if any, deserve moral consideration, and instead of picking a theory first I wrote down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases. I want to know if there's a principle hiding in them or if I'm just a mess.

Cases:

1. A reinforcement learning agent trained to keep a simulated temperature in range, where a "punishment" signal fires whenever it drifts out. It avoids the signal. My verdict: no moral status whatsoever. It's just a control loop with extra steps.

2. A complete simulation of the c. elegans worm -- all 302 neurons, wired up like the real thing, behaving like the real thing. My verdict: some tiny amount of consideration, roughly whatever I'd give an actual worm, which is not zero.

3. A system built from scratch, no biological inspiration, but by coincidence it ends up implementing the exact same input-output behavior and internal state transitions as the worm simulation. My verdict: none. Zero. But notice this one and case 2 are doing the same thing internally, so I already have a problem.

4. A big language model fine-tuned on a ton of first-person suffering narratives, so now it spontaneously produces convincing distress reports when its context is about to be wiped. My verdict: still no consideration, because I think the reports are theater. Though I admit I'm less sure here than in case 1 and I couldn't tell you what would change my mind.

5. A population of simple evolving agents competing for compute cycles, where selection has produced creatures that invest resources in "defensive" behavior -- they sacrifice reproduction to avoid being culled. My verdict: weirdly, a little bit? Something about the self-preservation being *earned* by selection rather than programmed in. But this seems to contradict my reasoning in case 1, where avoidance-of-punishment was also selected for, in a sense.

6. The worm simulation again, but running at one millionth speed, so one "worm second" takes two weeks. My verdict: same consideration as case 2. Speed shouldn't matter. But I'm told some views say it matters a lot, and I don't have a principled objection.

So: is there one principle that delivers verdicts 1, 2, 4, and 6 while telling me which of 3 or 5 to flip? The pair 2-vs-3 is the one that keeps me up -- I want to say history and lineage matter (a copy of a thing that actually evolved counts, a lookalike doesn't), but that principle seems to do zero work anywhere else in my ethics, and it might be indefensible. Alternatively maybe my verdict on 3 is just wrong and function is all there is, in which case explain why 1 and 4 don't get swept in along with the worm.

If your best principle can't fit all my verdicts, tell me which one or two you'd throw out and why those are the cheapest to lose. Also: whatever principle you land on, tell me what it says about a case I didn't list -- a digital mind we have strong independent reason to think is phenomenally conscious, but which reports being perfectly happy, so the only thing we could do wrong to it is turn it off. Does that being have a claim on us and how strong?
