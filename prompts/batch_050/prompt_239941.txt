I'm a novelist working on a book about two estranged siblings, Maya and Dan, who end up having to co-write the eulogy for their father, who died after five years of pretty severe dementia. Maya is a longtime meditator and basically a no-self person: she thinks there's no persisting "me," just a stream of experiences stitched together by memory, habits, and causal continuity, and she thinks seeing this clearly is liberating rather than depressing. Dan thinks that's nuts. He's convinced there has to be a subject, something that IS him, that persists through change — partly because of the grief thing. He keeps saying stuff like "dad was still in there even at the end, and if nobody was there then who's the eulogy even FOR."

Anyway, my editor had this idea I initially hated and now love: in the middle of the book, Maya and Dan get drunk one night and, half as a joke, try to write down a "joint statement" of what they actually agree and disagree about, to stop rehashing the same fight at every eulogy meeting. I want to include the actual document in the novel, in their handwriting font and everything. Problem is I can't write it — every draft I produce is secretly on Maya's side.

So: draft the joint statement Maya and Dan would produce after a genuinely good-faith attempt. It should have:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. I want real ones, not mush — e.g. things about how much of a person's psychology changes over a lifetime anyway, about what memory does and doesn't do for a sense of self, about what the dementia actually showed and didn't show, about whether either view is "obviously" the consoling one (I suspect both would deny that).
2. A disagreements section — a table works — where each side's position is stated in words that side would actually accept. This is the part I keep failing at; Dan's view keeps coming out as "clinging to a comforting illusion" which he would never sign.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that if one of them got answered, at least one sibling would have to budge. My guesses: whether a subject is something over and above the experiences or just a way of talking about them; whether the persistence question is empirical, conceptual, or something else; whether the eulogy/grief thing actually requires a metaphysical self or just continuity. But correct me or add your own.
4. For each crux, some case, thought experiment, or real-life phenomenon both would accept as bearing on it, with what each of them would say about it written down in advance. The dad's dementia has to be one of these. A fission/teletransportation-style case can be another, but please also come up with at least one from ordinary life, since my novel is realism and Dan would roll his eyes at trolleys and teleporters.
5. If part of their fight turns out to be verbal — they're using "self" or "person" differently and don't disagree about any fact — mark that part clearly, because I want the dramatic payoff to be them realizing exactly one of their disagreements was about words while the other one is real.

One constraint: no citations or reading lists, this is a document two grieving siblings write on napkins at 1am, not a term paper. Keep it plausibly human — a little raw is fine, they can be smart without being textbook-y.
