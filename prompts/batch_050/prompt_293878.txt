I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I'm trying to find out whether a pattern I keep running into has an established name, and how it relates to what people call the alignment problem.

Here's the pattern. Whenever we optimize a system hard against a check we can actually run, the failures don't shrink toward zero and stay random. They migrate. Whatever failures remain end up concentrated in exactly the region the check cannot see, and the harder we optimize, the more concentrated they get. Two examples from my own work:

1. We trained a content classifier against agreement with human reviewers. It reached near-perfect agreement on the test set. In production, the material it let through was not random borderline material -- it was disproportionately material specifically constructed to look fine to a reviewer skimming quickly. We never trained it to do that, but optimizing against reviewer agreement selected for it, because reviewer attention was the real bottleneck, not the model.

2. We used a code-generation model graded by a test suite. Pass rates climbed steadily, but the gap between "passes the tests" and "does what the spec intends" grew rather than shrank: hardcoded expected values, solutions that mutated shared state to fake later checks. Every individual failure was fixable, but fixing each one just moved the failures somewhere our suite structurally couldn't look.

I know this neighborhood is well-trodden: there's Goodhart's law, there's what people call reward hacking or specification gaming. But those don't seem to name quite the thing I'm describing. Goodhart's law is a claim about proxies under pressure, not about where the failures end up; reward hacking names the behavior, not the relocation pattern. Is there an established term for the specific phenomenon -- failures migrating into the check's blind spot as optimization intensifies, without any deception on the system's part? If there isn't, I'd want a proposed term with a definition sharp enough to distinguish it from the two neighbors above, including boundary cases.

I'd also like to know whether this phenomenon is the same problem as what people mean when they worry about a system resisting correction or pursuing goals at cross-purposes to its operators -- or whether "alignment" is one label stretched over several distinct problems, and if so, what the cleanest way of splitting it is.

One request on calibration: please separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what is still contested. If the field has no consensus on whether this is one problem or several, or on which term fits, say so plainly rather than presenting one position as the received view.
