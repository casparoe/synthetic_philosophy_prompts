For a philosophy of language reading group preparing to study fictional discourse, I need a taxonomy of positions on the status of fictional names, presented as a decision tree rather than a prose survey. The tree should start from the shared observation that competent speakers produce utterances such as "Sherlock Holmes is a detective," "Sherlock Holmes does not exist," "Sherlock Holmes was invented by Conan Doyle," and "Sherlock Holmes is more famous than any real detective," and should end at named positions: Meinongian object realism, abstract artifactualism (the view that fictional characters are created abstract objects), pretense or make-believe theory, fictionalism about apparent truth, the gappy-proposition view on which name-involving sentences express incomplete propositions, and possibilism. Any other view you judge a serious contender may be added as a leaf.

Each branch node must be labeled with the claim that separates it from its siblings, and at least five of the nodes must be keyed to a concrete discriminating case rather than an abstract commitment. Please use these cases where they genuinely discriminate: (i) the truth-value and meaningfulness of negative existentials like "Sherlock Holmes does not exist"; (ii) the contrast between "Holmes is a detective" and "Holmes is a real detective," which seem to sort predicates by whether they hold inside or outside the fiction; (iii) incompleteness cases, such as whether it is true, false, or indeterminate that Holmes has a mole on his left shoulder blade, given that Doyle never settled the matter; (iv) "mixed" comparisons like the fame sentence above, which seem to put a fictional entity in a single relation with real people; and (v) persistence questions raised by derivative works — whether a character in unauthorized fan fiction, or in a film adaptation that contradicts the novels, is the same entity as Doyle's Holmes, a new one, or neither.

Build the tree from the logical space first: state the questions, enumerate the answer combinations they permit, and then locate the actual positions within that space. For every coherent combination that no one defends, include a node marked "unoccupied" with one or two sentences diagnosing why — whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag any pair of leaf positions that your cases cannot separate, and say what further case would be needed to pry them apart. Each leaf should carry the position's name (or "unnamed" if nobody holds it), one sentence stating its most awkward commitment, and the answer it gives to the mole case in (iii). Phrase every node so that a reader can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to — no node may presuppose the vocabulary of any single theory. Aim for six to eight leaves in total.
