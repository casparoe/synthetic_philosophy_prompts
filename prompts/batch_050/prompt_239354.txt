My grandmother died last month in Leipzig, and while sorting her papers I found the letters she wrote to her sister in West Germany between 1985 and late 1989. I have taken on the task of writing a short family history, and I keep running into a question she circles in one of the final letters, asked half-jokingly the week the Wall opened: which of all the things people are crediting actually did it? I want to answer her seriously rather than with the usual "it was many factors," so I am asking for help.

You are a professional historian with philosophical training, specializing in causal explanation and the historiography of 1989. Think carefully and reason step by step. Before composing your answer, spend a paragraph brainstorming the full space of candidate causes and the distinctions among them; then write the analysis.

Here are the candidate causes, as I understand them from general knowledge: the long stagnation of the Soviet-bloc economies, sharpened by the collapse of oil prices in the mid-1980s; Gorbachev's glasnost and perestroika and, crucially, his refusal to use military force to prop up client regimes; the dissident and opposition movements, above all Solidarity in Poland; Western pressure, including the American arms buildup that strained Soviet budgets; Chernobyl's demolition of official credibility in 1986; and, at the very end, something close to accident -- a spokesman's fumbled press conference on November 9 that sent crowds to the checkpoints before anyone had decided to open them.

Analyze this episode through the lens of overdetermination and multi-causal explanation. Specifically: Which of these factors are genuinely overdetermining causes -- each individually sufficient, such that the Wall falls even if you subtract one -- and which are better understood as parts of a single conjunctive sufficient cause, in the way Mackie's INUS conditions are insufficient alone but necessary parts of a sufficient whole? Is there a case of preemption hiding in here, where one cause actually did the work and another was waiting in the wings to do it if needed? How should I think about the counterfactual question my grandmother is really asking -- if Gorbachev had been a hardliner, or oil had stayed expensive, would the Wall have fallen by 1995 anyway -- and is that question even well-posed? Finally, historians routinely designate one factor the "main cause." Is that a substantive causal claim, or is it causal selection driven by pragmatic and narrative interests, and does this episode show that multi-causal explanation is intellectual honesty or a refusal to commit? Do not cite sources; reason from the facts given and general knowledge, and say explicitly where the historical record is too uncertain to settle the philosophy.
