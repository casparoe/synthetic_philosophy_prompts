Two of my housemates (both did a bit of philosophy years ago) have been arguing about Boethius and divine foreknowledge for about a week now and I've been dragged in as referee. The setup: if God knows in advance everything I'll do, then apparently I can't do otherwise, so I'm not free. Boethius's answer, roughly, is that God isn't *in* time at all -- he exists in an eternal present and sees all of history at once, the way you might see a whole landscape from a hilltop, so there's no *fore*knowledge, just knowledge. Here's the argument as I've pieced it together:

(1) Dan says the move doesn't solve anything, it just relocates the problem. If God timelessly knows that I'll have toast tomorrow, then it's settled that I'll have toast tomorrow, full stop. Whether the knowing happens before the event or outside time altogether is irrelevant -- either way the fact of God's knowledge fixes the future. Moving God outside time is like moving the cage the bird is in; the bird still can't leave.

(2) Priya says he's missed the whole point. The threatening thing about foreknowledge is that it's *already there* before I act, like a written record I can't change. But Boethius's God doesn't know in advance any more than my seeing you cross the street *makes* you cross the street. God's knowledge is like observation, not prediction, and observation doesn't constrain what's observed. So the argument from foreknowledge to fatalism never gets going.

(3) Dan replied that the observation analogy cheats in a specific way: when I watch you cross the street, your crossing happens independently of my watching. But God isn't just watching -- everything that happens supposedly exists because God wills or sustains it. So the eternal God isn't a bystander at the hilltop, he's more like the author of the whole landscape, and an author seeing his whole book at once doesn't make the characters free. If anything timelessness makes it worse: the whole story is written before... well, not before, but written *timelessly*.

(4) Priya said that's just asserting that God's knowledge causes the events, which is a different argument from the foreknowledge one, and Boethius was answering the foreknowledge one. And anyway Boethius distinguishes (she said) between something being necessary full stop and something being necessary *given* that it's known -- the toast is necessary only in the second, harmless sense, like how if I'm sitting, necessarily I'm sitting. Dan said that distinction does no work: the only necessity anyone was ever worried about is exactly the conditional one, since nobody thinks my breakfast is necessary like 2+2=4. What matters is that *I* can't do otherwise, and conditional necessity gives me precisely that.

(5) Finally Priya tried a different tack: Dan's whole objection smuggles in temporal language. He keeps saying God's knowledge "fixes" or "settles" the future, but fixing and settling are things that happen *at a time*. Take time away from God and the metaphors lose their grip. Dan's counter was that timelessness then does too much: if God genuinely sees tomorrow in an eternal present, then tomorrow must already be real in some sense, so Boethius has quietly committed to the view that past, present and future all exist equally -- and on that view the future is just as "there" as the past, which is arguably fatalism in a posher outfit. So the solution either fails (Dan's move 1) or succeeds only by buying a metaphysics that's just as bad.

Neither of them will give an inch. Please go through the five moves and say who's right at each step and why -- especially whether the conditional-necessity distinction is doing anything, and whether Dan's final move (that timeless knowledge requires the future to be already real) is the clever point he thinks it is. Also: what are they both missing, if anything?
