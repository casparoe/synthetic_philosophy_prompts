My officemate has an arguement about the four-color theorem that I can't accept but also can't refute. (1) Mathematical knowledge has to be checkable by the knower: if you can't in principle work through the proof yourself, then what you have is testimony, and testimony can give you mathematical belief but not mathematical knowledge. (2) The four-color theorem's proof requires verifying an enormous set of map configurations by computer; no human has checked all of it by hand or could in a lifetime. (3) So nobody knows the four-color theorem; we just trust the machines. (4) But then apply the standard consistently: no living person has worked through every page of the classification of finite simple groups, nobody checks the arithmetic their calculator does, and every engineer who signs off on a bridge trusts simulation output she couldn't reproduce by hand. (5) So by (1), almost no mathematics is known by anyone, and no computation-dependent engineering fact is known either. (6) That's absurd — someone plainly knows the bridge design is sound, and mathematicians plainly know results whose proofs sprawl across dozens of papers. (7) So (1) is false, and we do know the four-color theorem after all. Here's the problem: he accepts (6) and cheerfully concludes that almost nothing is known — he says calling it absurd is just sentimentality about expertise. I want to defend (6) without making mathematical knowledge collapse into "whatever the experts say." Which step should I actually attack, and what does attacking it cost? I'd most like to keep both the idea that math knowledge is different from trusting a reliable source and the claim that we know the four-color theorem. Please answer in American English.
