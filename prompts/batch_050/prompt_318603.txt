Question: The debate between the Mu'tazila and the Ash'arites over good and evil is often described as an Islamic version of the Euthyphro dilemma. Explain how each school's position maps onto one horn of that dilemma, and give one strength and one difficulty for each position.

Student's answer: The Euthyphro dilemma, posed by Socrates in Plato's Republic, asks whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because it is loved. The Mu'tazila take the first horn: they held that good and evil are objective features of acts, knowable by unaided reason prior to revelation, so God commands justice and prohibits injustice because these acts genuinely are just and unjust. On their view, God is essentially just and never acts contrary to justice, and this connects to their doctrine of human free will — developed through the theory of kasb, or acquisition of acts — which they needed in order to hold people responsible without making God the author of evil. The strength of this position is that it preserves the meaning of moral praise of God; calling God just is not an empty tautology. Its difficulty is that it seems to subject God to a moral order independent of Him, arguably compromising divine omnipotence and sovereignty. The Ash'arites take the second horn: an act is good because God commands it and evil because He forbids it, and reason on its own can establish the fundamental truths of morality, since God could not be bound by any external standard without losing His transcendence. The strength here is that it safeguards divine omnipotence and makes revelation morally central. Its difficulty is that it risks making morality arbitrary — if God had commanded cruelty, cruelty would be good — and it seems to reduce "God is just" to "God does whatever God does."

Overall, the student's answer captures the main contours of the debate correctly.
