In a machine-learning reading group, someone argued against adding a third hidden layer to our model by invoking Occam's razor — "the simplest hypothesis that fits the data is the one we should prefer" — and nobody could say what that actually licensed. I'd like a careful disambiguation. Lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing: syntactic simplicity (fewer parameters, shorter description in some notation), Kolmogorov-style complexity (length of the shortest program that generates the hypothesis), and the Solomonoff-style claim that a prior weighting hypotheses by 2^(−K) is epistemically privileged rather than merely convenient. For each, say what would have to be true for it to be a justified rule of inference rather than an aesthetic preference — in particular, how it survives the objection that "simplicity" is relative to a choice of language or universal Turing machine, and whether the invariance results that limit that dependence (order-one constant shifts) are enough to ground a practical model-selection rule. Then say which readings would actually support my colleague's move against the extra layer, and whether any reading of the razor does real work that cross-validation doesn't already do.
