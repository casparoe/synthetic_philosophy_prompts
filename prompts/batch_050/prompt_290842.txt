In a reading group on metaethics that I attend, we recently got stuck on the idea that there might be reasons that exist without any reasoner -- what one participant called free-floating rationales. The thought was that a consideration can count in favor of some action or attitude even if no agent ever entertains it, endorses it, or is in a position to act on it; on this picture, the reason is simply out there, and our reasoning practices occasionally latch onto it. To defend the coherence of this, one member reached for a comparison that the others found immediately compelling: the situation is exactly like mathematics, he said. Just as a theorem was true, and its proof existed to be found, long before anyone proved it, a reason to do something can obtain without anyone having thought it. The analogy did a lot of quiet work in the room -- it moved several people from suspicion that a reason without a reasoner is like a gesture without a gesturer to acceptance that reasons are discovered, not made.

I am not satisfied, but I want the disagreement made precise rather than left as a vibe. Please audit the analogy as an argument for free-floating rationales. Set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the theorem, the proof, the mathematician, mathematical truth itself, and the sense in which the proof was already there. Then identify where the mapping breaks, distinguishing the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. In particular, consider whether the analogy can distinguish reasons from mere truths (plenty of mathematical facts are true but no one should do anything about them, so what in the source domain corresponds to the normative force the target needs?); whether it smuggles in a realism about mathematics that someone could accept about proofs while denying about oughts; whether it proves too much, licensing free-floating counter-reasons, obsolete reasons, or reasons no possible agent could ever recognize; and whether the dependence of reasons on agents can be relocated -- say, to a counterfactual about what ideal reasoners would converge on -- without collapsing the analogy.

Finally, deliver a verdict: once the disanalogies are on the table, can the comparison still carry the conclusion that reasons without reasoners are coherent, does it support only a weaker thesis (specify which), or was it doing the persuading all along? If the analogy fails, say whether a better one exists for the same conclusion -- or whether the conclusion should be abandoned with it.
