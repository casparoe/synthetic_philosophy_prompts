I teach a second-year undergraduate philosophy of science seminar — 16 students, one 90-minute session — and I want an in-class activity that makes the demarcation problem feel like a live problem rather than a museum piece. My students arrive assuming that separating science from pseudoscience is straightforward and that falsifiability obviously does the job; I want them to discover, by trying to state a criterion themselves, that every simple rule they propose either excommunicates bona fide science or admits pseudoscience, and that falsificationism in particular should collapse in their own hands before they ever hear Popper's name. Design the activity: the case material (a set of theories and practices to be sorted, chosen so that any crisp criterion misfires on at least one of them — I would like at least one historical case of the Vulcan sort, where the respectable side survived precisely by the ad hoc, immunising manoeuvre the criterion was meant to exclude, so that students cannot tell at the time whether they are looking at a Neptune or a Vulcan); the sequence of rounds, with rules for how groups propose, test, and amend their criteria and timings for each; and a debrief that maps what happened onto Popper's falsificationism, the problem of auxiliary hypotheses, and the suggestion that demarcation may be a matter of family resemblance rather than a bright line. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for instance, students retreating to institutional criteria such as peer review, which the case material should be designed to punish — and be frank about which feature of the real historical problem the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in words.
