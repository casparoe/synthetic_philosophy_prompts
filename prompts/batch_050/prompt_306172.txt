I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a Philosophy of Mind module, and my seminar leader has asked me to run a thirty-minute revision session for the first-years on eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires. The students have just finished weeks on the identity theory and functionalism, so this is their first encounter with the suggestion that a mature science might not just locate our everyday mental vocabulary in the brain but discard it entirely. Several of them have already told me the view is obviously crazy, which is exactly the attitude I need to work through rather than around.

Don't explain eliminative materialism to me; plan how I should explain it. Specifically, I want:

1. The order in which the components have to be introduced and why -- what depends on what. My instinct is that the students first need to see folk psychology as an empirical theory with posits (something they've never questioned, since they treat "I believe that p" as a transparent report rather than a theoretical attribution), then the distinction between reduction and elimination of a theory's posits, then the claim that folk psychology might be a candidate for the latter. Tell me whether that's the right dependency structure, and correct it if not.

2. The one example or contrast that should carry the whole session. I'm currently planning to build it around the pair of phlogiston (a posit eliminated outright) and lightning (a commonsense category kept because it turned out to be something -- electrical discharge). Tell me whether this contrast can bear the weight, what the example must have and must lack to work for first-years (I suspect it has to require no chemistry beyond what they half-remember from school, and must not smuggle in the conclusion by picking a case where elimination was obviously deserved), or propose a better carrier and explain the trade-off.

3. The misconceptions the students will form, in the order they'll form them, and the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off. My guesses: that the view is self-refuting (the eliminativist believes there are no beliefs); that eliminativists are denying we have experiences or pains rather than denying a particular theoretical account of propositional attitudes; that elimination and reduction are the same thing with different amounts of politeness; and that this is just behaviourism in a lab coat. Confirm, refine, or add to this list.

4. One question I can pose at the end that would show the idea has landed -- something that a student who merely memorised the slogan "beliefs are like phlogiston" would answer badly.

5. What I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so, rather than gesture at -- e.g., the empirical details of what might replace folk psychology, or the finer intra-eliminativist disputes.
