I direct a mid-career fellowship for journalists -- sixteen fellows each year, drawn from data desks, investigative teams, and longform features -- and I run one three-hour Saturday workshop per cohort. Over the past two cycles I've watched a shift that I find genuinely alarming: the fellows arrive at correct answers faster than any group I've taught, and can explain those answers less. Ask how a figure in a draft was obtained and you get a confident gesture at the tool, not an account of the source. I've started calling the underlying condition "verification debt": the running total of claims a journalist has put their name to but could no longer reconstruct the justification for from primary material if the scaffolding were removed. What I want from you is a workshop activity that makes the fellows feel the weight of their own verification debt before anyone names it -- not a lecture about the epistemology of testimony, but a structured exercise in which the debt surfaces on its own.

A few constraints and desiderata. The exercise should involve real journalistic work in miniature -- reporting, checking, or defending claims -- with rules and a timeline I can actually run, materials that need nothing fancier than laptops, printed handouts, and the room, and some small but genuine stakes so that being caught short costs something. It must work for a mixed group: the data journalists will be comfortable with tooling and may be the most exposed, while the features writers may underestimate theirs, and I don't want either camp to feel the game was rigged for or against them. The crucial design problem is that participants should discover the gap between holding a true belief on reliable authority and understanding the thing well enough to catch the authority failing -- the moment where the tool or the intermediary silently degrades and they find out whether they ever had the claim or were only ever borrowing it. The concept of verification debt should not be uttered until the debrief; if someone asks at the outset what the workshop is about, I want to be able to deflect.

In the debrief section, map the moments of the exercise onto the underlying epistemology: the difference between knowledge by testimony and understanding, why a perfectly reliable source can still leave you worse off than a merely good one if it teaches you not to keep the derivation warm, and what distinguishes prudent reliance on experts and tools from the kind of dependence that quietly hollows out the professional judgment the reliance was supposed to serve. Then tell me, honestly, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- I anticipate defensiveness, at least one fellow treating it as anti-technology moralizing, and at least one who games the scoring -- and how to handle each. Finally, name the feature of the real phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce: my suspicion is that in actual practice the scaffolding never announces its withdrawal and nobody schedules the audit, and I'd like your view on whether the exercise should gesture at that or leave it for the closing discussion.
