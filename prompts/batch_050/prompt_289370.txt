I'm putting together a unit for a philosophy of science course on whether methodological rules actually describe good science as it happens, and I want a careful case analysis rather than another restatement of the textbook. Take the 1919 eclipse expeditions -- Eddington's team on Príncipe and the sister expedition to Sobral, photographing stars near the eclipsed Sun to test Einstein's prediction that starlight passing near the Sun would be deflected by roughly twice the Newtonian value -- and analyze the episode through the lens of Popper's falsificationism. The broad facts as I want you to take them: general relativity made a specific, novel, quantitative prediction where Newtonian physics (supplemented with a plausible light-has-mass assumption) made a different one; the expeditions measured deflections from photographic plates of varying quality, with some plates showing more and some less; the announced result favored Einstein and made him famous overnight; and later commentators have questioned how the plates were selected and weighted, while the basic conclusion has been confirmed by many subsequent measurements.

First, run the analysis in Popper's direction. Popper held up Einstein as the model scientist precisely because the theory staked itself on a risky prediction that could have gone the other way, in contrast to theories he considered unfalsifiable. So: what, exactly, counted as the falsifying observation in 1919? Was it a single outcome, a range, a statistical verdict across plates? Whose theory was on trial -- bare general relativity, or general relativity plus a stack of auxiliary assumptions about optics, atmospheric conditions, telescope behavior, and plate measurement? Map the episode onto Popper's logic of conjectures and refutations as cleanly as you can, identifying what plays the role of basic statement, what plays the role of background knowledge, and where the risky prediction lives.

Then tell me where the lens distorts. If the plate selection was even partly judgment-laden, does the episode show that no observation report is ever theory-free enough to falsify by itself (as Popper himself partly conceded when he called basic statements conventions), and if so, what is left of the asymmetry between verification and falsification? Consider also the counterfactual: had the result gone against Einstein, is it plausible that the physics community would have discarded general relativity rather than the plates, the telescopes, or an auxiliary hypothesis -- and if not, does the episode actually vindicate falsificationism as a description of scientific practice, or only as a normative ideal? Finally, say what the episode does to the demarcation criterion itself: does 1919 confirm that risky-prediction-passing is the mark of science, does it reveal the criterion to be about communal decisions rather than logic, or does it show that the criterion was never really about single dramatic tests at all?
