Write a dialogue between two epistemologists, Priya (a conciliationist) and Marcus (a steadfast type), who've each independently checked the same long informal philosophical argument: Priya concluded it's valid, Marcus found an equivocation at step 14. They've now compared notes thoroughly and each still thinks they're right. And naturally they also disagree about the meta-question — Priya thinks they should both move substantially toward the middle, Marcus thinks sticking to your guns after rechecking your work is perfectly rational.

The tension I most want explored: Priya notices that by her own lights she should lower her confidence in conciliationism itself, since Marcus is her peer and rejects it. Let Marcus press this hard — does conciliating on the meta-issue mean she's rationally required to go steadfast on the first-order dispute, which looks like the view defeating itself? Priya needs a real answer, not a wave at "higher-order evidence is different." And Marcus shouldn't get off easy either: he has to give a non-question-begging answer to the symmetry challenge — why should the person who's right about step 14 stay put rather than the person who's wrong, when both feel equally certain from the inside? Also let them wrestle with what "splitting the difference" even means when the answer is binary (valid or not): is a .5 credence in "valid" a coherent resting point, or does conciliationism predict something unstable here?

Keep both of them sharp and charitable, real back-and-forth, no strawmen, no tidy resolution if the disagreement doesn't actually resolve. And if you think the setup rests on a confused premise — say, about what peerhood requires or whether you can conciliate toward a view that tells you not to conciliate — flag that up front instead of politely writing around it.
