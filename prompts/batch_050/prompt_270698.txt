I've been thinking about moral trade in the Ord-style sense, and I want a thought experiment built to order. Construct a case in which two parties with genuinely different terminal values reach a moral compromise that is a strict improvement by each party's own lights, under these constraints: the setting must be realistic (a household, a legislature, a grantmaking panel -- no dictators, no utility monsters, no Omega); the gains from trade must flow from the difference in values itself, not from differing empirical beliefs, differing risk attitudes, or one party simply being better informed; and, the hard part, each party must regard the end the other party is trading toward as not merely less valuable but positively bad -- so a shared-currency story, where both parties secretly value the same thing and just weigh it differently, is ruled out. The scenario itself should be one paragraph, and the verdicts must be generated by the values you assign, not stipulated.

After the scenario, show me each party's ledger: what they give up, what they get, and why the deal beats both the status quo and unilateral pursuit of their own values by their own accounting. Then tell me which structural feature of the case is doing the work -- diminishing marginal moral returns, veto power, separability of the issue dimensions, whatever it turns out to be -- and whether removing that feature collapses the case back into a mere modus vivendi that neither party has moral reason to honor. Finally, steelman the critic who holds that compromising on what you take to be evil is just complicity, and say whether the case survives.

I'd like the answer as continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
