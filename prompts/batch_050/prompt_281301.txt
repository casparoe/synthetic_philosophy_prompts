My roommate is in an undergraduate ethics seminar and has gotten a reputation for having surprisingly consistent intuitions about trolley-style cases, so we've started collecting her verdicts like data points. Here is her track record so far, with the cases as close to standard as I can describe them. In the classic switch case -- runaway trolley heading toward five workers, she can pull a lever diverting it onto a side track where it will kill one -- she says pull. In the footbridge case, where the only way to stop the trolley is to push a large stranger off a bridge so that his body halts it, she says firmly do not push. In the loop variant, where the side track curves back around to rejoin the main line before the five, and a single heavyset person is standing on the loop such that the trolley striking him would stop it in time, she says pull the lever -- she insists this is morally like the switch case, not the footbridge case, though she struggles to articulate why. Presented with a transplant case -- a surgeon who could kill one healthy patient to distribute organs to five who will otherwise die -- she says obviously not. And in a flood case, where an engineer can open a sluice gate that diverts an existing flood from a village of five hundred toward farmland where one home will be destroyed with its occupant inside, she says open it.

Now we're arguing about a new case she hasn't heard, and I want to predict what she'll say before I ask her. A runaway trolley is bearing down on five workers. There is no side track and no bridge. The only available intervention is a detonator wired to an abandoned concrete shed sitting directly on the tracks: the explosion would destroy the shed and bring the trolley to a halt well before it reaches the five. However, the blast will throw chunks of concrete into the ravine behind the shed, where a lone hiker -- whom you can see from where you stand -- will certainly be killed by the debris. Predict her verdict, and defend the prediction.

The part I actually care about is the reasoning. Identify at least two general principles that each rationalize her entire track record -- her pattern of yes/no verdicts across the five cases above -- but that deliver opposite verdicts on the demolition case. For each principle, state it precisely enough that it would still be usable on cases we haven't thought of, show how it yields each of her five prior verdicts, and say what it predicts here. Then tell me which principle you think is really hers, given her insistence that the loop case is like the switch case, and what the demolition case would have to be modified into in order to cleanly discriminate between the two principles -- i.e., propose one further case on which they disagree and where the loop/switch analogy doesn't already settle the answer. If you think her track record is actually inconsistent and no unified principle fits it, say so, but then explain which single verdict you'd have to flip to restore consistency, and why that's the right one to sacrifice.
