I'm a Year 12 student taking our school's philosophy elective, and this term the teacher has us peer-grade one another's practice essays before the real exam, using the same rubric she'll use. I've been assigned the essay below, written by a classmate. I want to be fair and rigorous, so I'd like you to grade it and draft the comments I'll hand back.

The practice exam question was:

"Every day, each of us imposes small risks on others without asking: we drive to work, cook with gas, host gatherings during flu season. Yet we also think some risk impositions are wrong even when nobody ends up hurt. Consider two cases. (1) Aster's Fireworks: the town of Aster holds an annual fireworks display over a residential neighborhood of about two thousand people. Careful estimates put the chance of a fatal malfunction in any given year at roughly one in fifty thousand, and the expected benefits to the town — enjoyment, tourism, tradition — plausibly outweigh the expected costs. The residents were never asked, and one of them, Marta, objects every year. (2) The Shortcut: Dev regularly drives home over the legal alcohol limit through a residential street, and an actuarial estimate of the annual risk he imposes on the people living there comes out equal to the risk imposed by the fireworks. He has never hurt anyone. If the numbers really match, are the two risk impositions morally on a par? If not, what explains the difference? In your answer, state and evaluate at least two candidate principles for distinguishing permissible from impermissible unconsented risk imposition — for example, expected net benefit to those exposed, actual consent, or reciprocity of risk within shared practices — and defend a verdict on both cases."

Here is the essay I'm grading:

"The central puzzle in this topic is that endangering someone and harming someone are not the same thing. The drunk driver who gets home safely has injured no one, yet almost everyone agrees he did something wrong. So whatever makes unconsented risk imposition wrong cannot depend on outcomes; otherwise we would have to excuse the lucky drunk driver, which amounts to letting moral luck do all the work. That is the right starting point, and it rules out any purely outcome-based account.

The most natural principle is expected net benefit: an unconsented risk imposition is permissible just when the expected benefits to those exposed outweigh the expected costs to them. The fireworks pass this test easily, since the neighborhood's share of the town's enjoyment and revenue exceeds the tiny statistical risk each resident bears. And where a risk really is worth imposing on people, they have in effect consented to it: any rational person would agree to the arrangement, and in large-scale cases hypothetical agreement is what consent actually amounts to. You cannot poll two thousand residents about fireworks, so the calculation stands in for their permission.

A Kantian might object that imposing any risk on a person without consent fails to respect them as an end in themselves. But as stated this proves too much, since it would forbid driving to the grocery store. A better rival is the reciprocity principle: risks are permissible when they arise within practices where everyone imposes roughly similar risks on everyone else over time. This handles driving, but it cannot handle the fireworks, because the residents impose no comparable risk on the town. So reciprocity fails, and expected net benefit remains the best principle.

The difficult case is Dev the commuter. If his risk equals the fireworks risk, the expected-benefit principle seems to treat them identically, which is an uncomfortable result. But the two cases can still be separated: Dev's risk serves no public purpose, whereas the fireworks benefit the whole town. Because his drunk driving lacks any purpose beyond his own convenience, it is impermissible even at the same numerical risk, while the fireworks are permissible.

So my verdict: the fireworks are a permissible unconsented imposition, Marta's objection notwithstanding, and Dev's driving is impermissible even though he has never hurt anyone. The wrongness of risk imposition is real but must be judged by principle, not by whether anyone ends up harmed."
