I'm a retired chemist who's been reading philosophy lately, and I keep running into prioritarianism — the idea (as I understand it) that a benefit matters more, morally speaking, the worse off the person receiving it is, so we should weight improvements to the badly-off more heavily even at some cost in total welfare. I also read about the so-called leveling-down objection to plain egalitarianism: if equality is what matters, then making the better-off worse off while helping nobody should count as an improvement in at least one respect, and that seems nuts.

Here's the real case I want analyzed through that lens. A few years ago Lowell High School in San Francisco, a public school with famously selective merit-based admissions, scrapped its admissions test and switched to a lottery like the other schools in the district. The stated reasons were equity — the school's student body didn't reflect the city's demographics, and the board thought selective admissions were part of the problem. (This was later reversed after a big public fight and a school board recall, but set the sequel aside.) As far as I can tell, nobody claimed the change would create new resources for the worse-off schools; the argument was more that selective schools themselves are unfair, that concentrating high-achieving kids in one building is bad for everyone else, that kind of thing.

So, questions. First: does prioritarianism condemn the Lowell change, endorse it, or just stay silent? The lottery kids who lost their Lowell spots seem made worse off, and it's not obvious any specific worse-off kid is made better off in a way prioritarianism can weigh — but maybe I'm wrong about that, so walk me through the strongest prioritarian case for the change (peer effects, resource concentration, whatever) and whether it actually pencils out conceptually, not numerically. Second: is this a genuine real-world instance of the thing the leveling-down objection warns about, or does it only look like one because I'm ignoring the defenders' empirical claims? In other words, does the objection depend on a factual premise (that no one benefits) that almost never holds in practice, and does that make the objection less interesting than philosophers seem to think, or more? Third: one thing that bugs me as a scientist — a lot turns here on whether you individuate outcomes as 'this kid got into Lowell' or 'the school system's distribution changed'. Does prioritarianism have anything principled to say about which description is the right one, or is that a hole in the theory?

Finally, the two-directional part: does the Lowell episode actually test prioritarianism at all, or does it just show that the theory goes quiet exactly when real politics gets going? Be honest about where the theory runs out instead of forcing it to give a verdict.
