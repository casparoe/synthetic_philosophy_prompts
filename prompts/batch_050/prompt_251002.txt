I am currently serving on a jury in an attempted murder trial, and the experience has unsettled me in a way I did not expect. Whatever verdict we reach, the defendant will be punished far less severely than if his victim had died, even though by all accounts he intended the death and was stopped only because the gun jammed. A philosophy-reading friend told me this asymmetry would have seemed backwards to Peter Abelard, who in his Ethics (also known as Know Thyself) located sin entirely in the consent of the will rather than in the deed. Out of curiosity I tried to reconstruct Abelard's position as a rigorous argument, and I now find that it seems to prove far more than I can accept. Please act as a specialist in medieval philosophy and the history of ethics, think carefully through each step before answering, and tell me where the argument goes wrong, if it does. Here is my reconstruction. (1) What makes a person morally culpable for wrongdoing is consent of the will to what the person believes to be wrong; the outward deed adds nothing to culpability, since the deed can be hindered by factors wholly outside the agent. (2) Consent is present just as fully when the intended deed is thwarted as when it is carried out, since thwarting happens after the consent is complete. (3) Therefore the thwarted attempter is exactly as culpable as the successful wrongdoer. (4) Just punishment tracks culpability and nothing else. (5) Therefore attempted and completed wrongs should be punished identically, and our sentencing practice of punishing attempts less is simply irrational. (6) Furthermore, since consent is consent to what one believes wrong, a person whose conscience is mistaken or dulled, and who therefore does not believe the act wrong, does not consent to wrongdoing at all and so does no moral wrong in acting. (7) So whether one does wrong depends entirely on the state of one's beliefs about right and wrong, never on what one actually does to other people. (8) But a consequence of (7) is that the callous person with a corrupt conscience wrongs no one in committing a harm, while the scrupulous person with a tender conscience can wrong someone merely by acting with a misguided reservation; moral education, by sharpening conscience, would then do nothing but multiply opportunities for wrongdoing. (9) That conclusion is absurd. (10) Therefore (1) is false, and the deed itself must carry some of the moral weight after all. I would most like to keep (1) and (4), since both strike me as deeply plausible, and I note that the conclusion of (5) is one some legal theorists have actually defended, so I am unsure whether the argument overreaches at (5), at (8), or earlier. Please identify which premise or inference you think should be rejected, explain why it appeared sound, distinguish carefully between consent to an act and consent to a known wrong if that distinction matters, address whether culpable ignorance changes the analysis of (6), and state plainly what each way of blocking the argument costs, including what Abelard himself would have to concede. I would like your answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, written as one would write a thoughtful letter, and in American English.
