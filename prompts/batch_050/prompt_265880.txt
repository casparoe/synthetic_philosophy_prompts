I'm preparing teaching materials for a graduate seminar in philosophy of biology, and the unit on the "survival of the fittest is a tautology" objection always goes the same way: students nod along with the charge, then can't reconstruct it, then can't say whether the propensity interpretation of fitness actually answers it or merely renames it. What I want from you is a decision tree that sorts the positions on this debate by the claims that divide them, rather than by the names in the literature. The tree should start from the objection itself (roughly: if fitness is defined by differential survival and reproduction, then "the fittest survive" explains nothing) and branch on questions my students can answer before they know any labels. Please build the branching around these diagnostic forks, phrased as concrete questions: whether fitness is a genuine causal property of organisms (something like a disposition that does work in producing survival) or merely a summary statistic over actual outcomes; whether fitness claims are about actual descendants or expected/probabilistic descendant counts, and whether that expectation is objective or only our epistemic device; whether the environment-specificity of fitness (fit relative to what?) undermines or merely qualifies the explanatory reading; and whether a propensity that only ever manifests as survival can be tested independently of the survival it is invoked to explain.

I'd like the leaves to cover at least: a dispositional propensity view on which fitness is a causal power that varies independently of its manifestations; a statisticalist or deflationary reading on which fitness differences are bookkeeping and selection is a statistical phenomenon rather than a causal one; a view conceding that "the fittest survive" is a near-truism but insisting that fitness earns its keep as a predictive or unifying quantity rather than an explainer; and a view on which the objection dissolves because natural selection was never in the business of explaining individual survival, only trait distributions. If some combination of answers in the space is unoccupied, say briefly whether that's because the position is incoherent, merely unpopular, or overlooked. To make the sorting concrete, run each leaf through one test case: two field voles, identical in every heritable respect, where one is eaten by an owl by sheer luck and the other leaves ten offspring — does the surviving vole count as fitter, and does the theory have to say so? Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate. Keep the whole thing — tree, leaves, test-case results, and flags — under 700 words.
