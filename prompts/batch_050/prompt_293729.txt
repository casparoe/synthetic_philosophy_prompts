You are a philosopher of science with long experience in the mechanisms literature — the debates about how mechanisms are individuated, composed, and identified across contexts. I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of scientific practice, and I need you to construct a decision tree. Please think carefully and work through the problem in stages rather than jumping to a finished product.

The theme of the seminar is this: every judgment that something is an *anomaly* — a fault in a machine, a mutant phenotype, an unexpected experimental result, a drug that stops working — tacitly depends on a prior judgment that we are still dealing with the *same mechanism*. If a cell reroutes its signaling around a knocked-out kinase and the pathway keeps working, is that the same mechanism behaving surprisingly, or a different mechanism behaving exactly as it should? If a repair shop replaces every gear in a clock with functionally equivalent parts of a different design, is a later failure a malfunction of "the clock" or the normal behavior of a new device? Anomaly detection, in science and engineering alike, silently presupposes an answer to the question of mechanism identity — and different answers draw the line between "anomalous operation" and "normal operation of something else" in very different places.

Write a decision tree that takes a user from the starting commitment "I observed system S produce output O′ where I expected O" to a named position on what makes a mechanism the same mechanism over time, across instances, and across substrates. Each branch node should be a diagnostic question built around a concrete case — a rewired metabolic pathway, a quartz movement swapped into a mechanical watch case, a neural circuit in which a duplicated gene has taken over a component's role, an algorithm reimplemented on new hardware, a biological structure co-opted for a function it did not originally serve, a repaired machine with substitute parts — phrased so that a user can answer without already knowing which position the answer leads to. Leaves should include at least: strict identity of entities and activities; identity by causal-graph or organizational isomorphism; functional or input-output equivalence; etiological or selected-effect identity; and a purpose-relative pragmatism on which "same mechanism" is always relative to a contrast class. Mark any leaf with no committed defenders, and flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot pull apart.

Structure your answer in three parts. First, brainstorm: list eight to ten candidate diagnostic questions and briefly note which you discarded and why. Second, present the tree itself (ten to fourteen nodes), with each leaf carrying its position's name and one sentence on its most awkward consequence for anomaly detection — what it would over-count or under-count as anomalous. Third, a short coda: argue for which leaf would best serve a working scientist's anomaly-detection practice, and explain why that is a different question from which leaf is metaphysically correct.
