I chair the quality committee at a regional hospital, and the question in front of us has recently become uncomfortably personal: my father is scheduled for elective spinal surgery here in six weeks, and I must decide whether to recommend that his surgeon use the minimally invasive protocol we piloted last year or the standard open procedure. The committee is deadlocked, and I suspect the deadlock rests on a statistical confusion I only half understand.

The figures from our pilot, covering about 800 patients, are these. Overall, the new protocol shows a higher 90-day complication rate than the standard procedure, roughly 12% against 9%. Yet when patients are divided into four bands by their preoperative frailty score, the new protocol does better within every single band. Then a colleague on the committee re-sliced the same data by length of hospital stay -- three days or fewer versus more than three days -- and within those two groups the advantage vanishes; the standard procedure looks as good or better. A second colleague argues that the entire stratification exercise is misguided and that only the bottom-line aggregate matters, since that is what a randomly arriving patient should expect. A third points out that the new protocol was deliberately designed to shorten hospital stays, and that surgeons who adopted it tended to be our most technically skilled ones.

Rank the following five positions by how much weight each deserves in my decision, from most to least, with one sentence of justification per position: (a) trust the aggregate complication rate; (b) trust the frailty-band breakdown; (c) trust the length-of-stay breakdown; (d) suspend judgment until a randomized trial assigns patients to protocols; (e) weight the results by which surgeons self-selected into the pilot. Then give me a single number between 0 and 1 expressing your confidence that the new protocol is genuinely the better choice for a patient like my father, who is 71 with a moderate frailty score.

Please structure your answer in four named sections, in this order: "The Ranking" (the five positions ordered, one sentence each), "The Decisive Detail" (one paragraph explaining which way of slicing the data is the right one and why the other slicing is misleading), "What Would Change the Order" (three or four bullet points on evidence that would rearrange your ranking), and "Advice for My Father" (at most three sentences, including your numerical confidence and its justification).
