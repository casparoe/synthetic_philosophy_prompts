Okay so I write novels, mostly quiet domestic stuff, and I've been circling a new one that basically rips off the Abraham and Isaac setup without the Sunday school framing. My character is a guy, a translator, lives in Copenhagen-ish nowhere, and he becomes convinced -- not via a voice from the sky, something slower and deniable, a sequence of things he can't explain any other way -- that he's being asked to do something genuinely awful. Not kill his kid, I'm not doing that, but something with the same structure: a demand that violates the thing he loves most and that everyone around him would rightly condemn, where refusing means losing whatever the demand is coming from. So the teleological suspension of the ethical is basically my plot engine.

Here's my problem. I've been reading around the secondary discussion of Fear and Trembling and there's a ton of it, and I can't use all of it in 300 pages. A novel isn't a seminar; I get maybe three or four live questions that the book can actually stage and worry at, and the rest has to be scenery. So: which open questions or contested implications of the teleological suspension matter most for dramatizing this situation, and which ones are overrated as raw material for fiction?

Some candidates I've been chewing on, feel free to reject the framing: whether the suspension only makes sense if the command really is from God (and what my book loses if I keep it radically ambiguous whether it is), whether Abraham's silence -- the fact that he can't explain himself to Sarah or Isaac or anyone -- is the actually interesting part rather than the sacrifice itself, whether the knight of faith / tragic hero distinction gives me a way to structure what makes my character different from a garden-variety zealot, and the ugly one: whether the whole framework is just an apologetic for fanatics and the novel has a responsibility to take that seriously as a live option rather than a misunderstanding to be cleared up.

Rank them (or your own better list) and defend the ranking -- I want to know what makes a question load-bearing for a narrative about someone doing the indefensible on faith, not just that it's unresolved in the literature. Also tell me which implication people keep drawing from the Abraham story that you think doesn't actually follow, because I'd love a character who believes that implication and is wrong. Roughly: what would a reader who knows this debate cold walk away from my book wishing I'd engaged, and what would they not miss at all?
