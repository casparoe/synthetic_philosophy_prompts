I'm writing an essay on Twelve Angry Men for a political theory course and I want to read it through Habermas on communicative rationality, and I'd like your help stress-testing the reading before I commit to it. The set-up seems almost suspiciously perfect for it: twelve blokes locked in a room, formal power differences suspended (the foreman has no extra vote), nothing to do but talk, and a rule that nobody leaves until they agree. Juror 8 even opens by saying he doesn't know the kid is innocent, he just wants to talk it through -- which sounds awfully like action oriented towards mutual understanding rather than towards winning.

But the more I think about it, the less sure I am the film is on Habermas's side. Some of what moves the verdict is the better argument, but a lot of it isn't: the knife business is theatre, the timing reconstruction with the old man's testimony is basically a stunt, Juror 7 switches because the wind changes direction, and Juror 3 gets emotionally waterboarded until he breaks down rather than being argued round. And the film never tells us whether the defendent actually did it -- the jury only has to reach reasonable doubt, which is not the same as consensus on the truth. Habermas's claim, as I understand it, is that validity is grounded in a consensus that could only be explained by the unforced force of the better argument. So does a verdict of 'not proven beyond reasonable doubt' even count as the kind of consensus his theory is about, or is the film solving a different problem from the one discourse ethics sets?

So, concretely: does Twelve Angry Men dramatise communicative rationality working under roughly ideal conditions, or does it quietly show that real deliberation only ever gets anywhere by mixing communicative and strategic action -- and is the film itself aware of the difference? Where does the ideal speech situation reading genuinely illuminate particular scenes (the bigot's speech where the others literally turn their backs on him looks a lot like the group excluding him from the discourse by tacit agreement), and where does it flatten the film? I'd also like your view on what the film adds that the theory lacks: my hunch is something about how exhaustion, shame, heat and sheer physical discomfort do persuasive work that no account of validity claims quite captures, but I might be overreading.

One request: be honest with me rather than polite. If you think pairing a 1957 jury drama with discourse ethics is forced, or I've got Habermas wrong somewhere -- my grip on the communicative/strategic distinction is a bit shaky, especially on where perlocutionary effects are supposed to fit -- then say so plainly instead of playing along, and push back on the premise if it needs it.
