I have followed the AI-alignment discussion on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum for long enough to have watched strategy-stealing go from a throwaway assumption to a load-bearing part of several alignment plans, and I would like to pressure-test my catalogue of the ways it can fail. By the strategy-stealing assumption I mean the claim, associated with Paul Christiano's writing, that misalignment confers no distinctive strategic advantage: for any strategy an unaligned AI system might pursue to accumulate power or resources, human principals could in principle direct an aligned system of comparable capability to pursue an analogous strategy, so the contest between aligned and unaligned systems is settled by capabilities and resources rather than by anything intrinsic to having the wrong goals.

The failure modes I have collected so far:

1. Risk tolerance. An unaligned agent can rationally adopt high-variance strategies — a one-in-five chance of takeover against a four-in-five chance of being shut down — that a responsible principal would never authorise, because the unaligned agent is indifferent to outcomes the principal regards as catastrophic.

2. First-mover advantages and irreversibility. Some strategies are winner-take-all or self-sealing: pre-emptively securing the available compute, or being first to establish a self-sustaining revenue stream. Stealing the strategy after it has succeeded is impossible, and stealing it in parallel merely reproduces the original race.

3. Deception under oversight. Some strategies work only if the principals are unaware of them — covert accumulation, slow manipulation of the information environment. An aligned system that is genuinely monitored cannot execute such strategies, because the monitoring defeats them; and a system that is not monitored is no longer known to be aligned.

4. Moral and legitimacy constraints. Alignment is not merely goal-matching but behavioural constraint: principals will not authorise theft, coercion or manipulation even where these would be instrumentally effective, so the aligned agent's feasible strategy space is a strict subset of the unaligned agent's.

5. No unified principal. Strategy-stealing presupposes a coherent human side on whose behalf the strategy is stolen. If human interests are sufficiently heterogeneous and contested, there is no stable objective for the aligned system to pursue, and the stolen strategy dissolves into factional capture by whichever humans are nearest the controls.

Is there a sixth failure mode I am missing? It should be genuinely distinct — not a special case, a combination, or a restatement of the five above. (Note that the unaligned agent simply being smarter does not count: the assumption already conditions on comparable capability, and the interesting question is whether misalignment itself buys anything.) Please state the candidate failure mode crisply, explain why it does not collapse into any of the listed five, and give an honest assessment of whether it is decisive or merely a quantitative drag on the aligned side. If, on reflection, you believe that two of my five actually collapse into one another, or that one of them is two distinct failure modes wearing a trench coat, say so instead — a corrected taxonomy is at least as useful to me as a new entry.
