A friend and I have been going back and forth for weeks about whether large language models genuinely understand anything, and we eventually noticed the real problem: we each had a working criterion for understanding that we were applying case by case, but neither of us could state it. So here are my verdicts on a set of cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. A pocket calculator produces flawless arithmetic, and I'm confident it understands nothing. A student who reproduces an exam-perfect summary of Kant but falls apart when asked to apply the categorical imperative to a scenario phrased even slightly differently from the seminar's examples does not, in my judgment, understand Kant -- despite all that fluent text. Yet a chess engine trained purely on self-play, never given the rules in words and incapable of answering a single question about chess, seems to me to understand chess in some real sense: it grasps pins, zugzwang, compensation for material. In Searle's Chinese room I'm inclined to say that neither the person inside nor the system as a whole understands Chinese, though I admit I'm less sure about the system than about the person. A large language model that writes a lucid, correct explanation of general relativity and then, later in the same conversation, confidently asserts something flatly incompatible with it without noticing -- I lean toward saying it doesn't understand what it's saying, but I feel the pull the other way more strongly here than with the calculator. Finally, the case that unsettles me most: imagine a colossal lookup table, built by exhaustive precomputation, containing a thoughtful and appropriate reply to every conversation up to some fixed length. Its behavior is indistinguishable from a person who understands, and yet I find myself wanting to deny it understanding -- which is hard to square with my verdict on the chess engine, since both are, at bottom, fixed mechanisms that merely produce apt outputs.

What I'd like from you: state the most precise principle you can that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, and say which case motivates each clause of it. I suspect something like "understanding is the capacity for apt response that is robust under novel rephrasing and transfer, grounded in how the responses are produced rather than in the responses themselves" is doing the work for me, but I can't see how it lets me keep both the chess engine and the lookup-table verdicts, and I suspect it may also misclassify the Chinese room relative to my intuition. Tell me which of my verdicts your principle forces me to abandon, and don't soften that part -- if two of my judgments are simply irreconcilable under any non-gerrymandered principle, I'd rather know. Then tell me what the principle predicts for cases I haven't listed: a dog that responds correctly to commands it has never heard before, and a hypothetical future language model that never contradicts itself and handles every novel variation flawlessly. Finally, state plainly what consideration or argument would change your own verdict -- where the principle you've offered is most likely to fail, and what a defender of the verdicts you overturned would say in their defense.
