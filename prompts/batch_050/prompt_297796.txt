My friend sent me this writeup from a blog about something called strong longtermism and I want to know if it's actually a fair description or a hit piece dressed up as one. Here's the summary:

"Strong longtermism is the view, defended by some Oxford philosophers, that because there could be trillions of future people, the interests of future generations count for more than the interests of people alive today -- so the key moral priority of our time is positively shaping the far future, full stop. On this view, we can predict which actions now will have good effects millions of years out, and those predictions are what justify redirecting money and talent away from things like global poverty toward things like extinction risk. The obvious problem is that nobody can actually know the long-run consequences of their actions -- the same butterfly-effect logic that makes the view seem exciting also undermines it, since any action might turn out catastrophically bad far down the line. So strong longtermism refutes itself, and what's left is basically regular utilitarianism with a sci-fi marketing budget, which means it inherits all the usual objections to utilitarianism and adds nothing."

My gut says a few things here are off but I can't quite pin down where. In particular, the bit about future people's interests counting for *more* seems like the kind of thing the actual defenders would deny -- I thought their point was about how *many* future people there could be, not about discounting the present. But the blogger is generally pretty sharp so I don't want to just assume. Could you go through it and tell me which parts a careful strong longtermist would accept as stated, which are subtle distortions (and of what), and whether the "self-refuting" argument at the end still works once you fix whatever needs fixing? Also curious whether the last line about just being rebranded utilitarianism is right, since I thought at least some longtermists weren't utilitarians.
