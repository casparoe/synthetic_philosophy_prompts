I'm preparing a survey for a graduate philosophy of biology seminar on the causal-role (systemic) account of function associated with Cummins, and I want to be sure my catalogue of objections is exhaustive before I draft. Here is what I have so far. First, the overgeneration objection: because a function on this view is any causal contribution a component makes to some capacity of a containing system, the account confers functions on mere side effects — the heart's rhythmic noise contributes to some capacity or other just as surely as its pumping does, and nothing internal to the theory says why pumping is the heart's function while noise-making is not. Second, the malfunction objection: if a part's function just is what it in fact does, then no part can fail to perform its function, and the theory cannot make sense of a diseased organ that retains a function it no longer discharges. Third, the indeterminacy objection: which contributions count as functions depends on which containing system and which capacity the analyst chooses to explain, so functions come out as relative to explanatory interests rather than as objective features of the organism. Fourth, the asymmetry objection: the theory can explain what a present trait does, but our ordinary and biological notion of function is also supposed to explain why the trait is there at all, and a purely dispositional account is silent on that.

Is there a fifth objection, genuinely distinct from these four? By distinct I mean not a restatement, a special case, or two of the above run together: another instance of a spurious effect acquiring a function would just be overgeneration again, and an argument that the account misclassifies broken or inert organs is malfunction again. If you can produce a fifth, tell me which feature of the theory it targets, how it differs structurally from my four, and give it a compact statement suitable for a seminar handout. If you cannot, say so plainly rather than handing me a repackaged version of an objection already on the list. In either case, I would also like your honest assessment of how damaging the strongest objection on the table really is — in particular, whether a defender of the etiological (selected-effects) account can wield it confidently, or whether it cuts against selected-effect views too, since I would rather learn that in advance than during the question period.
