I’m a retired field ecologist who has only recently started reading philosophy, and I’ve been asked to moderate a small colloquium with the author of a new book on Goodman’s grue problem. Please draft ten interview questions for me, with a likely answer and a follow-up question after each one.

The guest defends a position she calls laboratory projectibility. Roughly, she accepts Goodman’s lesson that the formal fit between past observations and a hypothesis cannot by itself tell us which predicates are projectible. But she rejects both Goodman’s appeal to linguistic entrenchment and the common retreat to whatever predicates happen to appear in our best scientific theories. Her alternative is that a predicate becomes projectible when investigators have learned how to make its application counterfactually stable under deliberate interventions. Thus green is projectible not merely because we habitually use it, and not because mature physics happens to mention it, but because we know how color classifications survive changes in lighting, observers, instruments, sample preparation, and so forth. A gerrymandered predicate like grue, where an object counts as grue if examined before some future time and green or examined afterward and blue, fails because no corresponding intervention practice stabilizes it.

I want questions that go beyond letting her repeat that summary. In particular, I’d like the sequence to press on:

- whether deliberate intervention is itself being identified using predicates whose projectibility is already assumed;
- whether her criterion risks making projectibility depend on the current state of laboratory technology;
- how she handles predicates from historical or observational sciences, such as species, glacier retreat, or market crash, where controlled intervention is limited or impossible;
- what she says about a future community that develops elaborate intervention practices around grue-like predicates;
- how she distinguishes a genuinely stable predicate from one protected by ad hoc adjustments to instruments and background conditions;
- whether scientific revolutions show that intervention-stability is only consensus under another name;
- whether she is solving Goodman’s problem or just relocating it from confirmation to experimental practice;
- what her view implies for extrapolating beyond the range of conditions in which the interventions were tested.

I spent forty years around scientists, so I’m comfortable with technical questions, but philosophy jargon sometimes loses me. Make the questions pointed and natural to ask aloud, not miniature essays. Also make them progressively harder to escape with general talk about the success of science. Where the guest has an obvious reply available, include the follow-up that corners her a bit more. One or two questions may use a concrete ecological example if that helps, but please don’t turn the whole interview into bird counts or forest plots. And don’t ask biographical fluff like what first interested her in philosphy.
