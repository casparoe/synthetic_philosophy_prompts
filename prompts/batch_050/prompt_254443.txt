Consider two theorems that are commonly invoked to support the slogan that, for Bayesian agents, "the data eventually washes out the priors":

1. Posterior consistency (Doob-style). Fix a single Bayesian agent with prior π over a parameter θ governing an infinite data stream. Then for π-almost-every value of θ, the agent's posterior, conditioned on ever more data, converges to a point mass on the true θ.

2. Merging of opinions (Blackwell–Dubins-style). Take two Bayesian agents with priors P and Q over the same sequence space. If Q is absolutely continuous with respect to P (i.e., every event P assigns probability zero is also assigned probability zero by Q), then with P-probability one, the two agents' posteriors over the remaining future data become arbitrarily close (in total variation) as the amount of observed data grows.

Please compare these two results with an eye to what "washing out of priors" actually means. Structure your answer around the following:

(a) The two theorems have different logical shapes: consistency is about one agent's beliefs matching reality, whereas merging is about two agents' beliefs matching each other. Make this precise by answering: Can two agents' posteriors merge even though neither is consistent (i.e., neither converges to the truth)? Can an agent be consistent with respect to its own prior and yet fail to merge with another agent? Give an example or brief argument for each.

(b) Consider two agents forecasting an infinite sequence of flips of a coin with unknown bias p ∈ [0,1]. Agent A has a uniform prior over p. Agent B places half of its prior mass on the atom p = 1/2 and distributes the other half uniformly over [0,1]. Determine which of the two priors is absolutely continuous with respect to the other (the answer is not symmetric). Does the merging theorem apply, and if so, under whose measure does merging occur almost surely? Does it also occur almost surely under the other agent's measure in this case? Now modify the example: suppose instead that B's prior assigns probability one to p ∈ [0.4, 0.6] while the true bias is 0.7. What happens to consistency and merging now, and why?

(c) Finally, assess the dialectical significance of these results. They are sometimes used to argue that disagreements between rational Bayesian agents are merely temporary, or that science converges on consensus regardless of starting assumptions. Drawing on your answers to (a) and (b), explain carefully what is right and what is misleading about such uses. In particular, address: (i) the sense in which both theorems are indexed to a prior's own measure and why that limits what they can show across agents; (ii) what the theorems are silent about concerning rates of convergence and finite-horizon disagreement; and (iii) what changes if the true data-generating process lies outside the support of an agent's model class altogether (model misspecification).
