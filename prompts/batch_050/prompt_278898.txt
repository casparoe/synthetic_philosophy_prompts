At a recent workshop on aesthetics, a colleague presented what she called the distributed-causation argument against treating AI-generated images as creative achievements. Her argument ran as follows. (1) Creativity is an achievement property: an object is creatively made only if some agent is properly responsible for the features in virtue of which the object is novel, valuable, or interesting. (2) An agent is properly responsible for a feature only when its presence can be credited to the exercise of that agent’s intentional capacities—for example, because the agent intended it, foresaw it, understood why it occurred, or could have reliably controlled it. (3) Someone prompting a generative image system typically selects only broad constraints, while the system determines most of the image’s distinguishing details; neither the prompter nor the model’s designers intended those particular details, and the model itself lacks intentions, understanding, and reasons of its own. (4) Therefore, no one is properly responsible for most of the features that make a particular AI-generated image aesthetically interesting. (5) The prompter may discover, select, or curate a creative-looking result, but cannot thereby count as the creative maker of that result. (6) Hence using a generative model is relevantly unlike painting or drawing and more like finding a striking object on a beach and choosing to display it. (7) But the same reasoning also applies to photographers who rely on complex camera mechanisms, composers who use chance procedures, film directors who depend on actors and editors, and architects whose plans are materially transformed by engineers and builders. (8) Therefore, most tool-mediated, chance-mediated, and collaborative art is not a creative achievement either. That conclusion seems plainly too strong, yet each earlier step has some intuitive appeal.

Where exactly does the argument go wrong, if it does? Please identify the premise or inference you would reject, but do not simply say that AI art involves human input; explain what kind of responsibility that input must supply, and why responsibility for an overall result does or does not require control over its particular features. I would especially like an answer that distinguishes agency from authorship, creative achievement from mere causal contribution, and control from creditworthiness. I am reluctant to abandon the distinction between making something and merely finding it, since otherwise every lucky discovery would count as creative production; but I also do not want an account on which photographers, directors, and aleatoric composers are never genuinely creative. What is the least costly revision, and what must it say about cases in which a prompter engages in many rounds of selection and refinement, as opposed to accepting the first output?
