I'm giving the toast at my PhD supervisor's retirement dinner next month. She spent about thirty-five years defending prioritarianism -- the idea that how much a benefit matters depends on how badly off the person recieving it already is -- and for roughly the same thirty-five years she's been swatting away the levelling-down objection at conferences, which is the one where someone says her view (or anything like it) can't object to making everyone equal by dragging the better-off down to nobody's benefit. She has a stock line about how the objection confuses what her view values with what it forbids, delivered in a tone that suggests she'd rather be having a different conversation.

The problem is my draft. I read it back and every other line is some version of "she rose to the very top of her field" or "she outshone her peers" or "the most distinguished member of the department", and I can hear her voice in my head pointing out that praising someone for being above other people is celebrating the gap, i.e. celebrating exactly the bit her entire career says carries no weight. A toast where the praise is all about relative position is, on her view, praising the wrong thing. But the obvious fix -- finding nice things to say that apply equally to everyone in the department -- is basically levelling down in toast form, and she'd catch that too.

The room is mixed: half philosophers who will notice every move, half family, neighbours, and her Auntie Moira, who thinks philosophy is "the arguing thing" and will be watching me over a glass of sherry. What I need is a five-minute toast that: (1) honours her in a way her own view actually permits, so the praise has to be structured around what her work and teaching did for people rather than where it placed her above them -- and honestly it should make something of the fact that this is a constraint, not pretend it isn't there; (2) contains exactly one joke about the levelling-down objection that lands for both halves of the room (the catering at this dinner is famously stingy, if that's useful material); and (3) is a toast, not a seminar -- no "as Parfit argued", no numbered premises, the philosophy has to be load-bearing but invisible-ish to the sherry end of the table.

Please write it in British English, and keep it something I could actually say out loud without cringing.
