You are a political philosopher specializing in egalitarian theories of distributive justice, and you have run this exercise in seminars many times. I am preparing a self-assessment instrument for a second-year undergraduate political philosophy course: a decision tree that takes a student from the bare starting commitment "some economic inequalities are unjust" to a named position on distributive justice. Think carefully about the logical structure before writing; the quality of the tree depends on the diagnostic questions, not on the labels.

Please produce the following, in this order:

1. A brief brainstorming sketch (150 words at most) mapping the logical space before you build anything: which independent axes of disagreement the debate actually contains (for example: what the currency of egalitarian concern is; whether responsibility cancels injustice; whether equality is a distributive pattern or a social relation; whether what matters is strict equality, a threshold, or priority to the worse-off). Note which axes are genuinely independent and which collapse into each other.

2. The decision tree itself, presented as indented text or a numbered flowchart. Constraints:

- Between six and nine branching questions. Each question must be phrased as a concrete case or a plain claim that a student can answer yes or no without knowing any position names or technical vocabulary. Do not ask "do you accept brute luck/option luck?"; embed the distinction in a case.
- At least three of the branching questions must be built from these vignettes: (a) a skydiver who declined cheap insurance is seriously injured, and the question is whether the community owes him the same costly rescue as an identically injured lightning-strike victim; (b) a person raised on cheap food who, through a purely optional course, cultivates a taste for expensive wine and is then miserable on an ordinary income, compared with a person born with an equally expensive medical need; (c) a person born deaf whose material situation is fully compensated but who objects that the compensation regime forces her to present herself as pitiable and defective before officials; (d) a surplus that can either raise the worst-off person from 9 to 10 units of wellbeing or raise three people from 19 to 20, where 20 is widely regarded as enough.
- The leaves must include luck egalitarianism, relational (democratic) egalitarianism, sufficientarianism, prioritarianism, a desert-based view, and an anti-pattern (libertarian) view. Add any further named positions that naturally fall out of your question set.
- Each leaf carries the position's name plus one sentence stating its most awkward commitment -- the implication a defender least likes to say out loud.
- For every internal node, note in brackets which positions that answer eliminates.

3. A short designer's-notes section covering: why you ordered the first question first (which question does the most classificatory work, which is not necessarily the one that sounds most fundamental); any pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate and what further question would separate them; and any unoccupied combinations of answers -- positions the tree generates but nobody defends -- with a one-line diagnosis of whether the slot is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Keep the whole deliverable under 1,200 words. Do not include citations, references to specific texts, or a reading list; the named positions and the cases must carry all the weight.
