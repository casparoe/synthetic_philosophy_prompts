I've been reading LessWrong and adjacent blogs for years, and I want to write down my actual commitments about strategic rationality and see whether they survive contact with each other. Here they are:

1. The one result in game theory I'd defend as a theorem of rationality rather than a convention: a rational agent never plays a strictly dominated strategy, and under common knowledge of rationality the outcome of a game must survive iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies.

2. Mixed-strategy equilibria shouldn't be read as requiring literal coin flips. I take the equilibrium-in-beliefs reading: what matters is what each player believes about the others' play, and each player simply chooses some best response to those beliefs.

3. In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a psychological copy of myself, cooperation is the rational move — Hofstadter-style superrationality, or the more recent Löbian-flavored arguments. The correlation between our decisions makes defection lose.

4. In a guess-two-thirds-of-the-average game against ordinary people, playing anywhere near the unique equilibrium is a mistake; you should target something like 20–30, because real opponents don't iterate very far.

5. In the finitely iterated prisoner's dilemma with a known end, I accept that the backward-induction argument shows defection in every round is the only outcome consistent with common knowledge of rationality — but I also think two genuinely rational players who both understood this would find their way to cooperating for most of the game.

Please audit this set: which pairs are in real tension and which only look like it, ordered by severity; for each genuine tension, what the cheapest fix is and what it costs; and finally, which single revision would clean up the most. I want a verdict, not a menu of options — if one of these has to go, name it and tell me why.
