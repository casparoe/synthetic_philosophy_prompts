I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have been trying to work out when it is ethically acceptable to create or use a griefbot. By a griefbot, I mean an interactive program that generates new messages in the style of a dead person, rather than merely replaying recordings or displaying old messages. I am interested in the ethics, not in whether any particular arrangement would be legal or technically reliable.

While thinking about examples, I have found three distinctions useful, although I am not committed to them:

- Archival fidelity: whether the bot only recombines things the person actually said or wrote, or instead offers new judgements in their voice.
- Relational licence: whether the user was close enough to the dead person, and sufficiently authorised by them or by the circumstances, to receive such a simulation.
- Posthumous drift: whether the bot remains fixed at the person's death or continues learning from later events and users, thereby becoming unlike the person it represents.

These are my present verdicts:

A. A terminally ill woman, after receiving a clear explanation of the technology, authorises a bot trained on her own messages for her spouse alone. It is clearly labelled as a simulation, cannot learn from new events, and deletes itself after two years. This seems clearly permissible.

B. The same woman instead expressly forbids such a bot. After her death, her adult son builds one from her private messages because he believes it will help him grieve. This seems clearly impermissible.

C. A man dies unexpectedly without leaving instructions. His sister creates a bot from his public essays, talks and social-media posts, and only close relatives use it, knowing that it is a simulation. This seems permissible, although uncomfortable.

D. In an otherwise similar case, a widow creates a bot from private exchanges that include confidences supplied by living friends who never expected those messages to be reused. This seems impermissible unless the living contributors consent or their confidences are removed.

E. A dying father creates a bot for his eight-year-old daughter. It can retell family memories and explain values he explicitly recorded, but cannot answer questions about matters he never discussed. Her surviving mother can suspend or delete it. This seems permissible.

F. When that daughter turns sixteen, she wants the same bot to advise her about relationships, university and career choices by extrapolating from her father's general outlook. I feel this becomes impermissible, despite his original consent, because the bot would acquire too much counterfactual authority.

G. A dead woman had refused to forgive her abusive son. Her griefbot later tells her daughter, in the mother's voice, that she now wants the family to reconcile. The statement is invented from the model rather than drawn from anything she said. This seems clearly impermissible.

H. A murder victim's family brings a griefbot to a sentencing hearing so that it can appeal for mercy in the victim's voice, partly on the basis of things she said while alive. Reading her genuine writings aloud seems acceptable, but treating the bot as a kind of continuing witness does not.

I. A celebrity's estate authorises a paid fan bot. It discloses that it is artificial, but is designed to make lonely users feel personally remembered and to encourage daily conversations. This seems impermissible, even with the estate's consent, because it commercialises attachment to the dead.

J. A company offers a sunset bot that permits ten conversations, refuses to give new opinions, reminds the user that the person is dead, encourages contact with the living, and then permanently deletes itself. This seems not merely permissible but among the better uses.

K. A man gives fully informed consent to an open-ended bot that will keep reading the news, learning from users and revising its political and moral views for decades after his death. I still feel it should not be released, or at least that his authorisation alone does not settle the matter. I recognise that this is difficult to reconcile with my verdict in A.

Please identify the principle, or small set of principles, that best captures these verdicts. Do not merely classify the cases as respectful or disrespectful. I want the principle stated precisely enough to explain why consent is decisive in some cases but not others, why a simulation may be permissible for a child but become problematic later, and why commercialisation or self-deletion might matter. If no reasonably simple principle preserves all my verdicts, say explicitly which verdict should be revised and why that revision is preferable to adding another qualification.

After explaining the principle, apply it to two further cases: first, a dead novelist's authorised bot continues an unfinished novel in her style, with clear disclosure and proceeds going to her children; secondly, a bereaved partner uses a bot only during acute moments of grief, but the bot is permitted to tell him what the dead person would advise about an irreversible medical decision.
