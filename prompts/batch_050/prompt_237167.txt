I am a PhD student in political theory, and I currently sit on my graduate school's Student Resources Committee. We have been asked to settle the design of a new scheme, the "Consultation Bank": starting next year, every doctoral student will be credited 40 hours per semester of one-on-one methods and statistics consultation (a scarce resource the school currently rations informally through advisor connections). The committee is deadlocked over the allocation rule, and the disagreement turns out to be a live instance of the "equality of what?" debate in egalitarian theory.

Three proposals are on the table. Proposal A credits every student 40 hours, full stop -- equal resources, with conversion left to the individual. Proposal B allocates hours so as to equalize research progress and degree-related wellbeing across students, which in practice means far more hours for some (e.g., students from underprepared backgrounds, students whose projects require heavy quantitative work, students whose disability or caregiving load slows conversion of consultation into output) and correspondingly fewer for others. Proposal C aims to equalize what students can *do* with their doctoral training -- their effective freedom to pursue the research trajectories they have reason to value -- without committing the school to chasing each student's subjective wellbeing, on the thought that the school owes students genuine opportunity, not felt satisfaction.

The complicating details that divide us: some students freely choose low-consultation projects and would spend surplus hours wastefully; one student has "expensive tastes" in the sense that she can only feel her degree is going well if she publishes in top journals, and equalizing her wellbeing would swallow a quarter of the bank; several students with chronic illness convert consultation hours into output at roughly half the typical rate but report that the hours themselves substantially improve their experience of the program; and a vocal group argues that any departure from equal hours treats students as means to an institutional outcome rather than as agents responsible for their own ambitions.

Please write the committee's decision memo recommending one of the three proposals (or a precisely specified hybrid), addressed to the Dean. You are a political philosopher with expertise in distributive justice; write with the care and rigor of someone who has internalized the welfarist, resourcist, and capability-based positions and their standard objections -- expensive tastes, responsibility and ambition-sensitivity, the fetishism charge against resource metrics, and the paternalism charge against capability lists -- but reason from the arguments themselves rather than leaning on citations. Think carefully and slowly. Begin with a short private brainstorming section in which you identify the single strongest argument for each proposal and the single most damaging objection each faces; then write the memo proper. The memo must (i) state a clear, actionable recommendation, (ii) show exactly how the recommended rule handles the expensive-tastes student, the chronically ill students, and the low-consultation choosers, and (iii) give an honest, non-strawmanned treatment of the strongest consideration favoring the proposal you reject -- strong enough that a committee member who holds that view would feel their position was taken seriously, even if ultimately answered.
