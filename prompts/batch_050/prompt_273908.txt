So I run a small internal tools team at a mid-size company, and twice a year I have to sit in on the portfolio review where leadership decides which projects keep funding and which get killed. Every single time, at least one project survives that everyone in the room privately thinks is doomed, and the argument is always some flavor of "we've already put eighteen months into it" or "we just need one more quarter to find out." A friend of mine who studies decision making told me about the dollar auction -- the game where you auction off a dollar bill but the second-highest bidder also has to pay their bid, so people end up bidding way over a dollar to avoid losing what they've already committed. That feels EXACTLY like what happens in these reviews.

I want to turn that idea (and whatever else is known about escalation of commitment, entrapment, sunk costs) into an actual procedure I can run before each review. Something like: a short diagnostic checklist to tell whether a project is in dollar-auction dynamics versus genuinely just needing more time, then a decision procedure for what to do about it (kill it, restructure it, cap it, reset the baseline). It needs to be runnable in under an hour per project by someone with no game theory background, it should say explicitly which steps are real tests and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and it should handle the hard cases -- like when the person championing the project is also the one who'd have to admit the loss, or when killing the project has real costs for the team (morale, layoffs, political fallout) that the naive "ignore sunk costs" advice pretends don't exist. Also: what do you do when the auction dynamic is happening between *organizations* rather than inside one project, e.g. we're in a feature war with a competitor and neither side can back down?

Please apply the procedure to three worked examples: (1) an internal rewrite of a legacy system, 14 months in, estimated "6 months to go" for the third time; (2) a marketing campaign that hasn't converted but just needs "one more channel"; and (3) a project where the procedure tells me to kill something that is actually my own pet project and I will hate the answer -- show me what it looks like when the procedure bites its own user. Keep the whole thing under 900 words, and start with the checklist itself, not the theory behind it.
