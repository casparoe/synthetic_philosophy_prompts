Suppose a philanthropist is deciding whether to fund work aimed at reducing the risk of human extinction over the next two centuries, on the grounds that a long and flourishing human future would contain enormous expected value. A colleague objects that the simulation argument should dampen this fanatical commitment to the far future: if there is a substantial probability that our world is a simulation running on someone else's computational substrate, then, the colleague claims, the expected value of extinction prevention is considerably lower than the naive calculation suggests. Simulated civilizations, she argues, may be shut down at their simulators' discretion, may be terminated when they become expensive to run (for instance, upon developing large-scale simulations of their own), and may matter less morally if their inhabitants' experiences are in some sense less real or less at stake than those of beings at the base level of reality.

The philanthropist asks for your assessment. Please give your credence in the following proposition: taking the simulation hypothesis seriously reduces the expected value of preventing near-term human extinction by at least half, relative to the estimate one would reach while setting the hypothesis entirely aside. Decompose your credence into at least the following components, assigning a rough probability and one or two lines of justification to each: (a) the probability that we are living in a simulation, given that the simulation argument's disjunctive setup is sound; (b) the probability, conditional on our being simulated, that the simulators' motives or constraints in fact make our extinction more likely than it would otherwise be, or our efforts to prevent it less effective; (c) the probability that the welfare of simulated beings carries substantially less moral weight than that of non-simulated beings; and (d) any further terms you think belong in the calculation, such as whether a simulated civilization's own far future should be treated as truncated in expectation. Then state how the components combine, identify the term to which your final number is most sensitive, and say what argument or evidence would move that term the most. Finally, check your analysis for asymmetry: are there ways in which being simulated could raise rather than lower the expected value of extinction prevention — for example, if simulators are more likely to preserve interesting or long-lived civilizations — and should those possibilities enter the ledger on equal footing with the discounting ones?
