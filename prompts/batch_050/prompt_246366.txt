I rewatched It's a Wonderful Life at a holiday screening with some coworkers, and we ended up arguing about it until the venue kicked us out. My day job is software engineering, and I realized afterward that I instinctively model counterfactuals the way I'd debug: roll the program back, change one variable, re-run, and diff the outputs. But that is not what the film does. Clarence doesn't tweak a parameter; he deletes George Bailey from birth and re-rolls the entire town into Pottersville, where his brother drowned as a child, a transport full of soldiers died with him, his wife is an unmarried librarian, and the whole character of Bedford Falls is different. I got home and started writing something for a film-and-ideas discussion group I attend, and I want to take the film seriously as a piece of counterfactual reasoning rather than just a sentimental fable. Read the film through the philosophy of counterfactual history and the debate over contingency versus inevitability: what theory of historical counterfactuals does the film's construction actually presuppose, and does that theory survive scrutiny?

A few specific tensions I'd like the reading to engage. First, I gather some historians insist that a respectable counterfactual should change as little as possible to make the antecedent true (I've seen this called a minimal-rewrite rule), on pain of the exercise becoming unconstrained fiction. By that standard the film's counterfactual looks wildly non-minimal, since one man's absence cascades through decades of other lives. Is the film thereby committed to a strong contingency thesis, where single individuals have enormous downstream causal weight, and is that the same thing as the old great-man view of history, or something different? A structuralist friend in the group argues that someone else would have stood up to Potter, or that Potter wins or loses because of economic forces and not because of one loan officer, so the film is rigging the deck to reach its moral. Second, there's an epistemic asymmetry I can't get past: the angel shows George the counterfactual, so within the fiction the question of what would have happened is simply answered, whereas real counterfactual history can only ever be argued. Does the film smuggle in certainty that the genre of counterfactual reasoning can never honestly have, or is that just the license of fantasy? Third, the places where the film resists the reading: maybe it isn't making a historiographical claim at all but a moral one about a life's worth, and maybe Mary being unmarried without George says something about interpersonal inevitability rather than causal cascade. Tell me where the philosophical reading is strong and where it has to strain against the film to work.

I want the essay to be useful to people who haven't seen the film since childhood and to people who think about history for a living, so please be faithful in both directions: don't reduce the movie to an illustration of a thesis, and don't bend the philosophy to flatter the movie. Where the film's implicit picture of counterfactuals conflicts with how careful historians and philosophers actually treat them, say so plainly. And throughout, separate what is broadly agreed among people who work on this, for instance that counterfactual reasoning is indispensable to causal and historical thinking, from what is genuinely contested, such as whether large-scale historical counterfactuals have determinate answers at all or how much weight individuals carry against structure. Please don't present one side of the contingency-versus-inevitability dispute as the settled consensus; if the film effectively takes a side, note that it is taking a side in a live debate. If you can, end with what the dramatization adds that a seminar-room treatment of the same problem doesn't, and what it costs.
