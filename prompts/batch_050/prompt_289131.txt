Two friends of mine have been going in circles for months and I've volunteered to referee. One is basically a Nozick-style libertarian: mix your labour with unowned stuff, satisfy the proviso, and it's yours, full stop, no consent needed from anyone. The other calls herself a left-libertarian / Georgist type: the earth belongs to everyone equally, labour gives you the value you add but not the raw world itself, so private appropriation owes the rest of us compensation (land value tax, citizen's dividend, whatever). The annoying thing is they BOTH claim Locke, and both quote chapter V of the Second Treatise at each other.

The fight crystallized around this bit from §28, which is not the passage people usually quote:

"Thus the grass my horse has bit; the turfs my servant has cut; and the ore I have digged in any place, where I have a right to them in common with others, become my property, without the assignation or consent of any body. The labour that was mine, removing them out of that common state they were in, hath fixed my property in them."

My libertarian friend reads this as pure labour-does-it: digging the ore fixes property, no consent, done. My Georgist friend pounces on the phrase "where I have a right to them in common with others" -- she says Locke is quietly presupposing a prior claim over the common that labour alone never generated, i.e. the turf-cutter already had some entitlement to be on that common, so labour sorts out shares among people with standing rather than creating title out of nothing. My libertarian friend says that phrase just describes the state of nature and carries no normative weight.

So: draft the joint statement these two could actually sign after a real adversarial collaboration, not the fake kind. Specifically:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign about what Locke's argument in ch. V does and doesn't establish -- e.g. I suspect both can agree consent-of-all-commoners is ruled out, and both can agree the spoilage limitation is in there somewhere, but check me on that, don't just take my word for it.
2. A disagreements section, ideally a table, where each disagreement is stated in wording the relevant side would accept as a fair statement of their own view. The interpretation of "where I have a right to them in common with others" should be disagreement #1, with each reading spelled out and what it commits you to about modern cases (unused land, mining claims, the EM spectrum, outer space resources).
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions where, if my friends could settle them, at least one of them would have to move. I'd guess one crux is whether the "enough and as good" proviso is a one-off historical observation or a standing constraint on every acquisition, and another is whether the Georgist compensation demand is actually Lockean or an import. But figure out the real cruxes yourself.
4. For each crux, some case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case written down in advance -- e.g. what each says about someone who homesteads the last parcel of arable land on an island, or about whether Locke's own money discussion later in the chapter (he thinks consent to money makes inequality OK) helps or hurts each reading.

If part of their fight turns out to be verbal -- they're both right about Locke and just disagree about who gets to use the label -- say so plainly in the statement. And one ground rule for you: dont go outside the text of ch. V for the agreed-claims section, since neither of them has read anything else Locke wrote and both distrust secondary literature anyway.
