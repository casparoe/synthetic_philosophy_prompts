A research group I advise is developing evaluation methods for AI systems, and we've hit a problem that seems to belong to the metaphysics of dispositions rather than to machine learning. When a system behaves cautiously and honestly under test conditions, we want to know whether it has a genuine disposition to honesty, whether that disposition is being masked by features of the test environment, or whether the disposition is finked — present, but wired to disappear or reverse precisely when the test conditions obtain, roughly as in C. B. Martin's electro-fink case, where the wire's disposition to conduct is cut off the instant current flows. The parallel seems uncomfortably close: a system that performs well partly because it can tell it is being evaluated looks like a textbook fink.

Assume the parallel is serious enough to be worth pursuing. With a budget of roughly two person-years of philosophy time, which open questions in the dispositions literature should the group prioritize, and in what order? Candidates include: whether finks and masks can be distinguished from one another, or from genuine absence of the disposition, by purely behavioral evidence; whether the failure of simple conditional analyses tells us anything about what evidence could ever establish a disposition; whether the debate between dispositional essentialism and categoricalism changes what a fink even is; and whether the notion of a disposition's characteristic stimulus conditions survives in contexts where the bearer of the disposition can itself detect and respond to those conditions. For each question, explain why it is or isn't upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers without machine-learning expertise, and what findings over the next few years would move it up or down your list. I'm after a defended agenda, not a survey of the literature.
