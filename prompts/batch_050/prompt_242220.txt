My flatmate and I spent most of last night arguing about solar geoengineering, and we got stuck on something I want to think through more carefully. We were discussing the actual governance landscape, as I understand it: hostile modification of the environment is banned by an international convention dating from the 1970s, while peaceful modification of the stratosphere is essentially unregulated; fertilizing the ocean to draw down carbon is treated as a form of dumping requiring permission under a marine-pollution treaty, but nothing comparable exists for releasing reflective aerosols from a balloon or aircraft; and no body, treaty, or norm requires a state contemplating stratospheric aerosol injection to obtain anyone's consent before an outdoor experiment, or even before a full deployment. My friend's position was that this patchwork is mere historical accident -- each treaty was drafted to solve whatever problem happened to be in front of its authors -- and that it is a mistake to read any philosophy into it. My suspicion is the opposite: that the patchwork only makes sense, if it makes sense at all, on a particular and possibly inconsistent set of presuppositions about who has standing over the climate.

Here is what I want worked out. First: what does it presuppose about standing that a state may not dim the sun over an enemy's territory in wartime, yet may apparently dim it for everyone in peacetime without asking anyone? Second: the ocean regime treats adding iron to seawater as presumptively pollution, while injecting sulfur into the stratosphere is treated as a legal nullity. What conception of harm distinguishes those, and does it track anything defensible, or merely which medium the drafters happened to be watching? Third -- and this is where the argument got heated -- termination shock. If a deployment masks some warming and then stops abruptly, the warming arrives in a rush, faster than ecosystems and infrastructure can adapt. My friend says this just means deployments should be engineered to be robust, the way we engineer bridges to be robust. I think it changes the moral character of the act: beginning a deployment is not a one-off intervention but the creation of an open-ended obligation on future generations to keep it going, or wind it down slowly, whether or not they would have consented. What theory of intergenerational obligation, and of consent by the unborn, makes it permissible to create such a dependence -- and can any theory make it permissible without also condemning the emissions that motivated it? Fourth, moral hazard: the worry that the mere availability of a cheap thermostat erodes the political will to cut emissions. Does treating that as a reason against even research presuppose that we owe future people actual mitigation rather than merely a safe climate -- that the means of avoiding harm, and not just the outcome, matters morally?

Please don't simply restate these questions back at me. What I want is a reconstruction: what view or views of standing, harm, and intergenerational obligation would render the current patchwork coherent; which rival views it quietly rules out; where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, such that several philosophies would license exactly the same treaties; and -- if the patchwork is best explained as presupposing inconsistent things in different places -- precisely which parts conflict, and what minimal change to the practice would resolve the conflict.
