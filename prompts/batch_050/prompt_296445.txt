I manage post-excavation reporting for a regional archaeology service, and my situation has changed recently in a way I need help with. Until this year, interpretation of our developer-funded excavations was handled informally by whoever dug the site, but we have now been told that every report going to the planning authority must state, for each significant find or feature, what it licenses us to say about the people who left it -- and the planners have no patience for hedged academic prose. My team is competent at describing objects, but when it comes to inferring anything beyond function -- whether a deposit reflects belief, ritual, or some institution like a priesthood or a hereditary elite -- the quality of reasoning varies wildly, and I have no defensible standard for telling a junior archaeologist that a claim in their draft goes too far or not far enough. I am broadly aware of the classic worry in our discipline that inferences get shakier the further you climb from technology and subsistence toward social organization and then toward what people thought and believed, but a worry is not a procedure, and I cannot put a worry in a workflow document.

What I want is a decision procedure my team can actually run, per find or feature class, in roughly half an hour with the excavation records in front of them, before anything goes to the planning authority. It should be a sequence of ordered questions or tests, in the spirit of turning that epistemic worry into something operational: start with what an object's function and context directly support, and then specify what additional evidence must be present before a report may claim (a) deliberate symbolic treatment rather than practical disposal, (b) repeated or rule-governed practice rather than a one-off act, and (c) an institutional or belief-level interpretation rather than merely an individual's behaviour. For each step, say what counts as passing it (e.g., what makes deposition patterns, association with other classes of material, repetition across sites, or anatomical/anthropological evidence sufficient), and mark explicitly the points where the procedure must stop and hand the question to a senior specialist because no checklist can settle it -- I would rather the document confess its limits than pretend judgment has been eliminated.

Please work the procedure through three cases from my current backlog: a Bronze Age inhumation with a small set of grave goods that are rich by local standards but not unprecedented; a storage pit containing a deliberately broken quernstone placed over disarticulated animal bone, which the excavator wants to call a ritual closing deposit; and a dispersed hoard of debased coins that the excavator wants to read as an emergency offering. I expect the procedure to endorse some conclusions and refuse others, and I specifically want at least one case where it returns an answer my excavator will dislike, so I can see how the refusal is worded in a way a planning officer will accept. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do -- which kinds of overclaiming it will fail to catch, and what could make a cautious report wrong even when every step was followed correctly.
