A guy in my philosophy of science reading group has an argument he thinks kills the idea that science gets closer to the truth, and I can't find where it slips. It goes:

(1) Newtonian mechanics is strictly closer to the truth than, say, medieval impetus physics, even though both are strictly speaking false.
(2) A theory's content is just the set of its logical consequences; split that into its true consequences (its true content) and its false consequences (its false content).
(3) One false theory T1 is strictly closer to the truth than another false theory T2 only if T1's true content includes all of T2's (and then some) while T1's false content is included in T2's.
(4) Suppose that holds. Pick any truth t that follows from T1 but not from T2, and any falsehood f that follows from T1 (there must be at least one, since T1 is false). The conjunction t-and-f is a false consequence of T1, so by (3) it must be a consequence of T2. But then t follows from T2 after all, contradiction.
(5) So no false theory is ever strictly closer to the truth than another false theory unless the first just entails the second.
(6) But then (1) is false, and the whole notion of science approaching the truth through a series of false but improving theories is incoherent -- any false theory is as far from the truth as any other.

He insists every step is just logic plus common sense, and honestly (4) checks out when I trace it. I really want to keep (1), and (6) seems to prove way too much. Which step do I actually reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me for theories like Newton's?

Give me your answer, then a table summing it up with columns: step, verdict (reject/keep), why it looked fine, and cost of rejecting it.
