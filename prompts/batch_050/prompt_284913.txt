I'm taking a course on decision theory and the philosophy of economics, and I've run into a dispute about an analogy that our lecturer used in the first week. Introducing expected value, he told us to picture a gamble as follows: imagine that when you face a risky prospect, your life branches into many parallel futures, one for each possible outcome, with a copy of you living through each. Computing the expectation, he said, is like collecting the winnings and losses of all your parallel selves into a common pot and dividing it equally among them, weighted by how likely each branch is. A gamble is worth taking when your average self comes out ahead, and this picture, he argued, shows why maximizing expected value is the rational default for decisions under risk.

In my own reading I've since encountered the critique associated with so-called ergodicity economics, and its proponents seem to think this analogy is not merely loose but is precisely the mistake. Their standard example: a coin flip that either increases your wealth by 50 percent or decreases it by 40 percent, offered repeatedly. The expectation per round is positive (+5 percent), yet because gains and losses multiply rather than add, almost every actual sequence of repeated play trends downward (the per-round growth factor along a typical trajectory is the square root of 1.5 times 0.6, i.e. about 0.95). The critics claim the parallel-selves picture only delivers its verdict by quietly assuming that the lucky selves' winnings are redistributed to the unlucky ones, when in real life no such transfer ever occurs: you live exactly one trajectory, and what befalls you is the time average along it, not the ensemble average across your fictional counterparts.

Could you audit this analogy for me? First, give the explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the parallel selves, of the common pot, of the equal division, and of the assumption that the game is played long enough for the average to show itself. Second, identify where the mapping breaks down, and separate the breaks that matter for its intended use from those that don't — in particular, distinguishing cases where something like the pooling step genuinely exists (insurance arrangements, many independent repetitions, purely additive payoffs) from cases where it can't (multiplicative dynamics, absorbing states like ruin, one-shot irreversible choices). Third, say what the analogy smuggles in unnoticed — I'm suspicious of the equal division step and of the assumption that one's fate is fungible across possible worlds. Finally, does the analogy still license expected-value maximization once the disanalogies are on the table, does it license it only in a restricted range of cases, or was it carrying the conclusion by itself all along? If there is a better analogy for what an expectation actually computes, I'd like that too.
