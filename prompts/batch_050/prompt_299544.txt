I am a graduate student in history who has been reading the old dispute between the covering-law model of historical explanation (associated with Hempel) and its narrative rivals (associated with Dray and Gallie). What puzzles me is that both sides now seem to concede everything the other wants. Narrativists admit that a historical narrative is only explanatory insofar as its episodes are linked by defensible causal claims. Covering-law sympathizers admit that working historians almost never state general laws, and that the laws on offer, when stated, are truisms. Yet the debate has not closed; each side still insists the other misunderstands what explanation in history is. My hypothesis is that the real disagreement is not about the form of explanations at all but about what would vindicate history as a respectable discipline — one side needs history's epistemic credentials to come from somewhere law-like, the other thinks credentials can rest on the coherence and evidential fit of the story itself. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a live substantive disagreement about explanation that I am missing? What would actually count as progress, given your diagnosis?
