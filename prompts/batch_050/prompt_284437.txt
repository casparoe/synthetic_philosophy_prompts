I'm a third-year PhD student preparing an application for a summer research fellowship, and I need a two-page proposal (roughly 1200 words). My project grows out of a reading group I organized on two-dimensional semantics, where we kept circling a foundational worry that the standard presentations seemed to me to defer rather than answer. The anchor for my proposal is the following passage from David Chalmers's 2001 manuscript "The Nature of Epistemic Space," which we read in the group:

"It is natural to think of scenarios as possible worlds, and to think of a scenario in which P as a world in which P. But it is immediately clear that this will not work, at least on the most common contemporary understanding of possible worlds."

The problem I want to work on is what I think of as the construction problem for epistemic scenarios. If scenarios cannot simply be possible worlds (because, e.g., it is epistemically possible that Hesperus is not Phosphorus even though no possible world verifies that), then they need to be built some other way: as centered worlds, as sets of sentences in some idealized language, as equivalence classes under a priori coherence, or as primitives. But every construction I've encountered seems to smuggle in the very notion of apriority or ideal rational reflection that the framework is then used to analyze, and taking scenarios as primitive seems to leave applications like the scrutability thesis without the support they were supposed to get from the framework. The proposal should state this as a question with a determinate answer — something like: can epistemic scenarios be given a construction that is neither circular nor merely stipulative, and if not, which applications of the two-dimensional framework survive taking them as primitive? It should explain why the familiar defenses of the framework (that scenarios are just an intuitive device that any adequate theory can regiment its own way, or that the circularity is benign because the analysis is meant to be illuminating rather than reductive) do not settle the matter, and why this is a question worth a summer rather than a career.

The proposal needs the standard parts: the precise question and why it is open; the method, meaning what I would actually do week by week (e.g., weeks spent reconstructing candidate constructions and testing each against hard cases like necessary a posteriori truths and the epistemic possibility of deep logical falsehoods, then weeks on what primitivism costs); what the possible conclusions are and what considerations would decide between them; and an explicit account of what would count as the project failing — including the possibility that the construction problem dissolves on inspection, which I would need to be able to recognize rather than rationalize away. Please don't include citations or a bibliography; I'll add those myself. One constraint from the fellowship guidelines: the proposal must end with a short paragraph explaining, for a non-specialist committee member, why a question about the foundations of a formal framework matters to anyone outside the semantics literature — I'd like that paragraph to connect the construction problem to at least one downstream use of two-dimensional semantics in philosophy of mind or epistemology without overclaiming.
