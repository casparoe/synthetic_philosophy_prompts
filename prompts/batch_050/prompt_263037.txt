So I'm a novelist -- literary fiction, three books in, middling sales, one prize shortlist nobody remembers -- and I'm properly stuck on the moral machinery of my new novel, and before I go and read the actual philosophy I want a spec to read it against. Here's the setup. A woman in her fifties, formidably sharp, watches her mother go through a decade of dementia and draws up an advance directive: if I ever reach the point where I no longer recognise my children, withdraw everything except comfort care. Years later she is exactly there. She doesn't know her daughter. And -- here's the engine of the book -- she is, by every observable measure, content. Music on the radio, the garden in spring, custard creams, enormous pleasure in all of it. The daughter, who once promised to enforce the directive, is my narrator, and the final chapter is her deciding whether to.

I've bolted on two complications and I don't want the ethics underneath to be embarassingly naive, so before you give me verdicts I want the requirements list. What would a theory of the authority of an advance directive actually have to get right to be good enough to tell me what my narrator should do? I mean the whole spec: the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse (having a settled earlier judgement vs being capable of current preferences, for one; refusing treatment vs requesting it, for another), the facts from medicine and law it can't contradict (dementia patients can still want things; capacity is decision-specific, or so my GP brother-in-law keeps telling me). Give each requirement with the bit of my plot that motivates it, not just in the abstract.

Complication one: she revised the directive every few years, and the last revision was signed after an early diagnosis, when she may already have been slipping. So a decent theory presumably has to say something about which version of a person gets to bind the rest. Complication two: the hospital can now offer a cheap intervention that keeps her alive for years in her current state but won't restore recognition of anyone.

Then, the bits I most want: which desiderata are genuinely load-bearing and which are the hobby horse of one camp (the autonomy lot vs the welfare lot, as far as I can tell from the outside). Are any of them jointly unsatisfiable -- say, honouring the earlier self's most considered judgement and taking seriously the current self's evident contentment? If so, say so, because then the list is itself the result and my ending has to pick a side. Which single requirement knocks out the most candidate views? And if you want to score the two or three standard positions against the list at the end, fine, but keep it brief -- the criteria are the deliverable, I'll do the rest in the rewrite.
