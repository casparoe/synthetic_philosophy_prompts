Over the past few months I've been reading on my own about how ordinary, orderly societies come to commit extraordinary evil, and I've just finished Zygmunt Bauman's Modernity and the Holocaust. As I understand his argument, the Holocaust was not a relapse into pre-modern barbarism but something that modernity itself made possible: it ran on rationalized bureaucracy, on a division of labor that let each participant handle only a small piece of the process and never confront the final act, on treating human beings as objects of administrative problem-solving, and on the modern state's confidence that society is something to be designed and weeded like a garden. I now want to stress-test this thesis against a case Bauman never wrote about: the 1994 genocide in Rwanda. From what I've gathered, roughly 800,000 people were killed in about a hundred days, mostly with machetes and small arms, often by neighbors who knew their victims personally, with mobilization running through local officials, roadblocks, identity cards, and radio broadcasts rather than through anything resembling an industrial killing apparatus.

Please analyze the Rwandan genocide through Bauman's modernity thesis, in both directions. First, run the theory on the case: which features of Rwanda does Bauman's framework predict or illuminate (the administrative coordination, the census and identity-card system, the radio as a technology of mobilization, the way tasks were parceled out across an entire administrative hierarchy), and which features does it fail to predict or even get wrong (the face-to-face intimacy of the killing, the sheer speed, the low technology, the breadth of popular participation instead of a small specialized apparatus)? Be disciplined about the mapping and say plainly where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. Second, run the case back on the theory: does Rwanda disconfirm Bauman, or does it force a distinction between, say, the modern preconditions of genocide and the modern means of carrying it out? Finally, address the worry I take to be behind the German historians' dispute of the 1980s over the uniqueness and comparability of the Holocaust: if Bauman is right that the Holocaust is explained by general features of modernity, does that make it straightforwardly comparable to Rwanda in a way that dissolves its uniqueness, and is that consequence a cost of the theory or a point in its favor? I hold no strong view of my own; I want the honest two-way analysis.

At the end, summarize the analysis as a table with four columns: Feature (the aspect of the Rwandan case under consideration); Bauman's Verdict (what the modernity thesis says about this feature, or 'silent' if it says nothing); Fit (whether the case confirms, disconfirms, or sits awkwardly with the thesis, in one sentence); and Minimal Revision (the smallest amendment to Bauman's framework that would accommodate this feature, or 'none needed').
