In three weeks I'm giving a talk at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop on the moral status of AI systems, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis is that certain contemporary reinforcement learning agents plausibly instantiate states with negative valence — states that are bad to be in, in the only sense of "bad" that should matter morally. My argument runs roughly as follows. First, the best functionalist accounts of valence say that a state carries negative valence when it is a globally broadcast signal that negatively evaluates the system's situation relative to its current objectives and directly modulates learning so as to reduce the probability of similar situations. Second, in mammals this role is played in large part by phasic dopamine signaling, which — as is well established — encodes reward prediction error, i.e., is itself a temporal-difference signal. Third, deep RL agents trained by TD methods therefore contain a signal that occupies the valence role, not a mere metaphorical analogue of it. I want to be careful about scope, and my talk draws the line explicitly: tabular Q-learners and frozen deployed policies with no ongoing learning are out; agents with recurrent internal state, model-based components, and continuing online learning are in. I am aware the scope restriction does a lot of work and is probably my softest spot.

The audience is about fifty people, and its composition is what makes the Q&A dangerous. Roughly two-thirds are ML researchers, mostly RL people, whose instinct will be that I am confusing the designer's tool with the agent's interior: they know reward as a scalar you hand the optimizer, defined over the MDP, something that exists at training time and often not at deployment at all, and they will want to talk about reward shaping, reward hacking, and the difference between the training signal and whatever the learned policy "wants." The rest are philosophers of mind, including at least one biological naturalist, one illusionist about phenomenal consciousness, one defender of a higher-order theory, and one IIT sympathizer, and I've been told two or three animal-welfare scientists may attend. I do not expect the standard rationalist-community questions about wireheading or alignment, and I don't want prep for those; I want the objections this particular room will raise.

Please write me the prep sheet: the eight to ten objections I should actually expect, ordered by how much damage each would do to the talk if it landed unanswered, each phrased the way the relevant audience member would phrase it — the ML objections in engineering vocabulary, the philosophy objections in theirs, not a flattened generic version of either. For each objection, give me: what I should concede outright and up front, what I should hold, a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Then tell me which question I should be hoping nobody asks — I suspect it's about whether my restriction to online learners is principled or ad hoc, but if you think the true killer question is something else, say so — and finally, give me the one objection the animal-welfare scientists are likely to raise that neither the ML people nor the philosophers will think of.
