Last night a friend and I spent several hours arguing about naked statistical evidence, and we reached an impasse I want help dissolving — though not by taking a side yet. First I want the specification.

The argument concerned three cases. In the first, a pedestrian is struck at night by a delivery robot of a make operated by two logistics firms; the only evidence is dispatch telemetry showing that, of the robots active in that district at that hour, four of five belonged to SwiftHaul. There is no witness, no camera footage, and no recovered vehicle. In the second, a music festival discovers through an audit of its access-control system that roughly half of its attendees entered on shared credentials without paying, and it sues all ten thousand identifiable attendees for the ticket price, reasoning that each is more likely than not a gatecrasher. In the third, a crime-scene DNA profile matches the defendant, the laboratory reports a random-match probability of one in twenty million, there is essentially no other evidence against him — and the match was found by trawling a national database of twenty million profiles. My friend defends the hard line: legal proof is nothing but a probability threshold, whatever meets the threshold is proven, and the discomfort people feel about the first two cases is superstition that the third case exposes, since DNA evidence is equally statistical at bottom and yet everyone convicts on it. I resist this, but when pressed I could not state what feature distinguishes the third case from the first two without begging the question.

So: write the desiderata for a theory of legal proof — an account of when a fact-finder may properly find a disputed fact on the evidence — adequate for actual adoption by a jurisdiction that will soon face a great deal of telemetry-driven litigation of the first kind. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. At minimum the list must speak to: why the blue-bus and gatecrasher pattern seems insufficient while DNA and fingerprint evidence seem sufficient, or else why that intuition should be abandoned rather than accommodated; how an account moves from graded probabilities to the categorical verdicts legal systems actually issue; what distinguishes the criminal standard from the civil standard beyond the number attached; whether evidence that would have existed even if the defendant were innocent differs in kind from evidence that would not; whether proving each element of a claim to just over the civil standard thereby proves the claim as a whole; and what incentives a rule creates for parties to generate, keep, or conveniently lose statistical records. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp, and say which single requirement eliminates the most otherwise-plausible accounts. Most important to me: if some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, argue that as rigorously as you can, because it would mean my friend and I were really arguing over which requirement to sacrifice rather than over who was right. Close with a brief verdict on how pure threshold probabilism, individualized-evidence accounts, sensitivity- or safety-based epistemological accounts, and relative-plausibility or explanationist accounts fare against the list — and state explicitly what argument, case, or result would change your mind about that verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
