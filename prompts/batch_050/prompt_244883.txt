I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy TA-ing a first-year introduction to ethics, and next week I get one fifty-minute section on hard choices between incommensurable values. The lecturer has asked me to build the section around the small-improvement argument for incomparability: the idea that if you take two options that seem hard to rank, improve one of them slightly, and the choice remains hard, the original pair can't have been related by one being strictly better -- and that this pushes us toward admitting that some options are neither better than, worse than, nor equally as good as each other. I understand the argument itself well enough; what I want from you is not another run-through of it but a plan for how to teach it to students who have never encountered value theory and whose default assumption will be that for any two options either one is better or they're tied.

Plan the explanatory arc. What has to be in place before the small-improvement reasoning can even land as an argument rather than a strange stipulation -- I'm guessing students need the idea that better-than, worse-than, and equally-good might not exhaust the possible rankings, and before that perhaps the idea that hard choices aren't all tragic dilemmas. Tell me the order these pieces must come in and what depends on what. Then settle on the one carrying example to build the whole section around: I'm inclined toward a career choice (a life as an artist versus a life as a lawyer, with a modest pay bump to one side as the small improvement), but I want a verdict on whether that's right and, importantly, why not the alternatives -- why not start with a tragic choice like a parent choosing which child to save, why not a trivial consumer choice, and why not a case of choosing between lives saved and quality of life. What features must the carrying example have, and what must it avoid?

Then give me the misconception map. I predict students will collapse incomparability into at least three different things: plain equality or a tie, mere uncertainty about which option is better, and vagueness in the facts rather than in the value relations. Some will also think admitting incomparability means abandoning reasoned choice altogether, or that any tie can be broken by the tiniest further feature, which would undo the whole argument. For each misconception, tell me exactly where in the sequence it will naturally arise and at what point I should head it off rather than letting it fester. Finally, give me one question I can pose in the last five minutes that would show the argument has actually landed -- something that distinguishes a student who grasps that incomparability is a claim about value relations rather than about our ignorance from one who has merely learned to repeat the word -- and tell me what to deliberately leave out at this level, saying so openly in class rather than papering over it.
