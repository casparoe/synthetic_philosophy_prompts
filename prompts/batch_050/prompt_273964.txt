Two of my colleagues in the economics department got into a long argument last week, over drinks after a talk, about a thought experiment one of them had encountered: an agent is wired to a device with a dial of a thousand settings; each step up pays him $10,000 and delivers a slightly stronger electric shock, where adjacent settings differ so little that nobody can tell them apart, though the difference between setting 0 (a faint tingle) and setting 999 (agony) is enormous. I listened without taking a side, but I'd like an outside verdict on the exchange, which went roughly as follows. Marta opened: each individual step is rational — the gain is real and the cost imperceptible — so the agent who ends at the top with ten million dollars has made a sequence of rational choices and cannot be faulted; the discomfort people feel is squeamishness about the aggregate, not a defect in any decision. Daniel countered that the endpoint is strictly worse than the starting point on the agent's own scale, so a chain of individually rational steps has produced an outcome the agent himself would pay a fortune to undo, which he took to be a reductio of Marta's premise. Marta replied that the agent would pay to undo it only once the pain has become perceptible — that is, at the end — and that rationality governs each choice as it is made at its time, not the guarantee of outcomes one likes; nobody calls an investor irrational because a thousand individually sound trades happened to end in a crashed market. Daniel answered with a pump: suppose the dial turns both ways, but each downward step costs the agent slightly more than the last upward step earned; an agent running Marta's stepwise logic can be cycled up and down indefinitely, bleeding money, and any decision procedure that permits this is defective. Marta said the pump proves nothing, because it requires a sucker who keeps trading on impulse, whereas her agent could adopt a standing policy — resolve once, in advance, to stop at some chosen setting and never revisit the question — and that resolute choice dissolves the pump without conceding that any single step was irrational. Daniel's last move was to say that in that case the puzzle was never about the rationality of choices at all but about vagueness: "imperceptible increase" conceals a sorites, and the real mistake is assuming that an unbroken chain of indiscriminable steps guarantees indiscriminable endpoints, so both the pump and the resolute-choice fix are answers to a question that dissolves once the vague predicate is exposed. Marta's rejoinder was that the vagueness reading cannot be right, because the puzzle can be rebuilt without any vague predicate — give the agent perfect discrimination and let the payments compound slightly faster than the displeasure grows, and the same pathology returns — so the problem really lies in the structure of preferences, or in rationality across time, not in words. The conversation broke off there when the bar closed.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each step, say whether the point made is sound, unsound, sound-but-incomplete, or an equivocation, and on what grounds — not just who wins overall. I am especially interested in whether Daniel's pump argument actually requires the dial to be reversible, whether Marta's resolute-choice reply concedes more than she admits (since the policy itself must be chosen somehow), and whether the stipulation that adjacent settings are indiscriminable is doing work the puzzle cannot do without.
