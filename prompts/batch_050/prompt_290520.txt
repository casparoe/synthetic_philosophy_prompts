Having recently taken a seat on my city's infrastructure commission — where every meeting seems to circle the question of whether our institutions are declining or merely changing — I have been reading Gibbon's Chapter 38, the "General Observations on the Fall of the Roman Empire in the West," from *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*. One passage struck me as doing quiet but decisive work:

"The decay of Rome has been frequently ascribed to the translation of the seat of empire; but this history has already shewn that the powers of government were divided rather than removed. The throne of Constantinople was erected in the East; while the West was still possessed by a series of emperors who held their residence in Italy and claimed their equal inheritance of the legions and provinces. This dangerous novelty impaired the strength, and fomented the vices, of a double reign."

Please rank the following five candidate accounts of the Western collapse by how well each explains what Gibbon identifies in this passage — namely, that the trouble lay in the *division* of a governmental apparatus that remained fully staffed on both sides, not in its removal or absence: (1) escalating barbarian military pressure, (2) internal institutional and civic decay of the kind Gibbon himself emphasizes, (3) exogenous shocks such as climate deterioration and pandemic, (4) chronic lead poisoning, and (5) the revisionist view that there was no fall at all, only a gradual transformation of late antiquity. Give a sentence justifying each placement, state which account the passage most directly embarrasses, and close by saying whether Gibbon's "divided rather than removed" distinction survives scrutiny or smuggles in the conclusion it pretends to establish.
