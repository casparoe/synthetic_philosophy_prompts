So I work as a software engineer, no philosphy training, and I keep going back and forth on this with a coworker. My rough take: the orthogonality thesis (that intelligence and goals can vary independently, so a superintelligent system could want basically anything) is usually used as a premise in arguments that advanced AI is dangerous -- like, "it might want paperclips, so watch out." But it seems to me the thesis cuts the other way. If intelligence really is neutral with respect to goals, then there's no reason a smarter-than-human system would converge on power-seeking, self-preservation, resource acquisition, etc. Those convergent instrumental goals always get smuggled in via an assumption that the system cares a lot about achieving its terminal goal, or about its own continued operation -- which is exactly the kind of motivational claim orthogonality says you can't infer from intelligence. So the standard doom argument seems to need one foot on orthogonality (goals are arbitrary!) and one foot off it (all sufficiently smart agents want power!).

Please argue for my position: that accepting the orthogonality thesis seriously weakens, rather than supports, the instrumental-convergence case for existential risk from AI. Steelman the other side's likely responses (e.g., that convergence applies to whatever goals the system happens to have, or that we only need one bad goal in the space of possible goals) and show why they don't rescue the argument. If at some point you think my position actually breaks, say where and why rather than papering over it.
