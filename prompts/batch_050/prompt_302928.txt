Yesterday I spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about whether natural selection actually explains anything. Her closing move was to say that the whole theory boils down to "survival of the fittest," and that this is either an empty truism — whoever survives was, by definition, the fittest — or, if fitness means something independent of survival, an empirical claim that is constantly falsified, since well-adapted organisms get struck by lightning and maladapted ones get lucky. I replied that modern biology treats fitness as a propensity, a disposition to leave offspring rather than a record of having done so, and she said that just relocates the problem: either the propensity is measured by actual reproductive outcomes, in which case the circularity returns, or it isn't, in which case she wants to know what it even is. I didn't have a good answer, and the argument ended with both of us repeating our opening positions more slowly.

What I want is a careful disambiguation of the slogan itself. Lay out the distinct precise theses that "survival of the fittest" could be expressing — the trivially true readings, the substantive empirical readings, and any readings in between that are neither tautologies nor simple generalizations — and for each one say whether it is defensible and what would count as evidence for or against it. In particular, work through at least these candidate readings, and add any I have missed: the slogan as a bare logical truth about who survives; the slogan as the claim that traits correlated with survival in the past causally explain survival in the present; the slogan as a statistical claim about tendencies over populations and generations rather than a guarantee about individuals; and the slogan as a definition of fitness rather than a claim about the world.

Then tell me which of these readings my friend's argument actually needed in order to go through, and which reading I would need in order to fairly accuse her of attacking a straw version of evolutionary theory. I would also like to know where the propensity interpretation of fitness genuinely escapes her dilemma and where it merely relabels it — that is, what has to be true about the relationship between an organism's traits and its expected reproductive success for the slogan to be both non-trivial and well-supported. Present the readings as a numbered list I could bring to our next conversation, with a verdict on each.
