I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy, with a secondary focus in the history of ethics, and I am organizing an adversarial collaboration between two colleagues on the ethics of lying. I would like you to draft the joint statement this collaboration ought to produce — the document both parties could actually sign.

The first party is an Augustinian absolutist: she holds that every assertion believed by the speaker to be false and made with the intention of deceiving is morally impermissible, without exception — including lies to the murderer at the door, lies told under coercion, and lies whose omission would foreseeably cause great harm. The second party defends what I will call the threshold view: lying is pro tanto wrong because of what it does to trust and to the deceived, but the prohibition can be overridden, and in some cases reversed into an obligation, when sufficiently weighty goods are at stake.

Please structure the document as follows.

First, a numbered section of claims both parties can sign. I expect these to include, at minimum: a working characterization of lying that neither side treats as question-begging (in particular, wrongness must neither be built into nor excluded from the definition); the concession that the murderer-at-the-door case is genuinely costly for the absolutist and that the threshold view owes a non-ad-hoc account of where the threshold lies; and agreement that Augustine's own graded treatment of different kinds of lies — by the liar's purpose and the lie's object — shows that even the absolutist position has internal structure rather than being a single blunt rule.

Second, a statement of the residual disagreements, each phrased in words its own side would accept. Candidates I want you to sharpen, discard, or supplement: whether the wrong of lying is located primarily in the speaker's abuse of the faculty of speech or in the deception's effects; whether an audience's forfeiture of any claim to the truth (the murderer, the inquisitor) changes the act itself rather than merely the balance of reasons; whether misleading truths, evasions, and strategic silences differ in kind or only in degree from lies. Where you conclude that part of the dispute is verbal — say, because the two sides individuate "lies" differently — identify which part and show it.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, give a case or argument both parties agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. I want at least one crux tested against the following scenario of my own construction, rather than the standard repertoire: a community in which everyone publicly adopts the threshold view and lies whenever their private calculation crosses the threshold; within a generation, testimony there carries noticeably less default credence. Both parties must state whether this outcome bears on whether the wrong of lying is intrinsic or instrumental, and what it would show if it did.

Finally, a brief closing section in which each side states what it would count as decisive grounds to abandon its position.

Length is at your discretion, but favor precision of formulation over comprehensiveness; I will supply historical citations myself.
