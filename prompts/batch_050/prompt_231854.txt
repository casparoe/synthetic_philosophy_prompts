Last weekend I spent several hours arguing with a friend about how modern societies actually handle privacy, and we got stuck in a way I want help thinking through. Here is the practice that started the argument. Almost everywhere in the digital economy, privacy is administered as a matter of individual consent: websites show cookie banners and ask you to accept or reject tracking, apps request permissions one by one, and privacy laws are largely built around notice, choice, and the individual's right to access or delete their own data. The underlying picture seems to be that privacy is something each person owns and can trade away or keep, transaction by transaction. My friend argued that this picture is now obviously fictitious -- nobody reads the notices, the choices are illusory, and the whole apparatus is theater. I pushed back that the problem might not be that people fail to exercise control but that the practice is built on the wrong theory of what privacy is in the first place. Consider a few features that came up in our exchange: my decision to upload my DNA to a genealogy service discloses information about my relatives, who consented to nothing; a company that aggregates millions of people's behavioral data can infer facts about people who never used its service at all; and yet some privacy protections, like certain rights under European data law, cannot be waived even by a fully informed person who wants to trade them away. So here is what I want from you. Reconstruct the theory of privacy that the consent-based practice presupposes -- what it must take privacy to be, such that individual notice-and-choice is the right way to protect it. Then say whether that presupposed theory is one of control over one's information, of seclusion or being let alone, of information flowing only in contextually appropriate ways, or some mixture, and identify which features of the practice each rival theory would license and which it would condemn. In particular, tell me whether the non-waivable rights and the cases of third-party exposure are contradictions within the practice's own implicit theory, or signs that it quietly presupposes two incompatible views at once. Finally, say what would have to change in actual practice if we replaced the individual-control picture with a view on which privacy is partly a collective good, more like herd immunity than like property. I would like the answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
