For a philosophy of mind reading group I've been asked to circulate my position on Gödelian anti-mechanism in advance, and I'd like it stress-tested before the meeting. Here is where I've landed: (1) The Lucas-style argument succeeds against a specific target: no consistent formal system that a human mathematician could, in principle, fully survey and certify as consistent can capture that mathematician's mathematical competence, since the mathematician can recognise the truth of the system's Gödel sentence. (2) Nonetheless, the brain is a purely physical system and there is no reason to think physics is uncomputable at the relevant level, so I reject Penrose's invocation of non-computable quantum gravity as unmotivated. (3) Human mathematical practice is in fact consistent — I take our confidence in arithmetic's consistency to be as well-grounded as any belief we have, not a hypothesis up for grabs. (4) The computational theory of mind remains our best empirical framework for cognition, and I expect human mathematical reasoning to be explainable in computational terms. (5) Actual human mathematicians are fallible: they make errors, hold inconsistent beliefs locally, and their 'seeing' of Gödel sentences is defeasible.

Go through this set and tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to. In particular: does (1) even touch (4) once the difference between an idealised reasoner and an actual one is on the table, or does conceding (5) dissolve the argument in a way that also undercuts (1)? And is there a lurking conflict between (3) and the rest — if I were the sort of system Gödel's second theorem applies to, what exactly would my warrant for (3) have to be? Rank any tensions by severity, say what the cheapest revision would be in each case and what it costs, and identify the single change that would make the package most coherent. Please also distinguish what is settled in this literature (e.g., which moves are generally agreed to be fallacious) from what is genuinely contested among philosophers who work on it, rather than presenting a minority verdict as the consensus.
