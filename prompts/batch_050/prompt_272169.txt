In Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, a tangled hierarchy is a system of levels in which the level you took to be the top turns out to be acted upon by levels below it, so that the hierarchy folds back on itself; his canonical illustration is Escher's lithograph Drawing Hands, in which each of two hands is busy drawing the other. Hofstadter observes that any such tangle is made possible by what he calls the inviolate level: a level that does the tangling while remaining itself untouched by the tangle. In the lithograph, that level is Escher — the two depicted hands loop, but the artist who drew them both stands outside the loop.

Here is what puzzles me. If every tangled hierarchy requires an inviolate level above it, then the inviolate level itself — being a real thing in the world with its own internal structure of levels (Escher had a brain; brains admit of neural and symbolic description; Hofstadter himself insists that the human self is a loop of exactly this kind) — must in turn sit inside some further tangle, which requires its own inviolate level, and so on without end. The doctrine of the inviolate level therefore seems to generate an infinite regress of ever-higher authors, each drawing the one below. Alternatively, if the regress is to be stopped, there must be a highest inviolate level after all — but that looks like it reinstates precisely the tidy, untangled hierarchy with a genuine top that the notion of tangling was supposed to undermine.

So my question is this: how does Hofstadter block the regress? Does he simply accept an infinite tower of inviolate levels, or does the analysis of Drawing Hands quietly smuggle back in the ordinary hierarchy it was meant to replace? And if the actual answer is just that Escher happens to stand outside this particular loop, doesn't that reduce the inviolate level to a contingent feature of one example, rather than something the logic of tangled hierarchies strictly requires?
