So I've been reading about how certain moral practices actually ended, and there's this distinction I keep coming back to: some practices seem to die because of arguments (people gradually get convinced it's wrong), some because of interests (the economics stop making sense, like the claim that British slavery was abolished partly because it stopped being profitable), and some because of something weirder — like dueling, which apparently didn't end because people got better arguments against it (the arguments had been around forever) but because the honor code sustaining it collapsed, and gentlemen started finding the duel faintly ridiculous rather than obligatory. The practice didn't lose debates; it lost its audience.

Here's my question: give me one example of a practice that is widely accepted today but could plausibly end the way dueling did — that is, not through better arguments (plenty of those already exist and aren't working) and not through economic obsolescence, but through a shift in the honor code, where doing it stops being a way of earning respect and starts being embarrassing. Explain briefly why your example fits the dueling pattern rather than the slavery pattern, i.e., why interests and arguments alone won't kill it but a change in what confers status might. And note one disanalogy too, since no historical parallel is perfect — where does the comparison strain?

Keep the whole answer under 300 words. I'd rather have one well-chosen example analyzed tightly than a laundry list of candidates.
