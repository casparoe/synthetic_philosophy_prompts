A colleague in my department has an argument about the genetics of educational attainment that has been circulating in our reading group, and none of us can accept its conclusion, though we keep failing to say exactly why it fails. Here it is, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Across many large twin and adoption studies conducted in wealthy countries, the heritability of educational attainment and of measured cognitive ability is substantial — estimates commonly fall between 0.4 and 0.6. (2) A trait's heritability is the proportion of the differences between people that is due to their genes rather than to their environment. (3) If most of the differences between people in a trait are due to genes, then differences in people's environments can explain only a small residual share of the variation. (4) If environmental differences explain only a small residual share, then changing people's environments — through better-funded schools, tutoring programs, or enriched early childhood — can move outcomes by at most a small amount. (5) Policies whose effects are small relative to their cost should not be adopted. (6) Therefore, expensive interventions aimed at raising educational attainment are, on the whole, a poor use of public money, and we should expect this regardless of how well-designed the interventions are.

I should say what I would most like to keep, so your diagnosis doesn't just ask me to abandon everything. I want to keep (1): the twin-study literature is what it is, and I'm not interested in attacking the methodology. I also want to keep (5) as a piece of ordinary cost-benefit reasoning. And I want to keep the general idea that genetics is relevant to policy somewhere — I don't want the answer to be that genes and policy simply never interact.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of where this argument goes wrong. If the right move is to reject a premise, tell me which one, explain why it seemed plausible enough to get past a room full of careful readers, and state precisely what we give up by rejecting it — including any genuinely policy-relevant conclusions we can no longer draw. If instead the argument is valid and the right move is to accept a premise's cost, say so and price it. Either way, please also state what heritability estimates, properly understood, do license us to conclude, so the correction doesn't overshoot into the claim that they tell us nothing at all.

Finally, please be explicit about the fragility of your own answer: name the consideration or empirical result that, if it turned out differently, would change your verdict, and identify the step in your reasoning where you think you are most likely to be mistaken.
