On a governance forum I follow, "vote values, bet beliefs" gets quoted constantly, most recently to shut down a thread where members were debating whether the community should hold a direct vote on a contentious treasury policy. The person invoking it seemed to think it settled two things at once: that the community should never vote on policy questions, and that once the market has spoken on a policy, further disagreement is just confusion about one's own values. I suspect the slogan is doing more work than it can bear, but I can't pin down exactly where it breaks. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing — for example, a procedural division of labor between ends and means, an epistemic claim about which aggregation mechanism tracks truth better, a metaethical claim that values are not truth-apt at all, and an institutional claim about who may legitimately decide what. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or something actually false, and note what it quietly assumes — about the fact/value distinction, about whether value questions are ever free of empirical commitments, about who defines the market's payoff conditions and the ends the policy is scored against. Then tell me which readings, if any, would actually support my interlocutor's two uses of it, and whether the strongest defensible reading of the slogan is compatible with the community voting on the policy anyway.
