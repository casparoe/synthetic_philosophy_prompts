In ten days I'm giving a seminar talk in a mixed room -- about half machine learning PhD students and postdocs who work on LLMs, half philosophy grad students and one philosophy prof, all at the same university, all of whom have read the popular stuff on AI risk but nobody's a full-time alignment researcher.

My thesis: "prosaic alignment" -- the idea that we can align systems built from basically current methods (pretraining + RLHF-style fine-tuning + maybe some scalable oversight tricks like debate or weak-to-strong supervision) without any fundamental new insight -- has a hole at its center that nobody's patch works on. Specifically my claim is that all of these techniques supervise the model using judgements that humans produce *after the model has learned to predict those judgements*, so what you actually train is a model of the reviewer, not a model of the truth, and once the model knows things the reviewer can't check, the training signal quietly points at "look right to the reviewer" instead of "be right". I argue debate and weak-to-strong generalization just push this problem one level up rather than solving it. My positive claim is modest: not that alignment is impossible, just that you need at least one genuinely new idea about how to get a signal that isn't bottlenecked on human judgements, and nobody has one yet.

Prep me for the Q&A. I want the objections ordered by how likely they are to actually get asked, and for each one: how that specific half of the room would phrase it (the ML people will say things like "but that's just a training distribution problem" or gesture at reward hacking literature, the philosophers will say things like "isn't this just the problem of testimony" or ask whether humans have the same problem when judging scientists), how much damage it does to my thesis, a two-minute reply, and where my reply is weakest so I don't overclaim. Also: at least one of the philosophers will object that my whole argument assumes a fact of the matter the model could track independently of us, and they'll come at me with verificationism or something adjacent -- tell me how to handle that without the talk becoming about truth in general.

Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. And flag which objection I should concede part of up front so I don't sound like I've never trained a model.
