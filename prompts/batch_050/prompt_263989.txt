My reading group has been working through the literature on indicative conditionals, and for our next meeting I produced the argument below. Every step looks airtight to me, yet the conclusion seems to refute something I take to be nearly obvious, so I want a diagnosis, not a referee report on the algebra.

Notation: A and C are ordinary factual propositions (say, "it rained last night" and "the streets are wet"), and "A → C" is the ordinary English indicative conditional. Cr is an idealized rational credence function, with Cr(X | Y) defined as usual as Cr(X ∧ Y)/Cr(Y) when Cr(Y) > 0.

(1) Propositionalism: an indicative conditional expresses a proposition — something true or false that can be conjoined with other propositions, assigned a probability, and conditionalized on like any other content.

(2) Adams's Thesis: for any rational credence function Cr, Cr(A → C) = Cr(C | A) whenever Cr(A) > 0. This just codifies the Ramsey-test intuition that you evaluate an indicative by hypothetically adding the antecedent to your stock of beliefs and assessing the consequent on that basis.

(3) Stability: Adams's Thesis is a constitutive norm governing indicatives, not a coincidence of one's current state of opinion. So it holds for every rational credence function, including any function obtained from Cr by conditionalizing on a proposition with positive probability.

Derivation:

(4) Conditionalize on C (supposing Cr(C) > 0). By (3), the function Cr(· | C) must also satisfy Adams's Thesis, so Cr(A → C | C) = Cr(C | A ∧ C) = 1.

(5) Conditionalize on ¬C (supposing Cr(¬C) > 0). Similarly, Cr(A → C | ¬C) = Cr(C | A ∧ ¬C) = 0.

(6) By the law of total probability, which applies because (1) makes A → C a proposition: Cr(A → C) = Cr(A → C | C)·Cr(C) + Cr(A → C | ¬C)·Cr(¬C) = Cr(C).

(7) Combining (6) with (2): Cr(C | A) = Cr(C).

(8) But A and C were arbitrary. So (7) says that no indicative's antecedent is ever probabilistically relevant to its consequent — learning the antecedent of any conditional you accept could never raise or lower your rational confidence in its consequent. In the rain case: your credence that the streets are wet given that it rained must equal your unconditional credence that the streets are wet. That is absurd, and absurd in the way that earned arguments of this shape the label "triviality results": a language whose indicatives obeyed (2) and (3) could not express any interesting dependence at all.

I have checked the arithmetic and I believe the derivation is valid given (1)–(3), so I take the real question to be which premise is least costly to surrender. My strong preference is to keep Adams's Thesis — it seems to me to capture exactly what is right about the Ramsey test — and I am also reluctant to abandon the idea that indicatives say something true or false about the world. What I want from you: name the single premise or step a friend of indicatives should reject; explain why that premise looked harmless in isolation; then price the three exit strategies I know of — denying that indicatives are truth-apt contents at all, denying that Adams's Thesis survives conditionalization onto arbitrary new probability functions, and denying that a conditional expresses one fixed proposition across all those functions — saying which you judge cheapest overall, and what each strategy must say about the everyday validity of modus ponens and modus tollens for indicatives.

Please lead with your bottom line — which premise or step to reject — in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate the argument back to me.
