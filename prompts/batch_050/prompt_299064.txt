I'm in a graduate logic seminar that has spent most of the term on the Liar paradox, and I want a diagnosis of the field rather than another tour of the solutions. As far as I can tell, every major approach ends up stuck in the same structural way, and the stuckness is remarkably well documented: Tarski-style hierarchies handle the paradox but leave natural language's apparently single truth predicate unexplained and reintroduce the problem at the level of the hierarchy itself; gap theories in the Kripke tradition cannot say, in their own object language, that the Liar is not true, and saying it in the metalanguage just restarts the problem one level up; revision theories make truth unstable by design and then face strengthened versions built from the revision sequence; dialetheism accepts true contradictions and then struggles to express, within its own framework, the ordinary idea that the Liar is not also simply false in the way most claims are; and paracomplete approaches in the style of Field keep needing notions like determinateness whose iterations generate new revenge paradoxes. The literature has known all of this for decades, and the main effect of new work seems to be more refined revenge problems rather than convergence.

My working hypothesis, which I'd like you to engage directly, is that this debate stopped being about the Liar sentence a long time ago and is really a disagreement about which theoretical cost is bearable -- giving up expressive completeness, giving up classical logic, giving up the unrestricted T-schema, or giving up consistency -- dressed up as a disagreement about one sentence. If that's right, the impasse isn't a sign the question is ill-posed so much as a sign that the sides are ranking costs by different lights, possibly by intuitions neither side can argue for from neutral ground. But I could be wrong: maybe there is a hidden formal or factual question here, such as what natural language's truth predicate is actually like, that could in principle be settled. Tell me which it is, with reasons grounded in what the positions actually say and concede, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis -- a distinction nobody has drawn yet, a theorem showing which desiderata are jointly unsatisfiable, a piece of empirical linguistics, or an argument that the meta-question about what a solution owes us is itself undecidable -- or else argue honestly that no progress should be expected and explain why that doesn't make the debate worthless.

Please structure the response in four sections, in this order: The Stalls, Restated (about 150 words; where each family of solutions actually breaks down, not a survey); The Diagnosis (about 300 words; what kind of disagreement this is, engaging my cost-ranking hypothesis head-on and saying where it's right or wrong); What Would Move It (about 200 words; the specific kind of result, distinction, or evidence that would count as progress, and whether anything in the existing literature already comes close); and Bottom Line (about 75 words; one paragraph, no hedging).
