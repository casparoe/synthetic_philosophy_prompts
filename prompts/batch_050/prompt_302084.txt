I'm a novelist, which is a slightly mortifying thing to admit while asking for philosophy help, but here we are. I'm writing a novel set at Oxford in 1956, and the climax is the vote in Convocation over whether to give Harry Truman an honorary degree — the one where Elizabeth Anscombe, pretty much alone, stood up and argued the university was about to honour a man responsible for what she considered murder, forced an actual vote on it, and lost with only a handful of others voting non placet beside her. In my big scene, a young tutor who's read Anscombe's pamphlet Mr Truman's Degree argues it out with an older colleague who thinks the protest was sanctimonious grandstanding, and I desperately want the philosophy in their exchange to be right rather than the caricature version. The part of the pamphlet I keep circling is the numbered reconstruction early on (section II, I think), where she doesn't start with the bombs at all but walks through how we got there: the qualified British promise in 1939 not to bomb civilians unless the Germans did it first, the demand for unconditional surrender which she calls the root of the evils because an unlimited war aim more or less forces the most ruthless methods, the propaganda about modern war being indivisible so that every civilian is really a combatant, and so on.

Here's what I need, and I'd like it to run in both directions. First, interpret that reconstruction: is Anscombe offering a causal explanation meant to soften the verdict on Truman (he inherited a machine already pointed at city-killing), or is it meant to harden it (everyone saw the logic and chose not to resist it), or something slipperier? Second, apply her machinery to the decision itself. Her pamphlet's central move, as I understand it, is the distinction between what you intend and what you merely foresee, plus the insistence that whatever you deliberately take as a means to your end you do intend, so the foreseeable-but-unintended defense fails for a bomb dropped on a city. Run that rigorously: on her view, what exactly was Truman's intented end, what were his means, does it matter that Japan had made peace feelers he knew about, and does the older colleague's best counterargument — that the deaths were a side effect of destroying Japan's war-making capacity, not a means — even get off the ground given how she defines murder? Third, and this is where I hope the novel earns its keep, turn it around: what does the episode do to her position? Does the case expose that her intention/foresight line depends entirely on contested descriptions of the act (dropping a bomb on a city vs. shortening the war), so that double effect can be made to condemn or absolve almost anything by redescribing? Does her own causal story about unconditional surrender quietly concede that individual decision-makers are less blameworthy than her verdict requires? And is it a gap that she says so little about what Truman should have done instead — blockade, demonstration on an uninhabited target, conditional surrender — given she's explicit that she's not a pacifist?

If it helps, the thing I'm really after is which of my two characters has the better of the argument by Anscombe's own lights, not who's more sympathetic — the young tutor keeps quoting (well, paraphrasing) her at the older man, and I want to know where she'd actually say her opponent has a point. Also, if there's a spot where the standard historical summary I've sketched above is wrong or too tidy in a way that matters philosophically, flag that rather than politely working around it.
