I work as a software engineer, with no background in philosophy, and I would like help adjudicating an argument on my company's internal forum. The firm has proposed dedicating 3% of engineering time to preparing for low-probability, high-impact events: cascading infrastructure failure, a compromised core dependency, that sort of thing. One colleague defended the plan by saying it is no different from buying home insurance: you pay a small, certain premium against a small chance of an enormous loss, so expected value is all that matters. A second colleague replied that this is just Pascal's wager in secular dress, the theologian's argument that one should believe in God because the potential payoff is infinite, and that since that argument is fallacious, the insurance defence falls with it. The first colleague conceded that Pascal's wager fails, but claimed it fails only because of the many-gods problem (one cannot prepare for every incompatible deity at once), and that our case has no such problem, so the insurance logic goes through cleanly.

Could you audit the insurance analogy? Set out what corresponds to what — the premium, the payout, the actuarial probability, the insurer — and then identify where the mapping breaks down. I am particularly interested in whether the finiteness of our potential losses genuinely makes the analogy stronger than Pascal's original wager, whether the many-gods problem is really absent in our case or merely reappears in another form, and what the analogy quietly assumes about probabilities being known and well-defined. Finally, please say whether either colleague's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and offer a better analogy for this kind of decision if you can think of one.
