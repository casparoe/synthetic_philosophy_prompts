So my roommate and I have been going back and forth on Weber's Protestant ethic thesis for like two weeks now (neither of us is a sociologist, we're both just stubborn), and we've hit a wall. The sticking point is this passage from Chapter 1 of The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism, where Weber is pushing back on the idea that the Reformation just loosened the Church's grip and let economic life flourish:

"It meant the repudiation of a control which was very lax, at that time scarcely perceptible in practice, and hardly more than formal, in favor of a regulation, of the whole of conduct which, penetrating to all departments of private and public life, was infinitely burdensome and earnestly enforced."

My roommate reads this as Weber making a genuinely bold causal claim: that a change in values -- specifically the ascetic, calling-oriented ethos that came with this much harsher discipline -- was itself a real cause of the rise and character of modern capitalism, not just window dressing on top of material changes (trade routes, technology, institutions, the wealth of the towns that happened to go Protestant, which Weber concedes in the surrounding pages). I read the passage more deflatingly: all Weber is really doing here is flagging a puzzle (why did the most economically advanced regions accept a *stricter* church?), and the actual causal thesis is the kind of thing that can never really be established -- too many confounders, unfalsifiable psychological mechanism about salvation anxiety, etc. We basically agree about the facts and disagree about what "a cause" even has to mean for the thesis to go through.

So here's what I want from you. Give me your actual credence -- a number between 0 and 1 -- that a Weber-style claim is true, stated precisely as: a change in religiously grounded values was a genuine contributing cause (not the only cause, but a real one, doing non-redundant work) of the distinctive character and timing of modern capitalism in Protestant Europe. But don't just give me the number. Decompose it into the sub-questions you think the credence actually depends on -- I'd guess things like: how well the raw empirical pattern (Protestant overrepresentation in capital ownership, management, technical education) survives controlling for the confounders Weber himself lists; whether culture/values can ever be a real causal variable at the macro-historical level or whether that talk always reduces to material and institutional factors; whether the specific psychological mechanism Weber posits (anxiety about election channeling into methodical worldly work) is remotely plausible as human psychology; and whether later attempts to test the thesis quantitatively have come out looking supportive or not. Give a rough probability for each sub-question with one line of justification, say how you combine them into the final number, tell me which sub-question your final answer is most sensitive to, and what piece of evidence or argument would move it the most. Then, based on your decomposition, tell me whose reading of the passage is more likely right -- my roommate's "bold causal thesis" reading or my "puzzle-flagging" reading -- since I suspect that also decomposes into some of the same terms.
