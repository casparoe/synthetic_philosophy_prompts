I sit on the treatment-review panel of a mid-sized private oncology clinic, and next month we vote on whether to adopt a prognostic tool that would change how we allocate an expensive, punishing course of combination chemotherapy. The tool assigns each patient a probability of surviving two years given the treatment, and the proposal is that patients below a threshold be offered gentler palliative care instead. I'm a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, so the calibration data they showed us made immediate sense to me: amongst patients the model scored at 30 per cent, roughly 30 per cent survived, and so on across the range. But the discussion has surfaced a disagreement I can't resolve, and it's philosophical rather than statistical. One of our senior oncologists argues that the model's numbers are merely frequencies over a reference class -- aggregates about people-like-this-patient -- whereas the patient in front of her is a single individual who will either survive or not, and that treating the model's output as *the* probability for that individual is a category error dressed up as rigour. She'll grant the model is useful and still deny that any particular patient "has" a 30 per cent chance in a sense we should defer to.

Complicating matters, a second, independently validated model, calibrated on largely the same historical data, gives noticeably different probabilities for some individual patients while performing equally well at the group level. So even if my colleague is wrong and deference to the model's chances is rational in principle, it's unclear what we're meant to defer *to* when two equally credentialed sources of putative objective chance disagree -- averaging them feels ad hoc, and I dimly recall from philosophy-of-probability reading (David Lewis's Principal Principle, I think) that the norm is to match your credence to the objective chance, which says nothing about which of two rival claims about the chance to believe. There's also a feedback worry I raised, Goodhart-adjacent: if we start treating low-probability patients less aggressively, their survival rates will presumably fall, and the model's gloomy predictions will become true partly because we believed them -- so the "objective" chances we'd be deferring to are partly manufactured by our own deference.

Please draft the decision memo I can table at the meeting: a clear recommendation to adopt the tool as proposed, reject it, or adopt it with specified conditions, with a bottom line up front. The reasoning should engage seriously with the individual-versus-reference-class objection rather than waving it away, should state how the panel ought to handle disagreement between two well-calibrated models, and should address the self-fulfilling-prediction problem. It should also give the strongest version of the case for whichever option you recommend against, since the losing side at this meeting will want its objections answered rather than dismissed. Write it in British English, and keep it to a length the panel might actually read -- no bibliography or citations, just the argument.
