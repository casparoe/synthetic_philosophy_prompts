So a guy in my reading group sprung this on us last week and it's been eating at me, because the conclusion feels obviously too strong but none of us could cleanly kill the argument. We've been going through the control-vs-alignment stuff (untrusted monitoring, honeypots, sandbagging evals, the whole agenda of "assume the model might be scheming and box it in anyway"). His claim is that the whole control project is secretly incoherent, and his argument goes:

(1) A safety measure only counts as *control* — as opposed to alignment — if it's supposed to work even when the model is actively trying to subvert it.

(2) Controlling an agent requires that some component of the overall system can reliably tell when the agent's actions are bad.

(3) Reliably telling whether an action is bad requires understanding the action and its likely consequences at least as well as the agent taking it does. (You can't catch what you can't comprehend.)

(4) So the checking component has to be at least as capable as the model being controlled.

(5) But anything that capable is, by the control framework's own lights, untrusted — the entire motivation for control is that capability at that level is exactly what you can't take on faith.

(6) So every control scheme either quietly trusts an untrusted component (which is just alignment with extra steps) or pushes the problem up a level, and then (4) and (5) apply there too. Regress.

(7) Therefore control without alignment is impossible, and the control agenda is a distraction from the real problem.

The reason I say this proves too much: run the same argument with humans and you get that no prison can hold an inmate smarter than the guards, no audit regime can constrain accountants cleverer than the auditors, no constitution can bind people more cunning than its drafters. Those conclusions are at least not a priori true — some of them look empirically false, or at least false in the cases where the institutions were designed well. So something in the chain has to give. But going premise by premise I couldn't find the one to shoot. Something feels off around (3) or (4), but everytime I try to say what, it comes out sounding like I'm just denying that smart models are dangerous, which I'm not.

If I had to pick things to keep, I'd most like to keep (1) and (2) — they feel close to definitional. And I suspect *some* version of the argument survives, because the reading group consensus was that he's gesturing at a real worry even if (7) is overreach.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
