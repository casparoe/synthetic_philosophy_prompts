Since retiring from laboratory work in chemistry, I have been reading my way through the philosophy of science, and a popular book on the demarcation problem recently presented Popper's verdict on Darwinism in the following terms: "Popper, who drew the line between science and non-science at falsifiability, concluded that Darwin's theory of evolution fails his own test. If fitness simply means leaving more offspring, then the principle that the fittest survive rules nothing out and can never be refuted; the theory is a tautology, and therefore not science at all. In his later years he adopted a gentler tone, but he never withdrew this judgment, and his authority accordingly remains available to anyone who denies that evolution is genuine science." Much of the first part rings true to me, but the conclusion sits badly, and I dimly recall having read somewhere that Popper revised his position at some point, though I cannot now say where or to what extent. Would you go through this summary piece by piece and tell me what a careful reader of Popper would accept, what misrepresents him and in which direction, and whether the closing sentence survives once the record is set straight?
