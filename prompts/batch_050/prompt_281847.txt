A friend and I argued late into the night about when discounting what someone says is an injustice rather than ordinary epistemic caution, and I would like my verdicts put in order. I had been defending the principle that a credibility deficit is unjust when it flows from assumptions about the kind of person speaking rather than from what was said or from the speaker's own track record. Test it against my cases:

- A woman reports severe abdominal pain in an emergency room; the staff assume she is being dramatic, send her home, and she nearly dies of a ruptured appendix. Injustice.
- A colleague who has cried wolf about deadlines for years warns that a project is about to fail; the team discounts him; he turns out to be right. No injustice — his record made caution reasonable.
- Jurors find a witness with a heavy foreign accent less credible than a native speaker giving substantively identical testimony. Injustice.
- Police give less weight to burglary reports from a neighborhood with a documented history of false reports. No injustice — just base rates. Yet a clinic that discounts pain reports from patients with psychiatric histories, on equally statistical grounds, strikes me as unjust, and I cannot say what separates the two.
- In 1960, a woman harassed by her employer has no concept of sexual harassment; she cannot articulate her experience even to herself, and everyone she approaches treats her as oversensitive. Injustice — though I concede I am unsure anyone involved could have done otherwise.
- A hospital's automated triage system systematically deprioritizes complaints phrased in non-standard dialects. Something is badly wrong here, but I hesitate to call it an injustice, since no one disbelieves anyone.

My friend insists that an injustice always requires a blameworthy hearer who could have believed otherwise; on that standard the last two cases drop out, which I resist. Give me a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause and an honest statement of which of my verdicts cannot be saved. Then tell me what it implies about the case I keep circling: a junior engineer whose correct warnings are ignored because her manager treats a senior colleague's contrary word as definitive — where the wrong, if there is one, seems to lie in believing the wrong person too much rather than her too little.
