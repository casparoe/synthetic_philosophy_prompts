So I got into a thing on Discord last night about whether definite descriptions "refer," and a friend-of-a-friend who's doing a philosophy minor dropped this summary of Russell's theory of descriptions, which he said he was paraphrasing from some lecture series:

"Russell's basic point is that definite descriptions aren't names. 'The present king of France' doesn't refer to anything, so a sentence like 'the present king of France is bald' isn't false, it's neither true nor false -- it misfires, kind of like a presupposition failure, the way 'have you stopped beating your wife' has no good answer. The analysis is that description-sentences unpack into existence-plus-uniqueness claims: 'the author of Waverley was Scott' really says there was exactly one author of Waverley and whoever it was, was Scott. And Russell thought ordinary proper names work the same way -- 'Aristotle' is just shorthand for a description like 'the teacher of Alexander' -- so language never hooks onto the world directly, only ever through descriptions."

Now, a couple of things here smell off to me. I've absorbed enough from years of reading LW-adjacent stuff about tabooing words and disguised queries that the "names are just descriptions" move sets off my alarm bells, and I vaguely remember that the neither-true-nor-false bit is actually associated with someone *else* (Strawson?) and that Russell's whole thing was supposed to be the opposite -- that the sentence comes out false, full stop. But I genuinely can't reconstruct why Russell would insist on that, or what hangs on it, and this guy is usually careful.

So: is this a fair summary? Don't give me a both-sides survey -- actually commit. Tell me which parts are right, which parts are a different philosopher's view being passed off as Russell's, and which parts Russell would have flatly denied. In particular I want to know: (a) what Russell actually says about "the present king of France is bald" and why he prefers that answer to the misfire/presupposition story, (b) whether the "names are disguised descriptions" claim is Russell's view or a common inflation of it, and what he'd say about language hooking onto the world *directly* given his views on acquaintance, and (c) whether the existence-plus-uniqueness paraphrase part, which is the bit I'm most confident is right, survives everything else being wrong.
