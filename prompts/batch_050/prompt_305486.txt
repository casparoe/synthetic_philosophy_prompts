I recently joined a computational neuroscience lab that is piloting a study of long-term meditators and chronic pain, and I have been quietly recording how my labmate — a committed predictive processing theorist — sorts the phenomena we discuss into her four favored categories: perception, learning, attention, and action. Her verdicts so far: binocular rivalry flipping every few seconds is perception; gradually improving at discriminating faint gratings over six weeks of training is learning; a sudden loud bang seizing your focus is attention; reaching for a glass is action; the thirty-second drift of felt hand position in the rubber-hand illusion is perception; and adapting one's reaching to prism goggles over ten minutes is learning. When I pressed her on the basis for these calls, she appealed to a sentence from Karl Friston's The free-energy principle: a unified brain theory? — "Perception optimizes predictions by minimizing free energy with respect to synaptic activity (perceptual inference), efficacy (learning and memory) and gain (attention and salience)." — but she seemed to me to read that parenthetical in a way that her own verdicts do not fully pin down.

The new case our study forces on us is this: an experienced meditator whose reduced pain is, on the standard predictive-processing story, implemented as durably lowered gain on interoceptive prediction errors, where the gain-setting itself was acquired through years of practice. I need to predict whether she will call this attention (because the operative mechanism is gain) or learning (because the gain-setting was slowly trained), and I cannot tell which commitment her track record actually embodies. Please respond as a philosopher of cognitive science with deep expertise in predictive processing, and think carefully: privately brainstorm the full space of classificatory rules consistent with all six verdicts before committing, though I want only the finished analysis, not the brainstorming. Format the answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) your predicted verdict on the meditator case, with rough confidence; (2) the single rule she is most plausibly running, stated precisely and tied to a specific reading of the Friston sentence; (3) at least two rival rules that fit all six prior verdicts equally well, noting what each predicts about the meditator; (4) which of the six earlier cases is the closest analog to the meditator case, with a defense of that choice against the obvious alternative; (5) one further hypothetical case, not yet discussed, whose verdict would best discriminate your favored rule from the strongest rival.
