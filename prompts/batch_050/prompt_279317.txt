I'm a PhD student in cognitive science working on concept learning, and I'm drafting a chapter on what few-shot visual induction -- of the sort demanded by Bongard problems (six example scenes on the left that instantiate some concept, six on the right that don't, infer the distinction) -- actually reveals about the format of human concepts. My committee is split between two camps, and I've gotten tired of reading-group debates where both sides gesture at the same classic problems and claim victory. So I want a pair of new problems engineered from scratch to force the camps apart.

Here is the specification. Construct two original Bongard-style problems, call them Problem S and Problem R. Both must be fully specifiable in words (no actual drawings: describe each of the twelve panels precisely enough that a reader could sketch them), both concepts must be statable in a single English sentence, and both must be solvable by an attentive human within a few minutes. The twist is the target divergence. Problem S should be one where a pure exemplar-or-similarity learner -- a system that classifies new panels by their overall similarity to the stored left and right panels, using only the perceptual features given in the scene and weighted by attention -- reliably succeeds, while a rule-based learner that searches a space of compositional symbolic descriptions ranked by minimum description length reliably fails or converges on the wrong rule. Problem R should be the mirror image: the rule-based learner nails it, while the similarity learner is systematically misled, i.e., the panels' raw perceptual similarity structure points away from the true concept and not merely fails to point toward it. Do not smuggle the divergence in by making one concept depend on absurdly fine-grained features (counting pixels, sub-visible curvature) or by using a gerrymandered concept no human would induce; the divergence should arise from the structure of the concepts themselves, not from handicapping one learner's input.

After presenting the two problems, demonstrate that the specification is met: walk through each learner's actual operation on each problem -- which stored exemplars dominate the similarity judgment in Problem S and why the shortest adequate rule there is longer than several seductive shorter-but-wrong rules; which rule the description-length search finds first in Problem R and which misleading similarity cluster defeats the exemplar learner. Then tell me which single structural feature of each problem is doing the work, and what happens to the divergence if that feature is removed while everything else is held fixed. Finally, steelman the losing side in each case: how would the similarity theorist try to recover Problem R by re-describing the feature space rather than admitting rules, and how would the rule theorist argue that the similarity learner's success in Problem S is just a symbolic rule wearing a distance metric's clothing? I want to end the chapter by saying whether the pair shows the two frameworks are genuinely competing explanations of few-shot induction or notational variants of one another, so close with a verdict on that question and what a third problem would have to look like to settle it definitively.
