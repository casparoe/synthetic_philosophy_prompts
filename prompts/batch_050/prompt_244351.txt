I'm reporting a long-form piece on the internal design of social media enforcement, and I keep hitting a conceptual tangle I want to resolve before I file. The platforms I've examined borrow the architecture of criminal justice — strike systems resembling sentencing guidelines, graduated suspensions, appeals processes, even reductions akin to time served — while insisting that removing an account is not punishment at all but something closer to a shopkeeper ejecting a disruptive customer: a matter of property and contract, not of justice. My question is what theory of punishment, if any, this practice actually presupposes. Does the proportionality machinery only make sense if users have some legitimate claim on the platform — an interest in an audience the platform is obliged to respect — such that enforcement is genuinely punitive and owes the sanctioned person justification? Or can a pure property-and-contract view explain the strikes and appeals some other way (deterrence, public relations), making the judicial trappings mere decoration? Finally, contrast this with crowd-driven pile-ons, which have no proportionality machinery whatsoever: does that absence show informal social sanction presupposes a different theory — expressive rather than punitive — or that it presupposes no theory at all? And what would concretely have to change if platforms were treated as exercising punitive power: due process, proportionality constraints, something like double jeopardy for coordinated cross-platform bans?
