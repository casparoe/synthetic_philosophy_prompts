I recently got made the incident review lead at my company (mid-size logistics firm, ~200 drivers) and honestly I got handed this because I was the only one who didnt say no. The job is: after any incident -- an accident, a near-miss, a compliance screwup -- I have to write a report saying who, if anyone, is responsible for it happening. And the cases that keep breaking my brain arent the ones where someone did something wrong, theyre the ones where someone *didnt* do something. The dispatcher who saw a weird route on the board and didnt flag it. The supervisor who was supposed to run a safety check that day and skipped it. The night-shift guy who could have closed the gate and just... didnt, same as every night.

Philosophy apparently has a whole literature on "causation by omission" and I want you to turn whatever it actually knows into a procedure I can run. Some things Ive half-absorbed that you should sanity-check and build on: the but-for test seems to both overcount (literally hundreds of people each "could have" prevented any given accident, including my drivers' family members who couldve phoned them that morning) and undercount (sometimes the but-for cause is something absurd like the company existing at all). There also seems to be a thing where omissions only count against a background of what was normal or expected -- the skipped safety check is different from some random passerby not intervening -- but I dont know how to draw that line when our written procedures are incomplete. And then theres the mess where TWO people each could have prevented it alone, so neither one individually passes the but-for test, but clearly someone should be on the hook.

So concretely, give me a step-by-step procedure I can run per incident in maybe 30-45 min, in order, that: (a) generates the candidate list of omissions without it being literally everyone on earth, (b) has tests for whether each candidate omission actually counts as a cause of this outcome rather than just a missed opportunity, (c) handles the two-people-could-each-have-prevented-it case without letting both off, (d) says what to do when the "expectation" the omission violated is unwritten -- custom, habit, "everyone just knows" -- and (e) is explicit about where the procedure bottoms out in a judgment call about norms rather than facts, because my reports get read by HR and legal and I need to flag which conclusions are procedure-output vs my call.

Then run it on three cases: (1) driver crashes after a dispatcher notices but doesnt act on a flag that the driver had logged suspiciously few rest hours -- procedure said dispatchers "may" escalate; (2) a warehouse injury where both the floor manager and the shift lead each independently skipped a check that either one's check alone would have caught the hazard; (3) a contractor (not our employee, no duties from us) who watched a leak spreading toward an electrical panel for an hour and said nothing. I suspect the procedure will say something I wont like about case 3, and possibly about case 1, and Id rather know that now. Also tell me what the procedure flat-out cannot settle, so I stop pretending a checklist can do it.
