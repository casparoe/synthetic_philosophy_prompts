My sister and I keep arguing about our late father's beliefs, and I would like help thinking through what is really at stake between us. Throughout his life he said, apparently with full sincerity, that he believed in an afterlife and that he would one day see his parents again. Yet when friends died he grieved as intensely as anyone, in his final illness he was plainly afraid of dying, and as far as either of us can tell he never made a single practical decision differently from our uncle, an avowed atheist. My sister concludes that our father did not really believe what he said — that his avowals were wishful thinking or social habit, since a genuine conviction that death is a passage to somewhere better would have to show up in how a person faces death, mourns, and plans. I resist this. His belief seemed to matter deeply to him, to give shape to how he spoke about loss and about his own mortality, even though it plainly did not function like his belief that the bridge on his commute was safe or that winter follows autumn. Who has the better account of what his believing amounted to? In answering, please analyze the structure of the disagreement itself: what does sincerity in a conviction of this kind actually require, is a belief's genuineness measured by its emotional and behavioral consequences, and can a conviction be fully real while regulating a person's life in a way that is altogether different from how ordinary factual beliefs do? I am not asking you to adjudicate whether an afterlife exists, only which of us has the more defensible picture of what our father's avowals meant.
