QUESTION: State the Third Antinomy of pure reason, sketch Kant's arguments on each side, and explain how transcendental idealism is supposed to resolve the conflict. Does the resolution establish anything about human freedom?

STUDENT'S ANSWER: The Third Antinomy concerns causality. The thesis holds that not all causality is causality according to laws of nature: there must be a causality through freedom, a spontaneous beginning of a series of events, without which no explanation would ever be complete, since every natural cause would itself demand a prior cause. The antithesis holds that there is no freedom and that everything happens solely according to laws of nature, since a cause operating outside the law-governed order of experience would be something we could never encounter in any possible experience. Kant resolves the antinomy by showing that both thesis and antithesis are false, because both rest on treating the world of appearances as though it were a world of things in themselves; once transcendental idealism is in place, the demand for an unconditioned cause dissolves. On the basis of this dissolution, Kant then assigns freedom to the noumenal self and strict determinism to the phenomenal self: because the intelligible character is not subject to temporal conditions, one and the same action can be empirically determined yet transcendently free. This establishes that human beings do in fact possess free will, which morality requires, and so the apparent conflict between freedom and nature is fully dissolved.
