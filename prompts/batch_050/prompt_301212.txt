So I'm a PhD student in theoretical physics who keeps drifting toward philosophy of physics questions, and I keep running into the slogan that gauge symmetry is "not a real symmetry, just a redundancy in our description." Most recently at our group meeting someone deployed it to shut down a question about the Higgs mechansim: the textbook story says the electroweak gauge symmetry gets spontaneously broken, but if gauge symmetry is mere redundancy then there was never anything there to break, so this whole way of talking is confused and the question dissolves. I feel like something's off with that move but I can't cleanly say what.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan might be expressing? I suspect there's a spectrum from the near-trivial (the formalism carries more variables than there are physical degrees of freedom) through substantive claims (Noether's theorems treating global and local symmetries differently, gauge transformations having no observable consequences, something like Elitzur's result ruling out spontaneous breaking of a local symmetry) up to something radical like gauge structure being pure convention with no physical content whatsoever. For each reading: is it a platitude, a substantive truth, or actually false? And crucially, which readings, if any, would actually justify the group-meeting move of dismissing "symmetry breaking" talk in the Higgs case -- since that's the work the slogan keeps being made to do?
