In the legal systems I am familiar with, a person can be detained in a psychiatric facility against their will, without having committed or been charged with any crime, provided two things are established: that they suffer from a recognized mental disorder, and that they pose a danger to themselves or to others. The detention is indefinite, lasting until clinicians judge the person well, rather than for a fixed term; it is administered by doctors rather than judges; and the detained person is classified as a patient, not a prisoner. Meanwhile, the very same legal systems refuse to detain dangerous people preventively in any other context: a violent temperament, a criminal record, even stated intentions to harm someone justify no detention at all until an act is committed. The diagnosis seems to be doing all the work. Someone with suicidal despair who receives a diagnosis of major depression can be committed; someone with identical despair who is judged to be responding to ordinary misfortune cannot.

What theory of illness does this arrangement presuppose? It seems to require that mental disorder is something a person *has*, like an infection, rather than something they *do* or choose -- otherwise treating it as a reason to override their will, rather than as a reason they give, would be hard to justify. It also seems to presuppose that such disorders are individuated by something other than social deviance, since if the diagnosis merely redescribed conduct the majority dislikes, the detention would be punishment by another name and the exemption from criminal law's safeguards would be indefensible. Thomas Szasz famously denied exactly these presuppositions, arguing that bodily illness is defined by lesions and physiological malfunction, that minds are not the sort of thing that can be ill in that literal sense, and that what psychiatry calls illness is conduct judged deviant against ethical and social norms -- so that involuntary commitment is moral enforcement wearing a medical disguise, whatever its intentions.

My question is whether the practice strictly needs the strong view Szasz attacked, or whether it is compatible with several weaker theories. Could a purely paternalist or harm-preventive theory license commitment without any concept of disease at all, and if so, why does actual law route everything through diagnosis? Does the contrast with the criminal law's refusal of preventive detention show that the two halves of the system presuppose inconsistent views of the same person's agency? Reconstruct the implicit theory of illness the practice actually runs on, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, identify where it is philosophically underdetermined, and say concretely what would have to change in commitment law if the system instead adopted a Szaszian view that so-called mental illnesses are problems in living rather than diseases. Throughout, indicate how confident you are in each part of your reconstruction, and single out the specific point in your answer you are least sure of and why.
