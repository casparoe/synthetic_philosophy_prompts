So I retired from my chemistry lecturer job two years ago and, to keep my brain from going soft, I joined a philosophy reading group at the public library. This month we're doing African thought, and we got onto the idea that a person isn't just born a person, full stop, but becomes one through relations with others -- there was a Nguni proverb along the lines of a person being a person through other people. Everyone nodded along, but I kept poking at it with cases, and my gut verdicts don't line up in any way I can articulate. I want to know what principle, if any, sits underneath them.

Here are my verdicts. (1) A newborn, one hour old, no relationships to speak of yet: fully a person, no hesitation. (2) A girl raised in an orphanage with rotating staff, never forming a single stable attachment: still fully a person, though I feel she's been wronged precisely because of what's missing -- and I can't tell if that means something was taken from her personhood or just from her life. (3) My old neighbour with advanced dementia who no longer recognizes his wife: fully a person, I'd be furious at anyone who said otherwise. (4) A woman in a permanent coma whose sister visits and talks to her every day: still a person, and the sister's devotion seems relevant somehow, though I couldn't say why. (5) A miserly uncle who cheated his own family, exploited everyone around him, and died unmourned -- people said of him that he'd stopped being a person, and honestly I agree: something in his personhood was genuinely diminished. (6) A hermit who walked out of the village forty years ago, cut every tie by his own choice, and wants nothing to do with anyone: still fully a person, no less than before. I realize (5) and (6) sit awkwardly together -- both men ended up relation-less, and I downgrade one and not the other -- but that's where my gut is. (7) The family dog that grieved at my father's grave: not a person, but clearly more than a thing. (8) A stranger who settled in the village and slowly, over years of weddings and funerals and shared work, became one of us: she became fully a person, and it came in degrees.

What principle captures all this? If personhood is constituted by relations, (1) and (3) look like they should fail, and if it's not, (5) and (8) look mysterious. Give me your best candidate stated precisely (or two, if you think the choice is between one principle that saves every verdict at the price of extra clauses and a simpler one that overturns a verdict or two), tell me which of my verdicts you'd sacrifice if you had to, and say what your account implies for the case I keep going back and forth on: the care home near me has a little robot companion the residents chat with every day, some of them confide in it more than in their own children -- is anything person-like accruing to it, or is that category confusion?
