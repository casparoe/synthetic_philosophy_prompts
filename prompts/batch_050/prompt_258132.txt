Homework question:

Let \(C\) be the unit circle. An unoriented chord can be represented by \((\theta,r)\), where \(\theta\in[0,\pi)\) gives the direction of the radius perpendicular to the chord and \(r\in(-1,1)\) is the signed perpendicular distance from the center to the chord. Each pair \((\theta,r)\) determines exactly one chord.

A proposed application of the principle of indifference treats \(\theta\) and \(r\) as independent uniform random variables.

(a) Under this proposal, what is the probability that the chord has length greater than \(\sqrt{3}\)? [4 points]

(b) Let \(d=|r|\) and \(s=r^2\). Suppose instead that indifference is applied to \(d\), so \(d\) is uniform on \([0,1]\), or to \(s\), so \(s\) is uniform on \([0,1]\). In each case, compute the same probability. [6 points]

(c) Does rotational symmetry, together with the fact that \((\theta,r)\) uniquely represents the chords, single out the uniform distribution over \(r\)? Explain. [6 points]

(d) Now generate a chord by independently selecting two uniformly random points on the circumference and joining them. Compute the probability that its length exceeds \(\sqrt{3}\), and say whether rotational invariance chooses between this method and the method in (a). [4 points]

Student's answer:

(a) A chord whose perpendicular distance from the center is \(r\) has length \(2\sqrt{1-r^2}\). Therefore
\[
2\sqrt{1-r^2}>\sqrt3
\]
exactly when \(r^2<1/4\), or \(|r|<1/2\). Since \(r\) is uniform on an interval of length 2 and the favorable interval has length 1, the probability is \(1/2\).

(b) If \(d=|r|\) is uniform on \([0,1]\), the condition is \(d<1/2\), so the probability is \(1/2\). If \(s=r^2\) is uniform on \([0,1]\), the condition is \(s<1/4\), so the probability is \(1/4\).

(c) Rotational symmetry by itself does not mathematically entail a radial distribution. However, once a direction is fixed, every chord corresponds to exactly one value of \(r\), and no value of \(r\) is geometrically privileged over any other. Indifference therefore gives a uniform distribution over \(r\). By contrast, \(d=|r|\) identifies pairs of geometrically distinct signed positions, while \(s=r^2\) is only a nonlinear redescription of the same basic coordinate. So these are not genuinely rival symmetries; they are just noncanonical ways of describing the uniform-\(r\) method.

(d) Let \(\Delta\) be the smaller angle between the two independently chosen endpoints. Then \(\Delta\) is uniform on \([0,\pi]\). The chord length exceeds \(\sqrt3\) exactly when \(\Delta>2\pi/3\), so the probability is \(1/3\). This conflicts with the answer in (a), which means that the endpoint method must fail to be rotationally invariant: otherwise rotational symmetry would make all legitimate methods agree. Thus rotational invariance does choose the uniform-\(r\) method over the endpoint method.
