I'm a journalist (science-and-ideas beat) working on a piece about the white horse dialogue — Gongsun Long, the School of Names, the whole "a white horse is not a horse" thing from ancient China — and I want to say something smarter than "wow, ancient people argued about weird stuff." My theory is the dialogue is really an early stab at a question linguists still fight about: when you stick a modifier on a noun, when does the result still count as a member of the noun's category, and when does it get kicked out?

My officemate is a philosopher of language and I've been casually polling her on English cases to reverse-engineer her rule. Verdicts so far: a fake Rolex is not a Rolex. A toy gun is not a gun. A former president is not a president. An alleged thief is not a thief. But a red barn is a barn, and a small elephant is an elephant — and she was emphatic that the elephant doesn't have to be small, period, just small for an elephant. A stone lion is not a lion. Imitation crab, she says, is delicious but it is not crab.

Next time she gets coffee I want to spring a new one on her: an artificial heart — the kind surgeons actually implant, that pumps blood and keeps a person alive for years. Heart or not?

Predict her answer, but honestly I care less about the prediction than the reasoning. As far as I can tell at least two different rules fit her whole track record — one where it's about the specific modifier word and whether it belongs to a privative class (fake, former, alleged, stone, imitation...) versus a harmless class (red, small), and one where it's about whether the object actually does the job that makes the head noun what it is (guns fire, hearts pump, presidents hold office, crabs are... crab). Maybe there's a third I'm missing, including whatever Gongsun Long's own rule was supposed to be. Tell me which rules you think genuinely fit her verdicts, what each one predicts about the artificial heart, which of her earlier cases is the closest analog to it, and your best guess at her actual verdict. And give me the one further case I could poll her on next that would best tell the surviving rules apart — ideally something sneakier than just running "fake" and "red" variants again.
