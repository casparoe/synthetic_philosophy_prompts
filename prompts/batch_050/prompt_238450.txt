I run a monthly workshop for about twelve participants -- graduate students and junior faculty in political theory and law -- and sessions are ninety minutes. I want an activity that lets them feel the force of precommitment: why a person, or a polity, might rationally choose in a cool moment to tie its own hands against a predictable future self, and what it costs when the earlier self's command actually arrives. Straight discussion leaves the idea flat, in my experience -- everyone agrees that binding oneself is sometimes wise, and no one experiences the pull to wriggle free.

Design a session with two phases. In the first, small groups draft genuinely binding rules for some collective decision they know will become tempting to defect from later in the session (how a modest but real prize is to be allocated, or how the remainder of the workshop itself is to be run), including whatever amendment procedure they choose to allow themselves. In the second phase, participants actually occupy the position of the tempted later self -- incentives changed, patience gone, clever readings available -- and must decide whether to obey, reinterpret, or formally amend what they wrote. The rules, roles, and stakes should be arranged so that the instructive failures happen on their own before anyone names them: creative reinterpretation of one's own text, the discovery that the amendment rule was under-specified, the near-irresistible sense that one's present judgment must be wiser than one's past judgment, and the difference between binding oneself and binding others who never consented. Include concrete mechanics, materials, a stake small enough to be ethical but real enough to sting, and a minute-by-minute timeline. The debrief should map specific moments of the session onto Ulysses contracts, constitutional entrenchment and amendment rules, and the dead-hand objection, and should end with an honest note on which features of genuine precommitment -- real time lag, actually changed desires, the impossibility of simply leaving the room -- the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in discussion.

Present the design as exactly five numbered items -- setup and stakes, drafting rules, the temptation phase, the debrief, and predictable failures with remedies -- each two or three sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
