I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and a couple of times a year I get drafted into running sessions at a weekend ideas festival — big room, flip chart, smart curious adults with zero background in whatever the topic is. This year I want to do something on moral cultures, basically the framework Campbell and Manning built: honor cultures, where your standing depends on reputation so insults have to be answered directly, sometimes violently, and handling things yourself is a duty; dignity cultures, where your worth is supposed to be intrinsic so the correct response to a slight is to shrug it off, and only genuinely serious stuff goes to the law; and victimhood cultures, where offenses big and small get taken to third parties and mobilizing public sympathy around one's victim status is how conflicts get fought. My hook is that I recently wrote a piece on online pile-ons, and an editor friend insisted the internet is just an honor culture running on likes, which I think is half-right and a great way into the question the framework always raises at dinner parties: is any of these three actually morally superior, or do they just distribute their costs differently?

So design me the session. About 18 participants, 90 minutes, movable chairs, no AV. What I do not want is a lecture with discussion tacked on. I want an activity or game where the three cultures emerge from the rules themselves — participants should experience how the institutional setup (is there an authority in the room? can it be trusted to be fair? does complaining cost you status or pay you status?) pulls otherwise nice people toward retaliating, shrugging, or publicly appealing. My vague skeleton is three rounds of the same underlying social game with the institutional rules changed between rounds, but I don't know what the game itself should be, how to generate believable offenses without anyone actually getting hurt or embarassed, or whether the skeleton is even the right one. Participants should not hear the terms honor, dignity, or victimhood culture until the debrief — I want them to notice afterwards what they became. The debrief should then land on the superiority question: make them argue for which culture they'd choose to live under, given what they just did, and make them pay for their choice by naming what it sacrifices.

Format the answer in exactly these sections, in this order: "The cover story" (what participants are told the session is about, ~100 words), "The activity" (the game itself: rules, roles, rounds, timings, ~500 words, the bulk of it), "The debrief" (how to map specific moments from the game onto the framework and how to run the superiority argument, ~300 words), "What will predictably go wrong" (failure modes when this is run with real adults and how to handle each, ~250 words), and "What the game can't teach" (the one feature of real moral cultures a 90-minute game cannot reproduce and that I'd have to supply in words, ~150 words).
