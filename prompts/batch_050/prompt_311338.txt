Like many longtime readers of rationalist blogs, I have seen the DNA–Gödel analogy deployed in at least three incompatible ways. One group treats DNA as a genuinely Gödelian object: a symbolic description that, through a coding scheme, helps produce the machinery that interprets and copies it. A second group says this is an abuse of terminology: Gödelian self-reference concerns truth, provability, and arithmetical interpretation, whereas DNA is just chemistry and has no semantic content until a human models it as a code. A third group says both sides miss the point: the important common structure is not logic but the genotype–phenotype loop, in which information specifies a constructor and the constructor preserves or modifies that information.

The argument seems permanently stalled. To make the underlying issue more precise, consider the following minimal typogenetic system. A strand is a finite string over four symbols. One region functions as a passive template; another is decoded by fixed rules into operations such as COPY, CUT, MOVE, and INSERT, which act on strands in the environment. A configuration is a causal quine when the machinery produced by decoding a strand is itself causally responsible for copying the strand that produced it. The copying need not be perfect, but the interpretation and replication loop must remain stable under some range of mutations.

Is this causal-quine condition enough to make the DNA–Gödel comparison substantive, or does it merely identify a much weaker family containing photocopiers, computer quines, crystals, and template-directed chemistry? Conversely, is something essential missing unless the strand can be interpreted as saying something about its own provability, consistency, or replication? Please diagnose what kind of disagreement the three camps are having: verbal, semantic, formal, empirical, or a dispute over which structural similarities deserve to count as explanations. Say which features would have to be preserved for the claimed isomorphism to earn that name—coding, syntax, semantics, self-reference, causal closure, error tolerance, open-ended evolution—and whether these features admit degrees.

I do not want a survey of famous formulations or a verdict on which side is rhetorically winning. I want an account of why the debate keeps regenerating, what hidden question each side is answering, and what would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a formal theorem about causal quines, a counterexample, a biological discovery, or an admission that the original analogy trades on an equivocation.
