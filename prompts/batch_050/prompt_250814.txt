I have been developing the following argument — I call it the conservation-of-competence argument — and it disturbs me because its conclusion seems to refute not merely one alignment proposal but a great deal of ordinary engineering practice. Here it is.

(1) Iterated amplification answers a hard question Q by having an overseer decompose Q into subquestions, obtaining answers to those from copies of a base model, and aggregating the results; distillation then trains a single model to imitate the amplified system's outputs, and the process iterates.

(2) Such a decomposition is trustworthy only if the overseer can verify two things: that the subquestions, if answered correctly, jointly suffice to answer Q, and that answering them is not subtly easier than answering Q in a way that invites the base model to optimize for looking helpful rather than for being right.

(3) Verifying that a set of subquestions jointly suffices for Q is at least as cognitively demanding as answering Q directly: to check that the parts cover the whole, one must understand the structure of the whole, and anyone who understands it that well could have answered Q without assistance.

(4) Therefore amplification never yields trustworthy performance on any question the overseer could not already answer alone; its apparent gains occur either on questions the overseer could have checked anyway or are unverified and hence untrustworthy.

(5) Distillation cannot add trustworthiness, since compressing a system into a single model preserves at most the information already present in it and typically loses some.

(6) By induction, no number of iterations produces an aligned system more capable than its overseer: the competence and alignment of the whole pipeline are bounded by the competence and alignment of the human at its base. Scalable oversight by decomposition is impossible.

(7) But this proves too much. We routinely build aggregates more reliable than any of their components — error-correcting codes from noisy bits, crash-tolerant systems from failure-prone servers, and, imperfectly, reliable science from unreliable individual researchers. If (2) through (4) were correct, none of that would work either.
