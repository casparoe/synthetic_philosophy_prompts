I am a novelist at work on a character whose defining trait is that she cannot let anything go: she refuses to sell the house she can no longer afford, keeps a jacket belonging to someone long dead, and will not trade a modest certain future for a risky better one. I want to write her sympathetically, but I need to understand what I am actually depicting. Please explain the conceptual issue at the heart of the debate over whether loss aversion and the endowment effect are biases or legitimate preferences, using my character as the running test case. In particular, I want help distinguishing two readings of her: on the first, her attachments merely reflect a quirk of framing, such that she overvalues things simply because they are hers and would never choose to acquire them at the prices she demands to give them up; on the second, her possessions and prospects are partly constitutive of who she is, so that losing them is a genuinely different harm from failing to gain their equivalents, and her asymmetry tracks something real rather than an error. Please address where the bias reading is strongest, where it breaks down or becomes question-begging, and what would have to be true of a person for the legitimacy reading to hold of her rather than excuse her. Give me your answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, in American English.
