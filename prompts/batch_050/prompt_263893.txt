Last weekend a close friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about the "man of his time" defense — whether it is ever legitimate to excuse a historical figure's wrongdoing on the grounds that they could not have been expected to see what we now see. She thinks the defense is always a dodge; I think it is sometimes valid but usually abused. We ended the evening unresolved, and in thinking it over since, I have noticed that my own position may be less coherent than I assumed. Here is roughly where I have landed, and I would like to know whether it hangs together:

1. No one is blameworthy for failing to act on a moral truth that was, given their time and place, genuinely inaccessible to them. Blame requires that the person could have seen the wrongness of what they were doing.
2. Jefferson is nonetheless blameworthy for owning slaves, because the antislavery case was accessible to him: it was live in his intellectual milieu, he engaged with it directly, and his own writings concede much of it even while he kept enslaving people.
3. Churchill deserves partial mitigation for his government's decisions during the Bengal famine, because wartime scarcity and competing demands genuinely constrained his options. Culpable choices made under real constraint are less blameworthy than the same choices made freely.
4. Our descendants will be right to condemn us for industrial animal agriculture, even though most of us sincerely regard it as unremarkable and feel no wrong in participating in it.
5. Praise and blame should track what the agent could have known and done, not the moral quality of how things happened to turn out.
6. Figures who saw the wrong clearly before their peers did — early abolitionists, say — deserve special admiration for precisely that achievement, over and above whatever credit they get for acting rightly.

Is this a consistent package? In particular: my friend claims that (1) and (4) cannot both stand, and that my treatment of Jefferson in (2) sits uneasily with my treatment of Churchill in (3), but she could not make either charge stick in the moment, and I am not sure she was even right about which commitments are the problem. I would like an audit rather than a lecture: identify the genuine tensions in this set, ordered by severity, and for each one say which commitment would have to give and what surrendering it would cost me. Where two commitments only appear to conflict, say so explicitly and explain why the tension dissolves on inspection, since I suspect at least one of her objections was of that kind. Finally, tell me the cheapest repair to the whole position — the single revision that restores consistency at the lowest cost — and what I would be conceding to my friend by adopting it. Please write your answer in American English.
