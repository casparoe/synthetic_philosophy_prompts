I'm giving a brown-bag talk at my university's school of public policy next month, and I want a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis is a deliberately watered-down version of the "singleton hypothesis" -- the idea that humanity is heading toward having a single decision-making agency at the highest level of world affairs. I'm NOT defending the sci-fi version (one AI takes over the planet, or a world government with an army). My claim is weaker and, I think, sneakier: the technologies that most plausibly pose civilizational-scale risks (engineered pandemics, autonomous weapons, maybe transformative AI) have the property that unilateral regulation doesn't work -- if one country restricts them and another doesn't, the risk barely goes down. So over the next few decades, states will face mounting pressure to build verification and enforcement mechanisms with real teeth, and whichever mechanism sticks will tend to expand its jurisdiction, because the logic that justified it for one domain applies to the next. End result: not a world government, but a single locus of veto power over a growing set of activities. A "singleton of last resort," I call it. I argue this is more likely than not by 2100, and that it's driven by the structure of the risks, not by anyone's ideology.

The audience: mostly international relations scholars (a couple of realists, several liberal institutionalists), a few economists who work on global public goods, and a political theorist who I know has strong views about technocracy and legitimacy. Nobody there works on existential risk or AI. They will have heard the phrase "singleton" at most in passing, and I suspect several of them will hear my talk as "tech person discovers international coordination and gets overexcited."

What I want from you: the objections I'll actually get from THIS audience, not the ones I'd get at an effective altruism conference or from a room of political philosophers. Order them by how much damage they'd do to my argument as stated. Phrase each one the way the person raising it would actually phrase it -- the realist's version sounds different from the institutionalist's version of the same worry, and I'd like both. For each: a two-minute reply, what I should concede upfront (I suspect I need to give ground on "jurisdiction creep" being historically overstated -- the League of Nations and the IAEA did not, in fact, snowball), and where my reply is weakest so I don't overcommit. Also flag any point where my argument quietly assumes that "this risk requires coordination" implies "coordination will happen," because I know that's a gap but I haven't figured out how much it matters.

Two more things. First, tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- the one where I'd have to either bullshit or admit the talk overreaches. Second, if the premise of my ask is confused -- e.g., if the "weak singleton" as I've defined it is either so weak it's trivially true (every veto player is a "singleton of last resort" over something) or still secretly needs the strong version's assumptions -- say so directly rather than playing along. I'd rather fix the thesis now than get told this by the political theorist in front of forty people.
