So I write novels, and the one I'm working on now has a narrator who's dying, which sent me down a philosophy rabbit hole about whether death is even bad for the person who dies. Epicurus (and later Lucretius with the symmetry thing -- you weren't bothered by not existing before you were born, so why be bothered by not existing after?) says it isn't, since once you're dead there's nobody around to be harmed. The deprivationists say of course it's bad, it robs you of the good years you'd have had. Nagel has that famous 1970 paper, and then there's fifty years of back-and-forth about when the harm happens, whether a harm needs a subject, comparisons between how things went and how they could have gone, etc.

Here's what strikes me though: everybody seems to know everybody else's moves cold, and nobody budges. The debate feels stuck in a way that even other old philosophy debates don't. My hypothesis, which I'm not married to, is that this isn't really one disagreement. I suspect it's partly a straight-up clash of starting intuitions (some people just find it obvious that being dead is a misfortune, others find it obvious that it can't touch you), and partly a disguised fight about what 'bad for' even means -- like whether welfare comparisons need an actual experiencer -- which is a bigger question neither side can settle inside the death debate.

Tell me if that diagnosis holds up, or if there's a better one. Is there a hidden formal or conceptual question doing the work here, or are the sides just answering different questions under one heading? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would progress even look like -- a sharper distinction, a new argument form, or should we just expect this one to stay stalled forever, and if so, why?
