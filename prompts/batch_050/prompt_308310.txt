I'm a journalist working on a long-form piece about how rational people should revise their beliefs when the evidence itself is equivocal — not a clean observation, but a glimpse, a half-heard remark, a reading taken in bad light. Jeffrey conditionalization is the obvious centrepiece, but my editor has complained that every illustration of it she has ever seen is the same: the cloth viewed by candlelight, the jelly beans, the colour of the car at dusk. She wants a fresh scenario for the sidebar, and she's given me a rather demanding brief.

Construct a thought experiment meeting the following specifications. First, it must be set in an ordinary professional context a general reader will recognise — a hospital ward, a trading floor, a newsroom, an air-traffic control tower — with no staged epistemology-lab apparatus. Second, the agent's uncertainty must concern which proposition their observation supports, so that a full Bayesian would Jeffrey-conditionalise rather than conditionalise outright. Third, and this is the crux: the scenario must contain two different partitions of the possibility space such that Jeffrey conditionalisation on one partition and Jeffrey conditionalisation on the other deliver materially different posterior credences for the same hypothesis, even though both partitions seem like legitimate descriptions of what the agent learned. In other words, I want a case that exhibits the partition-sensitivity of Jeffrey's rule rather than merely illustrating the rule working smoothly. Fourth, keep the arithmetic to a level I can reproduce in the article: priors, likelihoods, and posterior credences a numerate reader could check with a pencil.

After presenting the scenario, do the following. Walk through both Jeffrey updates step by step, showing the partition chosen, the new credences assigned to its cells on the basis of the observation, and the resulting posterior for the target hypothesis. Then identify the structural feature of the case that makes the two partitions available, and explain whether there is any principled way — rather than mere stipulation — to declare one partition the "correct" representation of the evidence, or whether the case instead suggests that the agent's evidence was never partition-neutral in the first place. Finally, anticipate the response of someone who thinks the divergence is an artefact of sloppy scenario-building rather than a real limitation of the rule, and say whether their objection succeeds. I'd like the whole thing to be rigorous enough that a philosopher reading the piece wouldn't wince, but told with enough narrative texture that my editor can picture it.
