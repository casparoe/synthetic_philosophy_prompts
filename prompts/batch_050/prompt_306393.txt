I'm putting together the final for my upper-level philosophy of language seminar, which this year is basically a semester on generics. I need one essay question, answerable in about 75 minutes, that I can give to students who have already read the standard intro material -- so anything they could answer by saying "generics tolerate exceptions, unlike universals" is dead on arrival. I've already banned them from simply re-running the ducks-lay-eggs and mosquitoes-carry-malaria cases.

Here's the angle I want. Midway through term I gave them a little battery of made-up cases to argue about, and I'd like the exam to build on those. The cases were:

(a) "Snurds molt in spring" -- snurds are an invented lizard; in the story, roughly 3% of snurds ever molt, but the ones that do, do it because of an internal hormonal program that's triggered by day length, and it's the same program in all of them. Most students wanted to call the generic true.

(b) "Vilbs have spotted tails" -- invented bird; in the story, 92% of vilbs have spotted tails, but purely by accident of a recent disease that killed off the unspotted ones; nothing about being a vilb disposes you toward spots. Most students got squirrely about calling this true despite the huge prevalence.

(c) "Teachers assign too much homework" -- in the story, about 40% of teachers do, and students were split in a way that tracked nothing I could see.

The question should make them say what these three cases together show about prevalence-based accounts of generics (i.e. that they can't be the whole story in either direction), and then push them on the harder part: what the extra ingredient might be -- whether it's something about the kind having a nature/mechanism that explains the property, something normative, something about what the speaker is doing conversationally -- and what work "the kind" is doing in that answer. I'd also like the question to force them to say whether the truth of a generic tells you anything at all about the exceptions (e.g. if "mosquitoes carry malaria" is true, what follows about a particular non-carrying mosquito -- is it abnormal? defective? just unremarkable?), because I think that's where the interesting pressure between the semantics and the metaphysics shows up, and nobody in class had a stable view on it.

Write the question itself (it should present the three cases in the question stem so the exam is self-contained), then a model answer of about 600 words, then a marking scheme: five must-hit points with marks adding up to 25, plus a one-line note for each point on the characteristic way a weaker answer fumbles it. Please also flag somewhere what you think the hardest part of the question is, so I can decide whether to give them a hint in the exam room.
