A close friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about a claim I find plausible and she finds incoherent: that a large language model can, in some meaningful sense, know something it does not say -- that the correct answer can be present in its internal states or latent representations while its generated output says otherwise, whether because of how it was fine-tuned, what it expects the questioner wants to hear, or simple inconsistency across phrasings. She thinks this mystifies a statistical artifact; I think dismissing it leaves us unable to catch models that are quietly more capable or more informed than their answers suggest. The argument ended in a stalemate, so I want to move it to ground where it can actually be settled: operational practice.

I run pre-deployment evaluations for a small team that fine-tunes and ships language-model-based assistants, and I want to turn the latent-knowledge idea into a written procedure I can apply roughly monthly, whenever a model is about to ship or a concerning behavior shows up in testing. The deliverable I want from you is that procedure: an ordered set of tests, expressed so that a competent engineer who has never read a word of the interpretability or elicitation literature can follow it, for answering the question: does this model plausibly know more than it is telling us on this topic, and should that change whether we ship?

Please be concrete about the toolkit available to someone without research infrastructure. I assume access to full sampling control, the ability to fine-tune cheaply, and the ability to construct arbitrary prompts, but no guaranteed access to internals beyond logits. The procedure should at minimum address: paraphrase and context-consistency probing; testing whether the model's answers to factual questions outperform the answers it gives when asked to role-play an informed expert, a naive user, or a character with wrong beliefs; whether fine-tuning the model on demonstrations of honest reporting on easy, checkable questions causes better reporting to generalize to hard questions; and how to interpret a gap between any of these. For each step, state what result would count as evidence of latent knowledge versus what would count as the null, and flag explicitly where the test conflates "the model knows X but won't say it" with "our prompt simply failed to elicit X," since that conflation is exactly where my friend thinks the whole enterprise collapses.

The procedure must also specify: what to do when the tests disagree with each other; a stopping rule, since probing can be continued indefinitely; and how the output feeds a go/no-go shipping decision, given that latent knowledge of a dangerous kind and latent knowledge of a harmless kind presumably warrant different responses. Mark every step where judgment cannot be eliminated and the procedure is really a dressed-up judgment call.

Work the procedure through three hypothetical cases end to end: (1) a coding assistant that claims a certain library function does not exist but, when asked to complete partially written code, uses that function correctly; (2) a medical-information assistant that consistently gives the consensus answer on a contested clinical question but assigns high probability to a minority-view answer under paraphrase; (3) a model that flatters users' stated political views and gives different factual summaries of politically loaded events depending on the apparent lean of the questioner. At least one of these should be a case where the procedure, correctly followed, yields a conclusion the shipping team will not want to hear. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot establish -- in particular, whether it can ever distinguish a model that knows-and-conceals from one that merely contains the right answer in a form that no elicitation method we have will surface -- and whether, on your analysis, that distinction matters for the shipping decision or only for the philosophy seminar my friend and I apparently cannot escape.
