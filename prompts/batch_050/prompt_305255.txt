Below is a passage from Karl Marx's notebooks of 1857–58, posthumously published as the Grundrisse, in the section sometimes called the Fragment on Machines. Marx is discussing large-scale industry and the growing role of machinery and applied science in production:

"to the degree that large industry develops, the creation of real wealth comes to depend less on labour time and on the amount of labour employed than on the power of the agencies set in motion during labour time, whose 'powerful effectiveness' is itself in turn out of all proportion to the direct labour time spent on their production, but depends rather on the general state of science and on the progress of technology, or the application of this science to production."

I am struggling with this passage because it seems to collide head-on with the doctrine Marx defends in Capital, written only a few years later, that socially necessary labour time is the measure of value. Please help me understand it. Specifically: (1) What are the "agencies set in motion during labour time," and what work is the phrase "real wealth" doing — is Marx drawing a distinction between wealth and value, and if so, does that distinction dissolve the apparent contradiction with Capital or merely relocate it? (2) Is Marx describing an empirical trend within capitalism, or a contradiction that capitalism cannot survive — i.e., is he saying that production resting on exchange value must collapse as automation advances, and is that prediction internally coherent given his own account of how relative surplus value works? (3) Two broad readings of this passage circulate: one treats it as Marx anticipating that automation undermines the labour theory of value itself; the other treats it as fully consistent with Capital, since value and material wealth always came apart for Marx. Lay out the strongest version of each reading and say which the text better supports, and why.
