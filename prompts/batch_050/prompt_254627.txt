I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology and I've made the mistake of volunteering to write up where my department's year-long argument has actually got to. The two camps: the new mechanists (the Machamer, Darden and Craver crowd), who reckon that explaining a biological phenomenon means describing the organised entities and activities that produce it, and that any model which doesn't bottom out in identifiable parts and operations is at best a how-possibly sketch; and the dynamicists, who think plenty of successful explanations in neuroscience and systems biology work precisely by abstracting away from parts — their go-to cases are things like treating circadian rhythms as limit cycles or giving a two-equation account of bacterial chemotaxis, where (they say) the mathematical structure does the explaining and the molecular details are mere implementation.

What's made the fight worse lately is that the systems biology group here trained a deep network that predicts cell migration better than any model either camp has produced, but nobody can say what the network has latched onto. Both camps claim it vindicates thier side, which suggests to me at least one of them is confused.

So: write the joint statement this adversarial collaboration should produce. I want the claims both sides can genuinely sign — about what a phenomenon is, about the role of intervention data in identifying components, about what the network result does and doesn't show. Then the exact disagreements, each phrased in wording the side itself would accept, not a strawman. Then the cruxes — I suspect one is whether prediction without decomposition can ever be explanatory, and another is whether the how-possibly/how-actually distinction does any real work — and for each crux, a case, experiment or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, with what each side predicts about it written down in advance. If you think part of this dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why.

One formatting thing: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just continuous prose, the way you'd write an email to the department. And British English please, none of the American spellings.
