I help run the grants panel for a small community foundation -- we dish out maybe £250k a year to local projects, four meetings a year, six of us on the panel, none of us trained in anything relevant. We keep hitting the same wall: some bids are about stopping awful things (the night shelter, a suicide prevention charity, a debt advice service) and others are about adding nice things (the summer festival, new kit for the youth football league), and we have no principled way to compare them, so it ends up being whoever argues loudest.

I read about an idea called lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, which as I understand it goes roughly: relieving suffering always takes priority over creating pleasure; there's a threshold of suffering bad enough that no amount of pleasure, however widely spread, can compensate for leaving it in place; but below that threshold, pleasures and pains can be traded off against each other more or less normally. That matches how our panel actually feels, but a feeling isn't a procedure.

Can you turn it into one? I want a set of questions we work through in order for each bid, what counts as a good enough answer to each, and how we're supposed to operationalise the threshold when nobody can measure suffering on a scale -- some proxy tests or comparison anchors a layperson can actually apply in a two-hour meeting. Be explicit about where the procedure has to stop and hand over to judgement, because I suspect that's a lot of places. Then run it on three of our real shortlist cases: the night shelter expansion, the summer festival (very popular, would be gutting to cut), and a mindfulness programme for local schools. At least one of them should be a case where the procedure gives an answer we won't enjoy, and say what we should do with that discomfort rather than just binning the procedure when it bites.
