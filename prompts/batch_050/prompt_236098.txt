I’ve recently joined the appeals panel for the small housing co-op where I live, and I’ve got my first genuinely awkward case. Please act as a philosopher of law who understands strict liability, negligence, mens rea and vicarious responsibility, but who can also think practically about how a little tribunal stays consistent. Think carefully and privately brainstorm several rules that could fit our track record before settling on an answer; don’t show me the raw brainstorming, just the refined analysis.

Our panel’s previous decisions are:

1. A resident left their own e-bike battery charging overnight in a shared corridor after two written warnings about fire risk. We upheld the fine.

2. A resident’s visiting brother propped open a fire door while moving furniture. The resident was asleep, did not know he had done it, and had generally told visitors not to wedge doors open. We upheld the fine, saying fire doors were too important for an honest-mistake defence.

3. A resident hired a reputable plumber who left a toolbox partly blocking the corridor for about half an hour. The resident had given the plumber written instructions to keep exits clear, but had not checked his work. We cancelled that fine.

4. A resident’s teenage son repeatedly left his bicycle in the courtyard despite his mother telling him not to. It was untidy and contrary to the rules, but nobody claimed it created a serious danger. We cancelled the fine.

5. A resident’s cleaner forgot to lock the bike shed after cleaning the flat. The resident had told her once, verbally, to lock it. Two bicycles were stolen, and we upheld the fine.

The new appeal is mine to help decide, unfortunately. Another resident hired a dog-sitter they had used several times before. They gave the sitter written instructions to close the gate to the bin store, but the sitter forgot. A fox got in overnight and spread rubbish across the courtyard. It took about twenty minutes to clear up, nobody was hurt, and nothing was stolen. The committee fined the resident £60 because the sitter was acting on their behalf. The resident says they took every reasonable precaution and did nothing blameworthy themselves.

I’m not asking whether the committee has the legal authority to impose fines — assume that it does. I also don’t want real legal citations or a bibliography. What I want is a prediction and recommendation based on our own precedents, which seemes to pull in different directions.

Please answer in British English, using exactly these sections in this order:

**Candidate rules** — around 250 words. Identify at least three plausible principles that could explain the earlier decisions, including at least one strict-liability principle and one fault-based principle. Say how each draws the relevant line.

**Predictions on the new case** — around 250 words. For each candidate rule, say whether the £60 fine should stand and why. Please distinguish responsibility for another person’s conduct from responsibility merely because harm occurred.

**Most likely verdict** — around 150 words. Give your best prediction of what our panel would actually do if it tried to remain consistent with its track record, and identify the closest earlier precedent.

**My recommendation** — around 150 words. Say what you think we should decide, even if that differs from the most likely prediction. Be honest about which principle we would implicitly be adopting and what earlier decision, if any, we would be narrowing.

**A case that would settle it** — around 100 words. Invent one further hypothetical appeal that would best distinguish the two strongest rules, and explain what each would say about it.
