I'm taking a grad seminar on historical method and we just spent two weeks on the distinction between revisionism and denialism -- the idea that revising received views of the past is what historians legitimately do all the time, while denial is something else: it starts from the conclusion and works backward, picks at the evidence selectively, applies standards of proof to the event it hates that it would never apply elsewhere, and treats any concession as proof the whole thing was fabricated. The seminar kept circling around whether this distinction is actually workable or just a way of saying "revisions I like vs. revisions I don't." So I want to test it against a real case.

Take the Irving libel trial. Roughly: David Irving, a prolific writer on WWII who had real archival discoveries to his name earlier in his career, sued Deborah Lipstadt and her publisher in England around 1996 after her book on Holocaust denial called him a denier and a distorter of evidence. Because English libel law put the burden on the defendants, Lipstadt's team essentially had to prove the truth of what she'd said about him, and they did it partly by having historians -- Richard Evans most prominently -- go through Irving's books line by line against the documents. The judge found in 2000 for Lipstadt, concluding that Irving had repeatedly and deliberately misrepresented the record. Analyze this episode through the revisionism/denialism distinction: does Irving's actual practice, as the trial exposed it, fit the criteria for denial rather than revision, or does it muddy them? Consider the awkward bits: Evans' method basically amounted to one school of historians adjudicating another's footnotes, which is what denialists always claim "the establishment" does; Irving framed himself precisely as a persecuted revisionist being silenced; and the verdict arguably turned on intent to deceive, which is hard to prove from the page. Does the trial vindicate the distinction, show it only works as a court-adjudicable standard about deliberate falsification rather than a general test, or show that in practice the line gets drawn by whoever has the resources to audit the archives?

I'd also like the analysis to run in the other direction: what does the episode suggest the distinction needs that it currently lacks -- and does whatever you conclude here travel to state-sponsored denial like Turkey's on the Armenian genocide, where the "persecuted revisionist" framing is backed by an actual government? Please flag how confident you are in your main claims, and single out the single point in your analysis where you feel least sure of yourself.
