I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm doing a piece on academic arguments that never die. My case study right now is the centipede game, and what I want from you is a diagnosis of the debate, not another textbook walkthrough -- I've read plenty of those.

Quick setup so we're on the same page. Two players, a pot that grows every round. On your turn you can grab the bigger share and end the game, or pass and let it keep growing, knowing that if the other player grabs next round you end up with less than if you'd just grabbed now. Backward induction says grab on move one, which is a pretty bleak outcome given how much money is sitting there late in the game. As far as I can tell the literature has been frozen on this since the early nineties. One camp, with a famous formal result behind them (Aumann's), says taking immediately follows from common knowledge of rationality, full stop. The other camp (Binmore and friends) says the reasoning eats itself: the induction only ever gets tested at nodes that rational play supposedly never reaches, so the moment anyone actually passes, the premise the whole argument runs on -- common knowledge of rationality -- has already been refuted, and passing can be the perfectly sane move. Nobody's disputing the math. The lab results aren't in dispute either; real people pass a lot, and each side just files that under "see, irrationality" or "see, rational play." Thirty years, zero inches of movement.

Here's my editor's working theory, which she wants me to check before I build the piece around it: the disagreement isn't about game theory at all, it's about what rationality demands at situations that rationality itself guarantees won't occur. Which makes it a close cousin of the logic thing where from a contradiction anything follows -- the camps are really disagreeing about whether "what should I do if the impossible happens" is even a well-posed question. Is she right? Or is there a genuinely substantive question buried in here -- about what common knowledge should commit you to, or about how you're suppose to revise beliefs when your model gets falsified mid-game -- where one side is just wrong and we could say so? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would progress actually look like: a better solution concept, an agreed semantics for reasoning from impossible suppositions, some experiment nobody's run, or is this one of those debates where the honest verdict is that it should be dissolved rather than settled?
