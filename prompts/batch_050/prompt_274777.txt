I'm a TA for an undergraduate course on practical rationality, and I'm grading homework responses to the following question. I'd like your help with one student's answer in particular.

The homework question was: "Explain Warren Quinn's puzzle of the self-torturer. In the case as Quinn describes it, a person has a device implanted with 1000 settings, from 0 (no pain) up to 1000 (excruciating agony). Each step up produces an increase in pain too small to be felt, and the person receives a substantial monetary reward for each step they take. Lay out precisely why this case is puzzling for theories of rational choice, explain what role the intransitivity of the person's preferences plays, and assess what the puzzle shows: does it merely illustrate that intransitive preferences are irrational, or does it cut deeper against the idea that rationality consists in choosing what one most prefers at each moment?"

Here is the student's answer:

"Quinn's self-torturer has a device with settings 0 to 1000. Each setting is imperceptibly more painful than the one before it, and each step up earns him $10,000. Because he cannot feel any difference between adjacent settings, he is indifferent between setting n and setting n+1 as far as pain goes, and the money then tips the balance, so he strictly prefers n+1 to n. Yet he strictly prefers setting 0 to setting 1000, since he would rather have no pain and no money than agony and ten million dollars. His preferences are therefore intransitive: by transitivity, the chain of pairwise preferences would require him to prefer 1000 to 0, but he doesn't.

The puzzle is that each individual choice seems rational — he always takes what he prefers — but the sequence of choices lands him at setting 1000, which he regards as far worse than where he started. The structure is essentially that of a money pump: someone with cyclical or intransitive preferences can be led around the cycle again and again, each time paying a small fee, until they are drained. The self-torturer is in the same position, being exploited step by step because of his intransitive preferences, except that here the cycle is a one-way ladder rather than a loop.

What does the puzzle show? Mainly, I think, that intransitive preferences are irrational and that anyone who holds them will predictably be made worse off. Quinn's point is that the self-torturer's mistake is located in his preferences: if he had simply formed transitive preferences, there would be no puzzle, since he would stop at the setting he genuinely judges best. So the lesson is that rationality requires transitive preferences, and once we see the intransitivity we have found the irrationality. The case also resembles the sorites paradox, since no single step makes a noticeable difference, but that resemblance is superficial and doesn't change the diagnosis."
