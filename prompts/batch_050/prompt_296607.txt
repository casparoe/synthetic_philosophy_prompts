I am a novelist at work on a book in which three of the principal characters are collectives: an ant colony, a port city, and a trading house that has outlived everyone who founded it. I take seriously the suggestion, associated with Hofstadter's Ant Fugue, that a colony might be minded in something like the way a brain is -- no ant means anything, yet the colony answers questions, nurses grievances, and can lie. My difficulty in drafting is that I slide between sentences where the collective plausibly does the thinking itself and sentences where "the city grieved" is merely shorthand for what its inhabitants felt, and my early readers cannot agree on which sentences are which. I would like a procedure I can run on any passage -- a working novelist, no philosophical training, a few minutes per page -- that sorts my sentences into literal attributions of mentality, defensible shorthand, and muddle, grounded in whatever philosophy of mind actually offers that survives the translation: levels of description, the question of whether the attitude depends on any particular member, what the intentional stance licenses. It should mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated rather than pretending to settle them, and it should include at least one test that can return an answer I will not like. I have been using a rough rule of my own -- if the sentence can be rewritten distributing the attitude over the members without loss, it was shorthand -- so please assess whether that rule holds up or misleads. Then run the finished procedure on three sentences from my draft: "The colony decided, at last, to forgive the gardener"; "The city remembered the flood the way a burn victim remembers the stove"; and "The firm no longer believed in the founder's vision, though no one inside it could have said so."
