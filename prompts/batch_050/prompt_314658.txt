I am a working novelist, and I would like the argument of the culture industry chapter of Adorno and Horkheimer's Dialectic of Enlightenment restated in the vocabulary I actually think in when I write. Their central moves, as I understand them, are roughly these: mass entertainment is standardized production; its apparent variety is pseudo-individuality, surface difference laid over an identical schema; style, which once marked an artwork's resistance to convention, has collapsed into formula; amusement functions as a prolongation of work rather than an escape from it; and the whole apparatus reconciles audiences to the world as it is by training them to want what they are given.

Translate each of these claims into my working terms, one by one: voice and point of view, character interiority, plot architecture and the obligations a genre contract imposes, the reader's identification with a protagonist, comp titles and editorial letters, the market's standing demand for the same-but-different, and the moment at the desk when I feel a sentence going dead into convention. Be as faithful as you can, and do not soften their claims merely to make them portable — if they would count my own prestige literary fiction as culture industry product, say so in my terms rather than letting me hide behind the genre/literary distinction.

Then I want the residue report. Which of their claims translate cleanly into craft-and-market talk? Which survive only in distorted form? And which have no expression at all in a vocabulary that tends to treat the reader's desires as given rather than manufactured, and that has no obvious place for their contrast between genuine art and administered product? Finally, give me your honest judgment about the residue: does it mark something a novelist's categories are structurally unable to see — a blind spot in how I think about my own work — or does it mark places where their critique leans on a distinction my framework is right to refuse?
