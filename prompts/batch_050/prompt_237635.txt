I serve on the adjudicative panel of a national professional body that licenses auditors, and I have been asked to help draft our decision in a case that has split the panel in a way I did not expect. A senior audit partner signed off on the accounts of a client that turned out to be materially fraudulent. The paper trail shows that eighteen months before the sign-off she drafted an internal memo flagging exactly the revenue-recognition practices that later proved fraudulent, and circulated it to her team with instructions to dig deeper. Over the following year, however, her subsequent memos grow steadily more sanguine: she reclassifies the suspect items under a plausible alternative accounting treatment, accepts increasingly thin management explanations, and eventually instructs juniors to stop raising the issue in meetings. When the fraud surfaced, colleagues testified that she appeared genuinely stunned, and she has sworn under oath -- credibly, most of us think -- that at the moment of sign-off she sincerely believed the accounts were sound. Our charter reserves the harshest sanction, revocation, for a "knowingly false attestation," with a lesser negligence tier otherwise. Everything turns on what to do with the interval between the early memo and the later conviction.

The panel has polarized around two framings, and I should be transparent that I drafted neither. One member circulated a note arguing on an organizational analogy: a firm can deceive a client even though no single employee holds the whole lie in mind at once, because the firm is partitioned and one part can hide things from another; on this view, the partner's earlier self effectively deceived her later self, and the intent present at the start should carry over to the attestation, making it knowing. Another member replies that a person is not a firm, that self-deception in the required sense may not even be a coherent possibility -- deceiving someone seems to require getting them to believe what you yourself disbelieve, and one mind cannot be on both sides of that transaction at once -- and that knowledge must be assessed at the moment of signing, full stop, which would cap the finding at negligence. I notice my own stake here: I recently caught myself "remembering" a missed deadline of mine in a way I recognized, with some discomfort, as a story I had quietly revised over several weeks. So I am reluctant to treat sincere later belief as automatic exculpation, but I also cannot quite see how to convict someone of knowingly attesting something they did not, at that moment, know to be false.

Please write a decision memo to the full panel recommending which tier the finding should fall under and why. I need an actionable bottom line, but also: a set of criteria we could apply to future cases of this general shape, where a professional's sincere present belief appears to have been manufactured by their own earlier motivated conduct; a direct engagement with whether genuine self-deception is possible and, if the answer is no or only in a deflated sense, whether that actually settles the culpability question or merely relocates it; and an honest, non-caricatured treatment of the strongest argument on whichever side loses. If you think the panel's two framings share a false presupposition, say so and say what the memo should do instead.
