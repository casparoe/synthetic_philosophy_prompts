A study guide I am using summarizes Machiavelli's account of fortune and virtù as follows: 'Fortuna, for Machiavelli, is sheer luck — the unforeseeable ups and downs of political life. Virtù is the antidote: the adaptable statesman's capacity to read changing circumstances and recalibrate his conduct to fit them. Since virtù essentially consists in flexibility, Machiavelli's advice to the prince reduces to one maxim: keep your style of rule in step with the times, and you will master fortune as thoroughly as the great founders did.' I then came across the following passage in Chapter 25 of The Prince (in W. K. Marriott's translation), which made me doubt the summary:

"But a man is not often found sufficiently circumspect to know how to accommodate himself to the change, both because he cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to, and also because, having always prospered by acting in one way, he cannot be persuaded that it is well to leave it; and, therefore, the cautious man, when it is time to turn adventurous, does not know how to do it, hence he is ruined; but had he changed his conduct with the times fortune would not have changed."

Which parts of the study guide's summary would a careful reader of Machiavelli accept, and which misrepresent him? In particular, does the passage show that identifying virtù with flexibility gets Machiavelli's view wrong — perhaps even backwards — or can the summary be reconciled with the passage? And does the guide's concluding maxim survive your analysis: if men are by nature unable to change their ways, is the advice to adapt to the times coherent advice at all, or is Machiavelli's real teaching here something more pessimistic about who, if anyone, can escape fortune?
