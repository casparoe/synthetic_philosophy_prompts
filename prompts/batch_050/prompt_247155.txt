I am a PhD student working on moral desert and retributive theory, and for a dissertation chapter I have been treating karma -- understood purely as the philosophical thesis that a morally ordered universe eventually apportions flourishing and suffering in proportion to desert -- as a candidate answer to the problem of moral luck. To stress-test my own intuitions, I have been polling myself on a series of cases, and my verdicts refuse to line up. I would like you to act as an expert in moral philosophy with particular sensitivity to the luck problem, and to do some reflective-equilibrium work on my behalf. Please think carefully, begin by brainstorming at least three distinct candidate principles that could organize my verdicts, and then evaluate each one before settling on a recommendation (or concluding that some of my verdicts must be abandoned).

Here are my cases and gut verdicts. (1) A conscientious driver whose brakes fail despite scrupulous maintenance kills a pedestrian: I insist she accrues no karmic debt -- outcome-tracking karma strikes me as barbaric. (2) A man poisons his rival's tea, but the cup is knocked over by a cat: I feel strongly he deserves exactly what a successful poisoner would deserve, since nothing about him differs. Yet (1) and (2) together seem to commit me to a pure intention-or-character-tracking principle, and (3) troubles that: a woman who donates a fortune to famine relief purely to be admired at galas produces enormous good, and my gut says her karmic balance should improve somewhat -- less than a sincere donor's, but more than zero -- even though a pure character-tracking view gives her nothing, since her disposition would just as readily have funded something harmful if that had bought more admiration. (4) A bitter, vindictive man who simply never encounters an opportunity to harm anyone: I recoil at charging him for sins he never committed, but I cannot articulate why he differs from the thwarted poisoner, and that asymmetry bothers me most of all. (5) The traditional idea that karmic debts transmit across generations within a family: I reject it outright as collective punishment. (6) Yet if I reject (5), I struggle to say what work karma is doing in explaining the suffering of a child born into misery, which was supposed to be the theory's main selling point over a purely secular desert theory.

My tentative principle is that karma should screen off moral luck entirely, charging only what was fully within the agent's control, with a discount factor for motives that are contingently rather than constitutively connected to the good outcome. Please test this principle against all six cases, identify precisely which verdicts it saves and which it condemns, and repair it with as few additional clauses as possible -- for each clause, name the case that forces it. Then tell me what the repaired principle implies about two cases I have not settled: a person who sincerely but mistakenly believes that a harmful ritual benefits her community, and a person whose vicious character was itself the product of an abusive upbringing she did not choose. Finally, be honest about whether the resulting principle is still recognizable as a theory of karma at all, or whether it collapses into ordinary secular desert theory with cosmic bookkeeping attached -- if the latter, say so plainly rather than rescuing the label.
