For more than two decades, cosmologists and philosophers of physics have argued about Boltzmann brains, and as far as I can tell the debate has produced almost no convergence. The standard worry runs as follows. In any cosmological model with a sufficiently large or long-lived vacuum, observers who fluctuate randomly out of thermal equilibrium — complete with false memories of an ordered past — should vastly outnumber observers produced by ordinary thermodynamic evolution. So, conditional on such a model, I should expect to be a Boltzmann brain. But a Boltzmann brain's memories, perceptions and beliefs bear no reliable connection to reality. So accepting the model appears to undermine the very observations taken to support it. One camp treats this as a decisive reductio: a cosmology dominated by Boltzmann brains is cognitively unstable and is thereby ruled out on methodological grounds, whatever its other virtues. A second camp denies that the argument gets off the ground at all, holding that 'self-undermining' is not a coherent defect in a theory, since the probability that I am a Boltzmann brain depends on a choice of measure over observers and on contested principles for reasoning about one's own location within a world — and since no observation could ever distinguish the two observer populations in any case. A third camp quietly tries to construct models in which ordinary observers dominate, which the first camp reads as conceding the force of the argument and the second camp reads as solving a non-problem.

My working hypothesis is that there is not one disagreement here but two running in parallel: a formal disagreement about measure and self-location within a fixed model, and an epistemological disagreement about whether a theory may rationally be rejected on the ground that believing it would destabilise one's reasons for believing it. The camps talk past one another, on this hypothesis, because the first keeps answering the epistemological question while the second keeps answering the formal one.

Is that the right diagnosis, or is the impasse better explained in some other way — as a verbal dispute about what 'typical observer' means, as a hidden empirical question, or as bedrock methodological intuitions about what a physical theory may legitimately be faulted for, which neither side can argue for? Please work from what each side actually argues rather than from textbook summaries, tell me what kind of disagreement this really is, and say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a formal result about measures, a clarification of what self-undermining would have to involve, an agreed retirement of the question — or else make the case that nothing could settle it, and why.
