After seminar, a friend and I got into it about whether our professor's grading rubric is fair -- we'd both read the same syllabus and sat through the same lectures -- and when I wouldn't budge, she ended the exchange by telling me that when two equally informed and equally intelligent people disagree, the rational thing to do is to split the difference. The phrase bothered me all week, because it sounds like arithmetic but I can't figure out what it's actually claiming. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that "split the difference" might be expressing? I'm guessing some of them differ over who counts as a peer in the first place (equal evidence? equal cognitive ability? equal track record?), some differ over what the disagreement itself is supposed to be evidence of (that I'm mistaken, that my reasoning malfunctioned, that the question is harder than I thought), and some differ over how much adjustment is being demanded (meet exactly halfway, move some amount in the peer's direction, suspend judgment entirely, just double-check my work). For each reading, I'd like to know whether it's a near-platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or something demonstrably too strong -- for instance, I've read that the strong versions run into trouble when the disagreement is about whether the other person is your peer at all, or when many peers disagree with each other, and that there's a worry about whether the view undermines itself given that philosophers who have thought hardest about disagreement still disagree about it. Most importantly, tell me which of the readings, if any, would actually license the use my friend made of it, namely ending our dispute and declaring us both rationally required to converge -- because I suspect the only versions that do that work are ones nobody defends. Please present the readings as a menu with verdicts rather than picking one and explaining it, since I'd like to bring the taxonomy to office hours.
