I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on structuralism in the philosophy of mathematics, and I'd like your help thinking through a problem I keep getting stuck on. Please take on the role of an expert philosopher of mathematics and reason carefully, laying out the options before committing to a diagnosis.

Benacerraf's famous point is familiar: whether 2 is an element of 4 depends on whether we identify the naturals with Zermelo's or von Neumann's ordinals, and no mathematical consideration settles the choice, so numbers plausibly aren't sets at all. Structuralists take this to show that what matters is only the position a number occupies in a structure. But here's what puzzles me: the very same issue seems to arise *within* ordinary mathematics, where mathematicians don't hesitate at all. The natural number 2 is routinely identified with the rational 2/1, the real number 2.0, and the complex number 2+0i, and in standard set-theoretic constructions these are strictly different objects (the real 2 is typically constructed as a Dedekind cut or equivalence class containing the rational 2, not identical to it). Yet mathematicians treat these identifications as unproblematic truths, not as arbitrary stipulations in the way the Zermelo/von Neumann choice is supposed to be.

Please explain: (1) whether this intra-mathematical identification is genuinely the same problem as Benacerraf's, or a different one; (2) how an ante rem structuralist versus an eliminative (in re) structuralist would each handle it — does either view predict that "the natural 2 is the real 2" is true, false, or indeterminate, and is that prediction a virtue or an embarrassment for the view; and (3) whether the apparent asymmetry between the two cases (mathematicians shrug at one and legislate the other) tracks any real philosophical difference, or is merely sociological.
