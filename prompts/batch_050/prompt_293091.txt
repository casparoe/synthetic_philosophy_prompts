I recently agreed to referee a trade-press manuscript on rational choice and the long-run future, and I would like an independent reading before I write my report. One chapter discusses what decision theorists call fanaticism, and the author's characterization strikes me as broadly competent but possibly distorted in ways I cannot quite pin down. Paraphrased in my own words, the passage runs as follows.

When the value at stake is unbounded, the author explains, standard expected utility theory implies fanaticism: for any outcome available with certainty, no matter how good, there is some prize so enormous that even a one-in-a-googol chance of securing it should be preferred to the sure thing. He goes on to say that fanaticism is therefore nothing other than the injunction always to maximize expected value, and that it commits its adherents to treating risk aversion as straightforwardly irrational, since the fanatic must be prepared to stake everything on arbitrarily improbable payoffs. He then presents the St. Petersburg paradox and Pascal's mugging as the standard refutations of the view, and describes the two usual escapes — capping utility at some finite bound, and declining to count probabilities below some threshold as worth considering at all — as conservative, axiom-respecting amendments, in contrast to fanaticism itself, which he says is what runs afoul of the continuity axioms governing rational preference. He concludes that fanaticism persists today mainly as a rhetorical instrument in debates about existential risk, where it is used to argue that minuscule chances of affecting vast numbers of future lives swamp every mundane consideration.

My own sense is that the opening definition is fair, that something goes wrong in the middle section, and that the concluding diagnosis outruns whatever the errors would license — but I am not confident in this judgment, and I want the audit done properly rather than by intuition. Which parts of the characterization would a defender of fanaticism accept as accurate, and which would she reject as misrepresentations? In particular: is fanaticism really identical to the injunction to maximize expected value, and does it genuinely entail that risk aversion is irrational, or is something being conflated there? Are the two proposed escapes as innocent, and fanaticism as deviant, as the author claims with respect to the standard axioms of decision theory? And once the characterization is corrected, does anything survive of his final diagnosis — that the view functions chiefly as a debating tool in arguments about the far future — or does that conclusion depend entirely on the distortions that precede it?
