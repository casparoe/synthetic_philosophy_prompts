I am a novelist whose latest manuscript ends bleakly for nearly everyone in it, and early readers keep telling me, with apparent sincerity, that they are grateful for how much the ending hurt them. I want to understand my own readers better than I currently do. Respond as a professional philosopher of art who is also a careful reader of fiction, and think through the problem slowly rather than reaching for the standard survey.

Read Cormac McCarthy's The Road through the paradox of tragedy. Test the main families of solutions — aesthetic distance, pleasure in our own responding emotions, the safety of fiction as a kind of control, and tragedy as intrinsically valuable rich experience — against the concrete facts of the book: the father's death, the closing pages with the stranger's family, and the notable fact that many people reread the novel and nobody wishes it had ended happily. Here is the wrinkle I want taken seriously as a hypothesis: my suspicion is that the novel's draw is not any of those things but a kind of companionship in dread — readers love people and fear losing them, and the book lets them feel that fear fully, in company, without it being their own loss. If that is right, does it dissolve the paradox, rename it, or merely relocate it? And where does the novel itself resist every theory, mine included — what does it show about why we seek out sad art that no general account seems to capture?
