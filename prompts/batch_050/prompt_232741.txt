I am an undergraduate mathematics major in a first course on set theory, and we have just worked through the axiom of choice and its standard equivalents. There is a pattern in the proofs we have been shown that I cannot find discussed anywhere in my notes, and I would like to know whether it has a name.

The proofs all produce some maximal object — a basis for an arbitrary vector space, a maximal proper ideal in a ring, a complete consistent extension of a consistent set of sentences — by ordering the partial approximations by inclusion and invoking a maximal principle. The place where I keep noticing the same move is the verification that the union of a chain of approximations is itself still an approximation. In every case, the argument seems to be that if the union failed, the failure would already be visible inside finitely many of the pieces. A linear dependence relation involves only finitely many vectors, so if the union of a chain of independent sets were dependent, some member of the chain would already have been dependent. The union of a chain of proper ideals contains the multiplicative unit only if some member already did. A contradiction derived from the union of a chain of consistent sets of sentences cites only finitely many premises, all of which must already appear together in some member.

Meanwhile, I can construct properties where the analogous step fails: a chain of finite sets can have an infinite union, and a chain of bounded sets of real numbers can have an unbounded union, which is reassuring, since otherwise the same template would prove there is a maximal finite set of reals, and there is none.

My questions: does this feature of a property — that any failure of it is always witnessed by finitely many elements — have an established name, and is there a general theorem guaranteeing that maximal principles deliver maximal objects with such properties, so that the union step never needs to be checked case by case? The idea also reminds me of the compactness theorem from my logic course last term; is that the same phenomenon in disguise or a genuinely different one? Finally, are there standard applications of Zorn's lemma where the relevant property lacks this feature, and if so, how is the union step handled there? Please do not answer with a bare term alone: I want the precise definition, where the edges of the concept lie, and how it relates to compactness. Please write your answer in American English.
