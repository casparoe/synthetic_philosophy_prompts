You are an expert philosopher of technology and applied ethics. Think carefully, and begin with a brief list of the conceptual distinctions you will use.

In my seminar, a student encountered the following passage in an unnamed popular article:

Virtual worlds contain representations rather than genuine objects. A virtual sword is merely code and pixels, so it cannot literally do anything; its apparent effects are only changes in software. For the same reason, virtual theft or vandalism cannot cause real harm unless it also produces an offline loss such as money, injury, or damaged hardware. Deleting another player’s castle or taking a rare item is therefore morally like altering a cell in a privately owned spreadsheet: perhaps annoying, but not a genuine wrong against the owner. Verbal abuse in a game is different because abusive words pass between actual people rather than remaining merely virtual.

How fair is this characterization? Audit its major claims, identifying what is accurate, what trades on ambiguity, and what a careful defender of the reality of virtual objects and harms would reject. In particular, distinguish physical reality, causal efficacy within a virtual system, intersubjective or institutional reality, and fictionality; consider how persistence, scarcity, invested labor, shared rules, and restricted access affect the spreadsheet analogy; and assess whether the article’s contrast between virtual vandalism and verbal abuse can be maintained. Use familiar multiplayer-game examples if helpful. Conclude with your own defended verdict: can virtual theft or vandalism constitute a genuine wrong, and if so, under what conditions?
