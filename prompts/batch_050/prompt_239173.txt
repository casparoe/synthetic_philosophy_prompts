Ok so for my ethics class we've been talking about forgiveness and I keep going in circles, so here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and I wanna know if there's actually a principle behind them.

- My friend borrowed my bike without asking and wrecked it, then came over the next day super apologetic and offered to pay for it. Forgiving him feels not just fine but kind of right, like I should.
- My aunt says I HAVE to forgive my uncle for the stuff he did, because he's family. That feels totally wrong to me. Nobody owes forgiveness.
- A friend apologizes for something but it's like the fifth time for the same thing and he clearly isn't going to stop. Apologizing doesn't earn him forgiveness at that point, and I don't feel bad not giving it.
- Someone who hurt me never apologized and never will, but I've decided to stop being angry about it for my own sake. Everyone calls that "forgiving them" but it feels like a different thing to me, more like just moving on.
- But then: those parents who forgive the person who killed their kid, in court, even when the person shows no remorse at all. I think that's real forgiveness, not just "moving on," and it's honestly kind of amazing. But wait, that seems to clash with the last one? If forgiving without an apology is just moving on, how is this real forgiveness?
- My grandma says forgiving is always good, full stop, unconditional. But I feel like if you forgive literally everything no matter what, the word stops meaning anything? Like there's no difference between forgiving and just not caring anymore.
- Also is forgiving ever something you OWE someone? Like if they've done everything right, apologized, made it up to you, years have passed, and you still refuse -- at some point are you the one doing something wrong? My instinct says yes in extreme cases but I hate saying anyone is obligated to forgive.

Is there one principle that gets most of these? Especially I need help with the pair in the middle -- either forgiving-the-unrepentant is real forgiveness (so my bike example isn't special) or it isn't (so the parents in court are just "moving on" which feels wrong to say). Tell me which verdict I should probably drop, and what the principle would say about a case I didn't list, like forgiving someone who died before you could ever talk to them about what they did.
