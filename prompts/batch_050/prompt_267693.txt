I am preparing a sixty-five-minute meeting of an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Moore’s paradox. The students come from philosophy, linguistics, and computer science. They are comfortable distinguishing a proposition from a belief and an utterance, but they have not studied speech-act theory, formal epistemology, or pragmatics. By the end, they should understand why both the omissive form p, but I do not believe that p and the commissive form p, but I believe that not-p can be logically consistent while still being defective as assertions or expressions of belief. They should also see why the diagnosis changes when the person, tense, or embedding environment changes.

Design the lesson rather than simply explaining the paradox to me. Address the following:

1. The order of ideas. Specify what must be introduced before what and explain the dependencies. In particular, decide whether logical consistency, sincerity, assertoric commitment, and the first-person present-tense character of the canonical cases should be separated at the outset or introduced only as needed.

2. The carrying example. Construct one sustained example, preferably not another weather case, that can carry both the omissive and commissive forms and can then be transformed into third-person, past-tense, and embedded versions such as according to Maya or Maya suspects that. Explain what features make it suitable, what distractions it excludes, and where the familiar raining example should enter, if at all.

3. The misconception map. Identify at least six misconceptions these students are likely to form. Include, if you think they are genuinely likely, the claims that the sentence is straightforwardly contradictory, that the problem is merely lying, that no one can consciously believe a Moorean conjunction, that the phenomenon is only about assertion rather than belief, that a Gricean appeal to conversational implicature settles the matter, and that tense and grammatical person are incidental. For each misconception, state why it is tempting, where in the lesson it should be headed off, and what correction will not overstate the case.

4. The pedagogical diagnosis. Recommend the minimal diagnosis I should present as settled, which competing diagnoses I should mention without developing, and where I should explicitly mark the territory as contested. The students should leave understanding that logical consistency and conversational or doxastic defect can coexist, without being given the impression that one slogan resolves every version of the paradox.

5. The final check. Devise one question using a case the class has not discussed. It should test whether students can distinguish truth, assertibility, sincerity, rational believability, and embeddability. State what an adequate answer must contain and what wrong answers would reveal.

6. Deliberate omissions. Identify what I should leave out at this level—for example, formal treatments, historical scholarship, or technical accounts of assertion—and explain how to tell students that these omissions are principled rather than evasive.
