I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on moral aggregation and population ethics, and I'm trying to anticipate how my professor will rule on a case I want to raise in the final session, partly because my term paper argues that her positions commit her to more aggregation than she admits. Throughout the term she has given quick, confident verdicts on thought experiments involving the aggregation of small harms, and I've recorded four of them:

1. One person enduring a full day of torture versus arbitrarily many people each getting a single dust speck in the eye: prevent the torture. She was emphatic that no number of dust specks changes this.
2. One person suffering a mild tension headache for an afternoon versus arbitrarily many people each getting a dust speck in the eye: prevent the headache. When pressed, she said dust specks "don't add up to anything, full stop."
3. One person suffering a broken arm versus one billion people each suffering a day-long tension headache: prevent the headaches. She said the sheer number of headaches "clearly wins here."
4. One person dying prematurely versus arbitrarily many people each suffering a broken arm: prevent the death. She said broken arms, however many, "never get there."

The new case I want to put to her: one person dying prematurely versus ten billion people each losing the use of both legs for the rest of their lives. I'm fairly sure the four verdicts above don't jointly settle this, because I can see at least two general principles that fit her record but pull in opposite directions here — and possibly a third under which the record is simply silent.

Could you help me think it through? Specifically: reconstruct the candidate principles that are each consistent with all four verdicts; state what each principle predicts about my death-versus-paralysis case; give your best guess at what she would actually say, with reasons drawn from the texture of her earlier rulings; and if her record genuinely underdetermines the case, identify which single further thought experiment would most efficiently distinguish the remaining principles from one another. I'm less interested in which answer is morally correct than in which answer is hers, and in whether my term paper's charge of inconsistency has any footing.
