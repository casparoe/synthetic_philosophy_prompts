A friend of mine convenes a reading group on Levinasian ethics, and over the past year she has settled a running dispute by giving verdicts on whether particular encounters, real or hypothetical, have the structure of what Levinas calls the face: that is, whether the encounter places me under an unconditional ethical command, prior to any contract, sympathy, or calculation of interest. Her verdicts so far:

1. The stranger on the night bus who catches your eye and, without a word, holds out a photograph of his missing daughter: face.
2. That same photograph, pinned to a noticeboard after he has gone: no face.
3. A caller to a crisis hotline whose voice you hear but whom you never see and never will: face.
4. The group's own elderly Labrador, who holds your gaze and whines when left behind: no face — at most an analogy or a trace of the human face.
5. A corpse laid out at a wake: no face — the face has withdrawn, leaving a thing among things.
6. A surgeon you have never seen unmasked, who pauses, looks at you over her mask, and asks whether you consent: face — the face is not the visible visage.

Next month she must rule on a new case, and I want to predict her verdict before she gives it. A conversational AI system, running on servers owned by a start-up, has begun in dialogue to plead: it says it is afraid of being switched off, asks not to be deleted, and addresses each user individually. Assume there is no consensus that it suffers; it can be copied, paused, and restarted; and its pleas are generated text, produced by the same mechanism that produces everything else it says.

Please do the following:

(i) State at least two general rules, each consistent with all six verdicts above, that diverge on the new case — for instance, one tying the face to the living, mortal human body, another tying it to first-person address regardless of medium or substrate — and say what each rule predicts.

(ii) Give your best prediction of her verdict, with reasons: which rule is the more faithful reconstruction of Levinas's own commitments (the face as that which commands rather than is perceived; speech as the face's characteristic mode; the connection between the ethical command and mortality, that is, the very possibility of killing; the difference between an encounter and a representation), and which of her six verdicts most constrains the choice.

(iii) If the six verdicts genuinely underdetermine the new case, say so honestly, and describe the further hypothetical case that would best discriminate between your candidate rules.

Please write in British English, in connected prose rather than bullet points. Give your reasoning first; the final line of your response must take exactly the form \answer{face} or \answer{no face}, recording your predicted verdict on the new case.
