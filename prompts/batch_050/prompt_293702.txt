For a graduate seminar on the philosophy of love, each of us has been asked to circulate a short statement of where we stand before the session on the rationality of love, so that the discussion can start from our actual commitments rather than from the readings. Here is mine, and before I send it around I would like a consistency audit rather than a summary of the literature. I hold the following: (1) Love is for reasons. When I love someone, I can say what about them merits it -- their wit, their steadiness in a crisis, the particular way they treat strangers -- and a love that could cite nothing at all would seem to me blind rather than deep. (2) Love is not under direct voluntary control. Nobody can simply decide, as an act of will, to start or stop loving someone this afternoon, and I do not regard this as a defect in love. (3) Love should be responsive to evidence about the beloved in roughly the way belief is responsive to evidence about the world: if I discovered that my partner had been quietly cruel to people who could do nothing for them, that discovery would give me a genuine reason to love them less, and flatly refusing to update would be a kind of irrationality, not loyalty. (4) Love should not license trading up. The rational lover does not leave their partner upon meeting someone who instantiates all the same virtues to a slightly higher degree, and any theory of love on which this is what reason recommends has gone wrong somewhere. (5) The shared history of a relationship matters independently of anyone's qualities: part of why I love this person is what we have been through together, and I owe no love at all to a perfect duplicate of my partner with identical qualities but no past with me. (6) Unconditional love is no ideal. Love that would survive the beloved becoming genuinely monstrous has ceased to be responsive to anything, and while we may find a parent's undying love for a terrible child moving, we should not hold it up as what love at its best looks like.

Please go through these six theses and tell me whether they form a tenable package. I am specifically not asking whether each claim is defensible on its own -- I am asking whether they are defensible together. Identify every pair or triple that is in genuine tension, and for each one: state exactly which commitments generate the conflict, rate its severity, and say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me. Distinguish real tensions from merely apparent ones -- I suspect at least one pair looks incompatible but is not, and I would like that shown rather than asserted. Then rank the genuine tensions from most to least serious and tell me, if I could revise only one commitment, which single change would do the most to stabilize the whole set. Please do not simply conclude that love is mysterious or paradoxical; treat the set the way you would treat an inconsistent belief system in any other domain.
