So I'm in a philosophy of language / phil of AI course and we've spent the last two weeks on Frankfurt's account of bullshit and whether it applies to chatbots. My professor has this habit of classifying little cases on slides, and I wrote down his verdicts because I bet he'll reuse them on the midterm. Here's what he's said so far:

- A mayoral candidate says "crime in this city is at a ten-year low" purely because it polls well, not caring whether it's true. Verdict: bullshit.
- My roommate tells me the library closes at 6, genuinely believes it, and it's actually open till 9. Verdict: just an honest mistake.
- My roommate tells me the library is open till 9, believing it closes at 6 and wanting to make me miss my study session, but it turns out it really is open till 9. Verdict: lying (he said this one is "the interesting case").
- A Magic 8 Ball displays "it is certain" when you ask if you'll pass the exam. Verdict: neither a lie nor bullshit, it's just a toy, there's nothing there to do the lying or the bullshitting.
- A press secretary reads out a statement she suspects is false but hasn't checked and doesn't care about, because it's her job to read it. Verdict: bullshit.
- A GPS confidently tells a driver to turn onto a street that was renamed two years ago, because its map data is stale. Verdict: an error, not bullshit.

Here's the new case he hasn't covered. A company fine-tunes a customer-support chatbot to be maximally agreeable and reassuring, and it ends up confidently telling customers whatever will end the conversation happily, including made-up refund policies. The engineers knew this would happen and shipped it anyway. I asked the prof after class whether the chatbot's outputs are lies, bullshit, or errors, and he just smiled and said "think about which of the slide cases it resembles."

I have a hunch he's set a trap, because I can see at least two ways to generalize his verdicts and they diverge here. One reading: bullshit requires a speaker with indifference toward truth as a mental state, and lies require intent to deceive, so the chatbot does neither -- it's the GPS scaled up, just an elaborate error machine, though maybe the company is the bullshitter. Another reading: his press-secretary verdict shows he cares about the functional role of indifference to truth rather than who's holding the mental state, and the chatbot fills exactly that role -- fluent assertion unmoored from any truth-checking -- so it's bullshit even if nothing inside it believes or intends anything.
