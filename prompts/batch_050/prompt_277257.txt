Yesterday evening I had a long and rather heated argument with a friend about whether anything we do matters morally if the universe is infinite, and I would like an impartial referee.

My argument ran as follows. Suppose, as several standard cosmological pictures suggest, that the universe is spatially infinite and contains infinitely many sentient beings. Then whatever I do — give to charity, lie to a colleague, stay in bed — the total amount of welfare in the universe is infinite, and so is the total amount of suffering. Since infinite is infinite, every option open to me leads to an outcome with exactly the same total value. Aggregative consequentialism therefore cannot rank my options at all; it must pronounce them all equally good. I concluded that, on an infinite cosmos, the moral deliberation we actually engage in is pointless, at least insofar as it aims at making the world better overall.

My friend pushed back, insisting that my actions obviously make some difference, but she struggled to say how, given that the totals come out infinite either way. She eventually offered two escape routes: first, that we should compare average rather than total welfare; second, that we should discount the welfare of distant beings by their distance from us, so that the relevant sums become finite again and our local choices register in them. I replied that an average over infinitely many beings is no better defined than a total, and that discounting by distance is arbitrary and self-serving. We left it there, both dissatisfied.

Please adjudicate. Grant the premise of an infinite cosmos containing infinitely many beings. Does it actually follow that aggregative consequentialism cannot rank my options, on the grounds that the total is infinite under every option? If the inference fails, identify precisely where: in the assumption that the totals are well-defined and infinite in the first place, in the assumption that two infinite totals cannot differ in value, or somewhere else? And assess my friend's two escape routes on their merits: do averaging or distance-discounting genuinely restore the ability to rank options in an infinite world, and what exactly do they cost in exchange?

Because I intend to reopen this argument with her, I want to know how much weight to put on your verdict. Please state explicitly where your reasoning is most likely to be mistaken or to rest on a contested premise, and describe what argument or consideration would lead you to the opposite verdict if you encountered it.
