I'm a graduate student in philosophy who has spent the semester reading about self-knowledge, and I've realized I don't have a principled way to evaluate the theories I'm encountering. Every author seems to treat a different phenomenon as the central one: some build their whole account around the fact that I can typically say what I believe without observing my own behavior, others start from the unsettling experimental findings that people confidently give fabricated reasons for their choices (and never notice the fabrication), and still others fixate on the sense in which sincerely declaring an intention or resolution seems partly to *make* it true rather than merely report it. Before I commit to a position, I want the specification written out first: what would any adequate theory of how we know our own minds actually have to accomplish?

Please write the desiderata. For each requirement, give the case or phenomenon that motivates it and explain why a theory that fails there is inadequate rather than merely incomplete. I'd like the list to cover, at minimum: the asymmetry between how I know my own states and how I know yours (a theory that reduces self-knowledge to especially good self-observation seems to lose something, but what exactly?); the reliable error cases, including confabulation and the situations where people misidentify their own reasons, emotions, or even preferences; the apparent difference between knowing that you're in pain and knowing why you voted the way you did (are these even the same kind of achievement, and must one theory cover both?); and the odd authority of avowals, where saying what I intend seems partly constitutive of intending it. For each, indicate whether it strikes you as a genuine constraint or a parochial assumption inherited from one research tradition.

Two further requests. First, flag any pairs of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied -- for instance, I have a hunch that fully honoring first-person authority and fully explaining systematic confabulation pull against each other, and if the list itself turns out to be incoherent, that's a result I want to know about. Second, once the list is fixed, briefly score the main families of views against it (roughly: inner-perception or acquaintance accounts, rational-agency and constitutivist accounts, and inferential or interpretive accounts), but keep this short -- the deliverable is the specification, not a literature survey. Assume the purpose is a theory good enough for clinical and ordinary life, not just for the seminar room: it should tell us when self-reports deserve trust, not only how they work.
