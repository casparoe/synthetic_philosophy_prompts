ok so I'm tutoring a friend in an intro course on games and proofs and we keep butting heads over the same thing: he hears "player one has a winning strategy" and immediately asks "ok what is it, show me the moves," and I keep trying to explain that in Hex the whole point is that nobody shows you the moves. The argument, as I understand it, is roughly: suppose player two had a winning strategy; then player one just plops a stone anywhere on the first turn and then pretends to be player two, following that very strategy; the extra stone sitting on the board can't hurt you since having more of your own color is never a disadvantage, and if the strategy ever calls for playing where that stone already is, you just play somewhere else and keep going. Contradiction, so player two had no winning strategy, so player one must. And yet the proof hands you nothing -- no opening, no plan, no diagram. My friend calls it cheating and honestly I half sympathize.

Write me a villanelle that dramatizes this exact tension, the proof that guarantees a win while refusing to say how. The two refrains should be the two halves of that tension -- something like the proof's confident "a win exists" versus the maddening "but no one can produce it" -- and I want their repetition to feel like the argument circling: every time the thief steals the strategy, the second refrain should come back to undercut the theft. The final two lines should put both refrains side by side and let the contradiction do the work, the way the argument itself ends. Keep it accurate enough that someone who knows strategy-stealing would nod along (the arbitrary first move, the extra stone being harmless, the whole thing being nonconstructive), but it should also just read as a good poem to someone who's never heard of Hex -- more about the strange experience of knowing something is true without being able to show it. Nineteen lines, no title needed, and please don't resolve the tension at the end, because thats kind of the whole point.
