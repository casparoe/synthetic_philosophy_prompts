I work as a machine learning engineer, fluent in the vocabulary of representation learning: generative versus discriminative modeling, contrastive objectives, amortized inference, embeddings and latent spaces, and the standard talk about classifiers, decision boundaries, and causal grounding. I have been reading about the seventh-century Buddhist logician Dharmakīrti, and I would like his epistemology restated in that vocabulary, as rigorously as you can manage.

Take the core of his system element by element:

1. Perception (pratyakṣa) as a non-conceptual cognition that directly presents the unique, unrepeatable particular (svalakṣaṇa), which alone is fully real.
2. Inference (anumāna) and the three marks of a valid reason: the reason must be present in the subject of the inference, present in at least some similar cases, and wholly absent from every dissimilar case.
3. The theory of concepts as exclusions (apoha): to grasp the concept "cow" is to be able to exclude everything non-cow, not to apprehend a positive, mind-independent universal.
4. Causal efficacy (arthakriyā) as the criterion of the real: only what can produce an effect counts as existent, which disqualifies universals, relations, and persisting wholes.
5. Momentariness (kṣaṇikatva): whatever exists does so only for an instant, and the enduring objects of ordinary discourse are constructions imposed on a stream of momentary events.

For each element, give me the most faithful translation into the vocabulary of representation learning and probabilistic modeling, and be explicit about each translation move you make; do not smuggle in assumptions native to the target framework and present them as translation. Then give me a residue report: which parts of the system map cleanly, which map only under distortion, and which have no expression at all in my vocabulary. Where there is residue, say whether you take it to show that the ML vocabulary is missing something — for instance, anything corresponding to the reflexive, self-aware character Dharmakīrti ascribes to cognition, or to the normative and ultimately soteriological role he assigns to getting things right — or whether it shows that the original claim was doing less work than it appeared to. In particular, I want to know whether my standing suspicion is correct that apoha is simply the insight that a discriminative classifier needs no generative model of its categories, or whether that identification misses something essential to his position.
