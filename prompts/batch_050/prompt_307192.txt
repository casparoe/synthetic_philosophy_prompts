I am writing the theory-of-pleasure chapter of a dissertation on well-being, and before I survey the literature's contenders I want the standard of adequacy fixed in advance rather than reverse-engineered from whichever theory I end up liking. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of pleasure must deliver, where a theory of pleasure is an answer to the question of what all and only pleasures have in common in virtue of which they are pleasures.

The list should be built up from cases and constraints, with the motivating consideration stated behind each desideratum. At minimum, address the following and say whether each generates a genuine requirement or only a camp's prejudice: the heterogeneity problem itself (a theory must cover the pleasures of taste, of orgasm, of finishing a proof, of warm baths, and of hearing good news, and must say whether their unity is phenomenal, attitudinal, functional, or merely nominal); relief pleasures and pleasures of anticipation, which have no obvious standalone sensation; the case of unnoticed pleasure, as when one is absorbed in an activity and would retrospectively call it pleasant; false pleasures, where one enjoys what one believes is happening and it is not, which attitudinal theories allegedly handle better than felt-quality theories; the distinction a theory must respect among pleasure, enjoyment, liking, desire-satisfaction, and mere absence of pain; the requirement that pleasure be capable of explaining pleasure-seeking without being defined as whatever is sought, on pain of making hedonism about motivation trivially true; and the measurement constraint, that the theory make sense of intra- and interpersonal comparisons of intensity and of pleasure in infants and non-human animals.

I also want the theory tested against two constructed cases, and the desiderata should make clear what a theory must say about them. First, the swap: imagine a world in which the phenomenal character that accompanies orgasm here accompanies doing logic there, and vice versa, with behavioral and attitudinal profiles held fixed; does pleasure track the feel or the attitude, and must an adequate theory take a stand? Second, the isolation test: for any candidate common factor F a theory proposes, it should be coherent to ask whether a creature could have F while lacking pleasure's typical causes and effects, and the theory must say whether such a creature enjoys pleasure -- a theory whose factor cannot even in principle be so isolated has, I suspect, failed to identify anything.

For each desideratum, mark whether it is non-negotiable (any theory failing it is not a theory of pleasure) or negotiable (a commitment of one tradition, such as the phenomenologist's insistence on hedonic tone or the functionalist's insistence on a causal role). The part I most want is the coherence check: identify any pairs or subsets of desiderata you believe cannot be jointly satisfied -- for instance, phenomenal unity against full coverage of the heterogeneous cases, or the intelligibility of hedonism as a substantive thesis against a tight conceptual link between pleasure and goodness -- and explain why the tension is real rather than merely unmet by current theories. If the list itself turns out to be unsatisfiable, that is a result and I want it stated as such.

Finally, and briefly, score the main contenders against the finished list: felt-quality or hedonic-tone theories, attitudinal theories of the desire-based sort, Sidgwick-style accounts on which pleasure is consciousness desirable from the subject's point of view, homeostatic or functionalist accounts, and a theory built on the affective-neuroscience distinction between liking and wanting. Keep the scorecard short; the criteria and their tensions are the deliverable, not the verdict.
