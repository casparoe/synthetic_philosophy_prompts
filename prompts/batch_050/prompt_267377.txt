So I'm a novelist working on my next book and I want to steal the presumptuous philosopher thought experiment from philosophy of probability and turn it into actual fiction, but with a twist I haven't seen done. Act as a professional philosopher who also moonlights as a sci-fi editor.

Quick background in case its fuzzy: the self-indication assumption says you should reason as if you're a random sample from all possible observers, so theories predicting more observers get boosted. The self-sampling assumption says sample from observers in your actual reference class instead, so no boost. The presumptuous philosopher bit is that under SIA you could seemingly conclude the universe is almost certainly huge (or infinite) from your armchair, before doing any astronomy, which feels nuts.

Here's my setup: a generation ship's governing council has to vote on whether to activate one terraforming pod (one colony, ~10k people) or ten thousand pods. The catch is that the passengers' minds will be forked into the colonies, so the pod choice literally determines how many copies of each person exist. One councilor argues that, assuming SIA, each passenger should be near-certain they'll wake up in the big-colony branch *before the vote even happens*, and should therefore vote for the small colony, because being a tiny fraction of observers is the only way their existence would be surprising. Her opponent calls this reasoning insane and reaches for SSA. The vote happens.

Write me a short story (1200-1800 words) dramatizing this, but here's the hard part: the story itself has to take a stand, or at least make the reader feel the weight, on whether the councilor's reasoning is a reductio of SIA or a bullet its defenders should bite. Don't resolve it with a cheap trick (simulation reveal, it was all a dream, etc.). Before you start writing, brainstorm briefly: 3-4 ways the fork/vote mechanics could be staged so the anthropic reasoning is legible to a reader who's never heard of SSA or SIA, then pick the strongest and go. Think carefully about whose POV makes the philosophy land emotionally rather than as a lecture.
