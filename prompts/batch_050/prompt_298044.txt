I lead the interpretation team at a mid-sized museum of world history. We build or revise two or three galleries a year, and by now every gallery proposal seems to contain at least one collapse story: a ruined city, a vanished people, a civilization that fell. The labels I sign off on are read by hundreds of thousands of visitors, copied by teachers, and photographed for social media, and they are effectively impossible to correct once the paint is dry. At the same time, our labels are capped at about eighty words, our panel meetings run to a fixed schedule, and none of my writers holds a research degree in archaeology. What I need is a decision procedure my team can run on any proposed collapse narrative before it goes anywhere near a label.

Write that procedure. It should be a sequence of questions a writer answers about the narrative, in order, with what counts as an adequate answer to each, the points where an inadequate answer stops the process with a rejection or a demand to reframe, and the points where the procedure deliberately hands off to a human judgment call because the remaining question is one of values or framing rather than evidence. It must be usable in a single working session by someone who knows no archaeology beyond what the procedure itself contains.

The conceptual work I want it to perform, grounded in what archaeologists and historians actually argue about:

First, force the writer to specify what exactly is claimed to have collapsed -- population, political authority, literacy and monument-building, long-distance exchange, an elite culture -- since these move at different speeds and rarely fail together, and a label saying a civilization collapsed when a dynasty fell is a very different claim from one saying people died.

Second, build in the failure-versus-transformation distinction: whether the people in question died, dispersed, changed how they lived, or merely stopped doing the things that leave ruins, and what each answer implies for the words a label may use. Include a test for the empty-landscape move, the narrative device that lets a people vanish from the story when their descendants are still around to be insulted by the label.

Third, a timescale check: how many generations the alleged collapse spanned, and when slow attrition is being dishonestly compressed into a dramatic fall.

Fourth, a perspective check: collapse for whom, given that the end of a palace economy may look quite different to the people who maintained it than to the people it fed.

Fifth, a causal audit: whether the proposal rests on a single cause, whether the evidence cited is of a kind that can bear a causal claim, and -- crucially -- whether the central claim rests on a reading of the evidence that specialists currently dispute. Where it does, the procedure should say what the label must concede, not pretend the dispute away.

Then run the procedure on three gallery proposals, showing its working: a panel presenting the Classic Maya collapse as the mysterious disappearance of a people; a diorama telling the traditional Easter Island story of ecological self-destruction before European contact; and an interactive crediting the fall of the Late Bronze Age palatial world to a single invading group. At least one of these should be a case where the procedure kills or mutilates a proposal my curators love -- tell me which, and accept that consequence rather than bending the procedure to spare it.

Finally, two caveats. Say plainly what the procedure cannot do: which questions it answers mechanically, which it can only structure, and where it will misfire -- for instance, on very poorly documented cases, or on proposals about our own century, where using ancient collapse as a warning about the present raises the stakes beyond what a checklist can govern. And throughout, separate what is genuinely settled among specialists from what is live dispute: I do not want a label procedure that smuggles one side of an active controversy -- the pre-contact ecocide account of Rapa Nui being the obvious case -- into the gallery as though it were the consensus. Wherever the procedure embeds a contested choice, flag it and tell me what the alternative version of that step would look like.
