I am a literary novelist in the early planning stages of a long project, and I am trying to decide which philosophical questions about the self the book should take as its load-bearing structure. The premise, briefly: the novel follows one woman across roughly forty years, in five long sections set about eight years apart. In each section she is recognizably continuous with the person in the previous one — same body, same legal name, mostly overlapping memories — but her values, temperament, and central commitments have shifted enough each time that the people around her disagree about whether she is still the same person, and she herself gives different answers in different sections. I do not want the book to become a philosophy seminar in disguise, but I do want its philosophical spine to be real. I would rather build the novel around two or three genuinely open questions and do them justice than gesture at the whole debate.

I have been reading at the intersection of Buddhist anatta (no-self) doctrine and the Western personal-identity debate — psychological continuity, bodily continuity, narrative identity, and so on. What I would like from you is a defended agenda, not a survey: which open questions at this intersection matter most for a project like mine, and in what order? Please fix the prioritization explicitly to my purpose — what deserves sustained attention over five years of writing a novel, as opposed to what matters for a metaphysician or a meditation teacher — and defend the ordering. Plausible criteria might include: which questions are genuinely unresolved rather than quietly settled under some framing; which can be dramatized through a lived life rather than only stated in argument; which are upstream of the others, so that taking a position on them structures everything downstream; and which are most at risk of being handled in a clichéd way — the we-are-all-rivers treatment — unless the writer is careful. If you would use different criteria, name them and justify the substitution.

Where you can, please connect the two traditions concretely rather than treating them as parallel monologues. For instance, whether the anatta claim that there is no enduring self is best read as a metaphysical thesis, a phenomenological report, or a therapeutic recommendation matters enormously for what my protagonist could plausibly discover about herself, as opposed to merely decide or be told. And if you judge some part of this intersection to be overrated as material for fiction — questions that loom large in the literature but would sit inertly on the page — please say so and explain why.

Finally, close by telling me what would change your mind about the ranking: which considerations, if I found them convincing, should lead me to a different ordering, and where you take your own reasoning to be most exposed to failure.
