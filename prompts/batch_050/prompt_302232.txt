I recently took over as treasurer of a small investment partnership — six of us, pooled savings, decisions made by vote but with me proposing the options. In our first real dispute, one of my partners told me my instincts about risk were "incoherent in a way that will eventually cost us money." Since then I've been trying to get a grip on the coherence arguments for expected utility maximization (money pumps, the Allais paradox, representation theorems), and I want help figuring out what I actually believe.

Please act as an expert decision theorist. Think carefully rather than rushing to the standard textbook position. I'd like you to begin by brainstorming two or three candidate principles that might rationalize my verdicts below, and then settle on the one (or the combination) that does best, stating it precisely. For each verdict it cannot accommodate, say so plainly rather than quietly dropping it.

Here are my gut verdicts:

1. Paying a modest annual premium to insure my house against fire is rational, even though the premium exceeds the expected loss. Buying a weekly lottery ticket is not rational, even though the expected loss per ticket is smaller than my insurance overpayment. Both are negative-expectation gambles, so a principle that explains this contrast can't just invoke expected money.

2. Offered a choice between $500,000 for certain and a 10% chance of $2.5 million with nothing otherwise, I'd take the sure $500,000 without a second thought. But offered a choice between a 10% chance of $500,000 and a 1% chance of $2.5 million, I'd take the 1% shot at $2.5 million. I realize this is the Allais pattern and that no utility function over final wealth reproduces it, but both choices still feel right to me, and I'd resent being told one of them is a mistake.

3. Cyclical preferences feel different. If I genuinely preferred A to B, B to C, and C to A, and someone could charge me a small fee for each swap, I'd be pumped for money, and that does seem like a defect in me, not just a quirk. So: intransitivity bad, Allais fine. If there's a principled line between those, I want it; I suspect there is, something about what the objection actually turns on, but I can't state it.

4. Sunk costs. Staying at a bad film because I paid $15 for the ticket is foolish; the money is gone either way. But honoring a promise I made six months ago, when I'd now rather not, is not foolish, even though structurally it looks the same — the past consideration is fixed, and the forward-looking reasons cut against it. I think the difference has something to do with what my present choice commits me to going forward, or with what kind of person it makes me, but I can't articulate it.

5. When we last voted, I argued the partnership should reject a 50/50 bet that loses $80,000 or gains $120,000, even though I agreed each of us could easily absorb an $80,000 loss individually and the expected value is positive. My partner (the one who called me incoherent) says risk aversion at stakes that small is irrational. I still think declining was right, but I can't say whether I'm relying on loss aversion, on the bet's effect on our future options, or on nothing defensible at all.

A few things I want from the answer. First, the candidate principle(s) should state precisely which feature of a choice does the normative work in each case — is it the shape of a utility function, the structure of the menu, the separability of outcomes across states, or something about dynamic consistency over time? Second, tell me which of my five verdicts must be abandoned if I accept the best available principle, and whether abandoning it looks like a real cost or a bullet worth biting. Third, give me two or three new cases — ones I haven't considered — where my principle and the textbook expected-utility principle come apart, so I can check which side I land on. Finally, apply whatever we end up with to my actual situation: should the partnership have taken the bet in case 5, and is there any coherent framework in which my insurance purchase, my Allais choices, and my refusal of that bet all come out rational?
