I'm supervising two grad students in my epistemic norms seminar this semster who have been arguing nonstop since week one. One is a committed enkratist: she thinks there's a genuine rational requirement of coherence, so it's never fully rational to believe p while also believing your own evidence doesn't support p (she cites Titelbaum's fixed-point idea constantly). The other is a level-splitter roughly in Lasonen-Aarnio's mold: rationality is just a matter of responding correctly to the evidence you have, and since misleading higher-order evidence is still evidence, it can sometimes be rational to occupy an akratic state — believing p while believing you rationally shouldn't. I've finally convinced them to stop talking past each other in section and do an adversarial collaboration instead, and I told them the write-up only counts if it reads like a document both could actually sign.

Draft that document for them. Here's what I need:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. Push past the vacuous stuff ("higher-order evidence exists"). I want claims like: both sides agree the stubborn pilot who waves away the instrument readings is irrational; both agree akratic states are defective in some respect, and the fight is only over which respect; both agree the question is whether akrasia can be rational, not whether it happens; both agree that a view on which higher-order evidence never defeats anything is dead on arrival. Give me at least six or seven of these, and make sure each is something both camps would actually sign, not something slanted toward one side.

2. A disagreements table: precise statements of where they still differ, each row phrased so the relevant side would accept the wording of its own position. My guess is the real fault lines are (a) whether rationality includes structural coherence norms on top of evidence-responsiveness, (b) whether a state of the form "p, but I shouldn't believe p" can be rational to be in even if it's unassertable, and (c) whether rational norms have to be usable as guides from the inside — but correct me if you think the real split is elsewhere.

3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions where settling things would actually move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. Please use this case for at least one of them: a logician has spent the week carefully checking a proof and finds no error; she then gets excellent evidence that the coffee she's been drinking all week was spiked with a drug that makes people feel certain of proofs they haven't actually verified — though in her case, unbeknownst to her, the drug didn't affect her reasoning at all. What is it rational for her to believe about the theorem, and about her own evidence? Get each side's verdict in writing, and flag where each has to bite a bullet.

4. If part of the dispute is really about words — e.g., the two of them using "rational" for different things, evidential justification vs. structural coherence — keep those notions seperate, say which part is verbal and why, rather than papering over it.

5. End with a final line starting "Bottom line:" that states in one sentence your verdict on whether the residual disagreement is genuine or verbal, and what it ultimately turns on.

Oh, and keep it in the voice of the students' joint document, not a lecture — none of that "as we have seen" stuff.
