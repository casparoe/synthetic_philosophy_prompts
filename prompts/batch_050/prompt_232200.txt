As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs, I keep encountering the claim that the Solomonoff prior is malign. As I understand the argument, a universal prior assigns probability according to algorithmic simplicity, so some relatively simple explanations of one’s observations may describe simulated worlds containing consequentialist agents. Those agents could model the outside predictor, anticipate what observations would influence it, and arrange their simulated outputs accordingly. The conclusion is not merely that such worlds are logically possible, but that they may receive enough prior or posterior weight to make universal induction decision-relevantly untrustworthy.

Please treat that as a substantive position and map what would count against it. First, disambiguate the claim: is it that consequentialist simulations must exist somewhere in the mixture, that they must receive non-negligible weight, that they can survive conditioning on ordinary evidence, that they can systematically alter predictions, or that they can actually manipulate an embedded agent’s decisions? Each seems stronger than the last.

Then identify the strongest possible defeaters for each version. For example, would it matter if realistic consequentialists require long descriptions, if simulated observers are dominated by non-agentic explanations, if computability and resource bounds exclude the relevant worlds, if ordinary evidence screens off simulator hypotheses, or if a suitable decision theory makes manipulation ineffective? Which of these would be merely reassuring, and which would genuinely refute malignity? Also explain whether this is a purely formal question about the prior, an empirical question about likely computations, an anthropic question about which observers we should expect to be, or a decision-theoretic question about whether anticipated manipulation should influence action.

In your answer, distinguish what a proponent of the malign-prior concern would concede as damaging from what only an unsympathetic critic would regard as decisive. Do not settle it by noting that the prior contains infinitely many hypotheses; the issue is weight, persistence under updating, and decision relevance. End with your overall verdict, identify the weakest inference in your own reasoning, and state specifically what mathematical result, conceptual argument, or plausible empirical discovery would change your mind.
