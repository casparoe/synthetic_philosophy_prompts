I'm putting together a reading group on updateless decision theory (the LessWrong-style one where you pick a global policy mapping observations to actions, rather than choosing an act given what you've already observed). The objections I've collected so far: (1) the 5-and-10 problem, where a proof-based agent can be lured into spurious proofs that some bad action has high utility, since once it's decided to take an action it can prove anything about what happens if it doesn't; (2) the tie-breaking problem for symmetric agents -- two copies of the same UDT algorithm in an asymmetric coordination game can each predict that the other will do exactly what they do, so neither can sensibly end up in the better role; (3) the complaint that the whole thing leans on logical counterfactuals -- "what would happen if my algorithm output something other than what it outputs" -- which is arguably undefined rather than just hard; and (4) the worry that being updateless means never actually learning, so the theory only looks good if you smuggle in the right "logical prior" about when the policy is chosen. What's one more objection that's genuinely different from these -- not a dressed-up version of the logical-counterfactuals complaint or another symmetry/tie-breaking case? And tell me whether you think UTD's defenders have a decent answer to it.
