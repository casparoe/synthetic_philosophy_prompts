I'm putting together a problem set for my upper-level decision theory course (third-year undergrads who've done a semester of game theory, so they're comfortable with mixed strategies and backward induction but haven't seen games with imperfect recall). I want one long-form question on the absent-minded driver problem, worth 30 marks, doable in about 60-75 minutes.

Act as an experienced decision theory lecturer who has set this kind of exam many times. Think carefully, and maybe do a bit of brainstorming first about which misunderstandings actually seperate students who understand the problem from students who have just memorized the story, before you settle on the final question.

The question should use the classic setup: a driver on a highway with two indistinguishable intersections, payoff 0 for exiting at the first, 4 for exiting at the second, 1 for never exiting, and no memory of having passed the first intersection when he reaches the second. But it must contain at least one part that can't be answered by reproducing the standard analysis -- for instance a variation where the driver can buy an imperfect memory aid before the trip, or where the payoffs shift so the planning-stage optimum becomes a pure strategy, or something else you come up with. Your call, but make sure the twist genuinely changes the answer rather than just relabeling the setup.

Deliverables:

1. The question itself, split into 3-4 parts with marks per part. At least one computational part (e.g. find the optimal exit probability and the resulting expected payoff) and at least one conceptual part (is the divergence between planning-stage and action-stage reasoning a genuine paradox? can the driver coherently assign a probability to being at the first intersection?).

2. A model answer, roughly 700-900 words including the algebra.

3. A marking scheme that names, per part, the specific moves a full-marks answer must contain and the specific mistakes that should cost marks. I'm thinking of things like: conflating the planning-stage optimum with the best strategy simpliciter, treating the case as ordinary time inconsistency in the hyperbolic-discounting mold, assuming the driver can hold a stable belief about which intersection he's at, or solving the twist part by just rerunning the untwisted analysis.

One hard constraint: the question has to be fully self-contained. Most of my students won't have read anything on absent-mindedness or imperfect recall, so define everything the question needs inside the question itself, and don't let the model answer lean on outside literature either.
