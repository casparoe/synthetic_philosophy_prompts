Yesterday I spent hours arguing with a friend about what weakness of will actually is, and we kept circling the same cases. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher specializing in philosophy of action and practical rationality. Think carefully, and feel free to brainstorm candidate principles and stress-test them before settling on an answer.

Here are my verdicts, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them:

1. A dieter firmly resolves to stop at two drinks, then orders a third while fully aware of the resolution, and regrets it the next morning. Classic weakness of will.
2. A man who skips his planned workout because a colleague genuinely needs emergency help is not weak-willed; he revised his plan for good reasons.
3. A smoker who, after honest reconsideration, judges that this cigarette really is best all things considered, and smokes it, is not weak-willed either -- mistaken, maybe, but not weak.
4. A woman who knows she cannot trust her future self signs a contract that donates a painful sum to a cause she despises if she fails her resolution, and succeeds only because of it. I say she displays less strength of will than someone who resists the same temptation unaided.
5. A man who never makes resolutions at all, and simply does whatever he feels like each evening, is not weak-willed either -- there is no standard he falls short of. But I refuse to say he has more strength of will than the woman in case 4.
6. Someone who keeps his resolution only by never thinking about the temptation -- leaving the room, humming, changing the subject -- seems to me genuinely strong-willed, even though he'd likely fail if he confronted the temptation head-on.

Cases 4 and 5 are where my friend dug in: she says my verdicts there can't both stand, since I credit avoidance in case 6 but not the prior-avoidance of making no commitments in case 5, and I penalize the precommitter for a trick I praise in another form.

Is there one coherent account of weakness of will that delivers most of these verdicts? State your candidate principle (or two rival candidates) precisely, note which case motivates each clause, tell me which of my verdicts you think has to go if the account is to stay simple, and say what the resulting account predicts for two cases I haven't listed: someone who abandons a resolution because they have stopped caring about the goal it served, and someone whose preferences genuinely reverse near a reward in the way hyperbolic discounters' do, with no resolution ever in place.
