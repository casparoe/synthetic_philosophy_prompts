A philosopher friend of mine has a standing practice of pronouncing on whether described scenarios are conceivable and, if so, whether they are metaphysically possible. Her verdicts so far:

- A zombie world — physically identical to ours in every detail but lacking consciousness entirely: conceivable, and possible.
- A physically identical world in which red and green experiences are swapped: conceivable, and possible.
- A world containing a clear, drinkable liquid that plays the entire water role but is not H2O: conceivable, but not possible — that stuff would not be water.
- A world in which Hesperus exists but Phosphorus does not: conceivable, but not possible.
- A round square, or a married bachelor: not even conceivable.
- A largest prime number: conceivable at first glance, she says, but not on careful reflection, and not possible.

The new case: a world whose fundamental laws differ from ours — for instance, one in which gravity repels and nothing travels faster than sound — stipulated to be worked out consistently and in full detail. Is this conceivable? Possible?

I suspect her six verdicts are jointly consistent with more than one rule: roughly, that ideal conceivability (the kind that survives careful reflection) guarantees possibility, versus that conceivability settles possibility only for claims about consciousness and is trumped by a posteriori discovery everywhere else. Please work this out. Structure your answer as: "Candidate rules" (two or three rules, each fitting all six verdicts, a few sentences each); "What each rule predicts" for the new case (about 100 words); "My prediction" (which rule she is most likely running, the verdict it yields, and your reasons, about 100 words); and "The telling case" (one further scenario, not yet considered, whose verdict would best distinguish your favored rule from its main rival, about 50 words).
