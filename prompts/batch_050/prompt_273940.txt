Next term I am taking over a colleague's upper-level epistemology seminar, and I have committed to devoting one eighty-minute meeting to Sellars's attack on the myth of the given. The students will be third- and fourth-year philosophy majors: they know the classical empiricists reasonably well, they have some acquaintance with foundationalism as a thesis about justification, and most of them have heard the phrase 'myth of the given' used as a vague term of abuse without any idea what, exactly, is being called a myth. My experience is that this is one of the most frequently mis-taught ideas in the undergraduate curriculum, and I do not want to be responsible for another cohort that thinks Sellars denied the existence of sensations, or that he merely rediscovered the theory-ladenness of observation. Please plan the session for me rather than giving me your own exposition of the material.

What I need is the explanatory architecture. Specify the order in which the components must be introduced and justify the dependencies: my instinct is that the epistemic notion of givenness (a state that justifies beliefs without itself standing in need of justification) has to be cleanly in place before the myth makes sense as a target, and that this in turn requires the students to see the difference between a causal story about perception and a justificatory one — but tell me if you think that is the wrong way to sequence it, and what should come first instead. Then give me the one carrying example around which the whole session should be built. I had considered using a thermometer-style case (a device or creature that reliably registers red without possessing the concept red) to make the contrast between reliable differential response and epistemic uptake vivid, but I am open to a better vehicle; whatever you choose, explain what it must contain and, just as importantly, what it must not contain if it is to isolate the myth rather than a neighboring confusion.

Finally, I want a misconception map with timing: the errors these students will predictably fall into, in the order they are likely to surface, and the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact. I expect at least the following: conflating the rejection of the given with the rejection of non-inferential belief as such; reading Sellars as an eliminativist about sensory experience; assuming the argument is just coherentism warmed over; and the 'what about infants and animals?' objection, which I suspect arises precisely where the causal/justificatory distinction has not yet been made to do work. Tell me also what I should deliberately leave out at this level — I am inclined to omit the positive Sellarsian picture and the later reception in McDowell and Davidson entirely, and to say so explicitly — and close with one question or exercise I can set in the final ten minutes whose successful completion would show that the students have understood what the myth is, rather than merely learned to intone the slogan.
