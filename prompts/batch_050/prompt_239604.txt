I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and a recurring assignment genre of mine is the category dispute: astronomers voting on whether Pluto is a planet, psychiatrists arguing over whether a condition belongs in the diagnostic manual, biologists splitting over whether a specimen constitutes a new species, regulators deciding whether lab-grown tissue may be sold as meat. I write perhaps two such stories a month, usually on a two- or three-day deadline, for readers with no background in cognitive science, and my drafts are reviewed by an editor who is (rightly) suspicious of both "science has settled what X really is" and "it's all just semantics" as story frames.

I would like to turn the main psychological theories of concepts — prototype theory, exemplar models, the theory theory, and the literature on psychological essentialism — into a working procedure I can run on each dispute before I begin reporting. The deliverable should be an ordered sequence of tests or questions, expressed in words rather than formalism, that takes me from a raw dispute to three outputs: first, a diagnosis of which model of concepts best describes what each side in the dispute is actually doing; second, a classification of the dispute as genuinely empirical, purely verbal, or — the case I suspect is most common — a disagreement about which similarities and causal roles matter for some practical purpose; and third, a recommended framing for the story, including the framing "no scientific result settles this," even where that kills the news peg.

The procedure must be runnable in under an hour with no resources beyond the underlying papers or press releases and one expert phone call. Each step should state what to do when the relevant information is unavailable, and you should mark explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure rather than pretending the whole thing is mechanical. It must handle at least the following hard cases: natural kinds with hidden microstructure (species, chemical elements); socially constituted categories (money, citizenship, marriage); systematic divergence between expert and folk usage, as with the tomato being botanically a fruit; categories whose definition, once adopted, changes the world it describes, as psychiatric diagnoses do; and disputes in which both sides agree on every empirical fact yet continue to disagree. Where the academic distinctions resist simplification, tell me which ones must survive translation into newsroom practice and which can be dropped without loss.

Finally, apply the procedure in worked form to three cases: the Pluto vote, a contested psychiatric diagnosis of your choosing, and the lab-grown meat question. One of these applications should land on a conclusion I will not like as a journalist — for instance, that the most newsworthy version of the story is false — and you should say plainly what the honest version of that story is and why it may not be assignable. Close by stating what the procedure cannot do: which editorial failures it will not catch, and where a reporter following it correctly could still produce a misleading piece.
