So I've been going down a rabbit hole about school history curriculums and I keep noticing the same weird pattern. In the US you had the whole fight over the 1619 Project versus the 1776 Commission, where both sides seemed to agree that what matters most is which year gets cast as the nation's founding moment -- slavery's arrival or independence. In Japan the Ministry of Education screens textbooks and there's been decades of argument about how they should word the Nanjing Massacre or the comfort women issue, and Korea and China lodge formal protests when the wording shifts. South Korea's government actually tried to replace privately published history textbooks with a single state-issued one around 2015 and it caused massive protests. Meanwhile nobody has a comparably heated national row about, say, how maths or chemistry gets taught, and in plenty of countries the history syllabus is just set quietly by exam boards without anyone caring much.

Here's my question. What does this whole practice -- governments and publics treating the school history curriculum as a thing worth fighting tooth and nail over -- actually presuppose about what history education is *for*? Like, is the implicit view that history class is primarily a citizen-manufacturing exercise, and the content is contested because different factions want different citizens? Or does it presuppose that there's one true national story and error is dangerous, more like how people treat medical misinformation? Or that children are a kind of collective memory storage and the fight is really over what the nation remembers in fifty years? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) as charitably as you can, point out which rival views of history education the practice quietly rules out (e.g. would a purely skills-based view -- "we teach source criticism, dates don't matter" -- even make sense of anyone's behaviour here?), and say where the practice is underdetermined, i.e. compatible with several theories at once. Also: is the practice internally consistent, or do the same people presuppose different theories when arguing about their own country's textbooks versus other countries'?

After the prose bit, give me a table summarising it with these columns: Controversy | What the fighting treats history class as being for | Rival view it rules out | What would change under that rival view. One row per controversy (US, Japan, Korea at minimum), plus a final row for the quiet exam-board countries as a contrast case.
