So I'm a retired molecular biologist (38 years on protein turnover, if that matters) who picked up philosophy properly about two years ago, and I've been crawling through David Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature (1739). I got to Book I, Part IV, Section VI, "Of personal identity," and I've hit a paragraph I genuinely can't make sense of. Here it is, copied from the online text I've been reading:

"We have a distinct idea of an object, that remains invariable and uninterrupted thro' a suppos'd variation of time; and this idea we call that of identity or sameness. We have also a distinct idea of several different objects existing in succession, and connected together by a close relation; and this to an accurate view affords as perfect a notion of diversity, as if there was no manner of relation among the objects. But tho' these two ideas of identity, and a succession of related objects be in themselves perfectly distinct, and even contrary, yet 'tis certain, that in our common way of thinking they are generally confounded with each other."

A bit earlier he says that to explain personal identity he first has to "account for that identity, which we attribute to plants and animals", which is what caught my eye, because later in the section he works through examples like a ship that's been repaired plank by plank until hardly any original material is left, and everyone still calls it the same ship.

Here's where I'm stuck, in order of how much they bother me:

1. What exactly is the "confounding" he accuses us of? Is Hume saying that when we call the repaired ship the same ship, we've made a straight mistake — we've misapplied the idea of strict numerical identity to what is really just a succession of similar, causally linked ship-stages? Or is he saying something softer, like the word "same" was never tracking strict identity in the first place and there's no error at all, just a habit of the imagination? The word "confounded" sounds accusatory, but he doesn't seem to want us to stop talking this way, and I can't tell what he thinks the correct way to talk would be.

2. The part that really gets under my skin as a scientist: "this to an accurate view affords as perfect a notion of diversity, as if there was no manner of relation among the objects." Does that mean that, strictly speaking, the Tuesday ship and the Thursday ship are just two different objects, full stop, and their resembling each other and being causally connected counts for nothing toward them being one thing? Because in my old field nobody would blink at saying a cell is the same cell while its proteins are constantly degraded and resynthesized — turnover is just what maintenance looks like. I worry Hume is treating "invariable and uninterrupted" as the only legitimate standard and then declaring everything else a fiction, when maybe that standard is the problem.

3. Relatedly: is Hume even trying to answer the question "is it really the same ship?" or is he doing something else entirely — explaining why we can't help asking it, and why we answer it the way we do? If it's the latter, does he think the question has a right answer we're missing, or no right answer at all?

If it helps to anchor this: take the classic version where the old planks are collected and built into a second ship in the next harbour. On Hume's machinery, what happens? Can the imagination ascribe identity to both, to neither, or does his account just shrug, since both have equal claim on resemblance and causal continuity?

Please explain the paragraph itself line by line first (especially "a suppos'd variation of time" — why "suppos'd"?), then answer my questions. And if I've misread him somewhere basic, just tell me — I'd rather be corrected than humored.
