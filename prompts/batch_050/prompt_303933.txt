A book on the economics of climate change that I have been reading summarizes the debate over discounting as follows: 'When economists compare costs incurred today with benefits arriving decades or centuries from now, they discount the future, and the only real question is what rate to use. Discounting reflects a basic fact of human psychology: people are impatient and prefer goods now to goods later, so a benefit delivered in a hundred years is worth less than the same benefit delivered today, and any rational cost-benefit analysis must take that into account. This is the whole story behind the famous split between the Stern Review and William Nordhaus. Stern applied a near-zero discount rate and concluded that drastic emissions cuts are needed immediately; Nordhaus used a rate derived from actual market behavior and concluded that gradual action is optimal. So the climate policy dispute is, at bottom, a dispute about how impatient we should be: choose your discount rate and you have chosen your policy.'

Something about that final sentence bothers me, but I cannot articulate exactly what, and the book is written by a professional economist. How fair is this summary? Please go through its claims one by one: which would a careful economist accept, which are standard simplifications one finds in popular treatments, and which misstate either how discounting works or what the Stern–Nordhaus disagreement was actually about? In particular, I would like to know whether 'impatience' is really the only thing a discount rate can reflect, or whether other considerations (for instance, expectations about how well-off future people will be, or how much additional consumption matters to the better-off) get folded into the same number; whether the difference between Stern's and Nordhaus's policy conclusions comes down to impatience alone; and whether the book's closing claim — that the policy dispute reduces to a choice of discount rate — follows from anything true elsewhere in the summary. Finally, if some part of this debate concerns whether later well-being should count for less merely because it is later, as opposed to discounting for some other reason, please explain what that distinction is, which side Stern and Nordhaus took on it, and why it matters for how one should read the book's framing.
