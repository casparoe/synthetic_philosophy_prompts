I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and lately my beat has been swallowed by AI. My newsroom keeps having the same fight: when is it acceptable to write that a model "knows," "wants," "believes," or "is trying to" do something? My editor thinks such phrasing is irresponsible anthropomorphism and wants a style rule banning it; a colleague on the AI beat thinks the ban would force us into clumsy evasions and that the belief-language is often just the most accurate description available. This has sent me back to the philosophical literature I half-remember -- Dennett's intentional stance, the idea that attributing beliefs and desires is a predictive strategy whose legitimacy depends on whether it works rather than on what's literally inside the system, and the surrounding debate about whether patterns of belief-attribution track something real or merely something useful.

Here's my request, and it's a specific one. I am fluent in the vocabulary of journalistic practice -- sourcing, attribution, on-the-record statements, what a press office will confirm versus what you infer from behavior, the difference between writing "the Fed signaled" and "the Fed believes," the discipline of distinguishing what a source said from what a source meant. Restate the core of the intentional-stance position, and the strongest objection to applying it to AI systems, in *that* vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. For instance: is attributing a belief to a language model more like quoting a source, more like reporting what a source's behavior reveals despite their denials, or more like writing "the market expects a rate cut" -- an institutional attribution nobody thinks requires a mind? Work through the analogy carefully rather than gesturing at it.

Then give me the residue report: which parts of the philosophical debate translate cleanly into newsroom concepts, which parts you had to distort to fit, and which parts have no counterpart at all in the craft vocabulary of reporting. Most important to me: where the translation fails, whose fault is it? Is the residue something journalism's categories genuinely cannot capture -- a real question about minds that no amount of sourcing discipline settles -- or is it a sign that philosophers are arguing about something less substantive than it appears? I'd like to end up knowing whether my editor and my colleague are actually disagreeing about a factual matter, a pragmatic matter, or a matter that only looks deep because neither of them has the right vocabulary.
