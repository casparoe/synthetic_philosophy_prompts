I'm giving a short talk at a grad workshop on the pragmatists next month. My claim: Peirce's pragmatic maxim in "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" is not a theory of meaning and not proto-verificationism, the way it usually gets read, but a methodological rule about inquiry — roughly, don't take a dispute to be real unless settling it could change what you'd do or expect. The audience is mostly historians of early analytic philosophy (who will want textual fidelity to the 1878 paper and will pounce on anachronism), plus one James scholar who thinks James got the maxim right and that Peirce's later complaints about James were just Peirce being a crank, plus a few people working on verificationism who'll want to know how my reading differs from the Vienna Circle's.

Draft my prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do, phrased the way this specific crowd would put them (so the historians' versions, not generic "isn't this just positivism" ones). For each: what I should concede, what I hold, and a two-minute reply. Include how I handle the obvious counterexamples — disputes that seem meaningful but have no conceivable practical upshot, like whether the universe would be any different if some unobservable thing were otherwise. And flag the question I'm praying nobody asks.
