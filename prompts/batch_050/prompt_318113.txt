I'm applying for a one-year graduate fellowship and need a two-page research proposal. My project concerns John Stuart Mill's late engagement with socialism, above all the posthumously published Chapters on Socialism alongside the revised chapters on property in the later editions of the Principles of Political Economy.

My half-formed idea: the standard reading of these texts is that Mill was torn — sympathetic to socialist criticism of existing property arrangements and the wage relation, but unwilling to endorse any socialist program, and so he hedged by deferring judgment to future experience. Commentators tend to treat this as indecision, or as a temperamental split between Mill the liberal and Mill the radical. My suspicion is that the apparent hedge is actually a substantive position in its own right: Mill held that the choice between individualist and socialist organization of production could not be settled by a priori argument because it depends on facts about human character that were not yet in, and he thought the right institutional response to that kind of ignorance was neither laissez-faire nor planning but a plurality of competing experiments — worker cooperatives, peasant proprietorship, joint-stock arrangements — allowed to prove themselves against one another. If that's right, Mill's late position anticipates a distinctive epistemic argument for institutional pluralism, and the interesting questions are whether that argument is coherent (can a "wait for the experiments" stance avoid collapsing into a default endorsement of the status quo?), and whether it survives the objection that experiments in ownership are never run on a level playing field.
