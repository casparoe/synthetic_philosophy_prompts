I'm a novelist and my book in progress is about a woman deciding whether to have a kid, and the whole structure leans on that idea (L.A. Paul's, I think?) that some experiences are transformative in a way that breaks rational choice -- you can't weigh the outcome beforehand because the person who'd do the weighing afterwards doesn't exist yet, and the values you'd use to judge it are exactly the thing that gets replaced. I want a sonnet to use as the epigraph for part two, which opens right after she's gone through with it. The octave should sit inside the before: her trying to imagine her future self like peering through a keyhole, projecting her current wants onto someone who won't share them. The volta should be the transformation itself, the moment the old standpoint stops being available -- not fading gradually but closing like a door. Then the couplet lands on the uncomfortable bit: the choice couldn't have been made for good reasons, and somehow that doesn't make it a mistake. No philosophy jargon, it should read as a poem first, but someone who knows the arguement should be able to recognise its skeleton in the form.
