You're a philosopher of mind/perception who has also spent time around type designers, so you know both sides of this. Think it through carefully, and start by brainstorming a long list of candidate requirements before trimming it down.

Here's my question: what would a theory of letter identity actually have to do to be adequate? I.e. a theory of what makes every 'a' an 'a' -- the same letter across Times, Helvetica, my terrible handwriting, a neon sign, and a child's first attempt -- while 'a' and 'd', or 'b' and its mirror image, stay different letters. I want the desiderata list before I read any actual proposals. For each requirement give me the case or phenomenon that motivates it, and mark which ones are genuinely non-negotiable vs. which are just preferences of one camp (gestalt/topology people vs. feature-detection people vs. whoever). Some things I'd expect to show up, but correct me if any of these turn out to be bad requirements: it has to explain why single-storey and double-storey 'a' count as the same letter while 'n' and 'h' don't; it has to handle the fact that no fixed set of geometric features survives across all fonts and handwritings; it has to say something about whether mirror-reversal breaks identity ('b'/'d') even though mirror images are topologically identical; it has to deal with role-playing -- a letter can be built out of other objects (a swan as an 'S', people forming a 'Y') and still count; and it has to explain why a font made only of letters squeezed onto a uniform grid (like Hofstadter's gridfonts) still reads, barely, as those letters. Then tell me: which single requirement do you think knocks out the most naive theories, and is there any pair of desiderata on the list that you suspect can't be jointly satisfied? That's the part I care most about. No reading list needed, just the spec.
