After forty years in comparative physiology I have, in retirement, begun reading philosophy in earnest, and I have lately been working through Descartes' arguments that animals are automata. Whatever one thinks of the doctrine itself, reading it has alerted me to a habit of thought — in my former colleagues, in science journalism, and, I confess, in myself — for which I cannot find a name, and I should like to know whether one exists.

The habit is this: the very same form of inference is held to entirely different evidential standards according to which creature it concerns. When my granddaughter moves her biscuit tin and explains that her brother knows where she keeps it, no one demands that simpler explanations be excluded first. When a scrub-jay re-caches its food only on occasions when it was watched during the original caching, the reviewers insist that every associative alternative be eliminated before the animal may be said to know anything. When a neighbour's dog mopes by the door after its owner's death, dinner guests remark that this is mere disruption of routine; nobody would dream of saying the same of the widower next door, though the behavioural evidence is of much the same kind. In short, the inference from "behaves as if it knew or felt such-and-such" to "knows or feels such-and-such" is treated as innocent until proven guilty in human beings and guilty until proven innocent in every other animal.

Allow me to say what I am not asking about. I am aware that comparative psychology possesses a methodological maxim directing researchers to prefer explanations at the lower psychological level; but that is a rule of professional method, whereas the asymmetry I describe operates in people who have never heard of any such rule. Nor is the thing I mean ordinary scepticism about other minds, for the sceptic at least applies his doubts evenly, to his wife as well as to his spaniel. Nor is it simply over-attribution or under-attribution of mental states: those are the two poles between which the habit oscillates, whereas what interests me is the differential burden of proof itself.

So my questions are three. Does this asymmetric evidential policy have an established name? If not, which established concepts come nearest, how does the phenomenon I have described differ from each, and what would you call it — with a definition precise enough to mark its boundary cases, for a bare label will not satisfy me. And thirdly, returning to what set me off: is Descartes' own position an instance of it? He at least offers positive arguments, concerning the absence of language and the universality of reason, rather than merely shifting the burden of proof, which leaves me uncertain whether he belongs inside the phenomenon or beside it.

Please write your answer in British English.
