I chair the flood-risk panel for a mid-sized coastal city, and we set the minimum elevation requirements for new construction. Our two staff hydrologists — equally credentialed, both around 15 years in, no known track record differences — disagree about the projected 100-year flood level for the harbor district: one says 3.1m, the other 3.7m. The kicker is that this isn't a fresh disagreement. They've already exchanged all their data and models, walked each other through their reasoning line by line, met for hours over several weeks, and each still thinks their own estimate is right. One of them even nudged her estimate toward the other's after the first exchange and then moved back.

Draft a policy memo for the panel recommending what we should do: adopt one estimate, average the two, build to the range, or something else. The memo has to engage the underlying question of what rationality requires when epistemic peers disagree even after full information sharing — specifically whether a disagreement that survives seeing the other side's reasoning and evidence is a different beast from a fresh one, and whether the panel should treat its own experts' continued confidence as evidence in itself. Two wrinkles: the costs are asymmetric (underestimate and people drown, overestimate and we kill roughly $40M of development and half the council's political careers), and one panel member argues that asymmetry should settle the matter regardless of the epistemics — deal with that argument honestly. Also, whatever we do here sets a precedent, so give the panel a general rule for handling expert disagreement, not just a verdict for this one case.

Lead with the bottom-line recommendation in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, no restating the setup.
