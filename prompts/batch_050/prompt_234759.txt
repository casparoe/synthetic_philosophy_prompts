So I teach an upper-level seminar on normative ethics and every year we spend a week on aggregation problems -- Parfit, the repugnant conclusion, and that whole cluster -- and every year the torture-vs-dust-specks case is the thing students can't let go of. Fifty years of agony for one person versus a dust speck in the eye of some astronomically huge number of people, and the question of whether there's any number big enough to tip the scales. What always happens is half the room digs in on "tiny harms can't sum to a real one" and the other half insists they're just bad at intuiting big numbers, and nobody moves.

For the last day of class I want a poem to read aloud instead of my usual summing-up lecture, and I want the form to do the philosophical work rather than just decorate it. Here's my idea, though you can adjust if you see a better structure: the poem alternates two kinds of stanzas. The odd stanzas are the torture -- maybe literally the same stanza, repeated verbatim each time, because fifty years is fifty years, it doesn't grow or get worse, it just is, and I want the reader to feel the flatness of that. The even stanzas are the specks, and they should multiply: first one speck, described precisely (a mote, an involuntary blink, gone before you can complain), then ten, then a hundred, then a number that's too big to picture, each stanza somehow longer or more crowded than the last. The whole thing should end on a stanza where the two finally share the page -- but no verdict. The poem should refuse to say which is worse, and the refusal should feel earned, not coy.

Two hard constraints. First, no cheating on the specks: the moment a stanza smuggles in something worse than a dust speck (a headache, a stubbed toe, a bad day) the thought experiment is broken and a sharp student will call it out, so the specks have to stay genuinely negligible no matter how many there are. Second, don't make the torture lyrical or redemptive either -- it should be as monotonous in the poem as it would be in life. Somewhere in the 20-30 line range total. If a student who missed the week on aggregation reads it, they should still get a good poem; if someone who's read the literature reads it, they should be able to point at exactly where the "just add more specks" intuition starts to strain.
