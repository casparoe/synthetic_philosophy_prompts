I'm in 11th grade and we're doing a unit on classical Chinese philosophy in my history elective, and at lunch today my friends Priya and Jonah got into this whole argument about Mozi and Confucius that neither of them would drop. Quick background: Mozi argued for "impartial care" (jian ai) where you care about everyone equally, while Confucians thought love should be graded, strongest for family and thinning out toward strangers. Our teacher had handed out a chunk of Mozi's "Universal Love II" (in the W.P. Mei translation), and the part that started everything was this: "Whoever loves others is loved by others; whoever benefits others is benefited by others; whoever hates others is hated by others; whoever injures others is injured by others."

Priya's take was that this passage gives the game away. If Mozi has to sell impartial care by promising you'll get loved back, then the whole thing is really just self-interest with extra steps, and Confucius wins by default because graded love at least admits what people actually feel instead of dressing up a bargain as morality. Jonah said that's a total misreading of what the passage is even doing -- it comes right after an objector says impartial care is a nice idea but too hard to actually practice, so Mozi is answering the practicability challenge, not stating the foundation of the view. The real argument, Jonah said, is earlier: partiality is what causes states attacking states and families cheating families, so the doctrine stands or falls on that, not on reciprocity. Priya fired back that even if it's just a reply to an objection, the reply only works if people are motivated by payback, and Mozi's other examples in the same chapter are all about rulers getting people to do insane stuff (like starving themselves skinny) because the ruler encouraged it -- so Mozi's system seems to run on incentives and top-down pressure, not actual care, which makes it phonier than Confucianism, not less. Jonah's last move was that every moral view needs a story about motivation, Confucianism included (that's basically what ritual training is), and that graded love can't even explain what's *wrong* with nepotism -- if you cover for your dad when he does something shady, the Confucian seems to say that's just loyalty, whereas Mozi can at least say why that's corrupt.
