ok so i cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and my current long-term assignment is a big piece on what the present generation owes future ones -- climate mostly, but also nuclear waste and crumbling infrastructure. The reporting keeps funneling me back to Parfit and the nonidentity problem (i finally read Reasons and Persons over the holidays, big mistake for my sleep schedule), and before I put anything in print I want to audit my own positions, because my editor pokes holes for a living. Here's roughly where I've landed: (1) In the depletion-vs-risky-policy case, choosing the risky policy is straightforwardly wrong -- not some lesser category of wrong, either; it's as bad as if we'd made the very same people worse off. The fact that different people exist under each policy is no excuse. (2) In general I'm anti-utilitarian because morality strikes me as being about what we do to people: an act can't be wrong unless there's someone it's bad for. That's my guardrail against impersonal spreadsheet ethics. (3) Largely because of (1), I think our current emissions path wrongs future generations even under the scenarios where different policies would have led to different people being concieved. (4) I do think existence itself can harm someone: a child born with a condition so severe that their life is nothing but pain is wronged by being brought into being -- it's bad for them, not just impersonally bad. (5) At the same time there's zero moral pressure to procreate: a couple that decides not to have a child who would have had a wonderful life has done nothing wrong, and nobody loses out. (6) And I reject the repugnant conclusion outright -- any theory that ranks a gigantic population of lives barely worth living above a smaller flourishing society is disqualified, whatever the arithmetic says.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture: which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only look like they conflict, and for each real tension what would actually have to give. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging them all as equally bad, and for the worst one, tell me the cheapest revision and what it costs me elsewhere in the list -- since if I have to give something up I'd like to survey the damage before my editor does.
