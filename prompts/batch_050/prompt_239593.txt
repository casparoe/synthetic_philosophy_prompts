My friend and I got into a genuinely heated argument last night about when a legend turns into history. It started with whether the Trojan War "really happened" and three hours later we were somehow yelling about her own grandmother's war stories, so clearly we were not converging. I want to figure out what I actually believe by going case by case, and I need someone to find the principle underneath my gut reactions, if there even is one.

Here are my verdicts:

1. Trojan War: probably a real conflict at Hisarlik that got passed down orally, but the Iliad version is poetry built on top of it. So the war counts as history; Achilles doesn't.
2. King Arthur: nope. Even if some Romano-British warlord existed, the Arthur people mean (knights, round table, Camelot) is fiction. Not history.
3. The Exodus: I wobble here, but I lean toward "something like it" — a small-scale sojourn-and-escape memory — counting as historical, even though the evidence is about as thin as Arthur's. I know that's possibly inconsistent with #2. That's part of why I'm asking.
4. Romulus and Remus: pure legend, obviously, even though Rome clearly got founded by somebody.
5. The flood in Gilgamesh/Atrahasis: I want to say there's a historical kernel (some catastrophic regional flood) but the story is legend. Which has the same structure as Troy, but I rate it lower, and I can't say why.
6. My own great-grandfather bribing a guard to escape in 1943, per family oral tradition, zero documents: I treat this as history, full stop. But by my Arthur logic it shouldn't count, and that bugs me.
7. The Sea Peoples in Egyptian inscriptions plus matching destruction layers: clearly history, no problem there.
8. A town that invented a founding hero in the 1800s to attract tourists, which everyone there now sincerely believes: legend, obviously.

Is there a principle that captures most of these? My friend's was basically "a tradition becomes history once at least one specific, checkable claim in it survives external corroboration." That handles Troy and the Sea Peoples fine, but it kills my great-grandfather, and when I pointed out it would also make the tourist-hero count as "history" if the town's minutes confirm a founding committee met, she got flustered and we ordered more fries. So I'm open to her principle being repaired, replaced, or me being told which of my verdicts have to go. If no single principle works, give me the two or three strongest candidates and be honest about which of my verdicts each one sacrifices.
