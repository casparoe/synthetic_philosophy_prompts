In six weeks I am giving a forty-minute paper at the spring colloquium of a colonial Latin American studies institute, and I want the question period mapped in advance. The paper defends a reading of Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz's Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz, the 1691 letter she addressed to the Bishop of Puebla after he had printed her theological letter without her knowledge and, writing under the female pseudonym Sor Filotea, publicly admonished her to leave profane learning for sacred. The standing dispute about the Respuesta is whether its defense of women's learning amounts to a proto-feminist brief or to a baroque exercise in self-exculpation. I argue that both readings mislocate the argument, which is announced in the prologue, at the point where she pauses over her own near-decision not to answer at all:

"Perdonad, Señora mía, la digresión que me arrebató la fuerza de la verdad; y si la he de confesar toda, también es buscar efugios para huir la dificultad de responder, y casi me he determinado a dejarlo al silencio; pero como éste es cosa negativa, aunque explica mucho con el énfasis de no explicar, es necesario ponerle algún breve rótulo para que se entienda lo que se pretende que el silencio diga; y si no, dirá nada el silencio, porque ése es su propio oficio: decir nada."

and, a few lines later:

"de manera que aquellas cosas que no se pueden decir, es menester decir siquiera que no se pueden decir, para que se entienda que el callar no es no haber qué decir, sino no caber en las voces lo mucho que hay que decir."

My thesis, in one sentence: the defense of women's education in the body of the letter is an exercise of the device theorized here — labeled silence. Sor Juana concedes the public prohibition on women teaching and preaching and defends only private study, and that narrowing is the argument rather than a retreat from one: a silence that is labeled speaks, and a concession that is labeled argues. My three supporting moves are (i) the prologue explicitly theorizes strategic silence as something that must be marked if it is to mean; (ii) her acceptance of the Pauline ban on women teaching publicly, set against her insistence that study harms no one, enacts precisely that marking; and (iii) her long catalog of learned women — biblical, pagan, and patristic — functions as a rótulo that lets the general claim about women's capacity be understood without ever being asserted in her own voice.

The audience: two senior Sor Juana specialists, one a literary scholar committed to the proto-feminist reading who treats every concession in the letter as tactical irony, the other a historicist of Golden Age rhetoric who will say my prologue passage is the standard inexpressibility topos and nothing more; a historian of the seventeenth-century Mexican church whose real interest is censorship and ecclesiastical pressure, and who will bring up the biographical sequel — that within a few years she had stopped writing and disposed of her library; a visiting philosopher of education who will ask what any of this has to do with education as opposed to private erudition; and a room of graduate students who have read the letter once, in translation.

Prepare my Q&A sheet. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will actually come from this audience, each phrased the way the person most likely to raise it would phrase it, and for each: what I should concede outright, what I should hold, a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, and where that reply is soft. Then tell me which point in my own reading is the weakest and how I should defend it rather than hope it goes unnoticed, and identify the question I most hope goes unasked.
