I serve on the five-member Gaming Commission of a mid-sized American state, which licenses all charitable gaming within its borders. Next month we vote on an application from a consortium of hospitals to run a fundraiser they call the Doubling Draw. The rules are simple: after you buy a ticket, the operator flips a certified fair coin until it first comes up tails; if it comes up heads n times in a row before the first tails, you win 2^n dollars, so one head pays $2, two heads pays $4, three heads pays $8, and so on without any stated upper limit. The consortium proposes a ticket price of $30, has pledged its entire $50 million endowment as the payout reserve, and stipulates that if a winning run would exceed the remaining reserve, the player receives whatever reserve remains and the game closes permanently.

The staff analysis has split the commission. Our chief actuary points out that the game's mathematical expectation is divergent: each outcome 2^n occurs with probability 2^-(n+1), so every term in the expected payout contributes the same fixed amount, and the sum has no finite bound. On her view, a $30 ticket is, in strict expected-value terms, an arbitrarily good bargain for the buyer, and licensing the game at that price means licensing a slow transfer of wealth from the hospitals to the players; she recommends denial on the ground that no finite reserve can honestly back a game whose expected liability is unbounded. Our general counsel draws the opposite conclusion from what strikes me as the same mathematical fact: the probability of winning more than $1,000 is barely one in a thousand, the capped-and-reserve-limited structure truncates the game in practice, and no sensible person would pay anywhere near $30 for this ticket if the expectation argument were not there to dazzle them. She also recommends denial, but on consumer-protection grounds: the game is a gimmick that will either fail to sell or quietly exploit buyers' confusion about infinity.

Both of my colleagues seem to me to be leaning on a single piece of mathematics to reach their conclusions, and I am not persuaded that either has identified what a regulator should actually care about here.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the vote. It should:

- State the bottom line up front in one or two sentences: license, deny, or license with specified conditions.
- Work out what the game is actually worth to a buyer given the finite reserve. Because the payout structure is effectively truncated once the reserve is exhausted, compute or carefully estimate the truncated expected payout under stated assumptions, and say whether the $30 price sits above or below it.
- Address whether expected value is even the right criterion for a one-shot, heavily skewed gamble of this kind -- first from the buyer's side, then separately from the operator's side, where a single long run of heads early in the game's life could wipe out the endowment.
- Give the strongest available version of the case for the outcome you reject, in terms my colleagues could actually engage with at the meeting rather than a strawman.
- Specify any conditions your recommendation depends on -- a hard per-prize cap set at a particular level, a revised ticket price, a per-player purchase limit, a reinsurance or bonding requirement -- with brief justification for each.

The memo should be written in American English, in a formal register appropriate for a regulatory body, and run roughly 800 to 1,200 words.
