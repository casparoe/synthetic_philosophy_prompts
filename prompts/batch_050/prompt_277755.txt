I was discussing population ethics with some friends from my local EA reading group last weekend, and we got stuck on a position I've seen defended in rationalist-adjacent circles: tranquilism, the view that the only thing that matters morally is the absence of suffering and craving. On this view, pleasurable experiences have no value of their own beyond the relief or absence of craving they involve -- a perfectly content, untroubled state of mind is exactly as good as the most intense bliss, and adding a blissful being to the world is no better than adding a permanently contented one (or no being at all). My friends kept attacking a cartoon version of it ("so you'd press a button that replaces everyone's joy with numbness?"), and I suspect the strongest version of the view survives most of these objections, though I'm not sure it survives all of them.

Write a dialogue between two equally sharp philosophers hashing this out: a tranquilist who holds that value consists solely in the absence of suffering and craving, and a classical total-view hedonistic utilitarian who holds that positive experiences contribute value in their own right. They should not talk past each other. The tranquilist should have to answer the charge that their view is just a dressed-up preference for the void, and the utilitarian should have to answer the charge that "positive" pleasure is phenomenologically nothing over and above the relief or absence of craving -- that what we call joy is what craving-satisfaction feels like from the inside. Have them push on each other's actual arguments: the utilitarian might press on whether the tranquilist must be indifferent between a universe of perfect contentment and one of maximal ecstasy; the tranquilist might press on whether the utilitarian must prefer a barely-worth-living life with tiny net pleasure over a life of serene, cravingless contentment, and whether that's a bullet or an embarrassment.

The dialogue should run long enough for each side to concede at least one genuine point and to locate the crux of the disagreement somewhere more interesting than "you're wrong about the phenomenology" -- ideally they end up disagreeing about something specific and load-bearing that the reader can then examine for themselves. Both characters should argue in good faith throughout; no one gets strawmanned, and no one wins by definition.
