You are a philosopher of mathematics and physics who has spent years teaching the applicability-of-mathematics problem to mixed audiences of scientists and humanists. I need your help dissecting a slogan, and I want the dissection done with real care. Before composing your final answer, brainstorm privately: generate as many distinct readings of the slogan as you can, including uncharitable and eccentric ones, then prune, merge, and organize them. Only after that should you write the structured response described below.

Some context. At a conference dinner last month, a colleague in fundamental physics was defending his research program, which is built on notably elegant mathematics and has so far produced no confirmed empirical prediction. When I pressed him, he reached for the slogan made famous by Eugene Wigner's 1960 essay: "the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences." His argument ran: mathematics is unreasonably effective in physics; therefore mathematical coherence is itself evidence of physical truth; therefore the absence of experimental tests is less damning than I claimed. When I asked what "unreasonable effectiveness" actually amounts to, he dismissed the question as pedantic. I do not think it is pedantic, because his conclusion only follows if the slogan means something quite specific, and I doubt it can mean that.

Please do the following:

1. Present, as a menu, the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, ordered from near-trivial to radical. At minimum, distinguish among: claims about mathematics' role in merely formulating physical laws; claims about its delivering novel predictions nobody put in; claims about the same abstract structures recurring across apparently unconnected domains; and claims about what any of this shows concerning whether mathematics is discovered or invented.

2. For each thesis: state it precisely enough that it could be argued for or against; classify it as platitude, substantive-but-defensible, or implausible; and give the strongest consideration on each side.

3. Subject each thesis to two diagnostics. First, the patchiness diagnostic: mathematics is conspicuously less effective in economics, say, or in the study of turbulence, than in particle physics. Does that asymmetry count against the thesis, support it (by making effectiveness look contingent and hence in need of explanation), or leave it untouched? Second, the counterfactual diagnostic: imagine a universe in which every phenomenon required bespoke, disconnected mathematics, no structure ever recurring twice. Could the thesis even be stated there, and would its truth-value there differ from its truth-value here? Explain what this shows about what the thesis is really claiming.

4. Identify which reading my colleague most likely intends, and, more importantly, which reading, if any, would actually underwrite his inference from mathematical coherence to physical truth in the absence of experiment. If no defensible reading underwrites it, say so plainly and locate the exact point of slippage in the inference.

5. Close with a verdict: on its best reading, is the slogan a datum crying out for explanation, an explanation masquerading as a datum, or a rhetorical flourish?

Reason from first principles and your own understanding. Do not recommend readings, cite the secondary literature, or hedge by listing what others have said.
