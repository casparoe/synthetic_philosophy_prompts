I serve on the doctrinal review committee of an independent graduate seminary. The faculty appointments council has asked us to decide whether a candidate’s appointment in philosophical theology may proceed under our statement of faith. The statement says, in relevant part, that God’s knowledge of every actual and counterfactual free choice is exhaustive and infallible; that the divine decree does not causally necessitate any creaturely choice; and that no truth about a creature’s free action has its ultimate ground in a creature existing independently of God.

The candidate endorses what she calls plan-relative middle knowledge. She explains standard Molinism as the view that, explanatorily prior to God’s creative decree, God knows what each possible creature would freely do in each complete set of circumstances and uses that knowledge in choosing which world to actualize. She accepts that framework but rejects one element of the standard view. On her account, prior to the decree there are no unconditional truths of the form “If C were placed in circumstances S, C would freely choose A.” There are only plan-indexed conditional truths: within plan P1, C in S chooses A; within plan P2, C in S chooses B. God’s decree selects a plan, and only thereby makes one set of these counterfactuals true without qualification. She argues that this does not determine C, because the decree establishes the relevant counterfactual and the circumstances but is not a cause operating on C’s will. She also argues that it does not ground the truth in a creature, since every candidate plan exists only as content in God’s intellect.

The committee is divided. Some members believe the candidate’s view is no longer middle knowledge: if the counterfactual becomes true because of the decree, God cannot consult it in deciding which decree to issue, and the appeal to middle knowledge becomes circular. They also worry that the choice is infallibly fixed by a prior divine state even if it is not causally necessitated. Other members believe the doctrinal statement rules out only causal necessitation and creaturely independence, both of which the candidate expressly denies. They argue that rejecting her would turn a disputed theory of truthmaking into a test of orthodoxy. They also note that standard Molinism faces its own grounding problem, since creatures do not exist prior to the decree to make would-counterfactuals true.

Draft the committee’s written opinion. Recommend one of three outcomes: clear the appointment, clear it subject to a narrowly worded clarification, or withhold clearance. The opinion should distinguish the question of whether the view is philosophically defensible from the question of whether it violates the seminary’s doctrinal language; analyze explanatory circularity, truthmaking, and the relation between infallible determination and causal necessitation; address the strongest argument against your recommendation; and, if you recommend a clarification, supply wording the appointments council could actually adopt. The bottom line should be actionable rather than a request for further study.
