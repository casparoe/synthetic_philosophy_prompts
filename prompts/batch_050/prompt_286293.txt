Ok so I ran into this argument in a forum thread about AI safety and it's been bugging me for like a week, because the conclusion seems obviously too strong but every step looks fine when I stare at it. Laid out:

(1) A rational agent should only delegate decisions to a reasoner whose outputs it has positive grounds to trust. You don't get to trust a source just because you built it or because it would be convenient if it were right.

(2) For a formal reasoning agent -- one that works in some formal system S powerful enough to do arithmetic -- "positive grounds to trust" means: a proof, in S, that the other reasoner is sound, i.e. only derives true things. Anything weaker (it passed my tests, it agrees with me a lot) isn't trust, it's hope.

(3) Say agent A, working in S, wants to build a successor B that reasons in S, or in any extension of S. If A could prove in S that B is sound, then S would prove that S itself only derives true things, since B's system contains S. But by Löb's theorem / Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, no consistent S strong enough for arithmetic can prove its own soundness or consistency. So A can't prove B sound.

(4) So by (1) and (2), A cannot rationally adopt B's conclusions. A might build B but can't trust anything B says.

(5) But self-improvement that you can't trust isn't self-improvement -- a successor whose outputs you ignore is just an expensive paperweight. Genuine self-improvement requires adopting the successor's conclusions.

(6) Therefore no sufficiently powerful rational agent can ever genuinely self-improve.

(7) But this proves way too much. Humans do this constantly: I trust the calculator I built (well, bought) to out-reason me on arithmetic, I trust future-me after I've studied a topic more, whole scientific institutions are basically trust-in-successor-minds machines. And we can clearly *conceive* of an ideal rational agent that gets smarter over time -- it's not like the concept is contradictory the way a square circle is.

So something between (1) and (6) has to give, and I genuinely can't tell which. My instinct is that the slip is somewhere around (2)-(3), like maybe the kind of trust involved in "I checked this successor's design" isn't the same as "S proves S sound," but I can't make that precise. If I had to keep one thing it'd be (1), since without it rationality seems to just mean vibes.
