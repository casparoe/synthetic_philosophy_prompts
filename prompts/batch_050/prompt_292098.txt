I'm taking an undergraduate seminar on the epistemology of rational belief, and after last week's meeting two classmates got into an argument that I was asked to referee. I couldn't settle it, so I'd like a careful adjudication.

Background, briefly. The lottery paradox: in a fair 1000-ticket lottery with exactly one winner, it seems rational to believe of each ticket that it will lose, yet believing this of every ticket contradicts something you know — that some ticket wins. The preface paradox: an author who has carefully checked each claim in her book finds it rational to believe each one, while also rationally believing, on grounds of general human fallibility, that at least one claim in the book is false.

Leo's position: the two paradoxes are one problem. Both are generated by believing propositions that are individually highly probable but jointly inconsistent with something else you accept, so any adequate solution must treat them identically. His proposal: rational belief is not closed under conjunction — you may rationally believe each conjunct without rationally believing the conjunction — and this dissolves both paradoxes at once.

Mara's position: the paradoxes differ in structure, and the demand for a common solution is misguided. In the lottery, each belief rests on purely statistical evidence — the ticket's number is its only relevant feature — so the right lesson is that purely statistical grounds never warrant outright belief, only high credence. In the preface, the author has direct, case-specific evidence for each claim, and her modesty belief comes from an inductive track record; the right lesson there is different — that rational belief can tolerate inconsistency — so no closure principle needs to be sacrificed.

Leo's reply: "purely statistical" won't bear the weight. One can build intermediate cases — a lottery where you also have a little testimonial evidence about each ticket, a book whose claims the author has checked only carelessly — and any principle that resolves the two original cases differently will draw an arbitrary line through that spectrum. One phenomenon, one solution.

Mara's reply: the real disanalogy is not about evidence but about what is guaranteed. In the lottery you know in advance that your set of beliefs is inconsistent, because exactly one ticket must win. The author merely believes it likely that her book contains an error; a flawless book is compatible with everything she knows. So the lottery forces inconsistency with knowledge while the preface does not, and Leo's denial of closure is overkill for a case that never demanded it.

Leo's last move: the disanalogy is removable. Stipulate that the author knows — a reliable fact-checker has proved that exactly one of her claims is false. Now the preface case has the lottery's structure, which shows the modesty-based diagnosis was never essential.

Mara's last move: then it is no longer the preface paradox. The original puzzle is precisely about how ordinary fallibility can rationally coexist with confidence; the fact that you can engineer a stripped-down variant that mimics the lottery shows nothing about the original.

Please adjudicate. Who is right about which specific point — the unity of the paradoxes, the adequacy of denying conjunction closure, the statistical-evidence diagnosis, and whether the engineered preface case decides anything? What, if anything, are both missing? Say who is more right overall, and why. Keep the whole reply under 500 words.
