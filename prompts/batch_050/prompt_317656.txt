I run a small reading group on early modern science and theology, and for our session on the Galileo affair I want a decision tree that sorts the positions people actually take on what to do when Scripture and natural philosophy appear to conflict. You are a historian-philosopher of early modern science with long experience teaching this material to mixed audiences; please work carefully and precisely, and do not reach for the standard textbook summary of the affair.

The session turns on a passage from Galileo's Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina (1615). Early in the letter Galileo complains that his opponents "made the grave mistake of sprinkling these with passages taken from places in the Bible which they had failed to understand properly, and which were ill suited to their purposes," and he supports the charge with a doctrine he attributes to St. Augustine: "Now keeping always our respect for moderation in grave piety, we ought not to believe anything inadvisedly on a dubious point, lest in favor to our error we conceive a prejudice against something that truth hereafter may reveal to be not contrary in any way to the sacred books of either the Old or the New Testament." (The passage does not count toward the length guidance below.)

Please do the following, in this order:

1. Before drafting anything, briefly brainstorm (a short paragraph or list, then set it aside) the distinct yes/no questions on which participants in the 1615–1616 controversy actually diverged: whether scriptural passages that touch on natural phenomena are intended to teach astronomy at all, whether speaking according to ordinary appearances is a defect or a deliberate accommodation, what happens when a conclusion of natural philosophy is claimed to be demonstrated rather than merely probable, and who is entitled to judge whether such a demonstration is conclusive.

2. Write the decision tree. It should begin from a shared starting commitment that virtually everyone in the debate accepts — that Scripture cannot teach falsehood — and lead through your branch questions to six to nine leaves. Each branch node must be phrased as a question a reader could answer without already knowing which position it leads to; no node may mention an author's name or a label. The leaves must include, among others: the position the quoted passage itself articulates; Bellarmine's conditional view (reinterpret Scripture only if a genuine demonstration of the contrary is produced, and treat heliocentrism as a mere calculating device until then); the straightforward biblicist literalism of Galileo's clerical opponents; the instrumentalist position that astronomy saves appearances and says nothing about the real constitution of the heavens; and the double-truth or strict separation position that theology and natural philosophy simply speak to different subject matters. Where two of your leaves differ only over a fact (for instance, over whether a demonstration of the earth's motion actually existed in 1615) rather than over a principle, say so explicitly and explain whether your tree can separate them at all.

3. For each leaf, state in one or two sentences what that position is committed to regarding the quoted passage — does it accept the Augustinian principle Galileo invokes, reject it, or accept it while denying that it applies to the case at hand? This is the part I care most about: I want readers to discover that several of the positions can endorse the same principle and still land in different places.

4. Note any combination of answers your branch questions permit that corresponds to no position anyone in the debate (or in later apologetics) actually held, and say briefly whether you think that slot is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Aim for roughly 900–1,200 words total, excluding the quoted passage and the brainstorming. Number the branch questions so I can refer to them when I turn the tree into a handout.
