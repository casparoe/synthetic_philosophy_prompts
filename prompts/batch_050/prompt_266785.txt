I am a third-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of language module, and I am drafting an essay in defence of a Fregean solution to the puzzle of informative identity statements. My supervisor has told me the essay will only be any good if I can state what my position actually risks, so I am trying to map its exposure before I write.

My position is roughly this: the difference in cognitive significance between a true identity of the form a = a and one of the form a = b is fully explained by a difference in the senses of the signs flanking the identity sign, and no rival apparatus (metalinguistic accounts, Millian direct reference, two-dimensionalist reworkings, and so on) is needed to do that explanatory job. Sense, I want to say, is both necessary and sufficient for the solution.

My essay hinges on a passage from Gottlob Frege's 'On Sense and Reference' (1892) that my cohort discussed last week and that I find genuinely puzzling:

"The sense of a proper name is grasped by everybody who is sufficiently familiar with the language or totality of designations to which it belongs; but this serves to illuminate only a single aspect of the reference, supposing it to have one. Comprehensive knowledge of the reference would require us to say immediately whether any given sense belongs to it. To such knowledge we never attain."

Two worries follow from this passage for me. First, if grasping a sense only ever illuminates a single aspect of the reference, and we never attain comprehensive knowledge of the reference, does Frege quietly concede something that opponents can exploit — for instance, that sense cannot after all do the work of fixing which object is in question, or that the notion of 'grasping' a sense is doing unexplained explanatory labour? Secondly, if two competent speakers can both count as grasping the sense of a name while associating different aspects with it, is the notion of sense still determinate enough to explain why an identity is informative for one speaker and trivial for another?

What I want from you is an assessment of what would count against my position, organised by how decisive it would be. Please cover: which arguments or cases (historical or hypothetical) would show the sense-based solution fails, as opposed to merely inconveniencing it; which of these pressures a sophisticated Fregean would themselves concede as real threats rather than dismiss; whether any of the standard objections (that senses are elusive and lack clear identity conditions, that competent speakers can be ignorant or in error about the properties of what they name, that direct-reference theories explain informativeness otherwise, that empty names strain the framework) actually bite on the position as I have stated it; whether my reading of the quoted passage is fair, or whether Frege's concession there is harmless to the solution; and whether the position, as Fregeans typically defend it, has been qualified into something that no possible case could touch — and if so, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or simply what a framework-level thesis looks like.
