I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who has, in the last few years, turned to philosophy and fallen under the spell of Borges — above all his essay A New Refutation of Time, with its idealist denial that the successive moments of a life add up to anything. It has struck me that two developments in my old field since his death in 1986 bear directly on his preoccupations, and I would like a reasoned prediction of how he would have received each.

First, time crystals: phases of matter in which time-translation symmetry is broken, so that a system repeats itself in time the way an ordinary crystal repeats in space — recurrence made into physics rather than metaphysics. Would Borges have greeted this as an uncanny vindication of the circular time he toyed with in his essays, or dismissed it as a triviality beside his argument, which concerned not the periodicity of matter but the nonexistence of the river itself?

Second, the now-commonplace suggestion in quantum cosmology that time is not fundamental at all — that it emerges from a deeper, timeless description of the universe. Would he have taken this as belated scientific confirmation of his refutation, or would he have distrusted a denial of time arrived at through equations rather than through Berkeley and Hume?

Please ground your answer in his actual writings and intellectual temperament rather than in a generic portrait of him as a lover of labyrinths. Finally, state how confident you are in each part of your prediction, and single out the specific point on which you are least sure.
