I've been reading about costly signaling and the handicap principle for a course on the evolution of communication, and I keep finding that my intuitions about cases don't line up with the simple slogan that a signal is honest because it is costly. Here are my verdicts, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, unifies them. Stotting gazelles: honest signal, since a weak gazelle cannot afford to jump that way and the predator should update. The peacock's tail: honest for the same reason. A nearly broke man who buys an expensive watch on credit: deceptive, not honest, even though the watch is genuinely costly to him — maybe more costly, relative to his means, than a rich man's watch. A degree from an elite university: an honest signal of ability even if the graduate learned nothing, because the lazy could not have survived the admissions filter and the workload. A groveling public apology that costs the apologizer real standing: sincere. A perfunctory sorry-if-you-were-offended: worthless, because it costs nothing. But a handwritten letter, which costs almost nothing in money or risk, strikes me as often deeply sincere, and I can't reconcile that with my verdict on the cheap apology. I also read that some animal signals are cheap to produce but physically impossible to fake — a plumage patch whose color directly reflects parasite load, say — and my gut says those are honest too, which seems to contradict everything above. Finally, a rich man's watch bought with pocket change: honest, I think, but now I notice this verdict sits oddly next to my degree verdict, since the degree's cost is what did the work there and cost seems to do none here.

So here is what I want. First, state a principle — or the minimal set of competing candidate principles — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, and say precisely which clause of each principle is motivated by which case. In particular, I want to know whether the real work is done by costliness per se, by differential cost (the signal being cheaper for high-quality signalers than for low-quality ones), or by unfakeability simpliciter, and whether those three are rivals or one idea in disguise. Second, tell me which of my verdicts the best principle forces me to abandon, and why — I suspect the handwritten letter or the credit-bought watch has to go, but I can't see which. Third, apply the winning principle to cases I haven't listed: conspicuous conservation (buying the overpriced green product), tipping generously at a restaurant in a city you'll never revisit, getting a visible tattoo of your employer's logo, and a job candidate who works for free for a year. For each, say whether it counts as an honest signal on the principle, and to whom.
