A friend and I keep going in circles about Jeffrey conditionalization, so I want an outside opinion with actual numbers attached. Setup: I glance at a jellybean in bad lighting and slide from 50% to 80% confident it's red, without there being any crisp proposition I can point to that I now know for sure -- the evidence just shifts my probabilities directly. Jeffrey's rule says: hold your conditional probabilities given each member of the evidence partition fixed (rigidity) and average accordingly. The question I want your credence on: is that rule the uniquely rational way to update on uncertain evidence? I.e., an ideal Bayesian agent whose probabilities shift without any proposition becoming certain ought to update exactly as Jeffrey prescribes, full stop.

She's at like 0.9 on this, I'm at maybe 0.3. Give me your number, but mostly I want the reasoning decomposed. Sub-questions I think are load-bearing, though add your own if I've missed one:

1. Is there always some proposition the evidence makes certain (something like "it visually seems to me that it's red"), such that ordinary conditionalization on that proposition reproduces whatever Jeffrey's rule outputs? If so, is the rule even doing independent work, or is it a shortcut that's only as reliable as the reconstruction -- and does the reconstruction always exist, or only sometimes?
2. Is rigidity a genuine rational requirement or an artifact of how we chose to describe the evidence? An agent could have her conditional probabilities given the partition shift too, and nothing in the framework obviously forbids it -- so is the rule silently assuming the hard part?
3. The standard justifications (it's the least-committal update consistent with the new evidence; it minimizes expected inaccuracy or distance from the prior) -- do these establish uniqueness, or just that Jeffrey's rule is one admissible response among several? Does "uniquely rational" survive if the justification only delivers "a rationally permissible response"?
4. Non-commutativity: successive Jeffrey updates depend on the order they arrive in, unlike successive ordinary updates. Does that show the rule is incomplete -- needing a supplementary norm about integrating the order of evidential inputs -- rather than wrong, and is "incomplete" enough to sink "uniquely rational" on its own?
5. The deeper one: is updating on evidence a diachronic rational requirement at all, or is rationality purely synchronic, with update rules at most ideals of narrative consistency about your own past?

Assign a rough probability to each resolving the way my friend needs it to (or the way I need it to), show how those combine into your final credence -- noting where the sub-questions interact rather than multiplying naively -- and tell me which one your number is most sensitive to: if you could recieve one decisive argument settling exactly one of the five, which moves your final credence most, and which direction does it push?
