Write the specification for an adequate mathematical theory of the randomness of individual finite sequences.

The background puzzle: probability theory assigns randomness to processes, not to outcomes, and under a fair coin every 100-bit string has identical probability 2^-100, so it cannot distinguish the all-zeros string from an apparently haphazard one. Yet there is a strong intuition that the former is not random and the latter is. A theory of individual randomness should vindicate (or principledly reject) that intuition. Rather than asking which existing account is best, I want the adequacy conditions first. Specifically:

1. List the desiderata such a theory must meet. For each, state the requirement precisely, give the case, result, or intuition that motivates it, and indicate whether it is non-negotiable or a commitment of a particular camp. Among the requirements I would expect to see addressed, in whatever organization you find clearest:

   - The theory must apply to particular finite strings, not only to infinite sequences or to distributions, since the original question concerns the 100-bit case. It must therefore say something sensible where measure-theoretic notions of typicality are silent.
   - It must be reconciled with the probabilistic fact that all strings of equal length are equiprobable under a fair process — either by articulating the process/product distinction or by some other move, but the conflict may not simply be ignored.
   - It must recover the connection between randomness and incompressibility (a string counts as random roughly when no description shorter than the string itself exists), and it must explain why this criterion and the criterion of passing all effective statistical tests coincide rather than merely asserting both.
   - It must respect the invariance result that the choice of universal machine changes complexity only up to an additive constant, and it must confront what that constant does to verdicts about short strings.
   - It must be consistent with the relevant computability limits: the uncomputability of the complexity function, the Berry-paradox-style obstacle to any effective definition of "the smallest random number," and the fact that no computable procedure can certify the randomness of more than finitely many strings.
   - It must entail the frequency and normality properties we pre-theoretically expect of random strings (roughly half zeros in a long random binary string) without collapsing into a purely frequency-based criterion.

2. Identify any subset of these desiderata that is jointly unsatisfiable. In particular, address whether any theory can deliver absolute, machine-independent verdicts about the randomness of specific short strings, and if not, state precisely which requirements must be weakened (for instance, to degrees of randomness deficiency, or to asymptotic claims) and whether that weakening concedes the original motivating question.

3. State which single requirement does the most work in excluding naive accounts — frequency-based criteria in the spirit of von Mises, pure incompressibility criteria without the statistical-test correspondence, and any fully effective definition — and justify that choice.

The deliverable is the requirements list and the tensions among them, not a survey of the literature; historical attribution and proofs may be sketched but are not the point.
