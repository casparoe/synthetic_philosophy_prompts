I run a small interdisciplinary reading group — four moral philosophers, two political theorists, and an economist — and we have one semester (roughly twelve weekly meetings) to spend on the cluster of issues surrounding the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism. We all know the canonical statement of the objection and the standard replies at a survey level, so I don't want a syllabus or a summary of the literature. What I want is a defended agenda: which open questions in this area most deserve our twelve weeks, and in what order?

Here are the candidates I'm currently weighing, though you should feel free to add or subtract. First, whether the objection is really about aggregation at all, or instead about the permissibility of uncompensated sacrifices — some participants think the interpersonal/intrapersonal analogy (we accept trade-offs within a life, so why not across lives?) is the crux, while others think that analogy misses that the objection targets balancing as a method rather than any particular distributive outcome. Second, the objection's afterlife in population ethics: does separateness-of-persons reasoning push toward person-affecting views, and does it thereby inherit or dodge the non-identity problem? Third, the risk dimension: whether ex ante contractualist or complaints-based frameworks genuinely respect separateness in cases involving imposed risks, or whether they smuggle aggregation back in through expected harm. Fourth, whether the objection has real bite against the more sophisticated utilitarian responses (e.g., that the separateness of persons is itself a contingent psychological fact, or that the objection proves too much by ruling out obviously permissible trade-offs in rescue cases and public-health triage).

Please give me a ranked ordering of which questions we should spend our weeks on, with the reasoning made explicit: which questions are upstream of which (so that settling one changes how the others even get formulated), which are tractable for a group that meets two hours a week and won't master new formal machinery, and which you'd deprioritize because their resolution wouldn't actually change positions on the downstream debates. I'd also like to know what would move an item up or down your ranking — for instance, whether the population-ethics question jumps to the top if the rescue-case objection against separateness reasoning turns out to be decisive. The deliverable I'm after is a prioritized agenda with defended criteria, not a tour of everything that's been said.
