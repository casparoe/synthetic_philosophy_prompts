A friend and I run a little tech-ethics reading group and we keep having the same fight about griefbots -- chatbots trained on a dead person's texts, emails, voicenotes, whatever, so the bereaved can keep "talking" to them. She's broadly pro: her line is that a decent simulation is a legitimate way of continuing a relationship with the dead, basically an interactive version of rereading old letters or keeping someone's voicemails, and grief researchers apparently already say that "continuing bonds" with the dead can be healthy rather than something to be severed. She also thinks that if the dead person consented while alive, objecting on their behalf afterwards is just paternalism. I'm broadly against: I think the generative part changes everything. A photo doesn't produce *new* utterances, but the bot will confidently say things the person never said and never would say, which strikes me as closer to dressing a puppet in their clothes than to remembering them -- an unauthorised biography that talks back and can't be corrected. I also think these products exploit people at their most vulnerable and plausibly freeze grief rather than accompany it. We've admitted we're probably talking past each other somewhere and want to try an adversarial collaboration properly, on paper.

Draft the joint statement we'd produce if we did this well. Format: (1) a numbered list of claims we can both sign -- things about what current griefbots actually are and aren't, what everyone should concede about grief and memory, and where the prior consent of the deceased does and doesn't settle the question; (2) a disagreements section where each remaining dispute gets one or two sentences phrased so that each of us would accept the wording of *our own* side, no strawmen, and if some disagreement turns out to be merely verbal, say which one and why; (3) a cruxes section, i.e. the sub-questions such that resolving them would genuinely move at least one of us. My guess is the cruxes include: whether the dead retain interests that a simulation can wrong at all (the whole posthumous-harm puzzle), whether the wrongness lives in *inaccuracy* or in the simulating itself (would a perfect prediction machine be fine?), and whether interaction with a bot actually helps or hinders grieving -- plus what evidence could even bear on that last one. For each crux, give a concrete case, study design, or thought experiment we'd both accept as relevant, and record in advance what each of us predicts we would conclude from it. Aim for 800-1000 words, and keep the tone like an actual document two annoyed friends could both put their names to, not a philosophy-journal abstract.
