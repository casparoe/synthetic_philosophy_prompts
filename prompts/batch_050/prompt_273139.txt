Ok so I'm in an actual situation and my friends are split on whether I've done anything wrong.

Short version: I found out — through a shared calendar mishap, wasn't snooping — that a guy in my board game group is cheating on his girlfriend, who's also a friend of mine. I pulled him aside and told him I knew, mostly because I wanted him to come clean himself. He panicked and offered me $300 to not say anything. I didn't take the money and I'm probably going to tell her anyway. But a mutual friend who heard about the conversation said I was "basically doing extortion" and could get in real trouble, which seems insane to me?

Here's the thing I can't get past. Telling the girlfriend is not only legal, it's arguably the right thing to do. And taking $300 from him isn't inherently wrong either — if he'd owed me money or wanted to buy my old laptop, nobody would blink. So how does combining two fine things produce a crime? It's like 0 + 0 = 1. My friend says the difference is who initiates: him spontaneously offering money is a gift, me mentioning what I know first makes it a threat. But the literal same exchange can be framed either way, and surely morality can't hinge on who spoke first in a conversation.

What I really want is a principled line, because the obvious candidate principles seem to overgenerate. E.g. "it's wrong to demand payment for not doing something you'd be entitled to do" would also condemn a business saying "stop undercutting our prices or we'll tell your customers about your safety recall," which strikes me as a totally legitimate move. Is there a way of carving out what's actually bad in my situation that doesn't also swallow normal hardball negotiating? And separately: did *I* do anything wrong here, given I never asked for the money?
