I run a small reading group (we are all amateurs, no philosophers among us) that has spent the past two months circling the question of whether a hypocrite gets to blame other people. The argument started from an ordinary case — a colleague who constantly calls out others for arriving late while being chronically late himself — and it has hardened into two camps that I now think are talking past each other. One camp, which I'll call the Standing camp, holds that blame is something you need a kind of moral license for: if you are guilty of the very fault you condemn, and unrepentant about it, then your blame is out of place even if everything you say is true — the familiar reaction being something like "who are you to talk?" The other camp, the Egalitarians, holds that blame is just a response to wrongdoing plus disapproval, and that anyone who accurately perceives a wrong may voice it; on their view the hypocrite's blame is fully legitimate, and at most the hypocrite is being rude, off-putting, or imprudent, but not barred from anything. I'd like you to draft the joint statement these two camps would produce if they sat down for an honest adversarial collaboration — the document both would sign, not a verdict on who wins.

Please structure it in three parts. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can genuinely endorse — things like: the content of a hypocrite's accusation can be entirely true and the blamed person can be fully blameworthy; something about the hypocrite's blame strikes most people as off, and any adequate view must either explain that reaction or explain it away; there is a difference between a blameless stranger, a victim of the same wrongdoing, a reformed former offender, and a currently practicing offender, even if the sides disagree about which differences matter. Be careful here: don't smuggle in premises only one camp accepts, and don't water the agreed claims down into banalities. Second, a short table of the residual disagreements, each stated in wording both camps would accept as a fair description of their own position — I particularly want you to get exact about whether the dispute is over a *right* or *authority* to blame (something that can be lost and regained), over what the blame *communicates* and whether hypocrisy makes that communication self-undermining, or merely over the *appropriateness* of expressing blame, and whether these are actually three separate disagreements that have been collapsed into one. Third, identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving them would force at least one camp to revise. For each crux, describe a test case or consideration both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts. Cases you might use include: the reformed hypocrite (does repenting and changing restore whatever was lost?); the private blamer (does the Standing view depend on an audience, or does even silent, unspoken resentment require standing?); the wrongdoing the hypocrite *used* to commit versus one they merely *would* commit in the same circumstances; and whether a victim of the very same offense retains unlimited standing to blame the hypocrite back.

Two further requests. Where you suspect part of the dispute is verbal — for instance, that the camps are using "blame" to mean different things (an outward act of confrontation versus an inner attitude of resentment), so that one camp's "the hypocrite can't blame" and the other's "the hypocrite's blame is fine" might both be true under disambiguation — say so explicitly and say which formulation of the disagreement survives the disambiguation. And if, after drafting, you find that one crux has no imaginable evidence or argument that would move either side, flag it rather than papering over it; part of what I want from this document is an honest map of where the debate is resolvable and where it rests on brute conflicting intuitions. Write it as an actual document both camps could put their names to, in neutral language throughout.
