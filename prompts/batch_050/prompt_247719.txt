So my housing co-op just did something a bit unusual: instead of holding our normal election for the two open board seats, we drew names out of a hat from everyone who volunteered. Long story about why, doesn't matter. What matters is that afterwards at the bar two friends of mine, Marta and Deniz, got into an actual argument about it that's still going by text, and I want a referee.

Round one. Marta said the lottery was the fairest thing the co-op has ever done, because elections are just popularity contests that reward whoever is confident, photogenic, and has free time, and a random draw gives everyone an actually equal shot at power. Deniz said fairness isn't the point of elections, the point is accountability: you can't vote someone out of a hat. The threat of losing your seat is what keeps officeholders honest, and a randomly picked board member answers to nobody.

Marta came back with: we trust randomly selected people with much bigger stuff, namely juries deciding whether someone goes to prison, and nobody says juries are illegitimate because jurors can't be voted out. Random selection plus time to actually learn the issue plus a facilitator works fine, that's basically what citizens' assemblies do and they come up with sensible recommendations. Deniz said the jury comparison proves his point, not hers: jurors decide narrow factual questions under instructions from a judge, with professional lawyers on both sides. A legislature *writes* the rules, there's no judge to keep a randomly picked board on rails. And he added what he called the agenda problem: if the members are amateurs who rotate out, the permanent staff and the people who draft the options end up running everything, and those people are unelected AND unaccountable.

Marta's reply: elections don't solve that either, half of what politicians do is written by staffers and lobbyists, and at least you can't lobby someone whose name hasn't been drawn yet. Elections also select specifically for ambition, and the kind of person who spends months campaigning for a co-op board seat is maybe exactly who you don't want on it. Deniz said ambition is a filter, not a bug: wanting the job enough to work for it correlates with caring and with basic competence, and campaigning is itself a test of whether you can organize people and get things done. Marta said campaigning skill and governing skill have basically nothing to do with each other, and a randomly drawn board can just call in experts when it needs them. Deniz: then the experts rule, congrats, you've reinvented rule by unaccountable advisors.

Last round (so far). Marta floated a hybrid: keep elections for the people who propose stuff, but have a randomly drawn body that can only approve or veto. Deniz said a veto point with no accountability is just gridlock with extra steps, but I noticed he said it with less confidence than the rest, and Marta texted me afterwards claiming he'd basically conceded.

I'm staying out of it, genuinely not sure who's right. Can you go through the exchange point by point and say who's right about what? Not just an overall winner -- I want each distinct claim adjudicated seperately, including whether the jury analogy helps Marta or Deniz, and whether Deniz really did concede at the end. Also tell me what BOTH of them are missing, since I suspect neither has the full picture.

Two requests on format and epistemics:

1. Give me your verdicts as a table with these columns: | # | Point in dispute | Who's right (Marta / Deniz / both partly / neither) | Why | What both sides miss on this point |. After the table, a short paragraph on the overall winner if there is one.

2. At the end, tell me what would change your mind: for the two or three verdicts you're least sure about, name the specific argument, evidence, or real-world example that would flip them. I'd rather know where your reasoning is shaky than get a fake-confident clean sweep.
