My Kant reading group has been stuck for months on whether his ethics can give animals any real moral standing. In §17 of the Doctrine of Virtue (in the Metaphysics of Morals) Kant says our duties regarding animals are really duties to ourselves: cruelty to an animal is wrong because it dulls the natural fellow-feeling for suffering that morality later uses in our dealings with humans, and gratitude toward a long-serving old horse is owed only indirectly, as if the horse were one of the household rather than someone actually owed anything. Half the group says this settles it: animals can never be directly owed anything in Kant's system, and every attempt to fix that just stops being Kant. The other half says the conclusion doesn't even follow from his own premises — if the horse's suffering engages the same feeling that grounds duties toward humans, and Kant treats the animal's service as an analogue of merit, then animals are already inside the moral community and the "indirect" label is doing all the work.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A merely verbal one about what "direct duty" has to mean? A substantive one about what Kant's theory of value commits him to? Or two groups answering different questions — what Kant held versus what Kantian resources can support — under one heading?
