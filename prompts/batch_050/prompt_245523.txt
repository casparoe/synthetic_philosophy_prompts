Hi — philosophy PhD student here, working on self-knowledge, and I've managed to argue myself into a corner and can't find the way out. Setup: I'm in a cold room, 10°C, and the thermostat is creeping up imperceptibly slowly, say a hundredth of a degree a minute, until the room is at 30°C hours later. At the start I feel cold, at the end I'm comfortably warm.

(1) Feeling cold is luminous: if I feel cold, I'm in a position to know that I feel cold. (How could you feel cold and be unable to tell? That seems like the paradigm case of self-knowledge.)
(2) Margin for error: if at some moment I know I feel cold, then a moment later — after a change far too small to notice — I still feel cold. Reason: if the tiniest undetectable shift would flip me from cold to not-cold, then when I take myself to know, my belief is only luckily true; an identical belief formed in the very next moment would be false. Knowledge can't be that fragile, so knowing requires a buffer against nearby error.
(3) At the start I feel cold, so by (1) I know it. By (2), a moment later I still feel cold, so by (1) again I know that too. Keep going step by step... at 30°C I still feel cold. But I obviously don't. Contradiction.
(4) So one of (1) or (2) has to go — and the recipe generalizes to anything that fades gradually: pain, tiredness, looking red. It seems to show nothing about my own mind is luminous, that I can't really know I feel cold even when my hands are freezing.
(5) Worse, the same trick applies to "I know that p," since knowledge decays gradually as memory fades — so KK fails too: knowing p never guarantees being in a position to know you know p.

(4) already proves too much for me — when my feet are numb with cold, I take it I know I'm cold, full stop. Which step should I reject, why did it look fine, and what's the price of rejecting it? If forced, I'd rather give up (2) than (1), but I have a bad feeling the buffer intuition is doing real work there. Also, if the whole setup is confused — e.g. if I've equivocated on "know" or smuggled something into (2) that (1) can't support — please just say so rather than playing along with a broken argument. This is basically Williamson-style territory but I want to work out where I actually stand on it.
