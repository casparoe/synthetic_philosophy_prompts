My roommate and I got into it last weekend over whether you can sensibly forecast how long something like the American republic has left by looking at how long past empires and republics lasted, and he came out with an argument that I can't find the hole in, even though it clearly proves too much (it would also show that insurance companies are scam artists and that your doctor telling you the odds on a surgery is astrology). Here it is, roughly as he gave it:

1. To predict anything about a future event from history, you have to sort that event into a reference class of past cases and read off the base rate. (The US is a republic, republics last on average N years, so...)

2. Every event belongs to indefinitely many reference classes, and they give different base rates. The US is a: republic, democracy, settler-colonial state, nuclear power, continental empire, state founded on a written constitution, state with a two-party system, etc. The base rate of collapse differs across all of these, sometimes wildly.

3. There is no non-circular rule for choosing which class is "the" right one, because any such rule would have to be validated against past cases, and doing THAT requires picking a reference class of cases where the rule worked.

4. If there's no rule that forces one choice, the choice of class is arbitrary.

5. A method whose output is fixed by an arbitrary choice doesn't give you knowledge — by picking your class, you can get whatever base rate you want, so base-rate reasoning can "justify" any forecast.

6. So the outside view is worthless. Nobody has ever actually predicted anything from history; they've just dressed up their hunches in Roman Empire cosplay.

I really don't want to accept 6. I'd also like to keep 1 if I can, since the appeal of base rates is exactly that they check your inside-view overconfidence, and he says giving up 2 is just denying obvious facts. So: which step do I reject? For that step, explain why it sounded fine (it did!) and what rejecting it actually costs me. Then tell me whether the standard moves people make about the reference class problem — picking the most *relevant* class, looking at several classes and seeing if they converge, narrowing to the most specific class with decent sample size — genuinely defuse steps 3 through 5, or whether they just smuggle the inside view back in through the side door. If more than one step is load-bearing for the bad conclusion, say so and rank them by how much it hurts to give each one up.

Answer in American English please. And don't just gesture at "statisticians have thought about this" — I want to know which *premise* dies.
