ok so here's the deal. I got no philosophy background, I just read a lot and keep ending up in the same fight with people about whether what an author *meant* settles what a text *means*. Latest round was a song lyric the songwriter swears is about his dog while literally everyone else reads it as being about his divorce. I keep flip-flopping on it myself, so I want to sort it out properly instead of vibing.

Write me a decision tree / flowchart I can actually use on people (my book club, my brother, myself) to figure out what position they really hold on authorial intention and textual meaning. Some rules so it's actually useful:

- Build it from cases, not labels. Every branch has to be a concrete scenario someone can react to without knowing any theory. Things like: a poem found washed up in a bottle with no known author; a misprint that critics liked better than the original and the author then said "fine, keep the misprint"; a meaningful-looking text that turns out to have been produced by erosion or a predictive keyboard with nobody intending anything; an author who plants a hidden meaning on purpose that no reader has ever once found; a committee-written document where no two members meant the same thing by it. Use these or invent better ones, but every node is a case like that, never "do you believe in intentionalism."
- 8 to 12 questions max. Each leaf has to be a real, named position — the meaning just is what the author intended, intentions are irrelevant full stop, the reader constructs the meaning, the best interpretation wins regardless of intent, what a reasonable audience would infer the author meant, whatever set you think actually carves the space. You pick the leaves and commit to them, don't hand me a menu of twenty options.
- If you can, work out the logical space first: which combinations of answers are even possible, and if some combo has no actual position living in it, say so and take a real guess at why (is it incoherent? just ugly? unexplored?).
- If two positions genuinely can't be separated by any question you can write, flag it honestly instead of fudging with a mushy question.
- Commit hard. Pick which question goes FIRST as the most fundamental fork and defend that ordering — don't give me three possible trees and shrug. And at the end tell me straight out which leaf you think is actually correct and whether your tree's design was rigged in its favor or not.

Keep the questions answerable by normal humans at a book club, not grad students, since I really do plan to run people through this.
