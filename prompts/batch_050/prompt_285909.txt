I'm a docent at a small art museum, and I keep returning to a phenomenon I'd like to understand more precisely. A few years ago a painting that had hung in our main room for decades was reattributed: not by the artist we'd always credited, but by a skilled imitator working fifty years later. Nothing about the object changed. Same canvas, same brushwork, same light falling on it from the same window. And yet attendance in that room collapsed, the painting was moved to a hallway, and donors who had once been photographed in front of it now describe feeling embarrassed. Here's the part that puzzles me most: the imitator's other works have since become mildly valuable in their own right, collected as curiosities. So the value seems to track the actual causal history of the object -- whoever really made it -- rather than anything the eye can detect, and a work can regain some standing once its true history is itself interesting. Is there an established name for this feature of art objects, where a property that makes no perceptible difference nonetheless drives value, and for the way value follows the true origin story rather than the visible qualities? If it's adjacent to some known concept without being identical, explain the difference. And if nothing fits exactly, propose a term with a definition and two boundary cases -- one where it clearly applies and one where you'd expect it to apply but it doesn't.
