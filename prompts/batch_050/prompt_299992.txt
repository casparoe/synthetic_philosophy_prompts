I've been defending an argument in my metaphysics seminar and have come to suspect it proves too much, because its conclusion looks false to me. (1) Necessarily, if x and y share all the same properties, then x is identical with y. (2) Consider a possible universe containing nothing but two perfect spheres, each exactly two feet across, made of the same uniform stuff throughout, floating one mile apart in otherwise empty space. (3) The two spheres share all their intrinsic properties. (4) They also share all their relational properties: each is one mile from a sphere of exactly that description, each is in a universe containing exactly two such spheres, and so on. (5) So the spheres share all their properties. (6) By (1), they are identical, and the universe contains only one sphere. (7) But (2) described two, and that description seems perfectly coherent, so something has gone wrong. What troubles me is that if the argument is sound, the moral generalizes: no two things anywhere can be perfectly alike, so either every electron in the actual world secretly differs from every other, or reality obeys a constraint that seems to need more justification than the bare thought that properties settle identity. Where does the argument fail, if it does? I'd like to keep (1), which strikes me as close to definitional, but I want to know what I must give up instead, why the failing step looked innocent, and whether the obvious way of repairing (4) saves the argument only at the cost of making (1) trivial.
