I'm in a reading group that's been working through Sartre's "Existentialism is a Humanism" (the 1946 lecture), and last week two members, Maya and Dan, got into a fight that's now dragged on over two sessions and one very long group chat. It started from the part early on where Sartre describes the Christian objection to existentialism: "Everyone can do what he likes, and will be incapable, from such a point of view, of condemning either the point of view or the action of anyone else."

Here's the dispute as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Maya's opening move was that Sartre never actually escapes this reproach. If values are created by our choices rather than discovered, then when Sartre condemns bad faith he's just dressing up his own choice as a verdict on everyone else. The person in bad faith has simply chosen differently, and Sartre has no standing to call it a failing. (2) Dan replied that this misconstrues what bad faith is. It's not a rival value-choice, it's a factual error about yourself -- treating yourself as a fixed thing with a nature when you're actually free. So Sartre can condemn it the way you'd condemn a miscalculation, without smuggling in any objective values. (3) Maya came back with two points: first, that makes bad faith a mere intellectual slip, but Sartre obviously treats it as something culpable that we're hiding from, not an honest mistake; second, someone could perfectly well *choose* self-deception or comfort over lucidity, and nothing in Sartre's framework says lucidity about yourself has to outrank comfort -- that ranking would itself be a value, not a fact. (4) Dan's response was that you can't straightforwardly choose bad faith, because it essentially involves hiding the choice from yourself; it's not one free project among others but a refusal to be what you inescapably are. Freedom is a fact about us, not a value we happen to endorse, so the condemnation rests on fact, not preference. (5) Maya's last word was that this just relocates the problem: Sartre now owes us a reason why we *ought* to face facts about ourselves at all, and "you should acknowledge the truth about what you are" is itself a value claim, so one value -- authenticity or sincerity or whatever -- got smuggled in through the back door after all.

Neither of them will budge and I've stayed out of it so far. Can you adjudicate? Go move by move through (1)-(5), say who's right or wrong at each step and why, then give an overall verdict including what, if anything, both of them are missing about Sartre's position. Keep it under 450 words; the quoted passage doesn't count toward the limit.
