I am a retired molecular biologist who spent forty years running experiments, and only in the last few years have I taken up philosophy of mind in a serious way. Having worked through the literature as best an outsider can, I have come to accept a deflationary position on phenomenal consciousness, and I want to know, in the spirit in which I would once have stress-tested a hypothesis before committing a grant to it, what would count against it.

The position is roughly this. There is no phenomenal consciousness over and above access consciousness: for a percept to be experienced just is for its content to be available for report, reasoning, and the flexible control of behavior -- globally broadcast, in the workspace theorist's idiom. The apparent counterexamples dissolve on inspection. The overflowing richness of a scene, as in the partial-report experiments with letter grids, reflects access to generic or ensemble content rather than to every particular; the intuition that we experience more than we can access is a cognitive illusion generated by the fact that any item we probe turns out to be accessible, so that it feels as though all of them were already so. On this view, positing phenomenal consciousness that outruns access is explanatorily idle: it predicts nothing the access story does not already predict, and the hard-problem vocabulary that surrounds it mistakes the limits of third-person description for a further fact.

I do not want a recital of the standard objections, which I have read. I want the exposure map: what this position actually risks. Which experimental findings -- from inattentional and change blindness, from no-report paradigms that track perceptual switches without verbal report, from the lesion and stimulation literature on the neural basis of awareness, or from elsewhere -- would genuinely tell against it, as opposed to merely constraining which version of it survives? Is there an observation the deflationary view is committed to denying, such that the observation's confirmation would be fatal rather than awkward? Conversely, is there a finding the overflow camp needs that has simply failed to materialize? I would also like to know what the position's own defenders would concede as counting against them -- where they themselves locate the soft underbelly -- as opposed to what their critics say should count, since those two lists rarely coincide.

Two requests about calibration. First, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say for each whether it threatens the position as stated or only some inessential elaboration of it. Second, keep scrupulously separate what is settled among people who work in this area -- which experimental results are robust, which dissociations are no longer in dispute -- from what remains genuinely contested among informed parties, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus. If the honest answer is that the deflationary view has been qualified into a shape that risks nothing, and therefore explains nothing, I would rather be told that plainly than reassured.
