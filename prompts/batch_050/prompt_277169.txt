I serve as rapporteur to a standing advisory panel convened by our foreign ministry to give consistency reviews of proposed humanitarian interventions, and we are deadlocked over how our own record applies to a live referral. I would like an outside analysis before our next session.

First, our doctrinal anchor. The panel's founding note draws on John Stuart Mill's 1859 essay A Few Words on Non-Intervention. In our own summary of it, the essay defends non-intervention as the default among civilized states on two distinguishable grounds: first, that political freedom is only durably held by peoples who have won it through their own effort and sacrifice, so that outsiders who win a people's liberty on its behalf typically leave it unable to keep that liberty; and second, that where one party to a domestic struggle is sustained by the arms of a foreign power, intervention on the other side may be legitimate precisely because it restores the balance that foreign interference destroyed -- enforcing non-intervention rather than violating it. The panel has never decided how these two grounds interact when they pull apart.

Second, our track record, with the rationales recorded at the time:

1. Kosovo, 1999. We supported the NATO air campaign despite the absence of Security Council authorization, on the grounds that its operative aim was to halt an ongoing campaign of mass expulsion and killing, that the targets were military and security infrastructure, and that no one proposed to occupy and remake Serbia itself.
2. Iraq, 2003. We opposed the invasion, although the regime was murderous, on the grounds that no mass atrocity was imminent or ongoing, that the avowed aim was to overthrow and reconstruct the state, and that a long occupation was intended from the outset.
3. Libya, 2011. We opposed the campaign despite Security Council authorization and a credible threat to Benghazi, on the grounds that a protective mandate would predictably become a war for regime change, that the opposition was too fractious to govern what followed, and that no plan for the aftermath existed.
4. Rwanda, 1994. In a retrospective review we held that intervention should have occurred, expressly accepting that halting the genocide would have entailed the military defeat of the interim government -- regime change as a side effect of protection, which we distinguished from regime change as a goal.

Third, the live referral. The Republic of Kharovia, a state of some twenty million, has lost control of a province to a popular uprising and is besieging its principal city of roughly 700,000 people. There is credible evidence of sectarian massacres by security forces in retaken districts. The Security Council is blocked by two vetoes, but a twelve-member regional organization has authorized a civilian protection zone to be enforced by air power. The uprising has an armed wing holding part of the countryside, but it is fragmented into rival factions, and at least two neighbouring states are arming proxies. The regime is sustained by a foreign patron whose aircraft, advisers, and air-defense technicians operate across the country. Enforcing the protection zone would require dismantling the regime's integrated air defenses nationwide, and most analysts believe the regime would not long survive that. The regional body's mandate is written in protective language and is silent on the regime's future.

What I am asking for is a structured reading of our record, in four parts. (a) Set out the candidate decision rules under which our four prior verdicts are jointly consistent -- I can see at least two and suspect a third, one organized around the operative aim of the intervention, one around Mill's two grounds, and one around the prudential features of the post-intervention situation, but I want them stated precisely enough to generate predictions. (b) Say which of those rules diverge on the Kharovia referral, predict the verdict the panel would reach if it is to remain consistent with itself, and identify which prior case is the closest analog -- Kosovo, as an unauthorized but genuinely protective campaign, or Libya, as an air campaign whose foreseeable success would collapse a regime with a fractured opposition. (c) If you judge the record to genuinely underdetermine the referral, state candidly which principle the panel would be adopting with each available verdict, and whether the Mill-derived clause in our founding note settles the matter: the foreign-backed party here is the regime rather than the insurgents, which appears to place the case under Mill's second ground, but that ground was framed for wars of national liberation and it is not obvious that it transfers to atrocity prevention. (d) Describe the further hypothetical case -- the one not yet before us -- whose verdict would discriminate among the surviving candidate rules.
