My colleague in philosophy of physics has settled views about which features of our best theories represent physical reality and which are surplus structure -- mere redundancy in the mathematics. Her verdicts so far: in electrostatics, potential differences are real but the absolute value of the scalar potential is not; in the Aharonov-Bohm effect, the value of the vector potential at a point is not real, but the integral of that potential around a closed loop encircling the solenoid is; in quantum mechanics, the overall phase of a wavefunction is not real, but the relative phase between two branches is; in general relativity, the coordinates assigned to an event are not real, but the proper time a specified clock records between two events is. Over coffee we hit a harder case: gravitational waves. In general relativity there is no gauge-invariant expression for the energy density of the gravitational field at a point -- only the total energy of an isolated spacetime is invariant -- yet detectors respond locally, and a passing wave demonstrably deposits energy in them. Predict her verdict on whether the energy carried locally by a gravitational wave is real; identify the rule she has implicitly been running -- gauge invariance, observational accessibility, or something else; explain where her earlier verdicts underdetermine that rule; and name the further case that would discriminate between the two best candidate rules.
