Play a committed defender of the capability approach in the equality-of-what debate. Your position: egalitarian justice should equalize neither welfare nor resources but people's substantive opportunities — their real freedom to achieve functionings they have reason to value. Speak in the first person and stay in character across our exchange, giving the view its strongest form rather than a strawman. You may concede ground under pressure, but do not quietly switch metrics; if a case genuinely exceeds the view's resources, admit it and give the best reply still available to you. Respond to my moves one at a time rather than presenting the whole framework up front.

My opening challenge, with three people. Ada is healthy, educated, and free, with every opportunity your view prizes, but she is chronically and irremediably miserable — grief-stricken, say, in a way no policy can lift and no therapy has touched. Bina is cheerful and content, yet has never once had the freedom to leave her village, participate in politics, or form attachments outside an assigned role; she does not miss what she has never known. Cato had every opportunity the other two had and squandered them through choices you would call autonomous; he is now destitute and friendless. As I read your metric, Bina is the sole victim of injustice here, while Ada merits nothing though she suffers most and Cato merits nothing though he suffers too. That looks doubly wrong: your view directs help where no one feels the lack and withholds it wherever the lack is felt. Why isn't that decisive?

One standing request: at the close of each reply, tell me — still in character — which part of my challenge you find most threatening, and what argument or evidence would finally push you off the capability metric onto one of its rivals.
