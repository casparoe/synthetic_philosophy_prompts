I have been a devoted reader of rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade, and it has left me with habits I now want applied to a live institutional problem: treat a deliberating body's precedents as revealed preferences, reverse-engineer the rule that actually generates them, and identify in advance what evidence would change the answer. I serve on the curriculum committee of a small philosophy department, and next month we take up the hardest petition of my tenure: a request that both courses in our logic sequence carry a short contextual note on Frege. The facts are not in dispute. Near the end of his life Frege kept a private diary, published only decades after his death, containing nationalist, anti-democratic, and plainly antisemitic entries; the scholar who did more than anyone to establish Frege's modern reputation wrote of his dismay at the discovery. A colleague supporting the petition argues the diary is philosophically relevant, not merely biographical: that it shows Frege extending his conviction about objective, person-independent law from logic to peoples and nations. I doubt that argument, but the committee's obligation is consistency with itself, not my doubts.

Our recent record:

1. Heidegger. A petition to remove him from the upper-level survey was denied; we kept him required but added a mandatory session on the interpenetration of his politics and his philosophy. Recorded rationale: students will encounter celebratory treatments elsewhere, and here the politics reaches into the work.
2. Hume. A petition to add a framing note about his infamous footnote asserting a racial hierarchy was denied. Rationale: a deplorable aside, but not load-bearing for anything we teach from him.
3. Locke. On our own initiative we added a framing note covering his colonial administrative work, his investment in a slave-trading company, and his role in the Carolina constitution. Rationale: his biography stands in direct tension with the chapters we assign.
4. A living analytic philosopher, whom I will not name. A petition to annotate his textbook over his extramural political speech was denied. Rationale: the syllabus is not an honor roll.

Here is what I want from you. First, the decision that best coheres with this record, defended rather than merely stated. Second, the closest analog among the four precedents, and why the resemblance runs deeper than the obvious rival candidate. Third, the two or three rules that each fit all four precedents and exactly where they diverge on Frege. Fourth, if the record genuinely underdetermines this case, an honest statement of what principle we would be adopting with each possible vote, including which past vote a decision to add the note would implicitly repudiate. Fifth, the single further fact about Frege's case that, if established, would flip your recommendation. I do not want a verdict on Frege's character, and I do not want citations or a reading list. I want the rule we are already running made legible to us before we vote.
