You're an experienced philosophy seminar leader who's run dozens of classes on Bernard Williams and utilitarianism, and you're known for activites that make students *feel* objections instead of just nodding along to them. Think carefully and brainstorm a bit before you settle on a final design.

Here's what I need: a runnable classroom activity for an undergrad ethics seminar (about 14 students, 80 minutes, they've read the "one thought too many" bit of Williams and the Jim case but half of them clearly didn't get it). The goal is to make participants *feel the force* of Williams's integrity objection from the inside — especially the specific point that certain decisions feel corrupted if you arrive at them through impartial calculation, even when the impartial answer is "correct" (e.g. it's fine, even obligatory, to save your spouse over a stranger, but something goes wrong if you had to *check with the moral law first*).

The twist I want you to build the activity around (this is the non-standard part, so take it seriously): put the students in a structure where their *reasons get filtered*. Concretely, I'm imagining something like: participants make an emotionally loaded forced choice in character (invent your own scenario, don't just redo Jim or the avalanche), and then in a second phase they have to justify their choice in front of some kind of mock tribunal or review board that only accepts a restricted currency of reasons — say, only reasons that any neutral stranger could in principle offer. The point is that they should *discover* on their own that their real reason ("she's my wife") either gets ruled inadmissible or sounds grotesque when translated into tribunal-speak, and feel the squeeze Williams is pointing at, before anyone names it.

Deliverables:

1. The scenario itself — make it fresh and specific, with enough texture that students actually care (a named character, a concrete stake, a time pressure). It should be a case where saving the loved one is intuitively fine, so the discomfort comes from the *deliberation*, not the outcome.
2. The rules and roles: who plays the decider, who sits on the tribunal, what counts as an admissible reason, what the tribunal can do (reject reasons? demand re-phrasing? overrule?), and any small stakes or scoring that keep people engaged.
3. A timeline for the 80 minutes, including a round where the rules change or intensify (e.g. a second decider faces a version where the impartial answer flips, so the tribunal procedure now *forces* the "wrong" choice — this is where integrity, not just one-thought-too-many, shows up).
4. A debrief guide: which moments map onto which parts of Williams (ground projects, the verdict of the "impartial standpoint," the famous thought about the rescuer's motivation), what will predictably go wrong when you run this (students gaming the tribunal, someone refusing to play along, the room getting actually upset) and how to handle it.
5. Be honest about the one thing this activity structurally *can't* reproduce — Williams's claim is partly about what it's like to *actually have* deep commitments over a lifetime, and a 80-minute role-play fakes that. Say what the facilitator has to supply verbally to patch the gap.
