I am writing a long-form magazine piece on a puzzle in the theory of markets: if trading were driven solely by differences in information among rational agents who share common priors, then the mere fact that someone is willing to take the other side of your trade tells you something, and after both parties reason about each other's willingness to trade, no mutually accepted trade should occur -- yet real markets clear enormous volumes daily. The explanations for observed volume I have already covered and do not need repeated: (1) differences in prior beliefs, so that traders simply disagree about probabilities rather than updating on one another; (2) noise or liquidity traders who transact for reasons unconnected to information, giving informed traders someone to trade with; (3) non-speculative motives such as hedging and portfolio rebalancing, where the parties are trading risk rather than disagreeing about value; (4) bounded rationality and overconfidence, so that traders fail to draw the inference that a willing counterparty is bad news; and (5) differences in risk tolerance, which permit mutually beneficial trades even under full agreement about the facts. Give me one more explanation that is genuinely different from all of these -- not a rewording, special case, or combination of any item on the list -- and explain the mechanism by which it generates trade. Then assess it candidly: does it actually dissolve the puzzle, or does it merely relocate it one step back? Close by saying what evidence or argument would lead you to withdraw the explanation, and where your own reasoning about it is most likely to fail.
