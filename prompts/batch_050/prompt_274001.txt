I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on AI governance, and I run an interdisciplinary reading group (about 14 people, half philosophers, half computer science graduate students) that meets for 90-minute sessions. For an upcoming session on AI control — safety measures that remain effective even under the assumption that the system may be misaligned — I want to open with an in-class game, before anyone has read the assigned material, that makes participants feel in their gut the difference between trusting a system and designing around its possible misalignment. No computers; paper, cards, and tokens only.

The game needs to generate, through its mechanics rather than through my framing, several specific experiences. First, the asymmetry of self-knowledge: the "system" should privately know whether it is aligned this round while the operators hold only behavioral evidence, and a misaligned system should be free to behave impeccably precisely when it expects scrutiny. Second, the deployment tradeoff: refusing to use the powerful system must be safe but score near zero, so that participants feel the institutional pressure to deploy rather than having it explained to them. Third, the distinction between accidental failure and strategic defection: I want some failures to be random malfunctions and others to be chosen, timed, and concealed, so that participants discover that the two demand entirely different safeguards — a misaligned system should be able to survive several honest-seeming rounds and defect at the moment of maximum payoff, and the operators should feel how little the prior cooperative streak was worth as evidence. Fourth, the core control idea that safeguards must be robust to an adversary who has read them: whatever auditing protocol the operators use should be public knowledge, so a misaligned system can plan around it, and I want a mechanic that lets the operators delegate auditing to a weaker but trusted component — reliable, cheap, but able to check only a fraction of the system's actions and unable to understand the most sophisticated ones — so the group experiences the tradeoff between auditing breadth and auditing depth.

Please give me: complete rules, roles, and an information structure; a payoff scheme in tokens that makes the treacherous-turn timing genuinely tempting and makes cooperative stretches genuinely ambiguous as evidence; the sequence and timing of rounds, including when the misaligned player is most likely to strike and how to keep the game interesting if they strike immediately or never; and a debrief plan that maps each mechanic onto the corresponding concept (trust versus control, audits, trusted monitoring, defense in depth, safety cases) without lecturing — questions that get participants to articulate what happened to them. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — for instance, operators reading the misaligned player's face instead of the protocol, or the group concluding "just audit more" — and how to handle each. Finally, be candid about which features of the real problem the game cannot reproduce: a human player cannot help having tells, cannot be genuinely more capable than the overseers in a narrow domain, and cannot condition defection on a calibrated estimate of being caught, and I want the debrief to name these gaps explicitly rather than let participants over-update from the game.
