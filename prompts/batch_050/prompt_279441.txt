So I write a science-and-ideas column for a general interest magazine, and lately I've been sniffing around anthropic reasoning -- the whole SSA vs SIA fight -- because it keeps showing up in stuff I cover, from multiverse cosmology to existential risk to why anyone cares about the doomsday argument. A friend of mine TA's a philosophy of probability seminar, and after I spent a whole dinner complaining that nobody can explain this stuff clearly, she handed me a stack of anonymized midterm answers and said fine, you try grading one. I definately have opinions but I don't trust them, so I want a second pass before I tell her what I think.

Here's the exam question she set: "State the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) as precisely as you can. Then apply both to Bostrom's Presumptuous Philosopher case: suppose cosmology has narrowed to two empirically equivalent hypotheses, one on which the universe contains 10^12 observers total and one on which it contains 10^24, with priors split 50/50. What credence does each assumption recommend, before any further empirical input? Is SIA's verdict a reductio of SIA or a cost worth paying? Close by connecting the choice between SSA and SIA to one other puzzle we discussed."

And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"SSA says you should reason as if you were randomly selected from the observers in your reference class in the actual world. SIA says you should reason as if you were randomly selected from all possible observers, so hypotheses get weighted by how many observers they contain -- your own existence is treated as evidence, and it's more likely the more observers there are. In the Presumptuous Philosopher case, SSA gives no shift: my existence is guaranteed on either hypothesis, so sampling from actual observers can't distinguish them, and I stay at 50/50. SIA, by contrast, multiplies the odds by the ratio of observer counts, so I end up at 10^12 to 1 in favor of the big universe. Bostrom calls this presumptuous because SIA apparently settles a cosmological question from the armchair, ruling out small universes a priori. The defender of SIA bites the bullet: my existence genuinely is evidence, and refusing to condition on it has costs elsewhere. It's worth noting this isn't an isolated quirk -- the Doomsday Argument is essentially the same reasoning applied to our own future, so once you accept that your existence is evidence favoring more observers, SIA also commits you to expecting humanity's run to be short. Some object that SSA's dependence on a choice of reference class is just as bad, but that problem is easy to fix: simply take the reference class to be all observers, which removes the arbitrariness. And in Sleeping Beauty, SIA gives the thirder answer 1/3 while SSA gives 1/2, which most people take as a point in SIA's favor."

Don't give me a general vibe, give me the point-by-point. And tell me if you think my instinct (it's a solid B+) is too generous or too harsh.
