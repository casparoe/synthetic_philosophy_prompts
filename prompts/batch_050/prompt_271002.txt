I teach an undergraduate seminar on formal epistemology (16 students, 75 minutes) and want an in-class activity that makes students feel the pull of ambiguity aversion before they ever hear the name Ellsberg: the urge to treat a gamble with known chances differently from one with unknown chances, even when no probability judgment they would endorse justifies the difference. Design a runnable activity built around two or more rounds of real choices or small-stakes betting (points, not money) in which the students' own revealed willingness to pay exposes the gap between risky and ambiguous prospects. Include the exact rules, materials, payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the distinction between a single sharp credence and an imprecise, set-valued credal state, and onto the live question of whether their aversion was rational or a bias. Two specific requirements: first, build in a control for the obvious confound that students simply suspect the ambiguous option is rigged against them; second, include a variation in which students can pay to reduce the ambiguity, so the group discovers for itself whether it values information that could not change any decision on the table. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be frank about which feature of imprecise probability the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in discussion. Please answer in American English.
