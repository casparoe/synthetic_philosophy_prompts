So here's a position I keep coming back to: clinical equipoise -- the idea that a randomized trial is only ethical when there's genuine disagreement in the expert community about which arm is better -- is the right foundation for the ethics of RCTs. I know the main critique, that it's a confused leftover of thinking of trials as therapy and that research ethics should run on consent plus social value instead, but I've never been fully convinced we can do without *something* like it. What would actually count against my view? I'm thinking: would it be fatal if it turned out real landmark trials almost never satisfied equipoise (investigators knew which arm they believed in), or is that just awkward sociology? Would a world where purely uncertainty-principle-based trials ran for decades with no scandal count against it, or would defenders just say equipoise was doing silent work anyway? Would evidence that equipoise language actively *delays* good trials in emergencies (early pandemic trials come to mind) be a defeater about the principle or only about its misuse? And the deepest one: if the research/therapy distinction holds, does equipoise simply answer a question nobody needed to ask, or does the view survive as a constraint on what investigators may *believe* while enrolling patients? Please give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely embarrassing, say which ones a committed equipoise defender would themselves concede as counting (not just what critics say), and be honest if the position has quietly been qualified into unfalsifiability. Answer as a numbered list, exactly six items, a sentence or two each, no intro or conclusion.
