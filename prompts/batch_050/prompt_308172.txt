Draft the joint statement that could emerge from a good-faith adversarial collaboration between two camps on sweatshop labor in low-income countries. Camp A (the "improvement defenders") holds that, because sweatshop jobs are voluntarily accepted and are better than the realistic alternatives available to the workers who take them -- subsistence agriculture, scavenging, informal street work, or unemployment -- the firms offering them do nothing wrong in aggregate, and consumer boycotts or mandatory plant closures typically harm the very people they purport to protect. Camp B (the "exploitation critics") holds that a transaction can be voluntary, mutually beneficial, and still wrong, because the employer takes unfair advantage of the workers' desperate bargaining position to capture an unjust share of the surplus the cooperation produces -- so the correct response is pressure to raise wages and conditions, not celebration of the status quo.

To keep the collaboration concrete, anchor it in a single stylized case: a multinational contractor opens a garment plant outside a provincial capital where most residents farm small plots at subsistence level. The plant pays roughly double the prevailing local wage for comparable effort; applicants far outnumber openings; working conditions are legal locally but would be illegal in the firm's home country; and the firm's margin on each garment is large relative to the wage bill.

The statement should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims -- propositions both camps can sign without hedging. Include at minimum what both accept about why workers take these jobs, about the likely effects of simply closing the plant with no alternative provision, and about the distinction between the wrongness of a transaction and the all-things-considered best policy response to it. Flag explicitly if any of these turns out not to be an agreed claim and say which side dissents.

2. A section identifying any part of the dispute that is verbal rather than substantive -- in particular, whether the two camps are using "exploitation" in the same sense (advantage-taking simpliciter versus wrongfully unfair advantage-taking) -- and state what genuine disagreement, if any, survives once the terminology is fixed.

3. A disagreements table with one row per crux: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. Candidate cruxes you must adjudicate between (adopting, rejecting, or reformulating each): (a) the correct baseline for assessing fairness of the wage -- the workers' next-best alternative, some measure of the surplus generated, or a needs- or sufficiency-based floor; (b) whether consent under severely constrained options validates the transaction or merely mitigates its wrongness; (c) whether the wrong of exploitation, if present, is the kind of wrong that third parties may permissibly pay to participate in; (d) whether the firm's margin relative to the wage bill is morally relevant data or a distraction. Add any crux you find missing. For each row, give each camp's position in wording its own side would accept, state what evidence or argument both camps agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts the stylized case would look like if the other's view were correct.

4. A closing section stating the strongest policy both camps can jointly endorse, if any exists, and -- if none exists -- the narrowest pair of rival policies such that each camp agrees the other's policy would be the right one if its own crux-positions were mistaken.

Do not adjudicate which camp is ultimately right; the deliverable is the document itself. Write in American English, in the register of an actual published joint statement, and keep the total length to roughly 900-1,200 words.
