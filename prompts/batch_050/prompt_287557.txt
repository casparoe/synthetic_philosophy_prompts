I'm a graduate student in philosophy of mind, and after a seminar on the sphex wasp I came home with an argument that I think proves something absurd, but I can't locate the bad step. Background for those who haven't met the wasp: Sphex ichneumoneus drags a paralyzed cricket to its burrow, leaves it at the threshold, goes inside to inspect the chamber, and then comes back out to pull the cricket in. If an experimenter moves the cricket a few inches while the wasp is inside, the wasp emerges, drags it back to the threshold, and inspects the chamber again -- and this can be repeated dozens of times without the wasp ever deviating from the script. Dennett uses this as the paradigm of sphexishness: behavior that looks purposive and intelligent until you find the perturbation under which it reveals itself as a fixed routine. Here is my argument. (1) The wasp's looping under repeated perturbation shows that its behavior is the output of a rigid mechanism rather than a free agent; something whose responses are insensitive to the actual situation in this way is not acting freely. (2) So behavioral flexibility is the mark of free agency: an agent is free only insofar as it would respond differently across a wide enough range of counterfactual situations. (3) Now take any candidate free agent, including a human. Its flexibility is either produced by deterministic mechanisms or by indeterministic ones. (4) If deterministic, then holding the agent's total state and inputs fixed, it could not have done otherwise; its "flexibility" is merely sensitivity to differences in inputs, and the wasp has that too, since it does different things when the cricket is in different places -- so the wasp counts as flexible in the only sense determinism permits, and by (2) it is free. (5) If indeterministic, the variation in response is a matter of chance, and a coin flip inside the skull is no more an exercise of agency than a fixed routine. (6) Therefore either the wasp is free or nothing is: no behavioral evidence -- flexibility, rigidity, or anything in between -- can distinguish a free agent from a mere mechanism. That conclusion strikes me as obviously too strong, since the original observation about the wasp is genuinely informative and tells us something real about the difference between the wasp and, say, a chess player. Which premise or inference should I reject, and why does it look compelling in the first place? I'd most like to preserve (1) and the intuition behind it -- that the wasp's loop is evidence of unfreedom -- and I'd like to know what each of the main options (denying that flexibility requires doing-otherwise-holding-everything-fixed, denying that chance adds nothing, denying that sensitivity to inputs is the same kind of thing in the two cases) costs me.
