Write a villanelle about the luminosity of mental states and the KK principle — the thesis that if one knows, one is in a position to know that one knows. Assign the two refrains to the two sides of the dispute: one voicing the KK claim, the other the margin-for-error objection that knowledge requires a buffer between truth and error, so that iterating knowledge must eventually break down. Between the refrains, let the tercets stage the standard case against luminosity — a light slowly fading, a subject whose experience changes by increments too small to discriminate, each step still feeling like knowledge. The poem's structure should do the argument's work: each return of the KK refrain should ring less certain than the last as the margin thins, and the closing quatrain should land where the argument lands — the original knowing may survive; it is the knowing that one knows that the indiscriminable steps erode. The result should be accurate enough that an epistemologist steeped in Williamson's anti-luminosity argument would find the poem right rather than merely on-theme, and self-standing enough that a reader new to the debate could follow it as a poem.
