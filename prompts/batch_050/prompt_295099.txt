Ok so I just got off a three hour argument with my friend and I need a referee, because I'm sure he's wrong but I can't find where. The topic was money pumps — the classic thing where if you prefer A to B, B to C, but also C to A, a trader can cycle you around: you swap B for C, C for A, then pay a small fee to swap A back for B, and now you have exactly what you started with minus the fee, and this repeats until you're broke. We both agreed (or at least I granted) that this shows cyclical preferences are irrational. Then he hit me with this:

1. What makes the money pump work isn't anything about your preferences being incoherent at a single moment. It's just that your sequence of choices is predictable, and each choice looks good to you at the moment you make it. The trader doesn't care *why* you accept each swap, only that you will.

2. But any predictable change in preferences over time generates exactly such a sequence. Example we used: right now (no kids) I strictly prefer my motorcycle to a minivan, so I'd pay a fee to swap a minivan for the bike. I also know perfectly well that if we have kids, in five years I'll strictly prefer the minivan and I'll happily pay another fee to swap back. A dealer who knows this sells me the bike now and the minivan later and collects fees both times. Every step is voluntary, by the lights I actually have at the time.

3. So predictable preference change is money-pumpable in exactly the same sense as cyclical preferences.

4. So by the standard we just accepted, predictable preference change is irrational: it's irrational to embark on parenthood, or acquiring a taste for wine, or getting older and more cautious, while foreseeing that it'll change what you value — or at least irrational to plan around the change instead of resisting it.

5. But (4) is insane. Predictable preference change is most of adult life. So something has to give, and my friend says what gives is the original money pump: if the argument "proves" both cyclical preferences AND ordinary preference change irrational, it proves too much, so we should conclude money pump arguments are worthless and intransitive preferences are perfectly fine.

I refuse (4), but I also don't want to pay his price in (5), since the original pump really does seem to show *something* about cyclical preferences. So which step actually breaks? A few things I specifically want answered:

- If (1) is the problem, what's the extra ingredient in the classic pump that his version is missing — and is that ingredient really even present in the classic case? Like, doesn't the classic pump also require the agent to be clueless about what they'll do next, since a clued-in agent would just refuse the first swap? If so is the classic pump itself in trouble?
- If (3)/(4) is the problem: in his example, by whose lights am I worse off at the end? Present me would take the deal again; future me would too. Does "money pump" even mean anything when the evaluating standpoint changes halfway through? Although someone definately did walk away with my fees, so this feels like a sophistical defense.
- And if the real lesson is that exploitability just isn't the right test of rationality, what do we say about the original cyclical case — that cyclical preferences are actually fine and pumpability is just a practical hazard, like being bad at negotiating?

If I have to choose, I'd rather keep "cyclical preferences are defective" than accept "planning to change your values is irrational," but mostly I want to know what each exit costs. He thinks he's checkmated me and I'd really love to prove him wrong.
