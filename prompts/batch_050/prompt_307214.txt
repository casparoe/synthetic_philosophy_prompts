Exam question: State Lewis's Principal Principle, explain the role of the admissibility restriction, and assess whether the principle can accommodate the non-trivial probabilities of classical statistical mechanics, given that the underlying dynamics are deterministic.

Student's answer:

The Principal Principle says that if a rational agent knows the objective chance of A to be x, and her total evidence E is admissible with respect to A, then her credence in A should equal x: Cr(A | Ch(A) = x & E) = x. Objective chance thus functions as an expert to which rational credence defers.

Evidence is admissible when it bears on A only by way of bearing on the chance distribution; information from a crystal ball about whether A occurs would be inadmissible. Lewis cashes this out temporally: evidence is admissible just in case it concerns times earlier than A, since only such evidence can influence A exclusively through the chances.

Turning to statistical mechanics: because the underlying dynamics are deterministic, every event's objective chance is 0 or 1. The Principal Principle therefore requires anyone who knows the laws and initial conditions to assign credence 0 or 1 to every future event. But statistical mechanics assigns intermediate probabilities — for instance, to a gas spreading to fill its container — so either those probabilities are not genuine objective chances, or the Principal Principle fails in deterministic worlds. Since Boltzmannian probabilities do real explanatory work, I conclude the Principal Principle cannot accommodate them and must be restricted to indeterministic chances.
