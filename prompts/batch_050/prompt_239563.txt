You are a professional philosopher specializing in the interpretation of probability. Think carefully before answering: begin by brainstorming the range of rules that could plausibly generate the verdicts reported below, and only then commit to your analysis.

A colleague of mine in the philosophy department has settled views about when it is legitimate to assign a probability to an event, as opposed to merely reporting one's degree of belief. Her verdicts so far:

1. A coin she has personally flipped 1,000 times, obtaining 503 heads: the probability that the next flip lands heads is roughly 0.5, and saying so is fully legitimate.
2. A coin fresh from the mint that no one has ever flipped: assigning a probability of 0.5 to heads on its first flip is illegitimate as a probability claim. She permits saying "my credence is 0.5," but insists that this is a report about the speaker, not about the coin.
3. A particular cesium-137 atom: the probability that it decays within the next thirty years (roughly one half-life) is 0.5, and this is legitimate even though the atom can decay only once.
4. The trillionth digit of pi, which no one has yet computed: the claim that this digit is even with probability 0.5 is illegitimate. The digit is already fixed; there is nothing chancy about it, whatever our ignorance.
5. A weather service forecast of a 70 percent chance of rain tomorrow: legitimate, but only in virtue of the track record — on past occasions when this service issued a 70 percent forecast for the region, it rained about 70 percent of the time. A service with no track record would not be entitled to the number.
6. A novel cancer drug never yet tested in humans: it is illegitimate to state any probability that it cures the first patient. Only after clinical trials does such a claim acquire meaning, and the trials are what fix its content.

Now consider a case she has not ruled on. Engineers have built a one-of-a-kind uncrewed probe, incorporating several novel components with no flight history. It will be launched exactly once, and the design will then be retired. What is the probability that the probe survives its only launch?

Please do the following, in order. First, identify at least two general rules about legitimate probability ascription — drawn from the standard menu of frequentism, propensity theory, and subjectivism, or refinements and hybrids of these — that are each consistent with all six verdicts, and explain how each rule accommodates the trickier verdicts (especially 3 and 6). Second, state what each candidate rule predicts about the probe case, and show that at least two of them diverge. Third, give your best prediction of her actual verdict on the probe case, with your reasons for preferring one rule as an account of her thinking. Fourth, describe one further case, distinct from the probe, whose verdict would most efficiently discriminate between the two leading candidate rules, and say what each possible verdict would show. Conclude with one or two sentences on what her position amounts to philosophically if your prediction is correct, and whether you take that position to be tenable.
