I teach an upper-year political philosophy seminar and I'm setting the final exam (three hours, this question worth 40 marks). I need one essay question on decision under ignorance in the Rawls/Harsanyi debate, and I want it to be unanswerable by writing "Rawls = maximin because he's risk averse, Harsanyi = average utility because of equiprobability," which is what half my students produce every year no matter what I do. I was griping to my TA about this and we came up with a setup I'd like you to build the question around.

The setup: a constitutional convention is choosing among three basic structures, call them A, B, and C. The delegates know the society will have three positions (worst-off labourers, middle professionals, best-off entrepreneurs) and they have a reliable welfare index for each position under each arrangement, listed in that order: A gives (26, 60, 40), B gives (32, 44, 38), C gives (28, 50, 52). Behind the veil each delegate knows they will occupy one of the three positions but not which — and this is the crux — Rawls thinks they have no basis whatsoever for assigning probabilities over positions, whereas Harsanyi thinks rationality requires treating each position as equally likely. The question should have three parts: (a) determine which arrangement each rule selects — maximin, expected welfare under equiprobability, and minimax regret — where students should find that maximin picks B while the other two pick C, and then say whether the agreement between Harsanyi's rule and minimax regret tells us something deep or is just an artefact of these particular numbers; (b) assess whether the conditions Rawls himself lays down for when maximin is the rational choice rule are actually satisfied in the original position, and what this shows about the textbook claim that Rawls is simply assuming risk aversion; (c) a delegate proposes a fourth arrangement D with index (10, 68, 45), a gamble that enriches the middle while abandoning the worst-off, and students must recompute minimax regret, discover that the ranking flips so that A now wins even though D is selected by no rule, and say whether this menu-dependence should disqualify regret as a decision procedure under ignorance — and whether anything analogous threatens maximin or Harsanyi's averaging.

Along with the question, write a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme out of 40. The scheme should name the specific moves an A answer must make — correct regret tables both before and after D arrives, actually stating Rawls's conditions for maximin rather than gesturing at risk aversion, noticing that Harsanyi's averaging only follows if the equiprobability assumption can be defended, and a genuine diagnosis of why the flip happens — and the specific errors that cap an answer at a B or lower, like computing regret against the best overall outcome instead of column by column, or claiming the veil of ignorance just "is" maximin.
