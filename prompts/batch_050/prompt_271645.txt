I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on bargaining and collective rationality — 16 students, one 75-minute meeting. Next week I want them to feel a distinction they've so far only met on paper: between a Pareto improvement and what some recent work on agent cooperation calls a safe Pareto improvement. Roughly: a deal can be better for everyone than no deal, and still be rationally unacceptable to someone, because "better" is judged against what each party thinks happens if talks break down — and that fallback is usually private information, or actively contested. A safe improvement is one that's better for each party no matter what story they tell themselves about the fallback. The phenomenon I want them to feel in thier bones: visible surplus that never gets collected, because every concrete proposal is safe for one side and unsafe for the other, and the negotiation stalls even though everyone at the table can literally see the better outcome sitting there.

Design an in-class game for this. Players should receive private information about thier fallback position, there should be a payoff scheme with small stakes but no money, and the negotiation structure should put a mutually better outcome on the table while making the obvious ways of splitting it fail the safety test for at least one player. Ideally a second round introduces some device — a mediator, a commitment mechanism, forced revelation of fallbacks — and unfreezes things, so they feel the difference the device makes rather than just hearing that it matters.

Give me: the rules written as I'd actually hand them out, a practical way to generate and distribute the private info, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the formal notion. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (I expect students to ignore thier private info and just haggle), and be honest about which feature of the real phenomenon the game can't reproduce.
