Just got back from the pub where my mate and I spent about three hours going round in circles about whether you're ever rational to just stick to your guns when someone equally clever disagrees with you. He kept saying I was being a "steadfast partisan" (his words, he did a philosophy degree) and I kept saying his view makes it impossible for anyone to ever be right about anything contested. Neither of us budged, obviously. So I've tried to work out what I actually think by going through cases, and I want to know if there's a principle underneath it all. Here are my gut verdicts:

1. We split a cab and a curry with a third friend and disagree about whether her share comes to £23 or £27. We're both fine at maths, both saw the same bill. I think I should drop to roughly £25 and so should he. Full concession to the middle, no shame in it.

2. My sister, who I consider my equal in judgement about politics, thinks the planning reforms will lower rents in our city; I think they won't. My gut says I should become less confident but not give up my view entirely — maybe shift a third of the way towards her. Not halfway.

3. A colleague I rate highly thinks eating factory-farmed meat is morally fine. I've thought about it a lot and I think it's wrong. Here I don't think I should move at all. Splitting the difference on a moral view feels repugnant — like I'd be treating my convictions as a thermometer reading.

4. I distinctly remember our mutual friend being at my birthday dinner two years ago; three other friends who were there all say she wasn't. I think I should just concede — she's not there in the photos and my memory is the only dissenting instrument.

5. My mate (same one from the pub) disagrees with me about whether free will is compatible with determinism after four pints, when I've had one. Honestly I think I should mostly ignore his disagreement that evening, even though sober he's smarter than me.

6. This is the one I can't sit still on: a disagreement about my own headache. I say my headache is a dull 4/10; there's no one who could disagree with me as a peer because no one else has access. But my friend pushed me on whether there's *any* case where I'd take another person's word over my own about my own sensations, and I found myself saying yes — if I were on strong painkillers and a nurse who'd seen a thousand patients on the same dose said patients in my state usually underreport, I'd defer a bit. Which seems to contradict everything else I think about first-person authority, and possibly everything I said in case 3 too.

So: is there a single principle that delivers verdicts 1–5, and what does it do with 6? If no principle gets all six, tell me which verdict you'd overturn and why — I suspect 3 and 6 can't both survive, but I can't see which one to give up. I also want to know what the principle says about a case I haven't listed: I learn that a prediction-market-style aggregate of a hundred strangers disagrees with me, none of whom I've ever met or vetted. Do I owe them the same deference I'd owe one trusted peer, more, or less?

One more thing: don't just give me a confident-sounding answer. Tell me what would change your mind — which case or argument, if it went differently, would collapse the whole principle, and where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong. I'd rather have a wobbly principle with its failure points marked than a smooth one that falls apart in a week.

And at the very end, give me one final line starting "Verdict:" followed by either the principle stated in a single sentence, or, if you think no principle works, the case number of the verdict I should abandon.
