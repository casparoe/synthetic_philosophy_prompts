I am a novelist at work on a book whose central figure surrenders the thing he most wants for the sake of someone he loves, and I keep circling the question of whether I am writing about nobility or about waste. To sharpen the conflict, I want a reading of Dickens's A Tale of Two Cities through Ayn Rand's critique of altruism, and I want the reading to be faithful to both sides of that confrontation.

Take Rand on her own stated terms: her stipulation that sacrifice means surrendering a greater value for a lesser one, so that giving up something for a person one loves may count as self-interested rather than sacrificial; her claim that altruism, in the sense descending from Comte, treats another's need as a moral claim on one's life and treats the capable as owing their capacity to the incapable; and her portrait of the moral code in which the good are defined by renunciation and the wicked by appetite. Now read the novel against that. Sydney Carton is the hard case and should get the most attention: his death for Darnay is the book's redemptive climax, yet he has spent the novel insisting his life holds no value of its own. Is the scaffold a sacrifice in Rand's sense, or a strangely Randian act — the only assertion of self his life ever achieves? Consider too the others who give things up: Dr. Manette relinquishing the shoemaking bench and with it the self that survived the Bastille, Miss Pross losing her hearing in the fight with Madame Defarge, and the seamstress whose brief companionship on the scaffold frames Carton's death as a gift to a stranger, not just to Lucie.

Where does the novel confirm Rand's diagnosis — does Dickens's moral economy really divide characters into those who renounce and those, like the Marquis, who take? Where does it resist her — does the book show sacrifice creating meaning and social cohesion in a way her framework cannot register, or does it merely sentimentalize self-erasure exactly as she would charge? Finally, speak to the craft problem: what would it take for me to write a self-sacrificing character who neither preaches renunciation nor stacks the deck the way a Randian hero does, and what does Carton, precisely where the Randian reading of him breaks down, teach a novelist about that?
