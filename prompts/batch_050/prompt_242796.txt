I work at a small policy-analysis shop and we keep getting burned by historical analogies -- last month someone argued from Munich that a rival will only read restraint as weakness, someone else answered with Vietnam, and both sounded equally airtight. My director now wants me to write, once and for all, a standard that any use of a historical analogy in our memos has to meet, so we can stop re-litigating this on every project.

Act as a historically literate philosopher of social science and think carefully: brainstorm the way analogical arguments actually go wrong in practice (Munich, Vietnam, the Thucydides trap in US-China commentary, 1914 vs 1938) before you write anything final.

Then give me the desiderata: what a use of a historical analogy has to deliver to count as evidence rather than rhetoric, with the case or failure mode behind each requirement, each marked non-negotiable or nice-to-have. Things I suspect belong on the list, but push back where I'm wrong: it must say which features of the two cases are doing the work, since any two events are similar in infinitely many respects; it must deal with the fact that the famous lessons each rest on one vivid case while all the crises nobody made an analogy about stay invisible; it must explain what to do when the same record yields opposite lessons (1938 says stand firm, 1914 says don't sleepwalk into war); and it needs some rule for when to stop trusting the analogy. If any pair of desiderata can't be jointly satisfied, say so -- that's the part my director will care about most.

One more thing: be explicit about which requirements reflect broad agreement among historians or political scientists and wich are your own methodological judgment -- don't dress a contested view up as consensus, since the whole point of the standard is surviving scrutiny. A one-line scoring of our current practice (pick the analogy that fits the policy you already want, then find confirming features) against the list is welcome, but keep it at the end; the criteria are the deliverable.
