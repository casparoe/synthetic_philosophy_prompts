I'm a psychology postdoc running phenomenological interview studies -- the kind where you interview people about some lived experience (my current project is on the experience of waiting for biopsy results) and the goal is to describe the structure of the experience itself, not to explain it, measure it, or test a hypothesis about it. Everyone in this subfield talks about "bracketing" but honestly the standard practice is embarrassingly thin: write your assumptions in a reflexivity journal before you start, try not to ask leading questions, done.

I've read enough Husserl to suspect his epoché and the phenomenological reduction are doing something more specific than the reflexivity-journal version -- something about suspending the natural attitude's automatic positing of the world as simply there, and shifting attention from the object of experience to the way the object shows up in experience -- but if you asked me to actually do that on a Tuesday afternoon with a transcript in front of me I'd be lost. So that's what I want from you: turn the epoché / reduction into a genuine working procedure I can follow across a study. I do about three interviews a week and will have maybe twenty transcripts by the end, and analysis time is scarce, so it needs to be runnable in practice, not a meditation regime.

Concretely, I'd like:

1. The procedure broken into phases (before interviews, during interviews, during analysis), with the actual moves in each phase stated as things I can do or check, not attitudes I should cultivate. E.g. not "adopt a stance of openness" but something with a pass/fail-ish test attached.
2. For each phase, at least one concrete sign that the bracketing has failed and what the recovery move is -- like noticing mid-interview that I've asked a question that presupposes the participant's fear is "about" an objectively real prognosis, or catching myself in analysis coding a passage as denial when that's a psychological explanation, not a description of how the waiting feels from inside.
3. An honest note at each step about whether what I'm doing there is still Husserl's thing or a pragmatic borrowing, and whether the difference matters for a psychology study that was never going to be transcendental philosophy anyway. I want the fidelity police flagged in advance, not surprised by them at a conference.
4. The points where the procedure has to hand over to judgement because no rule will decide it -- and how to stop those points becoming a licence to just do whatever.
5. A worked run-through on two moments from my kind of study: one where a participant keeps offering their own causal theory of their anxiety instead of describing it, and one where the bracketing seems to be going fine but produces something so thin it's useless, so I can see what the procedure does when it works *too* well.
6. A short paragraph on what this procedure structurally cannot deliver, so I don't overclaim in the methods section.

Write it in British English please, and don't worry about citing chapter and verse from Husserl's books -- I care about the procedure being faithful to the ideas, not about page numbers.
