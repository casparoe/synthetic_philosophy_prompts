Ok so I'm a retired organic chemist (37 years at a pharma company, mostly process chemistry) and since retiring I've gotten really into philosophy and history of science. One thing keeps bugging me. I've been reading about "Whig history" -- the Butterfield thing where you write the past as if it was all stumbling toward us, the good guys who anticipated modern views vs the bad guys who held us back. Fine, I get why that's bad for political history. But for science it seems genuinely tricky, because science (unlike, say, constitutional arrangements) arguably DOES progress. Phlogiston really was wrong. We really do know more than we did in 1750. So when people say "don't write Whiggishly," I can't tell what's actually being forbidden.

So here's what I want: a spec sheet. What would an adequate criterion for non-Whiggish history of science have to deliver? Not a definition of Whig history -- I've seen definitions -- but the desiderata, with the case or consideration motivating each one. Some things I'd want it to handle, and you should tell me if I'm asking for the right things:

- It has to let the historian say Lavoisier beat Priestley without turning Priestley into a fool -- he was, by the standards of evidence available to him, doing something reasonable. So presumably there's a distinction between "wrong by our lights" and "unreasonable by theirs" that the criterion must respect?
- It has to deal with the selection problem: even a historian who scrupulously judges everyone by their own standards still CHOOSES which episodes to write about using present-day knowledge of what mattered. Is that already Whiggish, or is there a way a criterion can permit present-guided selection while forbidding present-guided judgment?
- It has to handle the cases where the "loser" was arguably right by the standards of the time -- Wegener and continental drift comes to mind, geologists weren't crazy to resist him -- and the cases where the loser was just wrong even by their own lights.
- It has to be consistent with the claim (which I believe, maybe naively) that science is genuinely progressive -- that "we know more now" is a fact, not just the victors writing history. If a criterion for non-Whiggishness forces you to give that up, I want that flagged, not smuggled in.
- It has to say something about anachronistic concepts generally -- like describing 17th century work as "chemistry" when the people doing it wouldn't have sorted it that way.

For each desideratum give me the case or consideration behind it, and flag which ones you think are genuinely required vs which are just one camp's taste (I gather historians and internalist philosophers of science fight about this). And the part I'd most like: are any of these jointly unsatisfiable? My chemist's intuition is that "judge past science only by its own standards" and "science really progresses" might not be compatible without some extra machinery, but I can't quite see what the machinery would be.

Also, if you think one of my bullet points is confused or rests on a false presupposition, say so rather than politely working around it. I spent my career getting told my experiments were badly designed, I can take it.
