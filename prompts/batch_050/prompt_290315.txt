Okay so I need a sanity check before I potentially blow up my financial life. I've been a pastry chef for about 11 years, and my husband and I want to open our own bakery-cafe this fall. We've saved around 60k and we'd take out a loan against the house for the rest.

Here's the thing. My brother-in-law, who does small business lending at a bank, sat us down last week and basically told us not to do it. His pitch: the large majority of cafes and restaurants dont survive more than a few years, and he has personally watched dozens of optimistic owners walk in with detailed binders full of plans and projections and then go under anyway. His exact line was "everyone's got a binder."

And look, I get it. The weird part is I've learned this exact lesson in other areas of my life. I used to bet on football based on my deep knowledge of the teams and I did worse than just copying the oddsmakers. Our kitchen reno took twice as long as I swore it would. When it's other people I see it clearly — my coworker was convinced her terrible boyfriend would change and from the outside it was completely obvious how that ends.

But this feels genuinely different, and I want to know if that's a real feeling or a trap. The failure stats describe the average owner. I have information those stats dont capture: I've run a kitchen for a decade, our neighborhood has zero decent bakeries and a big apartment complex just opened two blocks away, and my stuff sells out at every farmers market stall we've tested. My brother-in-law counters that every failed owner ALSO thought they had special information, and honestly I can't tell him he's wrong.

One more thing bugging me: the people who succeeded presumably all ignored advice exactly like his. So does following the stats just screen you out of ever being one of the success stories? Or is that itself a survivorship trick my brain is playing?

So my questions: is there any rational way to weigh "what usually happens to people like me" against "the specific things I know about my own case"? Is believing your case is different always irrational, or is there a principled way to tell when it isn't? And is there anything I could actually DO to figure out which one of us is being reasonable before we sign the loan papers — some test that doesn't just circle back to my own judgement, which is obviously the exact thing in dispute?
