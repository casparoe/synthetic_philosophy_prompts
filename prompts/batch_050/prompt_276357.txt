I am a high school senior writing my capstone philosophy paper on sweatshop ethics, and I keep running into the same wall: every author seems to argue by picking one example, announcing a verdict about it, and calling that a theory. Before I read any more of them, I want the specification first -- what a theory of wrongful exploitation would actually have to do to count as adequate for judging sweatshop labor specifically. Please write me that list of desiderata.

Here is what I need from it:

1. The cases the theory must get right. A sweatshop that pays above every local alternative is the obvious one, but the list should cover the neighboring cases too -- emergency price gouging, payday lending, selling a kidney, an unpaid internship, ticket scalping -- because I suspect the theory that handles sweatshops but says nothing principled about these is incomplete. For each case, say what verdict a good theory needs to deliver and why that verdict is a constraint rather than an intuition we could just discard.

2. The distinctions the theory cannot collapse. In particular: exploiting someone versus merely refusing to help them; exploitation that is wrong versus exploitation that is on balance justified; a bad deal versus a deal the other party had no decent alternative to; and wronging the worker versus wronging society at large.

3. The theoretical obligations. The theory must say something about why a transaction can be wrongful even when both sides consent and both sides benefit, or else it must deny this outright and accept the cost of saying sweatshops with voluntary workers are simply fine. It also must say whether the wrong (if any) belongs to the factory owner, the consumer buying the clothes, both, or neither.

To make this concrete, test your desiderata against a case I constructed: Meridian Textiles opens a factory in an impoverished region, pays double the prevailing local wage, and workers line up to be hired. However, Meridian also quietly funds political opposition to a proposed shipping port that would have raised wages across the whole region. In year one, every worker is better off with Meridian than without it. A theory that evaluates exploitation by comparing the deal to the workers' actual alternatives clears Meridian in year one; a theory that asks how those alternatives got so bad does not. I want to know which desideratum in your list adjudicates this, and whether answering the Meridian question should be a non-negotiable requirement or a matter of theoretical taste.

Finally: for each desideratum, give the consideration motivating it, mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp (e.g., Kantian disrespect views, unfair-advantage views, structural views), flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and tell me which single requirement knocks out the most existing theories. If some desiderata on the list turn out to be prejudices rather than genuine requirements, say so plainly. A brief scorecard of the main camps against the final list is welcome but optional -- the list itself is the deliverable.
