I write a column for a general-audience magazine that covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a piece on anthropic reasoning — specifically the dispute between the Self-Sampling Assumption (reason as though you were a random sample from your reference class of observers) and the Self-Indication Assumption (weight hypotheses in proportion to how many observers like you they contain). Below is my current draft. I'm not asking you to rewrite it; I'd like comments of three kinds. First, accuracy: have I misstated either assumption, the Presumptuous Philosopher case, or the way Sleeping Beauty connects to all this? Second, the invented case near the end is my own device for getting at the reference-class problem — does it actually do the work I claim it does, or does it smuggle in an answer? Third, throughout the piece, where am I presenting as settled something that people who work on this genuinely treat as contested? Please flag every passage where I've flattened a live dispute into a consensus, or conversely made a fairly agreed-upon point sound more controversial than it is.

The draft follows.

"Imagine God rolls dice before creating the universe. On one outcome he creates a cosmos with a trillion human lives; on the other, a cosmos with a trillion trillion. A philosopher learns the setup and reasons: if the larger cosmos existed, someone in my epistemic situation would be vastly more likely, so my existence is overwhelming evidence for it. She declares herself nearly certain of the bigger universe — from an armchair, before any telescope is pointed anywhere. This is the Presumptuous Philosopher, and she's meant to be a reductio of the Self-Indication Assumption, the rule that says you should favor theories in proportion to how many observers in your position they predict.

The rival rule, Self-Sampling, refuses her inference: treat yourself as a random draw from whichever observers actually exist, so the sheer number of observers a theory predicts doesn't, by itself, boost it. But Self-Sampling has its own embarrassments. It seems to commit you to the Doomsday Argument — the thought that since you're an ordinary draw from all humans who will ever live, humanity probably ends sooner rather than vastly later. And it gives what many consider the wrong answer in the Sleeping Beauty problem, where a coin flip determines whether Beauty is awakened once or twice with her memory wiped, and where halfer and thirder positions map closely onto the two anthropic rules.

The odd thing is that both rules, stated carefully, need a 'reference class' — the set of observers you're sampling from or counting — and neither tells you how to choose it. Count only exact duplicates of your current experience and SIA's multiverse-boosting mostly evaporates; count all sentient beings and SSA's conclusions shift again.

To see the problem, consider a case I've been using: the Incubator. A machine will create either one person in a red room or a thousand people in red rooms and one person in a blue room, by coin flip. You wake in a red room. SIA says: a thousand red-room people on one branch versus one on the other, so strongly favor the thousand. Now tweak it — you wake in the red room but with a sealed note in your pocket stating your exact neural configuration. If your reference class is 'observers with precisely this configuration,' the heads and tails branches may each contain exactly one of you, and the odds revert to even. Nothing about the world changed; only the description you sampled yourself under. The uncomfortable suggestion is that anthropic probabilities aren't facts about the universe at all but artifacts of how finely we individuate observers — which would mean the whole SSA/SIA dispute rests on a question neither rule was designed to answer."
