I am a PhD student in political philosophy, and this term I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate seminar on well-being and public policy: about 20 students, mostly second- and third-year undergraduates, with one 90-minute session available. We are about to begin a unit on national well-being measurement — indices in the family of the World Happiness Report, which rank countries largely on average life evaluations (Cantril-ladder style responses) modeled against factors such as income per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceived corruption. I want the students to feel, in their own decisions rather than through my telling, the force of two questions: whether such rankings actually measure happiness, and whether a country should organize policy around topping them. By the end they should have viscerally experienced how easily a published indicator comes apart from the thing it purports to track.

You are a veteran designer of interactive philosophy seminars and simulation games. Think carefully about the pedagogy before drafting anything: first brainstorm at least three candidate activity designs, note briefly what each would make participants feel and where each would fall short, then develop the strongest one in full.

Constraints:

- The ideas must show up in what happens, not in anything the facilitator says. Do not have the facilitator mention Goodhart's law, construct validity, or the World Happiness Report by name until the debrief.
- The activity must involve real trade-offs with small but genuine stakes (a posted league table, points, a token prize), so that the pressure to optimize the metric is felt rather than described.
- It must be runnable by me alone, in 90 minutes, with 20 students, using nothing beyond paper, a whiteboard, and a simple spreadsheet.

A suggested shape, which you may improve or replace: teams of four govern small fictional countries over several budget rounds, allocating spending among policy levers that differ in whether they improve citizens' actual well-being, raise the measured indicators, or both. At least one lever should raise measured life satisfaction while leaving actual felt well-being unchanged or worse (a national pride and optimism campaign, say); at least one genuinely valuable lever should be invisible to the index. Consider a round in which teams choose how the national survey office phrases or administers the well-being question, so that they discover measurement choices alone can move the ranking.

Deliverables:

1. Complete, self-contained rules and setup: country sheets, budget sizes, the lever menu with each lever's effects on (a) actual well-being and (b) the index, the scoring table converting allocations into ranking movement, and the round-by-round structure with timings.
2. A facilitator script, including how to announce league-table updates and how to handle teams that begin gaming the index early versus teams that play earnestly and lose.
3. A debrief plan: the sequence of questions that walks students from what just happened to the two seminar questions, mapping specific moments in the game onto the relevant concepts (Goodhart's law, the construct validity of life-evaluation measures, adaptation and reference effects, cross-cultural comparability, the political uses of rankings).
4. What will predictably go wrong when the activity is actually run — students reverse-engineering the scoring table, one voice dominating each team, the room treating the whole thing as satire — and how to handle each failure.
5. A frank account of which feature of the real debate the game cannot reproduce and that I will therefore have to supply myself in discussion.

Please write your answer in British English.
