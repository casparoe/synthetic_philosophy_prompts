I've been reading discussions among AI researchers about how to oversee advanced AI systems, and I keep running into the same analogy: supervising a model's reasoning step by step is like a math teacher grading a student's worked solution rather than just the final answer. People use it to argue that judging each step of a model's reasoning, instead of only rewarding outputs that look good, will give us more trustworthy systems — the way a teacher who checks the working catches lucky guesses, memorized answers, and copying.

I suspect the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, and I'd like an audit. Lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the student, the teacher, the written working, the final answer, the partial credit. Then identify where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that don't. Two worries in particular: a student's written working is usually what actually produced their answer, whereas I gather a model's stated reasoning might be a plausible story told alongside the answer rather than the thing that caused it; and a teacher can judge the steps because they understand the subject at least as well as the student, which seems to fail in exactly the cases where AI oversight is supposed to matter most — when the system outstrips its overseers. Once those disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that step-by-step supervision yields more trustworthy systems still stand, perhaps in a weakened form, or was the classroom picture doing all the work? And if there's an everyday comparison that fits the real situation better, I'd like to hear it.
