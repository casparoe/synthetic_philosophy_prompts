So I've been reading Hofstadter's I Am a Strange Loop and for my reading group I tried to write up an argument against the whole view, and now I'm annoyed because it seems to prove way too much. Here it is:

(1) On Hofstadter's picture the self isn't an extra ingredient. The "I" is a symbol the brain builds that ends up referring to the very system that contains it, and that tangled loop -- the pattern, not the neurons -- is what a self is.

(2) If what makes a self is the pattern, then anything that instantiates the same pattern (same symbols, same self-reference relations, same transitions between states) has the same self, no matter what it's made of.

(3) Any pattern like that is fully describable. In principle you could write down every state of the loop and every rule for how states lead to other states, in a huge book.

(4) But if the pattern is fully describable, then the book has the pattern too. It's the same structure, just written down instead of running.

(5) A book describing my brain's strange loop isn't concious. Ink arranged to describe sadness isn't sad.

(6) So either a sufficiently detailed book is a person, or (1) is false and the self is something over and above the pattern.

Both options in (6) seem nuts to me. The book conclusion is absurd, but (1) was the whole appeal of Hofstadter in the first place. And the argument generalizes -- swap the strange loop for any functional organization you like and it looks like it refutes computationalism about mind wholesale, which surely can't be refuted this cheaply.

If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) than accept that the book is someone. So: which step do you actually reject? Don't just list the standard objections and leave me to pick -- commit to a step, explain why it looked plausible, and tell me what dropping it costs. And if you think the argument is actually valid, then instead tell me which premise is cheapest to give up, and why that one rather than the others.
