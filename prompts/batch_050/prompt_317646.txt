Last weekend I got into a long and unresolved argument with a friend about whether omissions can genuinely cause anything. Her position was that talk of someone's failure to act "causing" an outcome is never literal causation but always a disguised judgment of responsibility — we say the gardener's failure caused the plant's death only because we blame him, and the causal work is really done by sun, soil, and so on. I came away thinking she's wrong, but I also couldn't articulate the principle behind my own reactions to cases. So here are my verdicts, and I'd like to know what, if anything, they add up to. First: a hired gardener fails to water the flowers he's paid to water, and they die — his omission caused their death, full stop. Second: a stranger on another continent also failed to water those same flowers — his omission is obviously not a cause of their death. Third: I promise a neighbor I'll water her plant while she travels, I don't, and it dies of thirst — I caused its death, and I'd say this even if the plant is of a kind that no one ordinarily waters, so that the "normal course of things" was for it to go unwatered and die anyway. Fourth: same promise, same failure, but unbeknownst to me a frost had already killed the plant the night before my first scheduled watering — my gut says my omission caused nothing, since watering would have made no difference. Fifth: two caretakers are each independently responsible for one of two things a plant equally needs, water and light; each assumes the other has done both jobs, both omit, and the plant dies — I want to say both omissions caused the death, even though neither omission individually made a difference, since the other omission would have sufficed. Sixth, the case my friend kept pressing: I declined to donate $50 to an effective charity this month, and someone plausibly died who would have lived had I donated — yet it does not feel to me as though I caused anyone's death, and I notice I want to resist that conclusion even granting the counterfactual.

So: is there a single principle that delivers all six verdicts, or at least five of them? I've tried a few candidates and each seems to fail somewhere. Pure counterfactual difference-making gets the stranger right for the wrong reason and is sunk by the two-caretaker case and arguably by the charity case. Adding a duty or expectation clause handles the gardener and the promise but seems to collapse back into my friend's view that the whole thing is moralized. Deviation-from-the-normal-course handles the gardener but founders on the third case, where the normal course was precisely for the plant to go unwatered. If you think one of these, or something else, can be repaired or refined to fit, please state the principle precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and must therefore overturn. Two further requests. First, I keep going back and forth on one case: I fail to water the plant as promised, but my housemate, unaware of this, stood ready to water it all week and refrained only because she wrongly assumed I'd already done it — did my omission cause the death, hers, both, or neither? Tell me what your principle says and whether I should let it settle my intuition there. Second, say what the principle predicts for cases I haven't listed: a sprinkler system that silently fails (can a machine's "omission" cause anything at all, and does that matter for my friend's reduction?), and an omission with a good outcome, as when I forget to send the message that would have triggered a cascade of events ending in harm, so that nothing bad happens — does my forgetfulness cause the happy outcome, and does your answer sit comfortably with the rest of the account?
