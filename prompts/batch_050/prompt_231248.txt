I'm in my last year of high school, and my physics teacher has a favorite saying she wheels out constantly: "A result that fails to disprove your hypothesis is evidence for it." She used it last month to explain why we repeat the pendulum experiment fifteen times rather than twice — every run that matches the prediction counts as one more piece of confirmation — and she used it again to reassure a classmate whose messy measurements still "supported" his hypothesis about friction because none of them flatly contradicted it. I've never objected in class because it sounds like plain common sense, and honestly it made me feel better about my own underwhelming lab reports.

Here's what's been bugging me, though. The statement "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "anything that isn't black isn't a raven." By my teacher's rule, observing a green apple — which plainly fails to disprove the second statement — should count as evidence for it, and therefore as evidence that all ravens are black. I raised this in class, half as a joke, and my teacher laughed and said that was obviously not what she meant. But when I asked what she did mean, the best she could offer was that science is about controlled experiments, which seems to change the slogan rather than explain it. I've been turning it over since: either the slogan is false, or it doesn't actually say what she needs it to say, or I have to accept that ornithology can be done from my fruit bowl.

Could you lay out the different precise claims that slogan could be making, from the most deflationary to the most ambitious? I suspect it might mean anything from "an observation consistent with your hypothesis is at least compatible with it being true," to "each positive instance raises your confidence," to something Bayesian-sounding where every non-refuting observation raises the probability by some tiny amount, to a restricted version that only counts observations drawn from a genuine test of the hypothesis. For each reading, I'd like to know three things: whether it's actually plausible on its own merits, whether it really supports the two things my teacher used it for (justifying fifteen repetitions, and crediting my classmate's null-ish results as support), and whether it lands you with the green-apple conclusion or offers a principled way to block it. Finally: is there any coherent reading she could have meant that does her classroom work without the absurdity, or does she have to give something up? Please present the readings as a list I can bring back to class — I fully intend to.
