I write literary fiction, and a dinner after a reading of my draft last week left me with a disagreement I keep turning over. Two friends of mine — Mara, a criminal defense attorney, and Theo, a memoirist — got into it over the antagonist of my manuscript, a man whose cruelty the novel traces, chapter by chapter, to an upbringing so brutal that by the final third of the book the reader possesses something close to a complete causal account of what made him. I did not take a side at the table, and I'd rather not; I want the dispute itself adjudicated.

Mara opened by arguing that my novel, whatever my intentions, is an exculpatory document. Her claim: resentment cannot survive total explanation. When we know only fragments of a wrongdoer's story, blame fills the gaps; but a reader who has been given every formative cause of a man's cruelty has been moved, willy-nilly, from the stance of a participant in moral life to the stance one takes toward a storm or a tumor — interested, perhaps sorrowful, but incapable of indignation. She thinks this is why real-life blame depends on a certain tactful ignorance: we decline to learn too much about our enemies precisely because we sense that knowledge dissolves the attitude. Blame, on her view, is something we protect, not something we discover.

Theo answered that this is refuted nightly by every reader who closes a well-made novel furious at its villain. Audiences resent fully rendered antagonists all the time; understanding origins is not excusing. He appealed to the old distinction between local excuses and global ones: showing that this act flowed from these causes may mitigate here and there, but the general fact that everyone has a causal history never yet talked anyone out of a grievance, and it shouldn't. Reactive attitudes like resentment and gratitude, he said, are not hypotheses about the metaphysics of desert that could be falsified by explanation; they are the fabric of regarding someone as a person at all, and treating people that way is not optional for creatures like us.

Mara replied that Theo had changed the subject. She wasn't claiming explanation is a general excuse that nobody can feel; she was claiming that for any particular person, sufficient understanding of another particular person makes resentment psychologically unavailable to them — and that the difference between the furious reader Theo describes and the reader of my manuscript is one of degree that becomes, at the limit, a difference in kind. The reader who resents a villain has been given a sketch of causes, not the causes. Hand her the whole ledger and watch the anger curdle into diagnosis.

Theo then said the view proves too much, because gratitude is the same kind of attitude as resentment, and understanding plainly does not erode it. A daughter who learns exactly what her mother's sacrifice cost, and what fears and compulsions drove it, does not thereby stop being grateful; often she is more grateful. If Mara were right, therapists and loving spouses would be constitutionally incapable of the warm half of moral life, having made the cold half impossible.

Mara's last move was to separate the two: gratitude, she said, does not require that its object be free in the way blame does — one can be grateful to good fortune, to a landscape, to a dog — so it survives explanation where resentment cannot. Theo closed by offering what he called the novelist's stance as a third position, neither participant nor clinical: the attitude of holding a person answerable for a life one simultaneously sees, in full, as fated. If that stance can be dramatized — and he said my manuscript dramatizes it — then it is psychologically possible, and Mara's impossibility claim fails at the level of human fact.

I would like a point-by-point verdict. Who is right about whether complete causal understanding of a particular person makes resentment unavailable to the understander? Is Mara correct that gratitude survives explanation in a way blame does not, or is Theo right that the two attitudes stand or fall together? And is the novelist's stance Theo describes a genuine third attitude toward persons, or merely a rapid alternation between the two familiar ones? Finally, is there some consideration — about fiction, about responsibility, or about what explanation actually does to a feeling — that both of them are missing?
