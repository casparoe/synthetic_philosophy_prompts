I've recently joined a small research group that assesses long-term risks to humanity — engineered pandemics, misaligned AI, nuclear war — and in my first month a senior colleague sat me down with the doomsday argument and told me it should change how I allocate the next decade of my career. I've reconstructed her version as carefully as I can, and although I'm convinced it proves too much, I cannot locate the step I want to reject. Here is the argument.

(1) Random position: I have no privileged information about where I fall in the sequence of all humans who will ever exist, so I should reason as if my birth rank were drawn uniformly at random from that sequence. (2) The datum: on the order of a hundred billion humans have been born before me, so that is my observed rank. (3) Likelihoods: conditional on humanity flourishing for millennia — a total population of, say, at least a trillion — the probability that a randomly sampled human falls within the first hundred billion or so is under ten percent; conditional on extinction within the next few centuries, with a total of perhaps two hundred billion, the probability of my observed rank is greater than one in two. (4) Update: my birth rank is therefore evidence favoring near-term extinction, shifting my credences by a factor of roughly five or more, and the size of this shift does not depend on the details of my prior — whatever I believed before learning my rank, I should now be that much less confident in a long future. (5) Genericity: nothing in steps (1) through (4) is specific to me, to this century, or even to Homo sapiens — the same calculation was available to a natural philosopher in ancient Alexandria, who would have concluded that civilization had only a few centuries left and would have been wrong; parallel reasoning applies to my position among all the years of my own life, to this era among all the eras in which the universe will host observers, and to our species among all the species that will ever exist. (6) Conclusion: either near-term doom is considerably more likely than our empirical risk models suggest — likely enough that refining those models matters less than the constraint this argument imposes from the armchair — or there is an error somewhere above.

My colleague presses (6) hard. If I reject the argument, she says, I owe her a principled diagnosis that doesn't also wreck probabilistic reasoning I rely on elsewhere: the standard case for dismissing Boltzmann-brain cosmologies, the inference from apparent fine-tuning, even the humble thought that I am probably not the very first human or the very last. My own confidence collapses somewhere around (5) — an argument whose form would have delivered a false verdict to essentially every past person in a position to run it cannot be a good argument for me now — but I cannot tell whether the poison enters at (1), at (4)'s claim that the update is prior-independent, or at some slide I have failed to mark as a separate step at all. Which step do you reject, why did it look acceptable in the first place, and what exactly does rejecting it cost the rest of your reasoning about self-location and evidence?
