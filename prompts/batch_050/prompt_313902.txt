I teach a small philosophy of science seminar, and next term we have a session on whether aesthetic criteria -- elegance, simplicity, "naturalness," the beauty of an equation -- should count as evidence for a physical theory, or whether they're merely psychological lures that have occasionally been lucky. My students keep treating the two sides as a stalemate of slogans, so I want something livelier to open the discussion. Please write an Onion-style news article, headline plus roughly 450-550 words, reporting on a fictional research institute (invent its name and its director; do not use any real, named living scientist) that has announced it will henceforth evaluate all theories of fundamental physics by blind audition before a panel of mathematicians and a poet, on the grounds that beauty has reliably tracked truth before, and that its experimental division will be dissolved or repurposed as a sculpture garden.

The humor has to come from getting the underlying debate right rather than from strawmanning it. The institute's press officer should justify the policy by appealing to the standard canonized success stories -- Maxwell's equations, Dirac's mathematical route to antimatter, Einstein's field equations -- as a straight-faced inductive argument, and the article should let a skeptical voice point out that this is induction on a cherry-picked sample. Somewhere in the piece, the disappointment of the naturalness program (supersymmetry and other elegant extensions declining to show up at the LHC) should surface as an awkward fact the institute waves away, ideally with a quote from a 'concerned' experimentalist whose ugly, ad hoc theory keeps matching the data. The multiverse may appear as either the reductio or the vindication of the policy, depending on whom the reporter asks. Keep it deadpan throughout: no winking, no paragraph where the satire is explained. If you invoke the views of real historical figures like Dirac, paraphrase them rather than putting words in quotation marks.

After the article, append a brief author's note (around 100 words, outside the fictional frame) in which you are candid about the epistemics of your own satire: identify the strongest, least mockable version of the position you are targeting -- for instance, beauty as a heuristic for constructing theories versus beauty as independent evidence for them -- state where the article exaggerates or leans on a contested reading of the historical record, and say what empirical result or historical argument would most undermine the satire's implicit verdict, i.e., what it would take for the institute's policy to stop being funny.
