I'm a PhD student in cognitive science (I mostly work on multi-agent learning models, so this is adjacent to but outside my actual research), and last week my reading group did Hofstadter's stuff on the self — the strange loops material, including the part where he writes about his wife after her death and what of her persists in him. It turned into a two-hour argument I still haven't settled, and I realized my own verdicts on cases don't obviously cohere. So: can an "I" be distributed across brains, and what principle am I actually using when I say yes or no? Here are my gut verdicts:

1. My best friend of fifteen years can predict my reactions better than I can and sometimes catches me acting out of character before I notice. Verdict: a real but small fragment of me genuinely lives in her head — not just a description of me stored there.
2. My grandfather died two years ago and the family stories about him keep getting more polished and less accurate. Verdict: nothing of him persists; a story about a person is not the person, however vivid.
3. Split-brain patients after commisurotomy seem to have two streams of experience in one skull. Verdict: yes, one brain can house two selves.
4. Flip it: two brains wired together as tightly as the two hemispheres of one brain (some future high-bandwidth link, so information flows between them as fast as across a corpus callosum). Verdict: still two selves, not one — the skull boundary feels like it matters. Though I notice this sits awkwardly next to 3.
5. A perfect scan-and-emulate copy of my brain made the day I die, running on a server. Verdict: that's not me surviving; it's a new person who remembers being me.
6. An ant colony where no individual ant knows the foraging plan but the colony behaves adaptively. Verdict: real colony-level cognition, though I hesitate to call it a self.
7. The widower who keeps internally consulting his dead wife — what would Margaret say — and whose actual decisions are shaped by an internalized model of her. Verdict: like case 1 but stronger; a fragment of her really does persist and act in him.

My tentative principle was: a self persists in a substrate to the degree that the substrate instantiates the self's characteristic patterns — the more faithful the copy, the more of the self is there. But case 5 wrecks it, since the upload is a far more faithful copy than my friend's rough-and-ready model of me, and I give them opposite verdicts. And 3 vs 4 suggests I'm smuggling in some parochial "one skull, one self by default" rule. So what I'd like: a principle (or two competing candidates) stated precisely enough to say which cases motivate which clause, an honest account of which of my verdicts the best version forces me to drop, and its prediction for a case I haven't listed — say, a person with advancing dementia whose personality is partly held together by a spouse and a caregiver who jointly remember and enforce her habits and preferences. Please don't just tour the standard positions in the personal identity literature; I want the actual reconstructive work. And be upfront about the epistemics: tell me how confident you are in the principle you land on, and single out the specific step in the reconstruction you're least sure about.
