I want a diagnosis of a debate I think has gone stale: the so-called problem of old evidence for Bayesian confirmation theory. As I understand the state of play: Bayesianism says evidence confirms a hypothesis when learning the evidence raises the hypothesis's probability. But evidence a scientist already knows has probability one, so it can raise nothing — yet standard examples everyone accepts, like the known precession of Mercury's perihelion confirming general relativity, involve exactly such evidence. The responses I know of fall into families: (a) bite the bullet and deny old evidence confirms; (b) use a counterfactual credence — how probable the evidence would have been had it not been known — and accept the awkward question of which facts get held fixed; (c) note that when the evidence was known, the theory often wasn't yet formulated, and say what really confirms is learning that the theory entails the evidence, which means giving up the assumption that agents know all logical truths; (d) treat the whole problem as an artifact of idealization and conclude that confirmation theory was never meant to describe actual historical episodes. These positions have all been on the table for decades, everyone in the literature knows them, and as far as I can tell nobody has moved.

My hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, not a conviction — is that the dispute is not really about probability at all but about what a theory of confirmation is for: a normative theory for ideally rational agents, or a reconstruction of how actual science assesses theories, and that the sides differ over whether confirmation is a timeless relation between propositions or a relation between an agent, a theory, and evidence at a time. Please assess whether that is the right diagnosis, or whether there is a live substantive question underneath — for instance, a genuine formal question about which counterfactual credence is well-defined, or a hidden empirical question about how scientists actually weight novel predictions versus accommodations. Support the diagnosis with what the positions above actually commit their holders to, not with a survey. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves the problem, a formal result, a change of question, or an argument that the impasse is permanent because the sides want different theories under one name. Finally, say how confident you are in the diagnosis overall, and single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of and why.
