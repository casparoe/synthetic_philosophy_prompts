I retired from molecular biology about three years ago and, having too much time on my hands, started sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local uni. Last week we did memes and the whole "Darwinian theory of culture" business, and afterward two regulars got into it over pints and neither would budge. I stayed out of it, but I've been turning it over since and I'd like an outside verdict.

The players: Dan, a condensed matter physicist who thinks memetics was a fad that died for good reasons, and Ruth, who did her doctorate in anthropology and thinks cultural evolution is a perfectly respectable extension of Darwinism. The argument as best I remember it, round by round:

1. Dan opened with: Darwinian evolution needs high-fidelity replicators. Genes copy almost perfectly, and that's what lets selection accumulate anything complicated. Cultural transmission is nothing like this -- a joke, a recipe, a fashion gets garbled and blended every time it passes between people, reconstructed from memory rather than copied. Lossy copying plus blending means no lineages stable enough for selection to work on, so "memes" is a metaphor at best.

2. Ruth replied that his standard is anachronistic and his fidelity bar is set too high. Darwin had no genes and no clue how inheritance worked -- he even dabbled in blending inheritance himself -- yet natural selection didn't need Mendel to be true. And actual biology is plenty lossy: mutation, recombination, bacteria swapping plasmids left and right, which is basically blending. If a bacterium doing that still evolves by selection, a joke mutating as it spreads can too.

3. Dan countered that horizontal gene transfer happens against a background of faithful vertical copying, and that's the whole difference -- strip away the high-fidelity replicator and there's nothing left for "evolution" to be about. Then he challenged her to name the unit: what exactly is the replicator when a casserole recipe changes at every retelling? Genetics at least has a principled answer to "what gets copied." Memetics never did, and he said that's why the field collapsed rather than matured.

4. Ruth said the replicator doesn't have to be an atomized little unit -- it can be a lineage, a chain of copies where each link causes the next even if every link differs a bit. We track species lineages through millions of years of change and nobody refuses to call that evolution just because generation one differs from generation hundred-thousand. Same with the casserole: there's a fact of the matter about causal descent even without perfect copying. Demanding a discrete unit, she said, is gene-chauvinism -- mistaking one implementation for the general theory, and biology of all fields should know better.

5. Dan's closing move: fine, but now the theory's gone vacuous. If any chain of influence counts as a replicator lineage, then literally everything cultural is "evolving" and nothing could possibly count against the view. A concept stretched that far stops predicting anything -- the problem isn't that the casserole isn't a lineage, it's that calling it one adds nothing over the ordinary talk of influence and tradition we already had.

Ruth's last word was that unfalsifiability might cut against the strong version of memetics but not hers, and then the pub closed.

So who's right about what? Please go move by move -- I'd like each of the five rounds rated, with a sentence or two on why. And tell me if there's something both of them are missing. I have my own hunch (thirty years of sitting through population genetics seminars leaves a mark) but I'd rather hear it fresh than anchor you on it.
