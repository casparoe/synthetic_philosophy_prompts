Theories of humour have been on offer for roughly two and a half millennia, and the menu has hardly changed. Superiority theorists, following Hobbes, take amusement to involve a felt triumph over others or over one's former self; incongruity theorists take the perception of a violated expectation to be essential; relief theorists, following Freud, treat laughter as the discharge of pent-up psychic energy; and more recent hybrids, such as benign violation theory, hold that humour arises when something is appraised simultaneously as a violation and as benign. Every entry on this list faces well-known counterexamples: puns and wordplay in which no one is bested, incongruities that elicit puzzlement or fear rather than amusement, laughter produced by tickling or nervousness that seems to involve no violation at all. Yet the debate continues, apparently with the same stock of arguments recycled by each generation.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is structural rather than evidential: the camps are answering different questions under a single label. Superiority theory reads most naturally as a hypothesis about humour's social and evolutionary function; relief theory as a piece of psychological mechanics; incongruity theory as an attempted specification of conditions on comic stimuli; benign violation theory as an account of the appraisal underlying the emotion of amusement. On this reading there is no single proposition over which the theories disagree, and the appearance of competition is sustained by treating laughter — a response with many heterogeneous causes — as the common data set that a theory of humour must explain.

Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a substantive disagreement here that could in principle be settled? If the former, say what would count as progress: splitting the field into its constituent questions, treating humour as a family-resemblance category rather than a natural kind, or something else entirely. If the latter, state the contested proposition precisely and identify what evidence — developmental, cross-cultural, comparative, or drawn from humour's pathologies — would actually discriminate between the theories. I want the diagnosis argued from what each theory in fact claims, not a survey of the positions, and an honest verdict on whether the question of what makes something funny is well-posed at all.
