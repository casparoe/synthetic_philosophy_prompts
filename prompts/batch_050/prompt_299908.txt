Ok so I run a small grants program (think ACX-grants-sized, not NSF-sized) that funds indie researchers, and I keep having to make a judgment call that I suspect has actual philosophy behind it: which projects are on a *degenerating* trajectory vs. a progressive one. Like, I know the Lakatos basics from reading around — hard core, protective belt, novel predictions — but knowing the vocabulary doesn't tell me what to *do* when someone's year-two report says "the anomaly turned out to be more complicated than expected, here's the revised model."

What I want is a decision procedure I can actually run at renewal time, maybe 30 minutes per applicant, that operationalizes the progressive/degenerating distinction: what questions to ask about the last funding period, in what order, what counts as a real novel prediction vs. a retrofitted one (this is the part I always get fooled on), when adding an auxiliary hypothesis is legitimate repair vs. ad hoc bandaging, and where the procedure has to punt to human judgment because it's a values call not a fact call. Mark the punt points explicitly, don't pretend they're checklists. I have no philosophy PhD on staff and neither does anyone reviewing these, so it needs to survive contact with smart laypeople.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a psi research group whose preregistered replications keep failing but who now say the effect is suppressed by skeptical observers, (2) an ML alignment project that keeps adding patches to its reward model each time someone finds a new exploit, and (3) my own favorite, a community doing rigorous self-experimentation on nootropics — I have a bad feeling the procedure will rule against them, so don't flinch if it does, I want to see the uncomfortable output not a hedged one.

Also tell me what the procedure can't do — e.g. presumably it can't tell me anything about a genuinely new field with no track record yet, but maybe there's more it misses.

Format: exactly twelve numbered items, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion — steps first, then the three worked cases, then the limits.
