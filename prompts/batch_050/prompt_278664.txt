You are a professional philosopher specializing in metaethics, with particular expertise in value theory and the wrong kind of reasons problem. I am a PhD student working on fitting-attitude analyses of value, and I would appreciate your comments on a sketch of an essay I am developing. Please think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the strongest objections before drafting your comments.

Here is my essay sketch:

The familiar problem for fitting-attitude (FA) accounts is this: if to be admirable just is to merit admiration, then a demon who credibly threatens to destroy the world unless you admire him seems to give you a reason to admire him, and hence seems to count as admirable, which is absurd. I rehearse the standard replies — the object-given/state-given distinction, appeal to the internal standards of the attitude, and appeals to normative background conditions — and argue that each either smuggles in the very notion of value the FA account was meant to analyze, or else generalizes badly to attitudes like envy and amusement.

My positive proposal is novel, as far as I know. I suggest we individuate reasons not by their source but by their modal profile: a reason is of the right kind only if it would still count in favor of the attitude in nearby worlds where the agent's incentives change but the object stays the same. The demon's threat fails this test, because the reason it provides evaporates once the threat is lifted, while genuinely fitting-making features of an admirable person survive. I then extend the test to epistemology: a demon who punishes you unless you believe that p generates, on my view, a wrong-kind reason for belief, and I claim my modal criterion neatly separates evidential from pragmatic reasons for belief.

Finally, I consider a self-created hard case. Suppose the demon's character is itself such that he would only ever threaten people who admire genuinely admirable things. Then admiring the demon tracks admirability across nearby worlds, and my test appears to classify the demon as admirable — an unwelcome result I do not yet know how to block.

My questions: (1) Is my presentation of the existing literature accurate and fair, or do I mischaracterize any of the standard replies? (2) Does the modal criterion actually avoid the circularity charge I press against its rivals, or does it merely relocate it? (3) How serious is the hard case in the final paragraph, and can you suggest a principled way to handle it without abandoning the proposal?
