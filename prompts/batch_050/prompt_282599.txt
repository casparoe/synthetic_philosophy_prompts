I'm a novelist, and I keep running into something I gather philosophers call the "deliberation crowds out prediction" idea, and I want the gut-level version of it. When I'm plotting a book, I genuinely can't seem to do both things at once: either I'm *deciding* what my protagonist does at the climax, or I'm *predicting* it, and predicting feels strangely passive, like I'm a reader of my own draft. But here's the part that bugs me — I say probabilistic-sounding things all the time anyway: "she'll probably forgive him, but I might have her walk out instead." Some days the character really does seem to decide for herself and I'm just finding out, and I don't think that's only a metaphor, it's a real working phenomenon every novelist will reconize.

So two things. First, an intuitive explanation of what exactly is supposed to be incoherent about assigning, say, 70/30 credences to my own still-open choice — why can't deliberation and prediction coexist? Don't just gesture at "it's up to you," because on its own that's the conclusion, not the argument. Second: is the novelist case actually a counterexample, or at least a pressure point? Writing fiction seems to split something — the deliberator and the predictor in me take turns, and the character-deciding-herself experience looks like prediction *of* a choice that is nonetheless mine. If the thesis survives that, explain why the novelist's phenomenology is misleading; if it doesn't, say what the thesis should really be restricted to. I'd rather have the intuition pumped properly than a survey of who's said what.
