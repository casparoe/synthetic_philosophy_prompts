Two years ago, shortly before my aunt died, I promised her that I would scatter her ashes at a particular stretch of coastline she loved, even though doing so is mildly illegal there and inconvenient for me, and even though she explicitly said at the time that she would never know whether I followed through. I intend to keep the promise, but when I tried to explain to a friend why I feel bound, I found I had no stable answer, and this has since spiraled into a recurring argument in my philosophy reading group that has now stalled in a way I find genuinely frustrating. Everyone agrees promises bind, at least normally. But the group splits three ways on why: some ground the obligation in the social practice of promising, arguing that the moral force comes from the convention and the good it does; others locate it in the expectations and reliance the promisor deliberately creates in the promisee; and a third faction insists the bindingness lies in the promisor's voluntary act itself, a kind of transfer of authority over one's own future conduct that needs no practice and no reliance to be real.

What bothers me is that each side seems to win exactly the cases the others can't handle, and the debate just cycles. My own case seems fatal to the expectation view, since my aunt can no longer expect or rely on anything, and yet I still feel bound. The practice people counter with a standard problem: two strangers marooned together, with no shared convention of promising, seemingly bound by a mutual promise anyway. The will theorists get asked why mere words, sincerely uttered, should change anyone's moral situation at all, especially when the promisee refuses to accept or releases the promisor. Nobody has conceded anything in months, and I'm no longer sure the sides are even disagreeing about the same thing. My working hypothesis is that the dispute is really three different questions wearing one label: why the institution of promising exists, what a promisor owes a promisee, and what explains the phenomenology of feeling bound — and that my own deathbed case feels decisive only because I'm switching between these without noticing.

I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive moral disagreement, a verbal one, or a case of three accounts answering different questions under one name? Is there a hidden empirical or psychological question doing the work — say, about how promising actually functions in practice or why we experience it as binding — or are we down to bedrock intuitions about particular cases that nobody can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a sharper distinction between the questions, a new test case the views haven't yet metabolized, or a reframing of the problem altogether? If you think the impasse is genuine and principled rather than an artifact of confusion, I'd like to hear that argued too, since an honest verdict of irresolvable disagreement would itself settle something for me.
