I'm in an environmental history seminar and we just spent two weeks fighting about whether it makes sense to say germs, volcanoes, or climate shifts actually did things in history -- smallpox clearing the way for the Spanish conquest, the Black Death reshuffling European labor markets, Tambora's ash giving everyone a year without a summer, the potato blight emptying Ireland. Half the class thinks this is just ordinary causal talk and nobody would bat an eye at it in a physics context, the other half thinks calling a microbe a historical actor is a category error that smuggles intentionality in where there's only mechanism, and our prof keeps warning that the whole framing slides into Diamond-style environmental determinism the second you stop paying attention (someone in class literally accused someone else of doing just-so stories with weather).

Before we get assigned the theory readings -- actor-network stuff, big history, the Annales longue durée crowd, the Marxist pushback -- I want the spec first. Write the desiderata for an adequate account of non-human causal contribution to history: which canonical cases it has to handle, which distinctions it cannot collapse (cause vs. background condition vs. trigger, mechanism vs. agency, nature applying pressure vs. societies being differentially vulnerable because of their own institutions and inequality), which results from epidemiology and climate science it has to stay consistent with, and which demands people put on such a theory are genuine adequacy conditions versus the house preferences of one camp. Give the case or consideration motivating each requirement, flag any pair of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied (I suspect the symmetry people want and the determinism guardrail pull apart, but I could be wrong), and say which single requirement knocks out the most existing positions. The theory should be good enough for actually writing causal explanations of events like the ones above, not just for winning seminar arguments, if that changes the list.

Format-wise: exactly eight numbered desiderata, each one a sentence or two stating the requirement plus its motivating case, with no introduction and no conclusion. After the list, add one short paragraph saying which requirement you'd defend hardest and what argument or evidence would change your mind about it -- basically, where the list is most likely to be wrong.
