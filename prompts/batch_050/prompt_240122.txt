I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on autonomy and paternalism, and I am setting the final exam (three hours, two essays chosen from four). I want one essay question on advance directives for dementia that cannot be answered by reciting the textbook opposition between precedent autonomy and present best interests. Build the question around this case: Elena Marsh, at fifty-two and in good health, signs a directive refusing all life-prolonging treatment, including antibiotics for any infection, once she can no longer recognise her children, and adds that no one is ever to tell her she is dying. Nine years later, with moderately severe dementia, she develops pneumonia. She is mostly cheerful — she sings, enjoys her meals, likes the ward's dog — but she cries "I want to go home" during transfers, pushes needles away, and, on one unsettled evening, says "I don't want to die", though she does not grasp that she is ill or remember writing any directive. Her daughter wants the directive honoured; her son argues that her present protests are her voice and deserve to win. The question you write should force students to rule for one side and, crucially, to say which feature of the case carries the philosophical weight: the identity relation between the directive's author and the present patient, the author's epistemic standing (she wrote without knowing from the inside what this life would be like), or the character of the patient's present valuing and whether "I want to go home" and "I don't want to die" can defeat a settled prior conviction. Please supply: (i) the question itself, answerable in about seventy-five minutes; (ii) a model answer of roughly 600 words; (iii) a marking scheme out of 25 marks with grade bands (roughly A: 18+, B: 14–17, C: 10–13) that names the specific moves an answer must make to reach each band and the specific errors that should cap a mark at each band — for instance, treating the daughter's and son's dispute as merely factual, or assuming the directive binds simply because it was autonomously made without addressing whether its authority survives the loss of the capacities that grounded it. Write everything in British English. The model answer must close with a final line of the form "Verdict: ..." stating in one sentence the ruling it defends and the consideration on which that ruling turns.
