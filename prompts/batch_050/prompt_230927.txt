In a popular book on rationality that I'm reading for my epistemology seminar, Aumann's agreement theorem is summarized like this: 'Aumann proved that two rational agents who have seen the same evidence cannot disagree. If each learns the other's probability estimate for a proposition, Bayesian updating forces the two estimates to converge, so genuine agreement to disagree is impossible. It follows that whenever intelligent, well-informed people persistently disagree — in politics, philosophy, or everyday life — at least one of them is failing to update properly, and lasting disagreement is therefore always a symptom of irrationality.' The book then leans on this to argue that deep, stable disputes between experts show that experts aren't really reasoning in a Bayesian way. I have a nagging sense that the theorem's actual premises are stronger than what this summary uses, but I can't pin down where. Which parts of the summary correctly state Aumann's result, which parts quietly swap its conditions for weaker ones, and does the book's conclusion about real-world disagreement follow from the theorem once it is stated carefully? Please distinguish what the theorem establishes from what the gloss claims, and say how much practical significance for ordinary disagreement survives the correction.
