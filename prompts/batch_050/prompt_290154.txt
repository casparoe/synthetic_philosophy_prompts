For an upper-level undergraduate seminar in normative ethics — fourteen students, one eighty-minute meeting — design a structured activity that makes the category of supererogation, acts that are morally good but not morally required, feel both indispensable and unstable. Some context for why I want this: we have just finished a unit on the demandingness of consequentialism, and in discussion my students were unanimous that obviously one is not required to donate one's spare kidney, work oneself ragged for strangers, or give away everything above subsistence; morality, they insisted, asks only so much, and going further is admirable but optional. When I pressed them on where exactly the line between duty and what lies beyond it falls, the confidence evaporated, but in a sterile way — they shrugged rather than felt the problem. I want an activity in which the pressure points arise from what they themselves do, not from anything I tell them. There should be no lecture beforehand, and the word supererogation should not be uttered until the debrief.

The activity should run on iterated forced choices with small, real stakes — say, points or tokens that determine something mildly desirable, structured so that participants repeatedly decide how much to contribute, sacrifice, or exert for others — and it should be engineered in two movements. First, the rules should reliably produce the intuition that certain contributions are praiseworthy but not owed, so that the group itself generates the duty/beyond-duty distinction. Second, the facilitator should then force the group to fix the exact threshold: at what contribution does one stop merely satisfying duty and start exceeding it? The point is for them to discover that wherever the line is drawn, an arbitrarily small step on either side of it seems incapable of marking the difference between obligation and saintliness, while refusing to draw any line collapses into the view that morality simply demands the best available action. Please specify the rules, materials, and the stakes scheme (no real money), a phase-by-phase timeline, and the debrief, which should map particular moments of the activity onto the paradox that the morally best action is apparently not required, and onto the sorites-like worry about thresholds. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — for instance, students treating the exercise as ordinary fairness bargaining rather than a question about the structure of morality, or converging on a line out of social pressure rather than intuition — and how to handle each. Finally, include a frank note on which feature of the philosophical debate the game cannot reproduce, such as the difference between having most reason to act and being required to act, and how the facilitator must supply it.

Please present the entire design as continuous prose, the way one would describe a lesson plan in a letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. And please write in American English.
