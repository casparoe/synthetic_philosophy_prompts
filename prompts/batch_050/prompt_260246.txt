So here's something that came up in my political philosophy seminar last week and I keep turning it over. In the immigration debate people constantly reach for the club analogy -- a country is like a private club, and just as the members of a club get to vote on new admissions, the citizens of a state get to decide who joins. Wellman defends a version of this in the literature, but my students deploy a much cruder version, and last week one of them used it to argue that the so-called boundary problem in democratic theory is overblown: since the members get to decide on admissions, there's no deep puzzle about who belongs to the demos, just ordinary gatekeeping.

I want you to audit this. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the club charter, the current members, the admissions committee, the membership fee, the applicant waiting outside. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. Some pressure points I'd like you to hit at minimum: clubs exist inside a pre-existing legal order that fixes who counts as a member and enforces the rules, whereas the demos is precisely the thing whose constitution is in question; club members typically joined voluntarily while citizens are born in; rejected club applicants usually have other clubs to join; and border enforcement coerces people who never consented to the club's jurisdiction in the first place. Which of these disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion my student drew, and which are just pedantic noise?

The real question: can the club analogy even speak to the boundary problem, or does it silently presuppose an already-constituted demos and therefore only bear on admissions policy for a demos whose membership is taken as given? If the analogy was smuggling in the very thing at issue, say so plainly and tell me whether the "no deep puzzle" conclusion survives once the presupposition is on the table. And if the club picture is the wrong source domain, is there a better analogy for legitimate decisions about membership -- a family adopting, a language community, a jury being empaneled, something else -- or is this a case where we should just stop reasoning by analogy altogether?
