I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in applied political philosophy, and our unit on the legal regulation of sex work has gone stale. The seminar has hardened into two camps. One camp defends the asymmetric or "Nordic" model, first enacted in Sweden in 1999 and since adopted in several other countries, under which selling sex is decriminalized but buying it, and profiting from another's sale of it, is not. This camp holds that prostitution is bound up with gender inequality and that the state's aim should be to reduce demand and shrink the trade. The other camp defends full decriminalization of consensual adult sex work, as in New Zealand under the Prostitution Reform Act 2003, and treats regulated legalization of the Dutch or German sort as a flawed halfway house. This camp frames the issue in terms of labor rights, bodily autonomy, and harm reduction. Students on both sides have read the same materials, yet their debates keep collapsing into slogan-trading: violence against women versus whorephobia, exploitation versus agency.

I want to force them into a more disciplined exercise, and I need you to model it first. You are a political philosopher with long experience in applied ethics, criminal law theory, and the empirical literature on prostitution policy, and you are known for scrupulous fairness to positions you do not hold. Your task is to draft the joint statement that the two best students in the seminar — one a committed defender of the Nordic model, the other a committed decriminalization advocate — could both sign after a term-long adversarial collaboration conducted in good faith.

Work carefully and in stages. Begin by brainstorming, in private, a long list of candidate claims: factual, conceptual, normative, and policy-level. For each candidate, ask two questions. First, would both parties affirm it without hedging, or does it covertly presuppose one camp's framework? Second, is it substantive, or is it the kind of anodyne claim ("both sides care about the welfare of people in the sex trade") that papers over the dispute? Then consider where the parties genuinely disagree, and for each disagreement ask whether it is a disagreement about what the world is like, about what matters morally, or merely about words. Only after this groundwork should you draft the statement itself.

The statement should have four sections.

Section one: agreed claims. A numbered list of eight to twelve claims both parties sign. These must be substantive and non-obvious, not consensus pieties. They should include, among other things, points each side might have expected the other to resist — for instance, points a decriminalization advocate might concede about the empirical weaknesses of the harm-reduction literature, or points a Nordic-model defender might concede about the harms of criminalizing sellers or about the distinction between trafficking and consensual adult sex work. Where the two parties agree on a form of words but mean different things by them (as arguably happens with "harm reduction" or "exploitation"), flag the agreement as superficial rather than listing it as genuine.

Section two: a disagreements table. Each row should state one residual disagreement as a single proposition that one side affirms and the other denies, with separate columns giving each side's own formulation of its position, phrased so that side would accept the wording as accurate and unstrawmanned. For each row, classify the disagreement as empirical, normative, or conceptual, and say whether the collaboration dissolved any apparent disagreement that turned out to be verbal. Distinguish carefully between the claim that prostitution is typically harmful or coerced under current conditions and the claim that it is inherently or necessarily so, since the camps divide over this differently.

Section three: cruxes. Identify three to five sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. For each crux, specify the evidence, comparison, or case that both parties accept as bearing on it — for example, what changes in violence, reporting to police, health outcomes, market size, or trafficking flows would count, and why cross-country comparisons are confounded. Record each side's prediction in advance, as the parties would want it written down before consulting the evidence.

Section four: a short closing note in which each party states, in one or two sentences, what finding or argument would cause it to revise its position. If a party's stated position is in fact unfalsifiable or immune to revision, do not conceal that — but do not editorialize either; let the asymmetry, if there is one, speak for itself.

Two constraints govern the whole exercise. The statement must not be slanted: a neutral reader should be unable to tell which camp you, the drafter, belong to. And it must not be vacuous: agreements must have content, and disagreements must be stated at the point where the parties actually diverge, not at the level of slogans. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words across the four sections.
