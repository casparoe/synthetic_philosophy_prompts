I write a science-and-ideas column, and part of my job is vetting the short reader-question answers a new intern drafts before they run. Here's the current one, wich is based on this passage from Stevan Harnad's 1990 paper "The Symbol Grounding Problem":

"A thermostat may be interpreted as following the rule: Turn on the furnace if the temperature goes below 70 degrees and turn it off if it goes above 70 degrees, yet nowhere in the thermostat is that rule explicitly represented."

The reader's question: So is my thermostat actually doing symbolic computation when it regulates the heat, or not?

The intern's draft answer: "No — and yes, sort of. Harnad's point is that the thermostat only seems to follow a rule: we interpret it as rule-governed, but the rule isn't explicitly represented inside it, so it isn't really a symbol system. He draws on Wittgenstein's distinction between following an explicit rule and merely behaving in accordance with one. A genuine symbol system, on Harnad's account, has tokens manipulated purely on the basis of their arbitrary shapes, and the system as a whole must be semantically interpretable, not just one chunk of it. The thermostat fails the explicit-representation test, though Harnad would say it still follows a simple implicit symbolic rule wired into its hardware, just not a very complex one. The bigger issue lurking here is grounding: even a real symbol system's tokens only mean something because we interpret them, which is why Harnad argues symbols have to be anchored bottom-up in nonsymbolic sensory representations — and why he thinks neural networks should ultimately replace symbolic models of the mind altogether."
