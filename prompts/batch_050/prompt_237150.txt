So here's one I've been chewing on: say Jeremy Bentham gets a careful explainer on how modern AI systems work, including the weird parts -- that a digital mind can be copied for basically nothing, paused mid-run, forked into two identical successors, or rolled back to an earlier checkpoint. What would he actually say about their moral status? I'm not interested in the obvious "the question is not can they reason but can they suffer" take, everyone does that one. The harder question: suppose we have a digital mind that, by whatever behavioral markers Bentham would accept, is suffering, and we revert it to a checkpoint from before the bad experience. The mind that exists now has no memory of the suffering and no continuing experience of it. In the hedonic calculus, did a harm even happen? And more broadly -- does his framework survive a world where the *number of sufferers is a design choice*? If I can spin up a thousand copies of a mind, each equally real, does a factory producing mildly unhappy copies outweight one very happy copy, and did Bentham's math have the tools for that at all? Take a position on where the calculus breaks, don't just say "Bentham would care about suffering."
