I am a novelist at work on a book set in a near-future Britain in which a condition I have invented — a persistent, low-grade grief that never resolves into acceptance — has just been added to the official schedule of recognised diseases, and that classification now governs access to treatment, sick pay and certain legal protections. My editor and I disagree about how much the plot should hinge on whether this condition genuinely is a disease, as opposed to a value judgement dressed up as a medical finding.

Please describe the most important implications of the debate between naturalism and normativism about disease for how I should handle this. Distinguish three things: first, which implications bear most on the plot mechanics — who stands to gain from the classification, and what concretely follows if its legitimacy is contested mid-story; second, which bear most on characterisation — how a character's inner life, self-understanding and relationship with her own suffering would plausibly differ depending on which view of disease the society around her implicitly takes; third, which alleged implications are overrated — conclusions people commonly assume follow from normativism or from naturalism that do not in fact follow. Assume the novel's society officially endorses something like Boorse's naturalism whilst the characters themselves hold a range of views.

Please write your answer in American English.
