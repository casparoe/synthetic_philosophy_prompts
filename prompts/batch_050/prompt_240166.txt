Imagine that Henry Sidgwick had lived long enough to observe the effective altruism movement as it exists today: people giving away substantial fractions of their income on the basis of cost-effectiveness calculations, graduates deliberately pursuing high-paying careers in finance solely to donate the proceeds, cause areas ranked by expected impact per dollar, and a surrounding literature arguing that well-off individuals are morally required to do much more for distant strangers and future generations than common-sense morality has traditionally demanded. Suppose further that he had read these arguments carefully and found them, on their utilitarian premises, largely sound. My question is how he would interpret all of this in light of the dualism of practical reason — his notorious conclusion that the egoistic imperative to maximize one's own good and the utilitarian imperative to maximize the general good are both self-evident, that they can conflict, and that he could find no rational principle by which one could be shown to take precedence over the other. Specifically: would he take effective altruism as evidence that the dualism is practically resolvable after all — that reflective, informed agents can simply be brought around to the point of view of the universe, so that the apparent standoff was really a failure of moral education rather than of reason? Or would he insist that the movement changes nothing philosophically: that a minority acting on universal benevolence no more refutes rational egoism than a minority of ascetics refutes prudence, and that the banker who keeps his bonus remains no more irrational than the one who donates it? A complication I would like you to engage with directly: Sidgwick hoped that the dualism might be dissolved by proving the existence of a God who aligns duty with self-interest, and effective altruism arguably offers a secular analogue — the suggestion that doing the most good tends to coincide with a flourishing, meaningful life. Would he have accepted that empirical reconciliation as a substitute for the theological one he thought was needed, or would he regard it as beside the point, since the dualism was for him a problem about the structure of practical reason rather than about whether altruism happens to pay? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — your best prediction of his overall verdict — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. Commit to a prediction rather than surveying options, but flag the points where your extrapolation depends on a contested reading of Sidgwick rather than on anything he clearly held. And if you think this question rests on a confusion — for instance, if I have mischaracterized what the dualism was a dualism of, or if the question as posed simply cannot be answered from his writings — say so plainly at the start rather than answering a flawed question politely.
