Next month I have to defend the design of a randomised trial to my hospital's research ethics committee, and I want to go in properly prepared rather than relying on the usual hand-waving about uncertainty. The background, briefly: for the better part of fifteen years, a particular maintenance therapy has been given more or less routinely to patients in our oncology department, not because any trial established it works but because it crept into practice on the strength of plausible mechanisms and some encouraging case series. I have come to suspect we simply do not know whether it helps, and I am proposing a trial in which half of eligible patients would be randomised to continue it and half to discontinue. My argument will be that the settled convictions of the individual physicians who have grown comfortable prescribing the therapy are beside the point, because what matters ethically is the state of the expert medical community, which genuinely disagrees; and further, that we can head off the objection that interim results will destroy the trial's ethical justification mid-course by pre-registering explicit stopping thresholds, so that what the committee is approving is not my current state of uncertainty but a policy specifying in advance when accumulating data would require us to halt and release the result.

The committee I will face is the real difficulty. It includes two senior oncologists who have been prescribing the therapy for a decade and who regard withholding it as depriving patients of care; a biostatistician who I suspect thinks adaptive designs let investigators have things both ways; a patient advocate whose instinct will be that anyone randomised to discontinuation is being experimented on; a hospital lawyer quietly worrying about liability if a patient in the withdrawal arm does badly; and a lay member who will ask the bluntest version of whatever everyone else is circling. I would like a prep sheet: the objections I should actually expect from this particular audience, phrased in the words each person would use rather than in textbook terms, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each with the reply I should give in two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front to buy credibility, what I must not concede because the trial collapses if I do, and the question I am privately hoping nobody asks. Where the literature and regulatory practice genuinely speak with one voice on a point, say so, and where people who work on this stuff disagree — for instance, whether equipoise is even the right framework for justifying a trial at all, or whether my 'approve the policy, not the belief' move is a real solution or a relabelling — mark that clearly as contested rather than letting me repeat a minority position to the committee as though it were settled.
