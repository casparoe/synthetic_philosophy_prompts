I'm preparing a seminar session on supererogation, and I keep hitting a wall on a structural question my students raised. Standard presentations say a supererogatory act is good to do but not required: omitting it isn't wrong. But when I press on real-life cases, two pressures collide. On the one hand, morality clearly ranks the beyond-duty act above the permissible minimum, and the person who would have benefited often seems *entitled to feel let down* when the agent settles for the minimum. On the other hand, if that disappointment is genuinely warranted, it's hard to see the agent as fully beyond reproach — and then the act starts to look obligatory after all. I want a case that puts this tension under maximum pressure rather than dissolving it, so I'd like you to construct one to the following specifications.

Build a single scenario, ordinary in scale — no life-or-death stakes, no heroism, no saints, something that could happen between neighbors, coworkers, or friends — in which all four of the following hold simultaneously: (1) act A is genuinely supererogatory, meaning omitting it is not wrong and the agent has no duty to do it; (2) doing A is nonetheless morally better than the permissible alternative B, so morality itself ranks the options; (3) the person affected by the choice would be warranted in a mild negative reactive attitude toward the agent if she does B — disappointment shading toward resentment — without being confused about her rights or the agent's duties; and (4) the agent, fully informed and conscientious, is neither practically irrational nor defective in character for choosing B, so no legitimate criticism of her as an agent is available. The pair (3) and (4) is the crux: most people concede one or the other, and I want a case engineered to hold both. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph, and don't stipulate the verdicts — the case has to generate them from its structure, or it's useless for my purposes.

After the scenario, do the verification pass: state the act, the alternative, the relationship in play, and show why each of the four conditions holds, with reasons rather than labels. Then identify the load-bearing feature — whatever it is about the relationship, the cost of A, or the agent's prerogative that keeps the case from tipping either into obligation or into mere niceness — and describe what happens when you remove it (the case should collapse in a predictable direction; tell me which). Finally, I want a committed answer, not a menu: take a definite position on whether conditions (3) and (4) can be jointly satisfied, and say how the strongest objection from the opposing camp should be answered — either the Strawsonian argument that warranted resentment implies a defect of good will (killing condition 4), or the scalar consequentialist argument that the morally better, low-cost option was simply required all along (killing condition 1). Tell me which horn you'd impale the case on if you had to, and why your construction survives the other one.
