I teach a 90-minute seminar on normative ethics to about 24 undergraduates, and we spend two weeks on the demandingness objection to consequentialism. Students reliably understand the objection as an argument they can recite, but I want them to feel the problem in their own choices before we name it: specifically, the ratchet structure of the objection — that a maximizing principle, given the standing opportunity to convert one's resources into far greater impartial good, does not merely demand occasional sacrifice but consumes every resource one cares about, with no natural stopping point.

Design an in-class activity built around iterated giving decisions with an unannounced end. Students should hold a finite stock of tokens convertible into a small personal reward (a points-to-raffle scheme or similar — no significant real money), and across repeated rounds they should face opportunities to contribute tokens to a pooled fund that is multiplied for a collective benefit, with the exchange rate improving as the game proceeds, so that refusing to give becomes harder to justify on impartial grounds each round. The design should ensure that a student who gives whenever giving is impartially best is steadily stripped to nothing, while students who protect themselves must draw an arbitrary-looking line and defend it.

Please specify: the full rules and materials; the payoff and multiplication structure, with actual numbers; how to keep the number of rounds hidden and why that matters; and a debrief that maps what happened onto the conceptual terrain — maximizing versus satisficing versions of the view, agent-centered prerogatives to weight one's own projects disproportionately, the line-drawing problem for such prerogatives, and the worry that ideal moral agents would be unrecognizable as people. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for instance, students who collude, students who give nothing from the first round and so learn nothing, or students who contest whether token losses bear on morality at all — and how to handle each. Finally, be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce: token trades carry no normative force, so the game's losses are not obligations; explain how the facilitator should supply that missing premise in debrief without letting the objection collapse back into an abstraction the students can again merely recite.
