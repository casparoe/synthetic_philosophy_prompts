I’m a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. At a design review for a personal-knowledge app, we were debating whether the app should merely store notes or actively manage them: it uses spaced repetition to decide what to resurface, preloads documents it predicts the user will need, merges duplicate notes, and rewrites reminders so that they are more likely to be understood later. A product manager defended the more intrusive design by saying, “Your phone is part of your mind.” A privacy engineer objected that the phone is only a tool and that “the mind ends at the skull.” I later learned that the first slogan resembles the extended mind thesis, but I still cannot tell what exactly it is supposed to mean beyond the obvious fact that phones help us think.

Please unpack “Your phone is part of your mind” into a menu of distinct claims it could be making, ordered from relatively trivial to increasingly radical. For each claim, explain its content precisely, state what would count for or against it, and give a verdict on whether it is a platitude, a plausible empirical hypothesis, a defensible but contentious philosophical thesis, or an overreach. Please distinguish at least the following possibilities, while adding any important ones I have missed: the phone causally assists cognition; the phone functions as part of a temporarily coupled cognitive system; some information on the phone literally counts as one of the user’s memories or beliefs; the relevant unit of cognition is the user-plus-device rather than the biological user; and the phone has mental states of its own. Address whether matters such as reliability, speed and ease of access, automatic integration, user control, trust, portability, and the device’s ability to modify its own contents make a philosophical difference or merely an engineering one.

Then apply the best readings to our case. If the app deletes a note after the user has come to rely on it, has the user forgotten something, lost an external record, or suffered something closer to cognitive damage? If the app supplies a conclusion the user immediately accepts but could not have reconstructed unaided, whose belief is it? If the same information were in a paper notebook, an implanted chip, a shared team dashboard, or a cloud service controlled by an employer, would that change the answer? Finally, say which interpretations of the slogan would actually support treating the app’s outputs as part of the user’s own thinking for purposes of design, consent, privacy, and responsibility, and which interpretations are being smuggled in merely because “part of the mind” sounds compelling. Please answer from conceptual analysis rather than by supplying citations, a bibliography, or a history of the debate.
