A friend of mine from the rationalist community has a long-running project of polling people on deception cases, and over the years I've collected her verdicts on the standard inventory. Her judgments so far:

1. Asked directly whether she dented the car, she says no, knowing she did. Verdict: a lie, and straightforwardly wrong.
2. Asked the same question, she answers — truthfully — that she took the car in for an inspection last week, intending the asker to draw the false inference that the dent isn't hers. Verdict: misleading, not lying, but exactly as blameworthy as the outright lie; the fact that the falsehood travels by implicature rather than assertion does no moral work.
3. She tells a visitor the library is open on Mondays, sincerely believing it is; it isn't, and he makes a wasted trip. Verdict: not a lie, and not blameworthy — an honest error.
4. At a party, asked how she liked the host's speech, she delivers extravagant praise so over-the-top that everyone present recognizes it as irony. Verdict: not a lie, because there is no intention to deceive.
5. She arranges a demonstration knowing a colleague will draw a false conclusion from it, and then stays silent while he does, though she could correct him with one sentence. Verdict: not a lie — silence and omission aren't assertions — but blameworthy as deception all the same.

Last week I gave her a new case, and she surprised herself by hesitating. She wants a collaborator to believe the grant deadline is this Friday, so that he'll stop procrastinating. She tells him, flatly, that the deadline is Friday, believing the deadline is in fact next Monday. Unbeknownst to her, the deadline was moved last week, and it really is Friday. The collaborator finishes early and is deceived about nothing.

Predict her verdict: is this a lie, and is it as blameworthy as case 1? In answering, do three things. First, state the principle her five verdicts are tracking, and show that at least two genuinely different rules are consistent with the track record — I have in mind, at minimum, a belief-based account on which lying is asserting what one believes false with intent to deceive, and an objective-falsity account on which the asserted content must actually be false. Second, say which rule better fits the spirit of her verdicts on cases 2 through 5 — especially her insistence that blameworthiness tracks deception rather than the assertoric mechanism — and use that to justify your prediction about the new case, including whether she'd want to call it a lie, an attempted lie, or merely a blameworthy intention that the world happened to frustrate. Third, name the further case that would most cleanly distinguish the two rules going forward — I suspect something in the neighborhood of asserting a believed falsehood while expecting not to be believed, as when one lies to an interrogator one knows will assume the opposite — and explain what each rule predicts about it and why the verdict there would be diagnostic.
