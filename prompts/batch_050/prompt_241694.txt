at the interfaith dialogue night at my college someone pulled out the blind men and the elephant to settle an argument -- you know the story, each guy grabs a different part, one says it's a rope, one says a wall, one says a tree trunk, and the moral is supposed to be that everyone's partly right and nobody has the whole truth. it got deployed to end a fight about whether two religions' claims about god actually contradict each other: "they're just describing different parts of the elephant." it worked socially but it bugged me all week and i want to know if it deserves to work.

so audit the analogy for me. give me the correspondence table first -- what plays the role of the men, the elephant, the grabbing, the rope/wall/trunk reports -- and then where it breaks. a few things i keep circling: the story is narrated by someone who can see the whole elephant, which seems like exactly the vantage point the moral says nobody has, so is the parable secretly cheating? also in the story all the reports turn out to fit together into one consistent object, but what guarantees that real clashing claims are complementary parts and not actual contradictions, or that one of the men isn't just hallucinating a rope? does the analogy have any way to tell an elephant-dispute from a dispute where someone is simply wrong?

and then the harder question. as i understand it the Jains built a whole doctrine out of this kind of move -- anekāntavāda, reality is many-sided, and every judgment is supposed to come prefixed with something like "from a certain standpoint" (syāt), so that apparently contradictory claims can both be true conditioned on different standpoints. does the parable actually support anything that strong? it seems to me it supports at most "your access is partial, so be humble," which is way weaker than "the contradiction is only apparent" or "reality itself is multifaceted rather than our descriptions of it." can the elephant get you from epistemic humility to the full standpoint-conditional picture of truth, or is something else doing the work there? and if the parable only carries half the load, what's a better analogy -- or is the whole style of arguing-by-parable the problem?
