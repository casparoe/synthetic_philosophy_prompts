I’m teaching a 75-minute session on practical rationality to about 18 undergrads, and I want an activity that makes Buridan’s ass feel like a real problem instead of a silly medieval meme. The hard part is that I don’t want them to merely choose between two literally identical prizes and then say “whatever, just pick one.” The game should put them under some time-sensitive incentive where endlessly searching for a non-arbitrary reason becomes costly, but where any obvious tie-breaker (left/right, first option, favorite letter, randomization, etc.) can itself be interrogated as either arbitrary or smuggled-in preference.

Design a game or structured simulation in which participants repeatedly face choices that are symmetric not only in expected payoff but also in their available evidence. Ideally, the rules should create pressure toward an interesting split in the room: some people decide immediately, some deliberate too long, some try to randomize, and some invent principles for tie-breaking. The point should emerge from what happens before I explain anything: that choosing without a preference may be unavoidable, but it’s not obvious whether the resulting choice is rational, irrational, or simply non-rational. Please don’t let the game collapse into “just flip a coin”; randomization should itself become philosophically interesting, since deciding to use a coin and deciding when to stop deliberating are also choices.

Give me concrete setup and materials, exact rules and incentives (no real money required), a round-by-round timeline, suggested variants for students who try to exploit the symmetry, and a debrief that maps different moments in the activity onto choice, preference, reason-responsiveness, and arbitrary but rational action. Also include a few likely ways the activity will go wrong the first time I run it -- e.g. students assuming there must be a hidden payoff difference, or refusing to play because the situation is artificial -- and how to handle that. Finally, be honest about which feature of the philosophical problem the activity can’t reproduce, especially whether real deliberation always contains tiny asymmetries that the game artificially removes.
