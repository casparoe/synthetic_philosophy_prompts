I'm writing a short essay for a reading group on the philosophy of perception / aesthetics, and I want some outside feedback before I embarass myself in front of them. The topic is the old question of whether we literally *see* things through photographs -- Walton's transparency thesis and the objections people have raised against it (eg that photos don't give you egocentric spatial info, or that you can't act on what a photo shows you). Here's the draft as it stands:

---

**Seeing Through, Halfway**

The transparency debate assumes seeing-through is all-or-nothing: either photographs are transparent and we literally see great-grandpa when we look at his portrait, or they're not and we merely infer what he looked like. I want to reject the framing. Transparency comes in degrees, and the degree is fixed by what I'll call the *bandwidth of counterfactual dependence* between viewer and scene.

A periscope is (nearly) fully transparent. If the ship turned ten degrees, the image would change accordingly; and this holds across a huge range of possible changes -- in lighting, position, occluders, whatever. A photograph preserves dependence only along the dimensions the apparatus was set up to record: brightness patterns at one instant, from one fixed vantage, within one frame. Everything else is flattened. So a photo is like a filter with a very narrow pass-band: the scene genuinely shows up in the image, but only a thin slice of the scene's counterfactual profile is transmitted to the viewer. Seeing-through isn't binary; it's a matter of how wide the channel is.

The predictable objection is temporal delay: we see stars that may have died millennia ago, and delay doesn't block seeing, so delay can't be what blocks transparency in photos. My view happily agrees. Direct star-seeing is high-bandwidth across time -- the dependence is continuous and ongoing, just delayed. A photo of the star is low-bandwidth: one frozen slice. The star case is therefore no threat to me; it actually fits my picture better than the orthodox one.

Second claim: live video calls are transparent, full stop. Some people resist this because the image on a screen is *computed* -- encoded, compressed, reconstructed. But eyeglasses reshape light too, and nobody thinks glasses make seeing indirect. What matters is the richness and responsiveness of the dependence, not whether the medium is "natural."

Payoff: staged photos and deepfakes. On the all-or-nothing view, a staged photo is still transparent (you really do see the actor), and a deepfake just isn't. My gradual view can say something subtler: a composited image is transparent *in patches* -- transparent to the backdrop, opaque to the inserted face. That seems to me exactly the right thing to say about them.

---
