I'm in an environmental ethics reading group and last week two of the regulars, Marcus and Sofia, got into a proper argument about climate spending that nobody managed to settle. I've tried to reconstruct it as faithfully as I can, and I'd like a referee.

(1) Marcus's opening move was that rich countries today shouldn't be making expensive emissions cuts; instead they should invest the equivalent sums in broad index funds and hand the pot to future generations. Growth compounds at maybe 4% a year, so by 2200 the pot is enormous, and future people will be richer and more technologically capable than us anyway, so they can buy adaptation, carbon removal, whatever they need. Making ourselves poorer now to spare richer people later, he said, is backwards. (2) Sofia replied that this only works if you smuggle in a discount rate, and that discounting future welfare just because it's future is indefensible: a flood death in 2160 isn't less bad than one now, and unlike ordinary costs, a lot of climate damage (ice sheets, species, tipping points) isn't something a richer 2200 can simply purchase its way out of, so the invest-instead comparison is rigged from the start. (3) Marcus countered with two points. First, that nobody really believes in zero discounting, because over long horizons it makes any tiny chance of an astronomically good or bad far future swamp everything, committing you to present people living in misery to shave a whisker off some speculative cosmic risk, which Sofia plainly doesn't accept. Second, he wheeled out the non-identity problem: the people of 2200 only exist because of the whole carbon-intensive path of history, different policies would mean different people, so they can't claim we've wronged them, since their alternative isn't a cleaner world, it's never existing. (4) Sofia accepted you can discount for growth and uncertainty but not for time itself, and said the non-identity move proves too much: by that logic you could excuse anything identity-affecting, even deliberately engineering a generation to have miserable short lives provided that policy was what brought them into existence. If a victim can't say "I'd have been better off otherwise" only because otherwise they wouldn't exist, our duty to them can't just dissolve; we should compare the outcomes impersonally.

Neither conceded anything and I genuinely can't tell where the truth lies. Please give me your adjudication as a numbered list of exactly six items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: items one to four rating each of the four moves above from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, item five on what both of them are missing, and item six on who comes out more right overall.
