Last weekend a friend and I spent the better part of a long dinner arguing about Singer's drowning child case, and I came away unsettled about my own commitments. My friend's position was the familiar one: if you can prevent something very bad from happening at a cost to yourself that is small by comparison, you are morally required to do it, and it makes no difference whether the person in danger is thrashing in a pond in front of you or dying of a preventable disease on another continent. I kept insisting that the principle, so stated, proves too much, but when he pressed me to say exactly where it breaks, I found myself giving a list of case-by-case verdicts rather than anything principled. I would like to know what principle, if any, actually captures my pattern of responses, so I will lay the cases out as honestly as I can.

First, the classic: a child is drowning in a shallow pond, I am the only passerby, and rescuing her will ruin a pair of shoes I care about. Clearly I must wade in, and anyone who walked past would be monstrous. Second, a variation: the same pond, but now I could instead, for roughly the price of those shoes, fund a highly effective intervention that would with near certainty save a child's life in a distant country. Here I balk: I think contributing is good and admirable, but I do not think I do something seriously wrong, let alone something monstrous, if I keep my money. Third, a different pond: the child is drowning in rough surf, and attempting the rescue carries something like a one-in-ten chance of my own death. I think attempting it is heroic but not required. Fourth: the same rough surf, same one-in-ten risk, but the child is in the water because I carelessly knocked her off my boat. Now I think I am required to try, at the very same level of personal risk I just called supererogatory. Fifth, the bystander case: a shallow pond, ten other people standing around equally able to help, all of them doing nothing. I think my duty to wade in is not diminished by their presence, though I also think that if a trained lifeguard were on duty, the duty would pass to her entirely. Sixth, the recurring case: a neighbor with a chronic illness needs someone to drive her to treatment twice a week for the indefinite future, and I am the only one reliably available. Each individual drive costs me little, but I do not think I am obligated to sign up for years of it, even though declining means she will often go without care.

So here is what I want. Either produce a single precisely stated principle of the duty of rescue that delivers all six of my verdicts, with each clause of the principle explicitly motivated by the case that forces it, or tell me plainly that no non-ad-hoc principle can do this and identify which verdict or verdicts I should abandon, and why those are the right ones to sacrifice rather than the others. My suspicion is that the second and fourth cases are the hardest to reconcile with the rest, but I am open to being told the tension lies elsewhere. Whatever principle you settle on, please also tell me what it predicts for two cases I did not list: whether I am obligated to register as a bone marrow donor when registration is nearly costless but actually donating involves real pain and a few days of recovery, and whether the ten idle bystanders from my fifth case each do something as wrong as I would do if I alone walked past the shallow pond. Finally, I would like the answer written as continuous prose, the way one would write a long email or letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
