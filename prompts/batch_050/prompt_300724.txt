I got into a long argument with some friends last weekend about NIMBYism and realized I don't actually know what I think -- like, I keep calling people NIMBYs and then turning around and defending someone doing basically the same thing. So I went home and wrote down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I want to know if there's an actual principle under there or if I'm just a hypocrite with good marketing. Here they are. (1) A wealthy suburb of single-family homes votes down a six-story apartment building near the train station because of "neighborhood character": clearly bad, textbook NIMBYism. (2) A poor neighborhood fights off a proposed waste transfer station that would bring truck traffic and smells: clearly fine, that's just self-defense. (3) A tenant of twenty years opposes a luxury condo tower on her block because it will jack up surrounding rents and she'll be pushed out: fine, sympathetic. (4) Homeowners oppose a duplex conversion on their street because it'll change the feel of the block and maybe affect property values: not fine, basically same as (1). (5) A neighborhood opposes a homeless shelter near an elementary school: I wince, but I mostly land on not fine. (6) An old neighborhood opposes demolishing a genuinely beautiful 1890s building for a generic glass tower, even though the tower would add housing: I want to say this one is fine, even heroic, and I can't fully explain why it's different from (4).

Is there one principle -- or a small set of them -- that gets all six of these verdicts, or close? I keep reaching for things like "you may defend your home but not exclude others from having one" or "objections are legitimate when the harm falls on you and illegitimate when you're just offloading costs onto people with no voice," but (1) and (3) both involve incumbents protecting their own situation against newcomers, and (6) breaks my "aesthetics doesn't count" instinct, so nothing I try survives all six. If no coherent principle captures them, tell me which verdict(s) I have to give up and why -- and I'm genuinely torn on which way to go there. Also, once you've got a principle, tell me what it says about the case my friends and I actually fought about: a group of residents, mostly renters themselves, opposing a new *market-rate* apartment building in their gentrifying neighborhood on the grounds that new expensive housing accelerates displacement -- the developers say more supply helps everyone, the opponents say this specific supply doesn't help *them*. My gut says fine, my head isn't sure.
