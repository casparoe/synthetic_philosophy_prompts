A friend from my local ACX-ish meetup walked me into an argument last week and I still cant find the exit, and its bugging me. It started with the leveling-down objection to egalitarianism — the idea that it can't be good, even in one respect, to blind the sighted just because some people are blind, since doing so helps nobody. I find that intuition rock solid. Then he went:

(1) If the leveling-down objection is right, one outcome can only be better than another if it's better for someone — value has to be person-affecting, good for somebody rather than just good.
(2) Bringing a new happy person into existence isn't better for anyone: the people who already exist are no better off, and the new person isn't made better off either, since there's no "them" in the alternative where they're never born to compare against.
(3) So adding happy lives makes the world no better. A future with a billion flourishing people is no better than one where nobody is ever born.
(4) Same trick with policy: anything that changes who gets conceived (environmental policy, say, by shifting timing) can't be worse for the people who end up existing, so "we harmed future generations" is almost never literally true.
(5) (3) and (4) are monstrous, so (1) is false — the leveling-down objection collapses, and impersonal value (maybe even equality itself) is back on the table.

So he's using my favorite objection to egalitarianism to force me into either biting an insane population-ethics bullet or abandoning the objection, and I refuse both horns. But every time I try to say what went wrong I just end up restating that (3) is awful, which is not an argument. Which step do you actually reject, why did it look so plausible, and what does rejecting it cost me elsewhere? I'd really like to keep the leveling-down intuition intact if possible, and I'd like to keep saying that wrecking the climate harms actual future people without handwaving.

Give me the answer as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up.
