I'm in a 300-level philosophy of technology course (mostly poli-sci and phil majors) and we just finished the unit on technological determinism: Winner's "Do Artifacts Have Politics?", the Long Island bridges, and then the replies arguing that artifacts don't determine anything, that the politics is all in the social context and how people interpret and use the thing. For the final paper (~15 pages) the prof wants each of us to pick ONE open question in this debate and take an actual position. No surveys allowed.

Here's the catch. I've been toying with my own test and I kind of want to build the paper around it, so factor that in. Call it the swap test: if you could replace the artifact with a written rule, a sign, or a paid guard and get basicly the same political outcome, then the artifact itself isn't political, it's just enforcement, and the politics belongs to whoever issued the rule. The bridges fail this test (a "no buses" sign plus fines does the same job as a low overpass), which looks bad for Winner. But then I noticed a sign can be ignored, argued with, or disobeyed, and a low bridge can't. So maybe the real political difference isn't artifact vs. rule but "cannot" vs. "must not" -- and my swap test misses exactly the thing that matters. Or maybe it doesn't, and the bridges really are just a rule with extra steps. That's potentially the paper.

Candidate open questions:

1. Is there any criterion that cleanly seperates "this artifact is political" from "this artifact's designers are political and used a thing"?
2. Does the whole debate survive the bridges case being empirically contested? Do we need a clean undisputed example, or can the thesis be reformulated without one?
3. The normative payoff: if artifacts DO have politics, what actually follows for engineers, regulators, democratic oversight -- anything concrete, or does the thesis overpromise?
4. Do recommender systems / AI change the game, since they seem to shape preferences rather than constrain actions? Does that vindicate determinism in a way bridges never could, or does the old interpretive-flexibility reply just apply again?

Rank these as paper topics and defend the ranking by the criteria: which questions are upstream of which (I suspect 1 is upstream of all the others but convince me), which are tractable for an undergrad with zero engineering background and no way to do fieldwork, and which are overdone or secretly fake questions. Also tell me straight whether the swap test does real philosophical work or is confused, since the paper hangs on it. And what would change your ranking -- say, if this were a journal article instead of a term paper?
