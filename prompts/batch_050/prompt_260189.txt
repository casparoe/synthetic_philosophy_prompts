I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for over a decade, and one claim I've absorbed from that world is that you should be able to state, in advance, what would change your mind. So here is a view I've formed, and I'd like you to do the adversarial work for me.

My position: the high institutional trust found in countries like Denmark and Norway is not more reasonable than the low institutional trust found in the United States, and is in one specific respect less reasonable. My reasoning is not the usual one about corruption or competence. It's that trust and suspicion differ in how they handle evidence. A suspicious public generates the very data that could prove its suspicion wrong: it files freedom-of-information requests, funds adversarial journalism, litigates, audits, and generally forces the state to operate in the open. If the government is in fact clean, suspicion will tend to find that out, because suspicion is always looking. Trust, by contrast, suppresses the generation of evidence about itself. A trusting public doesn't check, so if the institutions were quietly decaying, the trusting public would be the last to know -- its evidence base is stale precisely because its attitude is what made the evidence stop accumulating. On this picture, Scandinavian trust is a self-sealing equilibrium: it may be causally productive of good government (officials behave better when trusted, competent people join well-regarded institutions, and so on), but it is not responsive to evidence in the way a rational attitude should be. American suspicion, whatever its costs, at least keeps the measurement instruments running. So my conclusion is that if you must inherit one of the two attitudes as a cultural prior, suspicion is the epistemically defensible one, even in a world where trust produces better outcomes -- and I would distinguish sharply between an attitude's being beneficial and its being reasonable.

What would count against this? I want the strongest defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. In particular: which empirical findings, if they hold, would break the claimed asymmetry between trust and suspicion as evidence-generating attitudes? Are there mechanisms by which high-trust societies actually audit themselves better than low-trust ones, and would that dissolve my argument or merely complicate it? Could the whole framing be accused of conflating the reasonableness of an attitude in an individual with the reasonableness of distributing that attitude across a population, and if so, does the position survive the disambiguation? And I'd like an honest verdict on whether my view risks anything at all: if a defender can always say the trusting Dane simply hasn't looked hard enough, has the position been insulated past the point of falsifiability?

One constraint on your answer: commit. Don't give me a balanced survey of considerations and leave me to weigh them. Tell me which defeater you think is actually decisive, if any, and whether you think my position stands, falls, or survives only in a weakened form -- with your reasons for that verdict stated plainly.
