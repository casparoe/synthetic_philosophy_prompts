I've been reading a popular book on the interpretation of quantum mechanics, and it gives the following account of the problem of probability in the many-worlds interpretation. I'd like to know how much of it to trust.

The book says: 'In Everett's interpretation, nothing chancy ever happens. When you measure a quantum system in a superposition, the universe simply branches, and every outcome occurs in some branch, with certainty. This immediately creates the central difficulty for the view: probability is about uncertainty regarding which of several mutually exclusive outcomes will happen, and since all of them happen, there is no fact of the matter for probability to be about, and the Born-rule weights written into the wavefunction have nothing to attach to. Everett himself recognized that the squared-amplitude rule needed justification rather than mere assertion, and in the 1990s David Deutsch supplied the missing piece by proving, from decision theory together with the unitary formalism alone, that a rational agent in a branching universe must act exactly as if the squared amplitudes were probabilities of the branches. Most specialists now regard this derivation as having essentially settled the matter, so that the many-worlds interpretation recovers the Born rule while rival interpretations must simply postulate it. The remaining puzzle is the preferred-basis problem -- the question of why the branching follows the definite-outcome basis we observe rather than some other decomposition -- and critics concede that decoherence has resolved that one.'

The book is by a credentialed science writer, and the account is roughly consistent with other things I've read, but a few steps strike me as potentially too quick -- in particular, the move from the formal derivation to the claim that the probability problem is settled, and the description of what decoherence establishes.
