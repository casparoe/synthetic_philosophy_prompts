I keep bumping into the line 'the sin is in the consent, not the act' — most recently in an argument about whether attempted crimes deserve the same punishment as completed ones, where someone dropped it as if it settled the matter (you consented to murder, the bullet missing is just luck, so equal blame, done). I've also seen it used the other direction, to excuse someone who did something genuinely harmful while meaning well. So I want the sentence actually unpacked. Lay out the distinct claims it could be making — e.g. is it about where moral badness *resides* (in the will, in the deed, in the outcome), or about what we're *culpable* for, or about which cases of ignorance exculpate? There's a tricky fork I can't sort out myself: does 'consent' mean consenting to the deed as it actually is, or consenting to what you *believe* the deed to be? Those come apart hard in cases where someone does what is in fact the right thing while sincerely believing it's wrong (did they sin?), and in cases where someone does what is in fact wrong while sincerely believing it's permitted (are they off the hook even if the belief was lazy or self-serving?). For each reading on your menu, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive defensible thesis, or actually false, and flag which reading is doing the work in the attempt-crimes argument — because my suspicion is that the slogan only supports equal punishment for attempts on one specific reading, and that reading has consequences for the ignorance cases that the person quoting it wouldn't accept. If the slogan turns out to equivocate between readings depending on which case it's applied to, say so plainly rather than trying to rescue it.
