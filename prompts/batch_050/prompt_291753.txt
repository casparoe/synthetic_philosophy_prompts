You are a professional philosopher specializing in the philosophy of emotion and the theory of rationality. Please think through the following carefully rather than reaching for the standard textbook treatment; begin by briefly brainstorming the relevant correspondences before settling into the analysis proper.

I am drafting a seminar paper on the recalcitrance of emotion, and I keep encountering a defensive move that I want stress-tested. The move is aimed at the judgment theory of emotion — the broadly Stoic view, revived in recent decades by philosophers such as Robert Solomon and Martha Nussbaum, according to which fearing the spider just is, or essentially involves, judging it dangerous. The stock objection is recalcitrance: the arachnophobe sincerely avows that the spider is harmless, is neither lying nor confused, and yet still fears it; if emotions were judgments, she would be guilty of holding flatly contradictory beliefs, and that seems too strong a diagnosis. Defenders of the perceptual theory — the view, associated with thinkers like Ronald de Sousa, Jesse Prinz, and Christine Tappolet, that emotions are perceptions or perception-like representations of evaluative properties — respond with an analogy: a recalcitrant emotion is like a perceptual illusion, paradigmatically the Müller-Lyer case. The lines go on looking unequal even after you have measured them and know them to be equal, and nobody convicts you of irrationality for that; likewise, the spider goes on presenting itself as dangerous even after you know it is harmless. In the literature I am reading, this analogy is made to carry a substantial conclusion: that recalcitrance is no objection to any cognitivist theory of emotion, and that the recalcitrant subject is non-rational rather than irrational — her fear is a misperception, not a piece of inconsistent reasoning.

Audit this analogy for me, in both directions. First, give the mapping explicitly — what corresponds to the lines, to the measurement, to the persistent appearance, to the perceiver's unshaken higher-order knowledge — and identify which correspondences are actually load-bearing for the defensive use. Second, work through the disanalogies and grade each by whether it matters for that use: the encapsulation of early vision (illusions are mandatory and immune to what you know, whereas emotions seem at least somewhat trainable, extinguishable through exposure, and culturally variable — does that undermine the comparison or merely refine it?); the temporal profile (the Müller-Lyer appearance never fades no matter how often you measure, but phobic fear often does fade with therapy — is the analogy committed to a wrong prediction here?); and the normative asymmetry (we assess emotions as fitting or unfitting, reasonable or unreasonable, in ways we do not assess retinal appearances — does the analogy, taken seriously, erase a distinction we have independent reason to keep, and thereby push back toward the judgment theory it was meant to defeat?). Third, consider whether the analogy overreaches: it fits primitive, perception-like emotions best (spiders, heights, flying), so say what happens when it is extended to articulate, reason-saturated emotions like indignation, resentment, or guilt, where there is no plausible low-level appearance to play the role of the illusion. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion the analogy was deployed to support — that the recalcitrant subject is non-rational rather than irrational, and that judgment theories get recalcitrance wrong — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If you think a better analogy exists for what is going on in recalcitrance (a habit? a posture? a way of seeing-as? a state somewhere between belief and feeling?), propose it and state precisely what it explains that the illusion analogy does not.
