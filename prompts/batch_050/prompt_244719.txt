I cover science and ideas for a national broadsheet, and for the past decade one of my regular beats has been our constitutional court's jurisprudence on quotas and preferential selection. Next month the court rules on a new quota law affecting the national research council, and I want to file a preview piece that predicts the outcome and identifies the principle genuinely at stake. I would like your help with the reasoning.

Here is the court's relevant track record, in four decisions:

1. It upheld a law reserving a quarter of mid-level civil-service posts, for a period of ten years, for members of a caste-like minority that had been legally barred from public employment until two generations ago. Appointments to these posts are made by interview panels with wide discretion. The court stressed both the group's historic exclusion and the provision's sunset clause.

2. It upheld a permanent requirement that the boards of publicly listed companies include at least 40 percent of each sex. Board seats are filled by nomination committees, and the court dwelt on the opacity of that process and the self-perpetuating character of corporate networks.

3. It struck down a law reserving 15 percent of university places for a recently arrived immigrant group underrepresented in higher education. Admission is by a single anonymized national examination. The court pointed both to the transparency of the existing procedure and to the absence of any history of legal exclusion.

4. It struck down a law reserving 30 percent of primary-school teaching posts for men, defended by the government on role-model grounds. Hiring is by open competition. The court observed that men have never been excluded from the profession by law or custom, and dismissed the measure as a quota in search of a justification.

The new case: parliament has voted to reserve one-third of the seats on the research council's grant-awarding panels, for eight years, for members of the same historically marginalized minority as in case 1, a group still almost entirely absent from senior science. Panel members are selected through open application against published criteria, and the panels control a large share of public research funding.

What I need from you:

- Identify at least two general principles, each consistent with all four prior rulings, that the court might plausibly be applying. I can see two myself — one keyed to the transparency of the selection process, one keyed to remedying historical exclusion — but I suspect there are others, for instance one keyed to whether the position at issue confers gatekeeping power.
- State what each principle predicts for the research-council case, and make clear exactly where the predictions diverge.
- Give your best prediction of the actual ruling, with reasons: which precedent is the closest analog, and what the court's differing emphases in cases 3 and 4 suggest about which consideration is doing the real work.
- Say frankly what principle the court would effectively be adopting if it ruled each way, since that framing will be the spine of my article.
- Describe the single hypothetical future case whose outcome would most cleanly distinguish the competing principles from one another.

Please keep the analysis at the level of principle rather than legal doctrine; my readers are generalists, and the piece is due Friday.
