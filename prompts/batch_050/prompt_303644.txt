Exam question:
“Philosophers often worry that self-deception is paradoxical. The static puzzle says a self-deceived person would have to believe both p and not-p at the same time. The intentional puzzle says deceiving oneself seems to require intending to make oneself believe something one already takes to be false, which would apparently defeat the deception. A deflationary process view answers that self-deception need not involve simultaneous contradictory beliefs or an intention to deceive: a desire that q can bias attention, evidence-gathering, memory rehearsal, and interpretation over time until the person sincerely comes to believe q, even though an impartial inquirer with the same evidence would not. On this view, self-deception differs from mere wishful thinking because belief is actually produced or sustained; it differs from denial because the person may be able to offer reasons and may not consciously experience the evidence as threatening; and it differs from simply having a fragile or low-confidence belief because the motivational bias explains the belief’s formation and maintenance.

Case: Mara, a senior engineer, has staked her reputation on the claim that her team’s model is safe to deploy. Over six months, test results are mixed: several audits show rare but serious failure modes, while many routine benchmarks look excellent. Mara enthusiastically circulates the benchmark results, describes the audits as “adversarial nitpicking,” cancels a follow-up red-team exercise because the team is “too busy,” and keeps asking one sympathetic colleague to recheck the same favorable metric. When asked directly, Mara calmly says, “I’ve looked at everything; the risk is negligible.” Yet she twice postpones signing the deployment authorization and, when offered a large personal bonus contingent on signing immediately, becomes irritable and changes the subject. 

Using only the distinctions above: Can self-deception be possible as a temporally extended process rather than as a contradictory state or an act of intentional deception? Does Mara’s case count as self-deception on the deflationary process view, or is it better classified as wishful thinking, denial, or fragile belief? Explain which features are doing the evidential work in your classification.”

Student answer:
“Self-deception is possible on the process view because the paradoxes assume too much. The static puzzle assumes that self-deception must be a single state in which Mara believes both ‘the model is safe’ and ‘the model is unsafe,’ but the process view says the contradiction can disappear if biased cognition unfolds over time. The intentional puzzle assumes that Mara must consciously plan to fool herself, whereas her desire that the model be safe can shape what she notices without any plan to deceive. So Mara need not be both deceiver and deceived in the same instant.

Mara is self-deceived, not merely wishful, because she sincerely asserts that the risk is negligible and organizes her behavior around that claim: she circulates favorable results, dismisses the audits, and repeats the reassuring metric. The deflationary account also rules out denial, since Mara gives reasons and does not experience the audits as threatening. The strongest evidence is that she postpones signing and refuses the bonus. That shows that, at some level, she must know the model is unsafe; otherwise her hesitation would be irrational. Her public belief is therefore sustained against a prior, better awareness of the danger. This is exactly the kind of motivational maintenance the process view describes: the desire does not just comfort her, it manufactures the belief by filtering evidence. Finally, because the process view treats self-deception as non-intentional, Mara’s deliberate cancellation of the red-team exercise cannot count as strategic evidence management; it is just the output of bias. So the case is self-deception rather than fragile belief.”
