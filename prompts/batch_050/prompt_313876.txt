I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the ethics of climate change. In basically every op-ed, NGO report, and commencement speech I read on the subject, the same analogy ends up doing the heavy lifting: we haven't inherited the earth from our parents, we've borrowed it from our children. It's usually deployed to shut down a specific counterargument, namely the economist's point that it's rational to discount future harms somewhat, or to invest in growth now so that richer descendants can adapt later. The borrowing picture is supposed to show that this isn't prudence but something closer to theft: you can't spend someone else's money and call it an intertemporal tradeoff.

Before I lean on it in the piece, I want to stress-test it properly. Start by giving me the full mapping: what plays the role of the lender, the borrower, the principal, the interest, the repayment date, the default. Then walk me through where it breaks. Some things that nag me: a loan normally has a lender who already exists when the deal is struck and who can consent or refuse, whereas future people don't exist yet. Worse, a philosopher I interviewed pointed out that which future people will exist at all depends on the choices we make now, so the "lender" isn't even a fixed party, and I haven't been able to stop thinking about that. Also, a loan is supposed to be repaid in the same currency, but we can't hand back a stable climate — what we actually hand over is a bundle: a degraded atmosphere plus technology, infrastructure, institutions, longer lifespans. Is that repayment, partial repayment, or a different transaction entirely? And do the two halves of the saying do different normative work — does framing us as borrowers versus heirs change what follows? Once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion the analogy is used to support (that discounting future climate harms is morally akin to spending stolen funds) still stand, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If there's a better comparison — trustee, steward, tenant, something else — tell me what it buys and what it costs.

One format request: please answer in continous prose, the way you'd write me an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. I want something I can think along with, not a briefing doc.
