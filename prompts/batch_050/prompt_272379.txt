So I'm a retired research chemist (40 years in polymer science, if that matters) and about two years ago I fell hard for philosophy, mostly ancient stuff. My local continuing-ed program at the university lets alumni give evening lectures, and I've rashly signed up to give one defending Plato's take on drinking in the Laws. Yes, defending it. Here's the passage my whole talk hangs on, Laws Book I, 649d-e, in the Bury translation:

"And for the purpose, first, of providing a cheap and comparatively harmless test of these conditions, and, secondly, of affording practice in them, what more suitable pleasure can we mention than wine, with its playful testing—provided that it is employed at all carefully? For consider: in the case of a man whose disposition is morose and savage (whence spring numberless iniquities), is it not more dangerous to test him by entering into money transactions with him, at one's own personal risk, than by associating with him with the help of Dionysus and his festive insight?"

My thesis, roughly: the Athenian Stranger is making a genuinely scientific-sounding proposal here -- controlled inebriation as an assay for character and as training in self-command -- and that this is smarter than the modern reflex of treating intoxication as pure loss of control with nothing to teach us. I'm not saying we should bring back symposia, just that the idea of drunkenness as diagnostic and educational deserves a fair hearing instead of a shudder.

Now the audience. It'll be maybe 25 people: two or three actual classicists from the department (one works on Plato's political philosophy specifically), an addiction-medicine doc who comes to everything, a social psychologist, a handful of retired folks like me, and some undergrads who I suspect are coming ironically. Twenty minutes of Q&A.

Build me a prep sheet. For each one: how much damage it does if I fumble it, what I should just concede up front, my two-minute reply, and where that reply is weakest so I don't overreach.
