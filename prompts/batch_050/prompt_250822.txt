I sit on the board of a mid-sized nonprofit called FareShare Alliance (not the real UK one, we just picked a bad name) that moves donated food between about 40 regional warehouses and hundreds of local pantries. Everything inside the org runs without money: the central office in Chicago collects requests, looks at historical distributions and inventory spreadsheets, and decides who gets what each week. It is, frankly, going badly. Some warehouses hoard "just in case," some pantries get pallets of stuff nobody wants while staples run out, and the planning team is drowning. At last month's meeting this turned into an unexpectedly heated argument where one board member said we were living inside the socialist calculation debate -- Hayek's point that a central planner can't aggregate the dispersed, tacit knowledge that prices carry -- and another shot back that Walmart and Amazon are essentially enormous planned economies and do fine, so the problem isn't planning, its that our software and data are primitive, and a modern demand-forecasting/optimization system could make planning work where Mises said it couldn't.

So now we have three proposals on the table and a decision to make in six weeks. Option A: create an internal market -- give each warehouse and pantry a budget of credits, let them bid for shipments, and let internal prices emerge. Option B: double down on central planning but smarter -- invest in serious forecasting and optimization software and expand the planning team. Option C: some hybrid, e.g. prices for some categories and central allocation for emergencies or equity-priority goods. The board is split, and both sides are talking past each other: the Hayek person doesn't really engage with the "firms are already islands of planning and computation changes things" objection, and the tech person waves away the knowledge problem as if better ML automatically solves it.

Please draft the decision memo I'll circulate before the vote: recommend one of the three options (or a specific version of the hybrid) as our bottom line, with reasoning both factions can actually engage with. In particular, take seriously whether the Walmart/Amazon point really answers Hayek or just moves the bump in the rug, say what would have to be true for computation to genuinely beat prices here, and give the strongest argument for whichever option you recommend against rather than a strawman of it. Include concrete implementation steps and how we'd know within a year whether we chose wrong.
