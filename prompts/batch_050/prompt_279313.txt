Last night I had a long and frustrating argument with a friend about when a legendary tradition deserves to be called history. He insisted that the Trojan War counts as history because Schliemann's excavations at Hisarlik show something burned there in the late Bronze Age, but in the same breath dismissed the Exodus as "just a legend," and when I pressed him he could not give me a criterion that sorted the two differently rather than merely reflecting which story he happens to like. I want to settle this properly, so I would like you to build me a classification instrument for the debate.

Please act as a professional philosopher of history with a strong background in historiography. Think carefully and take your time. Before writing the final instrument, spend a few sentences brainstorming the logical space: what are the distinct questions on which positions about legendary traditions genuinely come apart, independent of which scholar or school holds them?

Then produce a decision tree that takes someone from the starting commitment "this tradition tells a story set in the distant past, and we have a text, some archaeology, or both" to a named position on the tradition's historical status. I want roughly seven to nine branch nodes and at least five leaf positions. Each leaf must be a genuine, nameable stance, and at minimum the tree must include leaves corresponding to: naive literalism/maximalism (the narrative is essentially a record of events); euhemerism or the "historical kernel" view (a real person or event survives under legendary accretion); minimalism or mythicism (the narrative is a late ideological or literary construct with no recoverable historical core); and the cultural/collective memory view (the tradition preserves transformed social memory, so asking whether the events happened as told is the wrong question). Add at least one further leaf of your own devising that fills a real gap in the space.

Constrain yourself as follows:

- Every branch node must be phrased as a question someone could answer without knowing which position it leads to, and each question should be tied to a concrete case. Use the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus as your recurring test cases, and for at least three of the nodes, spell out what answering yes versus no commits you to saying about one of these three.
- Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names: first identify the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space.
- Identify at least one combination of answers that no serious position occupies, and briefly diagnose why (incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored?).
- Flag any pair of leaf positions your tree cannot cleanly separate, and say what extra evidence or distinction would be needed to separate them.
- End with a one-paragraph application: run my friend's two claims (Trojan War as history, Exodus as legend) through the finished tree, and tell me whether his verdicts land in different leaves for principled reasons or whether the tree exposes an inconsistency in his answers.
