I'm putting together a 75-minute session for an undergraduate decision theory seminar (about 20 students, mixed math backgrounds) and want a classroom activity built around Good's theorem on the value of information — the result that, for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer, free information can never have negative expected value, and has strictly positive value whenever it might change what you do.

Design a game with real (small) stakes — points convertible to something modest like chocolate or a grade bump — structured so that students feel the theorem's force before they ever hear it stated. In particular, I want the following arc:

1. An opening round in which each student faces an individual decision under uncertainty and is offered free, decision-relevant information before committing. Some students will predictably decline the information (fear of regret, superstition about "jinxing" it, distrust of the source) — design the payoff structure so that decliners do measurably worse in expectation, and so that this becomes visible to the room when you aggregate results.

2. A second round that isolates the theorem's subtler claim: information about something that cannot change your optimal action is worth exactly nothing, and information that can is worth something. Students should have to decide which of several offered signals is worth "spending" attention on, and discover the distinction themselves.

3. A third round that probes the theorem's boundary: a setting where information seems to hurt — e.g., a strategic round where a signal is publicly observed and changes other players' behavior, or a round where a student follows the information rationally and still loses, and feels the pull of calling the information "bad." The point is for students to generate candidate counterexamples themselves and then locate exactly which assumption of the theorem each one violates (fixed prior, costless acquisition, correct conditionalization, no strategic leakage, ex ante rather than ex post evaluation).

Specify the full rules, exact payoff numbers, how to seed the randomness with materials available in any classroom (dice, cards, sealed envelopes), the timeline across the 75 minutes, and how to aggregate and display results so the pattern lands. Then write the debrief: map each round onto the theorem's statement and conditions, name it only at the end, and anticipate what will go wrong when run live — students confusing ex post regret with ex ante value, base-rate neglect making good information look harmful, decliners claiming they "knew" the signal was misleading — with concrete handling for each. Close with an honest note on which feature of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce (for instance, that it assumes the agent updates correctly, which real students provably don't) and what the facilitator must supply verbally.

End the response with a single line beginning "Summary:" that states, in one sentence, what the activity is designed to make participants feel.
