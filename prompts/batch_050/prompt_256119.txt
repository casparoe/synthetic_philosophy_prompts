I'm putting together the final exam for my second-year personal identity course and I want one essay question on Locke's memory criterion and Reid's brave officer objection that my students can't answer by regurgitating the lecture slides. Everyone knows the setup: the boy flogged for stealing from the orchard, the brave officer who remembers the flogging, the old general who remembers being the officer but not the flogging, and the transitivity problem this creates for Locke. So please don't just have them reconstruct that. Instead I want the question to add a twist I made up: a fourth stage where the general, now senile, remembers nothing of his youth at all, but a subordinate finds his childhood diary and reads it aloud to him, and afterwards he can tell you all about the flogging in vivid detail. The question should force them to say whether this restores his identity with the boy on Locke's account, and to grapple with the fact that the "memory" here was manufactured rather than retained -- which is basically the issue about whether memories need the right kind of causal history, though I don't want to hand them that phrasing in the question.

The question should be answerable in about 45 minutes and worth 30 marks. I'd like the response as a numbered list with exactly seven items and no introduction or conclusion: item 1 is the exam question itself (worded for students, casual framing of the diary twist included), items 2 through 6 are the five must-hit points of the marking scheme, each saying what the point is, how many marks it's worth, and one line on how students typically botch it, and item 7 is a model answer of around 400 words that would get full marks.

The five points should include things like: correctly stating that the transitivity failure is a problem about the logic of identity and not just a complaint about bad memory; distinguishing memory as constitutive of identity from memory as evidence for it; handling the diary case by asking whether a manufactured "memory" can do the same identity-work as a retained one; and at least gesturing at whether the problem can be fixed by switching from direct memory links to overlapping chains of psychological connection, plus what that fix costs (e.g. identity starts coming apart from what makes us care about survival). But feel free to redistribute or sharpen these if you think the marks land better elsewhere. The model answer should take an actual position on the diary case, not just survey the options.
