I am a retired invertebrate physiologist -- forty years on crustacean and cephalopod nervous systems -- who has lately been reading philosophy of mind, and I have been asked to run a session for a small reading group of fellow retired scientists (a chemist, two ecologists, an engineer) on the question of which animals are conscious. What I have noticed in our discussions is that my colleagues already hold definite views, but these views are driven by implicit methodological commitments rather than by any articulated position, and the standard taxonomy of theories does not map onto the way scientists actually reason about this.

Please design a decision tree that sorts a scientifically trained reader into a position on the distribution of consciousness across animals, working from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Begin from the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space. The leaves should include at least: global workspace theories with a cortical or forebrain requirement; higher-order theories; integrated information theory taken seriously enough that its generous extension to simple systems is on the table; panpsychism proper; illusionism; the view that consciousness requires language or a self-concept; and principled agnosticism. Note any unoccupied combinations and diagnose each briefly -- is the gap there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored?

The branch nodes are the real work. Each should be a concrete question a reader can answer without knowing which position it leads to -- not "do you accept the higher-order theory?" but something a physiologist could genuinely have an opinion about, such as whether specific behavioural markers (flexible integration of information, trace conditioning, self-directed behaviour, anesthetic sensitivity) are sufficient evidence in the absence of homologous anatomy; whether the absence of the relevant structure in a candidate animal settles the matter or merely shifts the burden; whether the felt character of experience is a datum a theory must explain or a confusion to be dissolved; and whether the hard cases should be resolved by continuity from the human case downward or by identifying a functional signature that could appear anywhere. Use the honeybee, the octopus, and the zebrafish as recurring probe organisms at the nodes where the positions genuinely come apart on them.

Phrase every node so that someone answers from what they believe, not from which label they prefer. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, and say why. The tree should fit on one page; a short accompanying note of at most a page may explain how to use it and where its discriminations run out. No bibliography or reading list, please -- I will supply references myself if the group wants them.
