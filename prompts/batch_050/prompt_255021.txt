I am a novelist, six chapters into a book whose protagonist suffers a small stroke in the insular region of his brain and emerges with pain asymbolia. He still feels everything: he locates the pinprick, grades its intensity as accurately as before, distinguishes burning from aching, and will tell you, if asked, that it hurts. What he no longer does is mind. He does not pull his hand from the flame, does not guard the injured arm, and sometimes laughs when jabbed. I had assumed the novel was about a terrible loss. A philosopher friend read the manuscript and told me it is about no loss at all, and she walked me through the following argument.

(1) Whatever makes an experience bad makes it so either in virtue of what the experience feels like or in virtue of the subject's aversion to it; there is no third place for badness to reside.
(2) My protagonist retains what pain feels like, since his reports, discriminations, and intensity judgments are intact; what he has lost is only the aversion.
(3) In him, then, the feeling is present, the aversion absent, and nothing bad appears to be happening: he says so, and everyone who meets him agrees. If the badness of pain lay in the feeling, we would have to say he is being harmed in a way wholly undetectable from within his own experience, and that his cheerfulness is an error about his own good — which seems impossible for the badness of an experience.
(4) So the badness of pain lies in the aversion, not the feeling, and no experience is bad for a subject who does not mind it, however it feels.
(5) Morphine famously leaves patients reporting the same pain while insisting it no longer bothers them, and any drug, injury, or upbringing that removes minding therefore removes harm.
(6) So burning a person dosed or raised into such indifference does them no harm while the indifference lasts — and my protagonist has lost nothing worth a novel.

I refuse (6), as a human being and as an author. But I cannot locate the false step. If I must give something up, I would rather surrender the tidy dichotomy in (1) than the testimony in (2). Which premise do you reject, why did it look sound, and what exactly does rejecting it cost?
