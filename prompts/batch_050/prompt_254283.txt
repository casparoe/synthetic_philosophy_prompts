So I write crime novels for a living, and somehow I also ended up on the five-person board of the Blackfen Writers' House, this little residency program in a drafty old farmhouse where four writers a year get to live cheap and finish their manuscripts. Usually our decisions are about whether the residents can have dogs (no) and whose turn it is to unfreeze the pipes.

Here's the real problem though. Back in March the river flooded, and one of our residents, Dana, basically dropped everything for three weeks -- slept in the attic, ran the pump, carried the whole paper archive (sixty years of residents' manuscripts and letters) up from the basement box by box, and organized the emergency repairs with the contractors. She lost a big freelance editing gig over it and her own book slipped like four months. Nobody asked her to do any of this. Her residency agreement just says she has to keep her room clean and not burn the place down.

Now the board is split, and the split is weird. Everyone agrees what Dana did was wonderful. The fight is about what to DO about it:

- Two board members want to write a new clause into the residency agreement -- a "community stewardship duty" saying residents are expected to step up in emergencies like this, with Dana's conduct as the model. Their thinking: if it was that good and that important, why leave it to chance next time?
- Two of us want to give Dana a one-time honorary fellowship and a small stipend (we can afford maybe 1500) and leave the agreement alone.
- One guy thinks we should do nothing but a thank-you note because any reward "commodifies generosity" (he reads too much theory imo).

The thing that keeps nagging me as a novelist: in my books the hero who goes beyond what anyone could demand of them is precisely the one we admire most -- but the moment you make the heroic thing a requirement, it stops being heroic and starts being just... the job? And then next flood, a resident who DOESN'T wreck their career for the archive becomes someone who failed a duty instead of someone who simply didn't perform a miracle. That feels backwards. But the codify-it people have a point too: if we praise it but won't require it, aren't we admitting we want the credit for the archive being saved while offloading all the risk onto whoever happens to be soft-hearted enough to do it? That's kind of exploitative, no?

(An old college friend of mine who does philosophy said the word for the first camp's mistake is ignoring "supererogation" -- stuff that's good to do but not wrong to skip. She said it over beers so I may be mangling it, please correct me if I got the idea wrong.)

Draft the decision memo I'll bring to Saturday's meeting. It needs an actual bottom line -- pick one of the three options or some specific hybrid -- plus reasoning tight enough that the two codifiers can respond to it point by point at the meeting, and please take their strongest argument seriously instead of strawmanning it. Also address the commodification guy briefly. About a page, and keep it in plain language, half the board hasn't read a philosophy paper since the 90s.
