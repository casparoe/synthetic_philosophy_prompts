I have no academic training in philosophy, but I've spent the last year or so thinking about what privacy actually is — prompted by the usual things (data brokers, a partner who reads over my shoulder, a doorbell camera pointing at the street). I find I have firm gut verdicts on particular cases but no idea what general principle, if any, they amount to. Here are the verdicts, stated as honestly as I can:

1. A neighbor flies a drone over my fenced yard and watches the footage alone, and I never find out. My privacy was violated — the fact that I never learned of it, and was never harmed, doesn't change that.

2. My doctor opens my medical file to treat me: no violation. The same doctor opens the very same file out of idle curiosity on a slow afternoon: a violation. Same eyes, same information, so whatever privacy is, it apparently isn't about what gets seen.

3. A journalist reconstructs my movements and finances entirely from my own public posts and public property records, without my consent: not a violation, or only barely a wrong. The material was public.

4. A friend at a dinner party reads my diary, which I carelessly left open on the kitchen table: a clear violation. My carelessness wasn't permission. I notice (3) and (4) are both cases of someone taking in, without my consent, information I had made accessible — so I can't say what separates them, and it bothers me.

5. A company collects nothing about me whatsoever but infers a health condition of mine from my neighbors' data and sells the prediction. This feels like a violation, yet nothing that was ever "mine" was taken or even touched, and I want to know whether that feeling can survive scrutiny.

6. Reading a dead person's letters against their known, stated wishes: I lean toward calling this a violation too, but I'm genuinely unsure whether the dead can still have privacy to lose.

What I want is a principle — or, if no single one works, two or three candidate principles — stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself, with the case that motivates each part. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and would have me surrender; I'd rather drop a verdict than adopt a principle contorted into fitting all six. Then tell me what your principle says about two cases I haven't settled: my mother, who legitimately knows about my divorce, passing the news to relatives without asking me, and my employer reading messages I sent on company equipment under a policy I signed but never read. Please keep the whole answer under 500 words.
