At a history department's after-dinner debate, two colleagues — Dr. Lang, a social historian who thinks individuals are mostly the froth on deeper currents, and Dr. Vogel, a biographer of political leaders who insists that some decades would be unrecognizable without particular people — are asked to judge a hypothetical prize essay on the following counterfactual: in December 1932, Adolf Hitler dies suddenly of a ruptured appendix, before ever becoming Chancellor. The essay question is: what does Germany look like in 1945? Write their dialogue (roughly 1,200–1,600 words) as they argue out what the correct answer is.

The two should be equally sharp, and neither should get the easy version of their position. Lang should not be allowed to wave at "structural factors" in the abstract: she must commit to a concrete alternative trajectory — who actually takes power in 1933 instead (a conservative authoritarian government of the sort that was already ruling by presidential decree? a military-backed regime? a restored but radicalized republic?), and whether that regime produces war, and of what kind. Vogel, in turn, may not rest on the sheer horror of what happened as proof that only Hitler could have caused it: he must identify the specific mechanisms by which one man allegedly made the difference — was it his personal decisions at key junctures, his grip on the party, his peculiar apocalyptic antisemitism, his appetite for risk — and he must say which features of the actual 1933–45 were overdetermined (likely to occur under many German governments: revising Versailles, rearmament, discrimination against Jews) and which were genuinely contingent on him (a war of annihilation in the East? systematic industrialized genocide? total destruction of the regime rather than a negotiated end?).

Have the dialogue turn on the two of them pressing a distinction between convergence and path-dependence: if removing one person changes only the timing and flavor of events, the structuralist wins; if it changes the category of what happens, the biographer wins. Each should grant the other's strongest point at least once — Lang that a different regime plausibly stops short of genocide, Vogel that some form of German revisionist aggression was very hard to avoid — and the exchange should end not with agreement but with each stating precisely what evidence or argument would force them to concede. Avoid caricature on both sides: no fatalist who thinks history runs itself, and no romantic who thinks one will moves nations.
