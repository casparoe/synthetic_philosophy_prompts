I am a novelist, and next month I am to defend a thesis in a university moral philosophy seminar: that consequentialist cluelessness — the worry that the distant, unforeseeable effects of any action swamp its foreseeable ones and so defeat consequentialist comparison — bites the writing of fiction harder than almost any other activity, since a novel's influence runs through the unpredictable inner lives of readers and no honest tally of a book's good or harm has ever been possible; therefore the novelist's obligations must rest on non-consequentialist grounds internal to the practice, not on outcomes. The room will contain consequentialists who know the cluelessness literature and will resist special pleading, non-consequentialists who may suspect I am smuggling in aestheticism, and a few literary scholars who think I have overstated how opaque reception really is. Prepare me for the question period, structured as follows: The Objections, ranked by likelihood and damage, each phrased in the vocabulary this audience would actually use, with a two-or-three-sentence reply and a note on where that reply is weakest (roughly 400 words); Concessions to Make Up Front (roughly 100 words); The Reply That Must Land, being the single answer on which the thesis stands or falls (roughly 100 words); and The Question I Am Hoping Nobody Asks, with my best available answer to it (roughly 75 words).
