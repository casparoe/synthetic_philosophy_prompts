I'm a novelist revising a manuscript I first drafted twelve years ago, and my editor keeps leaning on the Ship of Theseus. His argument is that just as a ship supposedly remains the same ship even if every plank is replaced one at a time, my book remains the same novel even if every sentence is rewritten, provided the protagonist, title, broad setting, and some continuity in readers' memories remain. He uses this to say I shouldn't worry that the final version might betray the first one, and also that we can market it as a revised edition rather than as a new book. That last part seems a little too convenient.

Audit the analogy instead of just retelling the puzzle. In ordinary prose, map the original ship, replacement ship, planks, gradual replacement process, shipwright, and observers onto the different versions of the novel and the people handling them. Then explain where the mapping fails. In particular, distinguish at least four things people casually mean by the same novel: the exact sequence of words, the abstract literary work, the fictional world and its story facts, and the public identity carried by the title, author name, marketing, and readers' memories. A changed word seems unlike a replaced plank because its contribution depends on the surrounding arrangement, and a rewrite can change the cause or meaning of scenes it leaves nominally intact. Also consider whether a novel has anything corresponding to the ship's continuous physical history, whether two later versions can both have an equal claim to be the original, and whether the persistence standard changes when we're asking about criticism, copyright, reader trust, or ordinary conversation.

Test the analogy against a few cases: fixing typos, cutting a subplot, translating the book, changing the ending, rewriting every sentence while preserving the plot, and hiring another writer to reconstruct the novel from my outline. Along the way, explain whether there can be a principled continutiy budget in which some features are traded against others, or whether that idea merely disguises a publishing decision as metaphysics. Finish by saying whether my editor's practical conclusion survives, and under which criterion of sameness it does or doesn't. Definitely don't turn this into a bibliography or name-check philosophers just for decoration.

Please answer in American English, and write the whole answer as continuous prose, like an email or letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
