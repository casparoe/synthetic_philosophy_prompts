So I've been teaching Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind on and off for years and I've ended up committing to the strong reading of Sellars: the attack on the myth of the given doesn't just knock out classical sense-data foundationalism, it shows that nothing can do genuinely epistemic work -- justify a belief, terminate a regress, count as evidence someone has -- unless it's already conceptually articulated. On my view the whole idea of pre-conceptual awareness guiding or warranting judgment is confused, not merely unmotivated, and the standard counterexamples (fineness of grain of color experience, animal and infant cognition, Peacocke-style scenario content) all trade on sliding between causal/informational sensitivity and justification, which are seperate things.

What I want from you is a map of my exposure. What would actually count against this position? Not the usual literature tour -- I mean: which specific arguments, cases, or empirical results (say from developmental psych or perceptual science) would be decisive if they held up, and which would merely be awkward and absorbable? What would the view's own defenders -- the McDowells and Brandoms of the world -- concede as a legitimate defeater, as opposed to what its critics throw at it? Is there a class of cases the strong reading genuinely cannot describe without gerrymandering "the conceptual" to fit (demonstratives seem like the obvious candidate, but maybe I'm missing worse ones)? And here's the part that nags me: whenever someone produces a candidate counterexample, the move is always to say either it's conceptual after all or it's merely causal and hence not epistemic -- has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that actually a defect for a thesis of this kind, which is supposed to be about the grammar of justification rather than an empirical hypothesis? Tell me what should move me, ranked by how decisive it would be, and note where even you think the counterevidence falls short.
