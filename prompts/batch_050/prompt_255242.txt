So the MMO I've played on and off since college announced last month that the servers go dark next spring, and my guild's Discord has been arguing about it nonstop. Some people have characters with thousands of hours in them, rare drops, skins they bought with actual money -- and the company's position is basically: read the EULA, everything in the game is licensed access to our servers, you never owned anything, we're deleting nothing that was ever really yours. Half the Discord thinks this is obviously fine, you clicked agree, bits on someone else's machine. The other half is talking like their house burned down, and honestly I get it? I spent real time and some real money on that account. Anyway this is very much giving the same vibe as the old LessWrong threads about whether uploads are people, where everyone's talking past each other about what the thing in question even *is*, ontologically.

Here's what I actually want to poke at, though. The same company's practices seem to commit to a view about virtual stuff that I can't make consistent. They ban players for stealing items from other people's accounts, and the ban appeals process treats that like a genuine wrong, not like moving bits around on hardware the company owns. Their moderation policy for the VR side explicitly says harassment in virtual space is real harassment because the distress is real. Their store sells skins for $25 and the marketing says "your collection." And yet when *they* wipe everything at shutdown, that's supposed to be harm-free, because none of it was ever real property. So the practice seems to say: what players do to each other through the game can be a real harm, but what the company does by deleting the game cannot be. If distress is what makes virtual harassment real, the distress over a deleted eight-year-old character is pretty real too. If it all reduces to "you consented to the terms," then why isn't account theft also just a contract dispute rather than a bannable wrong?

So here's my question: what theory of virtual objects and virtual harms does this whole arrangement actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit ontology as charitably as you can -- is there a coherent view where items are real enough to steal but not real enough to destroy, or where the harm lives in the person and not the object but item-loss doesn't count as harming the person? Also distinguish what the practice *strictly* presupposes from what it just happens to fit with: could a pure contractarian view (ontology is irrelevant, it's all licensing terms and assumed risk) explain every piece of it, or does the moderation side smuggle in something ontological that the EULA side denies? And if instead you took seriously the view that virtual objects are real and virtual losses are genuine harms, concretely what would have to change -- preservation obligations after shutdown, mandatory refunds, a right to export your stuff to a self-hosted server, or what? I definately don't want a legal memo about whether EULAs hold up, I want the philosophy underneath.
