So I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine — and I'm working on a feature about why scientists get so much more excited when a theory predicts something nobody's seen yet than when it explains something everybody already knew. The hook is the classic one: Mercury's weird orbit was on the books for decades before general relativity came along and explained it, and physicists at the time clearly counted that in relativity's favor. My editor keeps pressing me on whether that reaction is even rational, which sent me down the rabbit hole of what philosophers call the problem of old evidence: in Bayesian terms, if the scientist already knew the evidence, her credence in it is basicly 1, and conditioning on something you're certain of can't raise your credence in anything else. So how did the old data do any confirming at all?

The standard ways out (which I'll only gesture at in the piece, no citations needed from you) come in two flavors. Flavor one, the counterfactual fix: ignore what the scientist actually knew, ask what probability she *would* have assigned the evidence if she'd never learned it, and run the update on that. Flavor two, the logical-learning fix: what confirms isn't the evidence itself but the scientist's discovery that her theory entails it — she wasn't certain of the entailment beforehand, so there's genuinely something new to update on.

Here's the case I want built to order: a single realistic scenario — a working scientist, present day or recent past, no amnesia, no time travel, no demons — where the two fixes come apart. The counterfactual fix should say the old evidence strongly confirms the theory, while the logical-learning fix finds nothing to grab onto, because the entailment was never discovered: it was transparent to the scientist the moment the theory existed. That transparency has to be motivated by who she is (her expertise, the derivation being two lines for someone in her seat), not just asserted. The theory can't be cooked up to fit the data — it needs independent motivation, or intuition flips against me. And she has to be doing epistemically blameless science the whole way through. I want readers to feel that of course the old data confirm her theory, and then watch fix two flounder.

Then the bookkeeping: state the evidence, the theory, and her epistemic state at each stage; show the verdict each fix delivers and why, with the actual conditional probabilities doing the work rather than vibes; name the one feature of the case that keeps the two fixes seperate and tell me what happens if you remove it (my guess: it collapses back into a boring old-evidence case where both fixes agree — confirm or correct me); and end with the smartest move a defender of the logical-learning fix could make to stop this scenario from counting against them. Keep the scenario itself to a paragraph or two, since I have to be able to retell it in the piece without losing anyone.
