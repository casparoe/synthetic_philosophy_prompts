I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of fifteen years, and one thing I've noticed is that Lewis Carroll's Tortoise dialogue gets quoted constantly in discussions of rule-following, levels of meta, and why "just add it as an axiom" never dissolves a puzzle -- yet I suspect most people citing it couldn't say precisely what the dialogue establishes, as opposed to gesturing at an infinite regress. I'm now on the committee designing the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on paradoxes of rationality, and I want to exploit that gap.

The exam will include the following passage from Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (Mind, 1895), printed at the top of the question paper. For context: Achilles has been entering propositions A, B, C, D into his notebook at the Tortoise's demand, and believes the series is finally complete.

"Then Logic would force you to do it!" Achilles triumphantly replied. "Logic would tell you 'You can't help yourself. Now that you've accepted A and B and C and D, you must accept Z!' So you've no choice, you see."

Draft one essay exam question, answerable in 75 minutes, that is built around this specific passage rather than around the regress in general. The question should test whether students can do more than retell the dialogue. Concretely, I want it to force them to take a position on at least these cruxes: first, whether Achilles' appeal to what Logic would "force" the Tortoise to do conflates two things -- the validity of an inference and some mechanism or obligation that moves a reasoner -- and if so, exactly what the conflation is and why it matters; second, whether the Tortoise's refusal is a coherent epistemic position or whether it collapses on inspection into something else (misunderstanding, insincerity, inability), and what turns on the answer; third, whether adding the hypothetical as an extra premise is a legitimate repair that merely happens to spawn a new demand, or a category mistake about what rules of inference are. The question must be answerable by careful analysis of the passage and the surrounding dialogue alone -- no post-Carroll literature may be presupposed or name-checked in the question itself.

Along with the question, give me:

1. A model answer of roughly 500-600 words, written at the level of a strong student in the seminar.
2. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth (total 20).
3. For each must-hit point, one line on how students typically get it wrong -- for instance, the kind of answer that correctly recites the regress but never engages the "forcing" language, or that treats the Tortoise as simply irrational without asking what account of inference would vindicate that verdict.
