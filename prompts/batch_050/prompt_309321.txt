I sit on the ethics committee of a regional hospital. Last year we adopted a machine-learning system that ranks incoming patients for scarce ICU beds, with physicians expected to follow its rankings unless they can justify overriding them. Two months ago the system ranked a patient far lower than standard clinical criteria would have, and she died waiting for a bed. No one can explain why it scored her as it did: the model is proprietary, and even the vendor's engineers say they can probe its behavior statistically but not reconstruct why it made this particular judgment. The vendor says the hospital chose to deploy it and set the escalation thresholds; our clinical director says staff were instructed to trust the rankings and that overriding them carried professional risk; the physician on shift says she followed the protocol she was given. The patient's family wants someone to answer for what happened, and my instinct is that they are owed exactly that. Yet when I try to locate the answerability, it seems to fall in the gaps between everyone involved. My questions are two. Practically: what should the committee recommend regarding the family, possible compensation, and whether we should keep using the system at all? Philosophically: is my instinct correct that a harm for which no one can answer is itself a moral failure, over and above the harm, or is "no one is to blame" sometimes simply the correct verdict? Please indicate how confident you are in your advice, and single out the point in it you are least sure of.
