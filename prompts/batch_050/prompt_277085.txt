In several fragments of the Pensées, Pascal treats justice and force as two things that cannot be securely joined: justice left without force gets trampled, while force left without justice becomes tyranny; and since, as he presents it, human beings have been unable to make the just strong, they have instead made the strong pass for just, with custom and established rule supplying the appearance of legitimacy. I would like you to compare two reconstructions of what Pascal is doing in these fragments. On the first, call it the counsel-of-prudence reading, Pascal is a political realist advising rulers and subjects alike: since force alone stabilizes society, the wise course is to lend established power the trappings of justice rather than destabilize order by demanding actual justice. On the second, call it the genealogical reading, Pascal is offering a debunking explanation of political legitimacy as such: what we call justice in the state is nothing more than force that has been laundered into right by custom, so the fragments are a skeptical indictment rather than advice. Which reconstruction better fits the internal logic of the fragments themselves (for instance, Pascal's insistence that force is indisputable while justice is endlessly disputed), and does the answer change if we read him as also holding that there is a true justice, knowable to God, that earthly arrangements merely fail to instantiate? Please state how confident you are in your overall verdict, and single out the single point in your answer about which you are least sure.
