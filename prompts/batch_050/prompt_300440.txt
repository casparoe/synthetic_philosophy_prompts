I'm TAing for an undergrad political philosophy course, and the prof has asked me to come up with the main discussion case for our week on equality vs. priority. The problem is that every case I've come across is basically the same toy setup: two islands or two groups, one better off, and the question of whether to burn some of the better-off group's surplus just to close the gap. My students see the leveling-down objection coming a mile away, immediately file it under "crazy view nobody holds," and the discussion dies in ten minutes. I want a case where the leveling-down temptation shows up as a live, realistic policy option — the kind a real institution might genuinely flirt with — so that students discover the objection instead of being lectured about it, and so that at least a few of them feel the pull of the egalitarian side before they see where it leads.

So construct a thought experiment to these specs. (1) The setting should be realistic and institutional — a school board, a public health agency, an employer, something of that sort — and the scenario itself should be at most two or three paragraphs. (2) There must be a genuine Pareto improvement on the table: an option that makes some people better off and literally no one worse off, and it should be an independently attractive policy, not some contrived transfer scheme gerrymandered to trigger the objection. (3) A telic egalitarian — someone who takes inequality to be bad in itself, as a feature of outcomes — must come out either opposed to the improvement or committed to saying that some worse-for-everyone alternative is better in at least one respect; a prioritarian must come out firmly in favor of the improvement. Derive both verdicts from the views, don't just assert them. (4) Then construct a variant in which the very same final distribution of welfare is produced not by luck or circumstance but by a decision-maker's discriminatory choice, and show that deontic or relational egalitarianism now pulls apart from the telic version — that is, work out which egalitarians can condemn the variant while staying silent about (or even endorsing) the original case.

After the scenario and the verdicts, do the autopsy. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work in separating the telic egalitarian from the prioritarian, what happens to the verdicts if you remove it, and what the egalitarian's strongest move is to avoid looking committed to leveling down — is it the "bad in one respect but not all things considered" reply, retreating to sufficientarianism, going fully relational, or something else? Also flag any assumption you had to smuggle in to make the verdicts come out the way the specs require, because my sharper students will definitely find it and I'd rather hear it from you first.
