For an upper-level undergraduate seminar on practical rationality -- 16 students, one 75-minute session -- design an in-class activity that makes students feel the force of Kavka's toxin puzzle: the way a prize for sincerely intending to do something tomorrow becomes unreachable for a rational agent who already knows they will have no reason to follow through once the prize is secured. The activity needs real, small stakes: a classroom-safe stand-in for the toxin (something genuinely unpleasant enough that nobody would do it for free), a prize students actually care about, and a mechanism for crediting the intention that is visibly applied before the moment of action, so that students experience the gap between forming the intention and keeping it. Give me rules, materials, a timeline, a payoff scheme, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the puzzle -- in particular, what the activity shows about whether intending is something you can simply decide to do for a reason. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (for instance, students trying to game the intention-detection rule, or quietly converting the task into a prediction about themselves) and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the original puzzle the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply by hand.
