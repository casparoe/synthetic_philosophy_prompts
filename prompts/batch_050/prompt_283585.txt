In An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (Book II, Chapter XXVII, §3), Locke writes:

"In like manner, if two or more atoms be joined together into the same mass, every one of those atoms will be the same, by the foregoing rule : and whilst they exist united together, the mass, consisting of the same atoms, must be the same mass, or the same body, let the parts be ever so differently jumbled. But if one of these atoms be taken away, or one new one added, it is no longer the same mass or the same body."

Locke immediately contrasts masses with living things: an oak survives total turnover of its matter, a mass survives none. A statue made of clay seems to fall between these cases.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers disputing a statue sculpted from a lump of clay. One defends a strict Lockean line: the statue just is the mass, so replacing a single particle of clay destroys it and produces a new statue. The other defends the view that statue and lump are two numerically distinct objects occupying the same place, with different persistence conditions. The dispute must turn on this case: the statue is crushed, its particles stored for a decade, then reassembled atom-for-atom into a qualitatively identical statue. Is the reassembled thing the original statue? Each speaker must engage the other's reading of the Locke passage — including whether Locke's oak, not his mass, is the better model for artifacts — and respond to the other's actual arguments rather than restate their own position. Neither side should be a strawman.

Write in American English, in no more than 700 words.
