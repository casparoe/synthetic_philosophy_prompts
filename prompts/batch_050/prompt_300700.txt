As part of my TA duties for a master's seminar on bargaining theory and distributive justice, I have been asked to draft the long question for this year's final exam. The exam is closed book and two hours long; my question should take a strong student about fifty minutes and carry 40 marks. The students have covered the axiomatic characterizations of the Nash and the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solutions and are competent with calculus, but they tend to recite axiom lists without understanding what work each axiom does. I want a question that defeats that strategy.

Build the question around this problem and do not substitute your own numbers: feasible set S = {(x, y) : 0 ≤ x ≤ 2, 0 ≤ y ≤ 2 − x²/2}, disagreement point (0, 0), von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities, standard convexity and comprehensiveness so both solutions are well-defined. Frame it as arbitration — an outside party must impose a fair division — rather than as prediction of what self-interested parties would accept.

The question should have three or four parts requiring students to: (i) compute both solutions exactly, in closed form, and state each player's ranking of the two outcomes — the rankings are strictly opposed, and students should be made to register that fact and explain it; (ii) identify the single axiom on which the two characterizations differ (independence of irrelevant alternatives versus individual monotonicity, given Pareto optimality, symmetry, and scale invariance) and connect the computed divergence to what each axiom demands; (iii) adjudicate which axiom is better motivated when the solution is used as a standard of fairness, given that in applied settings each party's ideal point is typically a reported claim — the best outcome they can defend with, say, a commissioned study — and can therefore be inflated. The intended insight is that Kalai–Smorodinsky's sensitivity to the utopia point creates a manipulation incentive that Nash's axiom is immune to, while independence of irrelevant alternatives discards information about aspirations that a fairness standard arguably should use; but the question as posed to students must not telegraph this.

One trap I want priced: the utopia point here is (2, 2) with disagreement at the origin, so many students will wrongly assume the problem is symmetric and conclude the two solutions coincide.

Deliverables: the exam question with per-part mark allocations; a model answer of about 800 words containing the exact computations; and a marking scheme built on five must-hit components, marks summing to 40, each annotated with the characteristic error that should lose marks and roughly how many. No citations or reading list — name the solutions and axioms without bibliographic apparatus. If anything in my specification makes the question unfair or trivially guessable, flag it.
