So this came out of an argument at our local LW meetup and it's been eating at me. We were talking about whether instrumental rationality really needs anything more than true beliefs (the Sequences basically say the point of all the epistemic machinery is to end up believing true things), and someone went "sure, but knowledge is supposed to be better than mere true belief, that's like the oldest problem in epistemology." I went home and tried to actually build the argument that true belief is all that matters, and now it looks like it proves way too much. Here it is:

(1) Knowledge is true belief that satisfies some further condition X -- reliably formed, justified, safe, produced by proper function, whatever your favorite post-Gettier candidate is.

(2) The value of a product isn't increased by the reliability of the process that produced it. An excellent shot of espresso from a machine that works 99% of the time is worth exactly the same as a molecule-for-molecule identical shot from a machine that got lucky once.

(3) True belief is the product here; condition X is a property of the process that produced it.

(4) So the value of a true belief isn't increased by its being produced in an X-satisfying way.

(5) So knowledge is worth no more than mere true belief.

(6) But (2) didn't depend on which candidate X is -- it's a general principle about processes and products. So no condition anyone could bolt onto true belief could ever make the result more valuable.

(7) So either the Meno problem has no solution and knowledge genuinely isn't more valuable, or its extra value is completely mysterious -- and either way, sixty years of Gettier literature has been polishing a distinction that adds nothing of worth.

Step (7) is the part that makes me think this proves too much. Like, I can maybe swallow (5) in a hard-headed moment, but (7) seems to imply that when the Gettier guy gets lucky with the stopped clock, nothing of any value is missing compared to the person who actually knows the time -- and that when I double-check a belief instead of trusting a hunch, I'm doing something pointless as long as the hunch would've been right. That can't be right, since double-checking obviously does something.

If I have to keep things, I most want to keep (1) and (2) -- (2) especially, since the espresso point feels like it's just true. And please actually commit here: don't hand me a menu of Fregean-this and virtue-theoretic-that and leave me to pick. Tell me which specific step you reject, explain why it looked so plausible, and tell me honestly what rejecting it costs -- including whether the cost is "the value of knowledge turns out to be kind of weird and not the thing we thought we wanted." If you think the argument is actually sound, say that instead and tell me which bullet I should bite.
