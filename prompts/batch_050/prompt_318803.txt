I work as a backend engineer at a mid-sized fintech, and we recently started piloting a third-party risk-scoring tool as part of our lending workflow. That has made me pay more attention to the fairness-in-machine-learning literature than I otherwise would. Over lunch last week, a colleague who had just finished a long magazine piece on algorithmic bias gave me his summary of some mathematical results in that area, and I've been turning it over in my head ever since. I'd like to know how much of it to trust before I repeat any of it in a design review.

His summary, as best I can reconstruct it, went like this: researchers proved a few years back that if two demographic groups genuinely differ in the rate of the thing you're trying to predict — say, loan default or recidivism — then no algorithm can be fair to both groups at once. Any scoring system that is "calibrated," meaning its risk scores mean the same thing for members of both groups, is mathematically forced to make more mistakes overall about one group than the other, and any system that equalizes the mistakes is forced to be uncalibrated. From this he concluded three things: first, that fairness in machine learning is provably impossible in general, not just hard; second, that the well-known public dispute over the COMPAS recidivism scores — where journalists showed the tool erred differently for different racial groups and the company replied that its scores were calibrated — was therefore much ado about nothing, since each side was merely complaining about a different, equally unavoidable flavor of error; and third, that our company's only honest options are to abandon risk scoring entirely or to admit openly that whatever we ship discriminates against somebody.

Something about this smells like an overstatement to me — results like this usually have escape hatches, and "fairness is impossible" feels stronger than what a math theorem about specific statistical criteria could deliver — but I don't know the material well enough to say where the overreach is. Which parts of his summary would the researchers who proved these results accept as accurate, and which parts misrepresent what was actually shown? In particular: what exactly do the impossibility results assume and conclude, what would a careful defender of them object to in the way he phrased things, and do his three conclusions — especially the one about the COMPAS dispute and the one about our company's options — actually follow from the accurate parts of his summary?
