I teach a philosophy of law seminar for upper-level undergraduates, about 24 students, and I have a single 80-minute session to devote to the problem of naked statistical evidence -- the blue bus case, the gatecrasher paradox, and the uneasy fit between high numerical probability and proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

Design a structured classroom activity (not a lecture and not a conventional discussion) in which students serve as finders of fact in a sequence of staged liability or guilt determinations. The activity should be built so that students convict comfortably in some rounds and balk in others even when the error rates of the evidence are matched, so that the inconsistency in their own verdicts -- not anything I say -- is what surfaces the problem. Specifically, I want rounds that pair statistical or base-rate evidence (market share, stadium attendance figures, fingerprint-database hit rates, whatever you think stages best) against individualized but equally fallible evidence (eyewitness identification, a defendant's admission, a document), with the reliability numbers held fixed across the pair, so that in the debrief we can lay the two columns of verdicts side by side and ask what, if anything, justified the difference.

Please include: the full sequence of rounds with the actual case vignettes and the numbers in each (written so the matched error rates are not obvious to students at the time); the mechanics -- how verdicts are given, whether individually or in panels, whether public or private, and in what order the information is revealed; a scoring or small-stakes scheme that gives students a reason to vote sincerely rather than performatively, without real money; a timeline that fits the 80 minutes with slack for the debrief; and a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical issues -- the distinction between the probability that the defendant is liable and the evidence's connection to this particular defendant, the reference class problem, and any sense in which a verdict needs to be something other than a high credence.

Two further requests. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: where students will see through the construction, where a round's numbers will be challenged in a way that derails the intended comparison, and what students who already know the literature will do to the dynamic. Second, be frank about which feature of the real doctrinal problem the activity cannot reproduce -- for instance, whatever it is about testimony, narrative coherence, or the witness's presence in the courtroom that the staged versions only imitate -- and what I will have to supply in the debrief rather than let emerge from the game itself. Do not name the standard cases or the term naked statistical evidence anywhere in the materials students see; the vocabulary should only be introduced after the debrief has done its work.
