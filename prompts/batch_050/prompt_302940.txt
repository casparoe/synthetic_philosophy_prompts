I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science writing a dissertation chapter on the epistemology of modeling, and before I commit to a position I want a consistency check on the views I've been assembling. Here is where I've landed:

(1) I accept something close to Friedman's position in his 1953 methodology essay: the worth of a theoretical model is exhausted by its predictive success in the domain of its intended application, and the truth or falsity of its assumptions is beside the point. A model whose agents behave "as if" they were utility-maximizers is fully vindicated if it predicts well, whatever the psychological facts.

(2) I hold that the ideal gas law genuinely explains the macroscopic behavior of real gases, and not merely predicts it — and I think what secures this explanatory status is that its idealizations (point particles, no intermolecular forces) are controlled: we can say exactly which features of reality they omit, and the corrections can be reintroduced systematically, as in the van der Waals equation. This is roughly the Galilean picture: idealization as the deliberate, correctable neglect of factors known to be causally secondary.

(3) I also hold that Schelling's checkerboard model of residential segregation genuinely explains observed segregation patterns, even though its idealizations are not controlled in the sense of (2): there is no systematic way to "de-idealize" the model into a realistic one, and adding realistic detail tends to dissolve rather than refine the result. I take it the model explains precisely by stripping away everything but one micromechanism.

(4) I treat robustness analysis as the gold standard for trusting model results: a result that disappears under small perturbations of the idealizing assumptions is an artifact and merits no confidence, whereas a result common to a family of models built on different false assumptions (Levins-style "robust theorems") earns real epistemic warrant.

(5) On the strength of (1), I defend economists' routine use of models with patently false assumptions about rationality and information.

My worry is that this package looks coherent only because I haven't pressed on it. Please audit it. Which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, and how severe is each one? Which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection — for instance, I've been told (1) and (4) conflict, but I'm not sure that's right. For each genuine tension, state what would have to give, and rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damaging. Finally, identify the single revision that would restore consistency at the lowest cost to the overall position, and be explicit about what that revision sacrifices.
