Earlier this year I served on a jury in a robbery trial where the defense rested heavily on the claim that the defendant, given his circumstances and the pressure he was under, had no realistic alternative. Since then I have been reading about moral responsibility and have written down my current views; I would like a consistency audit before I discuss them with a friend who studied philosophy.

Here is what I hold:

1. Frankfurt cases are decisive against the principle of alternative possibilities. If a counterfactual intervener stood ready to force Jones's decision but never had to act, Jones is blameworthy for deciding as he did, even though he could not have decided otherwise.

2. Coercion nonetheless excuses: the cashier who hands over the money at gunpoint is not blameworthy, precisely because she could not reasonably have done otherwise.

3. Determinism, if true, would not by itself undermine responsibility, provided the action issues from the agent's own settled character through normal deliberation.

4. Manipulation does undermine responsibility: if a neuroscientist rewires someone's values so that he then commits a crime after flawless deliberation, he is not blameworthy, even though his deliberative machinery worked exactly as it should.

5. Ought implies can: no one is obligated to do what it is impossible for them to do.

6. A person can be blameworthy for a bad choice he could not have avoided making — for example, someone who callously decides not to help a drowning child, where, unknown to him, an intervener would have forced exactly that decision had he wavered.

Tell me which of these genuinely pull against each other and which pairs only appear to. For each genuine tension, state the cheapest revision and what it would cost. Rank the tensions by severity, and tell me which single change would do the most to stabilize the set.
