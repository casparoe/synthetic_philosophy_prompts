So I've been reading LW since basically forever and last month at our local meetup I got into this stupidly long argument about whether "I know X" ever means anything more than "I'm really confident in X." I ended up defending a cluster of positions and afterwards a friend who actually did some epistemology in grad school told me that at least two of my commitments can't both be true, then refused to say which ones because "it's better if you find it yourself." It's been bugging me for weeks, and I want to write this up properly, so: consistency audit please.

Here's what I hold, roughly in the order I defended them: (a) fallibilism about knowledge — knowing doesn't require certainty; I knew my flight left at 6 even though I could in principle have misread the itinerary, and knowledge is compatible with credence a notch below 1. (b) Something like the KK principle: if I know p, I'm at least in a position to know that I know p. My motivation is basically pragmatic — if knowing and merely being confident are indistinguishable from the inside, then knowledge does no work; I want "I don't just think this, I know it" to cash out in what I'm willing to assert, sign contracts over, bet on. (c) An anti-luck / margin condition: lucky guesses and borderline discriminations aren't knowledge. If I glance at a crowd and it happens to have exactly 200 people, or I try to judge by eye whether a tree is 60 or 61 feet tall and get it right, I don't know — for knowledge the belief couldn't easily have been wrong given how I formed it. (d) The surprise examination paradox is overrated: a teacher announcing "there will be a quiz one day next week and on the morning of the quiz you won't know it's that day" is saying something coherent, the students can know the announcement is true, and the backward-induction argument must go wrong somewhere even if I can't say exactly where. (e) Calibration is close to the master virtue for a forecaster: I'm decently calibrated, my 90% claims come true about nine times out of ten, and that's the standard I hold myself to.

What I want: which of these are actually in tension (and with which), which pairs only look like they're in tension but dissolve if you're careful, and what the least costly revision to the whole set is — what I'd have to give up and what it costs me dialectically. Rank the tensions by severity instead of treating them all as equally fatal. Also, if one of my motivations for a commitment secretly presupposes something incompatible with another commitment, flag that specifically, because I suspect the meetup argument is where I picked up the inconsistency and I have a bad habit of adopting premises that sound virtuous in isolation.
