As a philosophy professor preparing a graduate seminar, I am defending a view I call constrained aesthetic evidentialism. According to this view, mathematical or scientific elegance is genuine evidence only when it takes the form of constrained compression: a theory or proof accounts for a wider range of phenomena with fewer independent assumptions or adjustable parameters, while imposing enough structure to generate risky, non-obvious consequences. Unification, symmetry, and simplicity count only insofar as they produce that kind of compression. Familiarity, notational neatness, and the pleasure experts happen to feel do not. Thus beauty is neither an infallible sign of truth nor merely a psychological heuristic; it is a defeasible and theory-relative epistemic signal.

What would count against this position? Please map its exposure rather than simply offering standard warnings that beautiful theories have sometimes failed. Distinguish, where relevant, among objections from the psychology of expert judgment, the historical record, underdetermination, Bayesian accounts of evidence, and the possibility that constrained compression merely redescribes predictive or explanatory success. Identify the kinds of formal results, empirical findings, historical patterns, and mathematical cases that would undermine the view, and rank them from fatal to merely awkward. In particular, say which objections a defender should concede as genuine defeaters, which can be absorbed by further restricting the relevant sense of elegance, and whether those restrictions risk making the position circular or unfalsifiable.

Please also consider whether the account survives a distinction between two roles for beauty: guiding the discovery of theories and justifying belief in them after their consequences are known. If it applies only to the first role, explain how much of the original position remains. Conclude by stating how confident you are in your assessment and singling out the specific claim or judgment about which you are least certain.
