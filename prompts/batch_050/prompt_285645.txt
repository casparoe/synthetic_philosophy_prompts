I'm on the three-person board of a small family foundation and we give out maybe 20 education grants a year. Our fights keep going in circles because we can't agree on what we're even trying to make equal. One of us wants to give everyone the same dollar amount (but a rich applicant and a poor one get very different things out of $5k), one wants to top people up until they're equally well-off (but then someone with expensive tastes or bad luck swallows the whole budget), and one keeps saying we should fund whatever removes each person's actual barriers to getting an education, which sounds right but seems to mean wildly unequal amounts per person and nobody can say when we're done.

Please write us a decision procedure we can actually run at a meeting: the questions to ask about an applicant, in order, that would let us figure out which of these three notions of equal treatment we should be applying in their case (or whether we even have to pick just one overall), with what counts as an answer to each question. I gather philosophers have fought about exactly this -- equality of welfare vs resources vs capabilities -- so ground the procedure in what's actually at stake between those views, including where each one breaks down (the expensive-tastes problem, people who'd rather not be made "equal," disabilities where no amount of money buys the same outcome). Then run the procedure on three of our real recurring cases: a first-gen college student who needs tuition, a student with a chronic illness whose costs nobody can predict, and a kid from a wealthy family who wants funding for an unpaid arts internship. Flag the points where the procedure bottoms out in a values call we have to make as a board rather than a question with a right answer.
