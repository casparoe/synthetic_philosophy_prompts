I run a university reading group on the philosophy of mathematics, 12 students, and I have a single 100-minute session on non-constructive proof. Design an activity that makes them feel the force of the strategy-stealing argument in combinatorial game theory — the reasoning that shows, for Hex, that the second player cannot have a winning strategy, and hence (since Hex cannot be drawn and is finite) that the first player has one, all without exhibiting any strategy whatsoever. By the end, participants should have seen two things with their own hands: that one can be certain a winning strategy exists while being entirely unable to say how to play, and that this certainty rests on a fragile premise — an extra mark of your own can never hurt you — which they can watch fail in a neighbouring game.

Structure the session in two movements. First, participants play Hex on small boards (3×3 up to 4×4) and try to write down explicit winning strategies, hitting the wall of explcitness as boards grow; then, in response to a staged challenge, they must invent the stealing procedure themselves rather than be told it. Second, they apply the same reasoning to misère Hex (same boards, but completing your connection loses) and must locate the exact step of the argument that breaks. Constraints: no lecture before play begins; the expression "strategy-stealing" is not uttered until the debrief; everything must run with paper, pens, and printed boards; and any stakes or prizes must cost me under ten pounds.

Organize your answer under the following headings, in this order:

The pitch (one short paragraph): what participants will have experienced by the end, stated without technical vocabulary.

Materials and setup (about 100 words): boards, groupings, seating, and anything prepared in advance.

The activity, phase by phase (about 500 words): rules as participants hear them, the staged challenge, a timeline in minutes, and the exact prompts the facilitator uses to steer groups toward inventing the stealing move — including what to do if a group invents it in the first five minutes and what to do if no group does.

The debrief (about 250 words): map each phase onto the components of the argument (finiteness and determinacy, the impossibility of a draw, the harmlessness of the extra mark, the contradiction), and explain how to handle two predictable failure modes: groups who take "the first player has a winning strategy" to mean we know how to win, and groups who run the argument on tic-tac-toe and are confused because it only secures a draw there.

Honest limits (about 150 words): which features of the strategy-stealing idea the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply in words — including the gap between knowing a strategy exists and knowing what it is, and the risk that success on small boards misleads participants about what play on large boards is like.
