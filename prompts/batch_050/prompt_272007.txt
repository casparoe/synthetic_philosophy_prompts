Organisations differ strikingly in how they structure collective judgement. Some adopt deliberate sequencing rules: participants write down their estimates before any discussion begins, the most junior person in the room is asked to speak first, or votes are cast simultaneously and secretly. Others do the opposite: a live forecast or running tally is visible to everyone, so that each person commits only after seeing where the group stands. Both designs are presented as ways of getting the group to the truth, yet they embody opposite instincts about whether seeing others' views helps or corrupts one's own.

What epistemology of group judgement does each design presuppose? In particular: does the independence-protecting design presuppose that each member's judgement has evidential value only if formed without knowledge of the others' -- and if so, does that rest on a view of testimony as evidence to be aggregated, or on something thicker, like a duty to exercise one's own judgement? And does the visible-tally design presuppose that later judgers can rationally treat earlier ones' conclusions as a substitute for their own investigation? Please reconstruct the implicit theory behind each practice, say whether the two are actually inconsistent or merely tuned to different failure modes, and indicate what each practice would have to look like if the other's presuppositions were correct. Please write your answer in American English.
