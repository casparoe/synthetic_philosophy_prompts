Ok so I've gotten low-key obsessed with Newcomb's problem over the past couple months — the setup where a super-reliable predictor looks at you, puts a million in an opaque box if she predicted you'd take only that box, nothing in it if she predicted you'd take both, and a thousand in a transparent box either way. You show up, boxes are already filled, and you pick one box or both. I think it's from the 60s or so.

Here's what's driving me nuts. Everyone I argue with about this splits into one-boxers and two-boxers and I have literally never seen anyone switch sides. The one-boxers say: compare who walks away rich, the one-boxers get a million, end of story. The two-boxers say: the money is already placed, your choice can't change what's in there, and taking both gets you a thousand extra no matter what, so one-boxing is basically leaving money on the table out of superstition. The wierd part is both arguments feel airtight to me depending on the day, which makes me suspect the problem is with the question, not with me.

My working theory, tell me if this is dumb: the camps aren't really disagreeing about what to do in the room. They're disagreeing about what "rational" is supposed to track. One side thinks a rational choice is the one with the best consequences among the things you can still causally influence at the moment of choosing. The other side thinks rationality is about being the kind of agent who tends to do well, full stop, even if the doing-well was locked in before you walked in. If that's right, the whole thing is two groups answering different questions and both calling it "what should you do?", wich would explain why no argument ever lands.

But maybe I'm being too deflationary and there IS a substantive disagreement hiding in there. One thing I noticed: if the predictor is literally infallible, almost everyone I know one-boxes, and if she's like 60% reliable, almost everyone two-boxes. So maybe the real fight is about how much evidential weight a choice is allowed to carry when it carries no causal weight — like, is there a principled cutoff, or does each side just have a gut threshold?

So: (1) What kind of disagreement is this actually — verbal, substantive, two questions under one name, or something else? (2) Given your diagnosis, what would settling it even look like — a new axiom for decision theory, a formal result, everyone agreeing on a definition of rational, or is it genuinely unresolvable? Please don't just survey both sides, I've read the surveys, I want the diagnosis and the reasons for it.

And one more thing: flag what would change your mind. If you diagnose it as verbal or as two-questions-under-one-name, what would convince you it's a real substantive dispute after all? And tell me which step of your own reasoning you're least confident in.
