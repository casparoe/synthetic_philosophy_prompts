A colleague and I have been arguing about moral particularism, and she has pressed an argument that seems to force me to abandon the view, though I strongly suspect something has gone wrong along the way. I would like a diagnosis.

By particularism we mean the position associated with Jonathan Dancy, on which the behavior of moral reasons is holistic: a consideration that counts in favor of an action in one situation may count against it, or not count at all, in another. The stock illustrations: that an activity would give pleasure is ordinarily a reason to pursue it, but when the pleasure is taken in another person's humiliation it seems to count against; that you made a promise is ordinarily a reason to act, but a promise extracted at knifepoint arguably gives no reason at all.

Her argument runs as follows:

(1) If holism is true, then no morally relevant consideration has its reason-giving valence essentially; for any consideration, there is some possible set of circumstances in which it counts differently or not at all.

(2) If no consideration has its valence essentially, then no consideration's valence can be legitimately relied upon in advance of judging the particular case at hand.

(3) A moral judgment about a case is justified only if it is supported either by subsumption under a true principle fixing some consideration's valence, or by grounds that can be stated and assessed independently of the very judgment they are meant to support.

(4) The particularist rejects principles of the kind mentioned in the first disjunct of (3).

(5) Any ground offered under the second disjunct — that pleasure is good, say, or that needless suffering counts against — is, by holism's own lights, merely one more consideration whose valence is hostage to context; so it cannot be assessed independently of the judgment it is meant to support, and the problem recurs one level up rather than being resolved.

(6) Hence, if particularism is true, no moral judgment is ever justified; every such judgment is, epistemically, a guess.

(7) But some moral judgments are plainly justified — a nurse reasoning about a patient she has just met, a judge confronting a genuinely novel fact pattern.

(8) Therefore particularism is false: at least some considerations must carry their valence essentially, and generalism is to that extent vindicated.

The argument seems to me to prove too much. The same template appears to apply to theoretical and perceptual reasons — that a stick looks bent is a reason to believe it is bent, unless it is half-submerged in water — so if sound, it would apparently establish that no judgment of any kind is justified in circumstances one has not previously sorted through, which is absurd. Still, I cannot locate the misstep. (1) seems to be just what holism says; (3) looks like a tame statement of what justification requires; and the claims I most want to keep are the holist examples behind (1) and the commonsense verdict in (7).

Which premise or step should I give up, and why did it look fine? Please also price the alternatives: what the particularist must commit to in order to resist (6) without quietly collapsing back into generalism, and what the generalist must say about the motivating examples if (8) is allowed to stand.
