I got a small private donor to fund six months of part-time research (I'm keeping my day job), and I want to spend it on something in the s-risk neighborhood. Here's my half-formed idea — turn it into a real proposal.

The usual pitch for s-risk work goes: extinction is bad, but some futures are worse than extinction because they contain astronomical amounts of suffering, so reducing the odds of those futures might matter as much as or more than reducing extinction risk. Everyone in this space has heard that pitch a hundred times. What I can't find anyone having actually done is check whether the priority ordering between s-risk reduction and extinction-risk reduction holds up across different views in population ethics. My hunch is that s-risk work is more robust to moral uncertainty than x-risk work: preventing astronomical suffering looks good on basically any axiology, whereas preventing extinction only looks good if the future would otherwise have been net positive, which is a whole extra empirical assumption people smuggle in. But hunches arent fundable, and for all I know the hunch falls apart somewhere. We keep tripping over the fact that the sufferers in these futures are mostly beings who wouldn't exist otherwise, and I genuinly don't know whether that matters on person-affecting views or just changes the framing.

The question needs to be stated precisely enough to have an answer. Something like: for each major family of population axiology (totalist, averagist, person-affecting, suffering-focused or negative-leaning, maybe critical-level), under what empirical assumptions about the long-run future does s-risk reduction beat extinction-risk reduction as a priority, and is there any region of assumption-space where the ordering holds no matter what? Sharpen that; I doubt it's exactly the right framing yet.

The approach I have in mind is a map: take the axiology families, work out for each one what the priority verdict actually depends on, and sort the cruxes into genuinely philosophical ones versus empirical forecasts wearing a trench coat. The donor is a numbers person, so the proposal also has to say what a philosophical deliverable is good for when you're the one deciding where the marginal dollar goes.

Six months, part-time, one person, so you'll need to say what gets pruned — the axiology space can't be exhaustive, only decision-relevant. No bibliography; refer to positions and arguments by name or shape and I'll fill in citations myself.

Structure the proposal with these sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths: The question, sharpened (~100 words); Why this is still open (~150); The axiology map (~250); Why not the obvious alternatives (~100) — and here argue specifically against just picking one axiology and defending it, and against surveying what researchers in the field currently believe; What a result looks like, and what failure looks like (~150) — be honest that one failure mode is the whole thing collapsing into a purely empirical question about the future where the philosophy adds nothing, and another is every axiology giving answers too mushy to decide anything; and What gets cut to fit six months (~75).
