I'm putting together the syllabus for my upper-level seminar on agency and free will, and I've just been emailing back and forth with a colleague about the "sphexishness" problem -- the worry, going back to Hofstadter's discussion of the digger wasp, that apparently purposive behavior can turn out to be mechanical trigger-and-routine all the way down, and the follow-up worry that we might not be so different. I want one lecture built around a zoo of these cases: animal behaviors that look smart or goal-directed until you poke them and the rigid program underneath shows through. Here are the examples I already have on my list: the sphex wasp herself (paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the burrow entrance, leaves it there while she inspects the burrow, and if an experimenter nudges the cricket a few inches away she retrieves it and runs the whole inspection routine again from the top, indefinitely); the greylag goose who keeps completing her egg-retrieval rolling motion even after the egg is snatched away mid-roll; the mother turkey who will brood a stuffed polecat -- her natural enemy -- as long as it has a speaker inside playing chick cheeps, and attacks her own chick if it's deafened; army ants milling in a death spiral because each one just follows the pheromone trail of the ant in front; and male sticklebacks attacking anything with a red underside, including, famously, a red postal van visible through the aquarium window.

Here's my problem: that list is exactly the canonical five, the ones every philosophy of mind textbook trots out, and my students will have seen at least half of them already. I want a sixth case that is NOT on this list and not a disguised version of one of them -- so not just another insect with another fixed action pattern, not another "animal responds to a crude sign stimulus" case that's really the turkey or the stickleback in a new coat. Give me one example, ideally a real documented one (but if you genuinely can't think of a real case outside the canon, a carefully constructed hypothetical is acceptable as long as you flag it as such), where apparent flexibility or intelligence masks a rigid mechanism, or vice versa -- a case of real flexibility emerging from dumb-looking parts would actually be even more interesting to me. Explain the behavior, what reveals the rigidity (or the flexibility), and -- this is the part I care most about -- what it adds to the free will discussion that my five don't. Does it pressure a different intuition? Does it make the human-animal contrast look better or worse? Does it suggest a test for when behavior counts as genuinely responsive to reasons rather than merely caused by triggers? Also tell me honestly whether your case is as vivid and classroom-friendly as the sphex, because if its weaker I'd rather know now than halfway through lecture.
