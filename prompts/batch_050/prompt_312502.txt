I am a novelist at work on a book set in a near-future state that has reorganized its government around an official hedonic calculus. For one chapter I need an in-world artifact: the annual circular issued by the Bureau of Hedonic Standards, the agency charged with measuring, weighing, and compensating the pleasures of the citizenry. About 800 words, in flawless deadpan bureaucratic prose, with no winking at the reader.

The humor must arise from a genuine philosophical difficulty rather than a caricature. The Bureau's founding premise is quantitative hedonism: pleasure is a single stuff varying only in intensity and duration, hence measurable on one scale and fully commensurable across persons and activities. The comedy is the Bureau slowly drowning in the heterogeneity problem — the suspicion that the pleasure of a symphony and the pleasure of a warm bath are not larger and smaller quantities of one thing, and perhaps not even obviously the same kind of thing at all. Please build the circular around the actual dialectic:

- A restatement of the charter's commitment to commensurability, with the classical felicific calculus's cheerfully undiscriminating comparisons — the old pushpin-and-poetry equivalence — treated as settled administrative fact.
- A reform, introduced some years back and now the cause of a quiet institutional crisis, distinguishing higher from lower grades of pleasure, any amount of the higher outranking any quantity of the lower; and the Bureau's suppressed internal footnote conceding that a second scale is now needed to rank the grades, so that the single-stuff premise the Bureau exists to enforce has been abandoned.
- Field reports the Bureau keeps shredding: a citizen whose grief over a cancelled holiday refuses to be offset by an equivalent ration of custard; a veteran inspector transferred to a remote posting for arguing that the Bureau has conflated two distinct questions — whether all pleasure is good, and whether all pleasures are one kind of thing.

A fair-minded hedonist should be able to recognize the view beneath the exaggeration: the satire should land on the measuring and comparing, not on the claim that pleasure matters.

Commit, please. By the end of the circular it should be clear whether, in this world, the heterogeneity problem proves fatal to the calculus or is administratively survivable, and the document's own bureaucratic logic should carry that verdict rather than any authorial commentary. Do not hedge between the two outcomes.

Then, stepping outside the fiction, add a short note of no more than 150 words, in your own voice, identifying the contested philosophical claims the satire leans on — in particular, whether you treated the doctrine of higher and lower pleasures as a defensible refinement of hedonism or as a quiet surrender of it — and flagging any point where you took liberties with the positions being parodied.
