I just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics, and we reached an impasse that I'd like to see played out properly. My friend, a robust moral realist, holds that the debunking argument fails because it ignores the possibility that moral truths are constitutively tied to the very aims evolution selects for — cooperation, reciprocity, the avoidance of pointless suffering — so that the match between our evolved moral beliefs and the moral facts would be no cosmic coincidence. My reply was that this vindication comes too cheaply: if the constitutive story can be told after the fact for whatever beliefs a process happened to produce, then the same move could rehabilitate beliefs acquired through indoctrination, trauma, or a stray cosmic ray, and a vindication that works for everything vindicates nothing. Write a dialogue between the two of us continuing this argument. Both characters should be equally sharp and engage with each other's actual points rather than restating their opening positions; in particular, my friend should press me on whether my generalization test is fair, and I should press on what independently fixes which constitutive story is the right one. The dialogue should push toward the deeper question of what makes a causal explanation of a belief debunking rather than vindicating — the process's insensitivity to the truth, the contingency of which process we got, or something else — and it should end either with one side genuinely conceding ground or with a crisp statement of the precise point on which they still disagree.
