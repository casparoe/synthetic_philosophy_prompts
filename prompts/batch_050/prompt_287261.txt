Act as a philosopher of mind who specializes in computational theories of consciousness and moral patienthood, and who has spent time advising research funders on which conceptual problems in this space are worth supporting. Think carefully, and begin with a brief brainstorm of candidate questions before settling on your final ranking — I'd like to see which options you considered and rejected, not just the ones that made the cut.

Here is the situation. I advise a small grantmaker that can fund roughly three philosophers for two years each to work on the conceptual foundations of digital moral patienthood — that is, the question of which computations, if any, instantiate morally relevant mental states such as suffering. The funder's premise is that progress here requires settling some philosophical questions before empirical work (on AI architectures, interpretability, or welfare assessment) can be well-targeted. Your task is to propose and defend a prioritized agenda: which open questions are most worth those researcher-years, and in what order.

To make the task concrete, here are five candidate problems that keep coming up in our internal discussions. You may rank these, replace some with better questions of your own, or both:

1. The mapping problem. Triviality arguments of the sort associated with Putnam and Searle suggest that almost any physical system can be interpreted as implementing almost any computation, given a clever enough mapping from physical states to computational states. If some non-arbitrary constraint on interpretation (causal, counterfactual, or otherwise) can be found, computationalist theories of mind become substantive; if not, they may be empty. How much does the whole field of digital sentience depend on solving this?

2. The attribution problem. A large AI system contains many subsystems and passes through many internal states. Even granting that something in there suffers, which entity is the patient — the whole network, a particular forward pass, a temporally extended training run, the weights themselves? Different answers imply wildly different ethical and design conclusions.

3. Type versus token. Suppose a computation that instantiates suffering is run twice, or is run once but was guaranteed to be run anyway by a redundant backup process. Do the tokens count separately? Does it matter whether duplicate runs are synchronized step-for-step or merely functionally equivalent? This seems to matter enormously for any attempt to aggregate digital welfare, yet it rests on intuitions about identity that are rarely examined.

4. The simulation boundary. Is there a principled line between a system that undergoes suffering and one that merely represents or models suffering (say, a language model accurately predicting what a suffering person would say)? If the line is drawn by functional organization rather than by material substrate, it is unclear that any line remains — but abandoning the line entirely seems to license absurd conclusions about detailed fictional characters and weather simulations.

5. Valence without subject. Some theories hold that what matters morally is valenced experience, and that the existence of a unified subject who has the experience is a further, possibly dispensable, posit. If so, moral status could attach to computations that no plausible theory counts as a mind. Should the funder treat subjecthood as a load-bearing question or as a distraction?

For each question you rank, explain: (a) what other questions — in this list, in AI governance, or in the empirical study of machine consciousness — are downstream of it, in the sense that their resolution depends on or is constrained by its answer; (b) how tractable it is for a philosopher working without access to proprietary AI systems or empirical labs, and what a two-year output would even look like; (c) what considerations or findings would move it up or down your ranking. Please make your ranking criteria explicit (importance, tractability, neglectedness, downstream leverage, or whatever you actually use) and flag where those criteria pull in opposite directions. I am less interested in the final list than in the reasoning that generates it.

Finally, some epistemic discipline: for your top-ranked question, state the strongest argument that it is actually overrated — that it is less load-bearing than you claim, or that it will dissolve rather than be solved — and say what specific consideration, argument, or development over the next few years would most change your mind about the ranking as a whole. If you think the funder's premise itself is wrong (that conceptual work is not the bottleneck, or that these questions are not the kind that philosophy can settle in advance), say so directly rather than politely working within it.
