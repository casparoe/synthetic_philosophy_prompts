The public and scholarly debate over policing in the United States -- reform, defund, or abolish -- appears to me to have stalled in a distinctive way, and I would like a diagnosis rather than another contribution to it. The three positions, as I understand them, are these. Reformists argue that police are a legitimate instrument of the state's monopoly on force, that failures are failures of training, accountability, and culture, and that remedies such as procedural-justice training, consent decrees, body cameras, and revised use-of-force standards have shown at least modest, measurable effects. Defunding advocates argue that the marginal dollar spent on police produces less safety than the same dollar spent on housing, mental health crisis response, and violence interruption, and they point to pilot programs in which non-police responders handle certain call types without incident as proof of concept. Abolitionists argue that both of these framings miss the point: on their view, the function of policing is the management of disorderly and marginalized populations rather than the production of safety, this function is visible in the institution's history and in the recurring failure of reform cycles, and therefore no reallocation within the existing institution can succeed.

My hypothesis about the stall is that each side has quietly adopted a criterion of success the other two do not accept, so that the same evidence counts differently for each: reformists treat a reduction in misconduct complaints as progress, defunders treat a shift in budget shares as progress, and abolitionists treat both as co-optation that extends the institution's life. But I am not confident in this hypothesis, and I can think of at least two rivals. Perhaps the real disagreement is empirical and merely hard: the three sides hold different beliefs about how much of what police do produces deterrence at the margin, and about what would happen to violent crime in specific neighborhoods under various replacement arrangements -- questions on which the existing evidence is thin because the relevant large-scale experiments have never been run. Or perhaps the disagreement is about the relevance of an institution's origins and history to its present justification: whether a damning genealogical account of policing, even if accurate, bears on what the institution does now, which is itself a contested philosophical question rather than a factual one.

Please diagnose the disagreement. Using what each side actually argues, tell me which of these it fundamentally is -- a clash of success criteria, an unresolved empirical question about counterfactual safety arrangements, a dispute about the normative weight of institutional history, some combination, or something I have not considered, such as the sides answering different questions (one about optimal allocation of a fixed state function, another about the legitimate scope of that function) under a single heading. Then, given your diagnosis, specify what would count as progress: a distinction that would dissolve part of the dispute, a class of evidence or natural experiment that would be decisive for it, or an argument that the impasse is what we should expect given the structure of the disagreement and that no resolution is available.
