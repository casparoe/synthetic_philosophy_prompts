Here's a little thought experiment I've been chewing on, and I'd like you to argue a definite side of it rather than sit on the fence.

Everyone knows the standard move against the St. Petersburg paradox: the casino can't actually promise unbounded payouts, because there's only so much money in the world, so the game's real expected value is finite and modest. Fine. But now consider what I'll call the St. Petersburg franchise. The house caps your payout at its total assets — say £10 million, so the game is now worth only about £24 in expectation, end of story. Except the house's £10 million isn't sitting in a vault: the entire stake is itself invested in a capped St. Petersburg game run by a bigger house up the road, whose £10 billion assets are in turn staked in a yet bigger game, and so on up the chain, each house capping its liability at whatever it happens to hold, which depends on how the games above it are resolving. No house ever promises more than it has. Every individual game in the chain has a bounded payout and therefore, by the standard move, a small fair price.

My question: argue that the standard "the bank can't pay" resolution either dissolves the franchise or doesn't, and take a firm stance on which. If you think it dissolves, then explain how a chain of games each with a puny fair price can collectively be worth buying into for more than the sum of those prices — or deny that it can. If you think it doesn't dissolve, explain where the unboundedness has snuck back in given that nobody, anywhere in the chain, has promised anything they can't deliver. Either way, I'd like you to also say whether the paradox is really about the size of payoffs at all, or about something to do with who owes what to whom, which is my hunch but I could well be wrong.
