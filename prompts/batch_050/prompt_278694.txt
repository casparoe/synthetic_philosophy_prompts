I'm giving a paper at a grad aesthetics workshop next thursday defending a strong version of immoralism. My claim isn't the weak one (that a work can be great despite being immoral) but that a work can be aesthetically better because of its immoral perspective. Rough argument: when a film or novel gets the audience to actually inhabit an immoral outlook from the inside, to feel its pull rather than just observe it, the moment you catch yourself doing it and recoil is itself an aesthetic achievement -- a morally safe work can't produce that specific experience. I call it the complicity payoff.

The room will be: a few ethicists who've fully internalised Gaut-style ethicism and Carroll's moderate moralism, two people who work on narrative engagement and imaginative resistance, and one person I happen to know thinks the entire immoralism literature rests on unrepresentative examples (Triumph of the Will and not much else). Prepare me for the Q&A: the objections this crowd will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that person would phrase it, with a short reply and a note on where my reply is soft. I especially need to know whether the "you're just redescribing discomfort as aesthetic value" worry is fatal or survivable. And include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks.
