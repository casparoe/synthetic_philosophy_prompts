Violence Has Not Disappeared, It Has Changed Its Shape

People who say the world is becoming less violent usually mean something fairly specific: an ordinary person today is less likely to be murdered, killed in battle, tortured by the state, or die in a raid than an ordinary person in many earlier societies. That sounds right to me. If I had to choose a century to be born into, without knowing my sex, class, country, or ethnicity, I would not choose one where small wars and private revenge were everyday facts.

But I think this claim is often turned into something much bigger: humanity has become less violent. That is a different claim, and it is less clear. A person can be less likely to die violently while living inside a system capable of producing more violence than any earlier system. Modern states, prisons, police forces, border regimes, surveillance systems, and weapons all concentrate violence. Sometimes they reduce everyday bloodshed by making the threat of overwhelming force loom in the background.

I want to call this stored violence. Stored violence is violence that is not currently being inflicted, but is organized, ready, and part of why people behave as they do. A society may have very few public punishments because everyone knows what the authorities can do. There may be fewer brawls because the police arrive quickly. There may be fewer local wars because a central government has a monopoly on force. The visible violence drops, but the machinery of violence becomes much larger.

This creates a measurement problem. Suppose one medieval village has twenty fights in a year and two deaths, while a modern city has almost no fights because conflicts are quickly processed by courts, prisons, fines, evictions, and psychiatric institutions. By a body count, the modern city is obviously better. I would rather live there too. But if we ask how much coercion is involved in keeping order, the answer is not so obvious. A fistfight is easier to see than the threat behind a legal order.

The per-capita issue makes this even stranger. Imagine a world with one billion people and ten million violent deaths in a century. Now imagine a world with eight billion people and twenty million violent deaths in a century. The second world has twice as many violent deaths, but each individual’s risk may be lower. Which world is “more violent”?

The statistician says the second is less violent because the rate is lower. The grieving person might say that twenty million corpses are worse than ten million. Both seem to have hold of something. Rates tell us about the danger faced by a representative person. Totals tell us about the amount of suffering in the world. There is no neutral way to decide which one is the real measure of violence unless we first say what question we are asking.

There is also a problem about what counts as violence in the first place. Most people count killing and assault. Fewer count imprisonment, threatening someone with imprisonment, forcing a family to move, denying medical care through a bureaucracy, or making workers accept dangerous conditions because the alternative is poverty. I am not saying all coercion or preventable suffering is violence. That would make the word too broad. But counting only moments when someone physically strikes someone else can make modern institutions look gentler simply because their force is indirect, delayed, or carried out through paperwork.

Another possibility is that some violence has been displaced rather than reduced. Rich countries may have safer streets partly because production has moved to poorer places with dangerous factories, pollution, land grabs, and weak worker protections. A consumer can enjoy a peaceful life while the harms that support that life happen somewhere else. It is like moving the slaughterhouse outside the city and then congratulating the city for becoming less bloody.

At the same time, I think this argument can become unfair. Almost every improvement can be redescribed as violence that has been hidden, stored, or exported. If crime falls, we say the police threat did it. If war becomes rare, we say deterrence did it. If people obey laws voluntarily, we say the threat is internalized. At some point the theory cannot lose. Any peace becomes proof of concealed violence. That seems too convenient.

So maybe the right conclusion is boring but important: there is no single answer to whether the world is becoming less violent. There are several questions. Are individuals less likely to suffer direct physical attacks? Probably yes in many places, compared with many earlier periods. Is there less total suffering caused by human institutions? That depends on the period, the place, and what suffering we count. Is there less capacity for violence? Definitely not; modern weapons and institutions have made the capacity enormous. Is humanity morally less cruel? I do not know how anyone could measure that.

The safest claim is that everyday interpersonal violence has declined for many people, while violence has also become more centralized, more technologically powerful, and sometimes less visible. That can be a genuine improvement without being a simple story of moral progress. A world can contain fewer beatings and still be built on the possibility of catastrophic force. Peace is not always the opposite of violence. Sometimes it is violence held in reserve.
