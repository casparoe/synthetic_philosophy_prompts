In a seminar on the philosophy of mind last week, we got onto self-deception -- the everyday kind, where someone stays in a failing relationship, or a politician convinces himself the polls are wrong, or a patient hears the diagnosis but somehow doesn't take it in. In the middle of the discussion a classmate shrugged and said, "Look, you can't lie to yourself," as though that settled it. And here's my problem: I'm pretty sure I've heard the same one-liner used to make almost the opposite point in other contexts -- sometimes as a comforting claim that deep down we always know the truth and so self-deception is superficial, sometimes as a flat claim that self-deception is impossible and we're all just lying to *other people*, and once (in a self-help book, admittedly) as advice to stop trying to fool yourself because it can't be done anyway.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that sentence might be expressing? I'm imagining a menu of readings, from the near-trivial (it's incoherent to intend to deceive someone while intending that they believe you, if they're the same person) through the substantive-but-defensible (there's some sense in which full belief of both p and not-p can't be sustained, or the 'deceiving' part of your mind always retains the truth somewhere) to the empirical or radical (no one has ever actually been self-deceived; every apparent case is really insincerity, wishful thinking, or ordinary irrationality). For each reading, please say whether it's a platitude, a live substantive thesis, or simply false, and flag what would have to be true about belief -- its transparency, its compartmentalization, whether it comes in degrees -- for that reading to hold. Most importantly, tell me which readings, if any, would actually vindicate my classmate's use of the line as a conversation-ender: which of them, if true, would show that the seminar's whole topic is empty, and which would leave self-deception standing?

I'd like you to take a clear position on which reading is most plausible and whether the slogan, on that reading, is worth keeping, rather than just cataloguing options. And at the end, please say what would change your mind: is there a specific argument -- for instance, a successful account of how the mind could be partitioned so that one part deceives another, or a reductio showing even that model collapses into something else -- that would lead you to a different verdict, and where in your reasoning do you think it is most likely to break down?
