I host a long-form philosophy interview podcast, and for our next episode we are recording a "dead philosophers" special: our guest is Adam Smith, and I have insisted we discuss The Theory of Moral Sentiments rather than The Wealth of Nations. I need nine or ten interview questions, ordered so the conversation builds in difficulty, and for each question a follow-up keyed to the answer Smith is most likely to give on the basis of the book's actual arguments — follow-ups that assume his standard reply and press one step further, rather than simply inviting him to restate the view.

About midway through the interview I intend to read him back the following passage from Part III, Chapter I ("Of the Principle of Self-approbation and of Self-disapprobation") and press him on it:

"Were it possible that a human creature could grow up to manhood in some solitary place, without any communication with his own species, he could no more think of his own character, of the propriety or demerit of his own sentiments and conduct, of the beauty or deformity of his own mind, than of the beauty or deformity of his own face. All these are objects which he cannot easily see, which naturally he does not look at, and with regard to which he is provided with no mirror which can present them to his view. Bring him into society, and he is immediately provided with the mirror which he wanted before. It is placed in the countenance and behaviour of those he lives with, which always mark when they enter into, and when they disapprove of his sentiments; and it is here that he first views the propriety and impropriety of his own passions, the beauty and deformity of his own mind."

At least three questions should turn on this passage directly: whether the thought experiment commits him to the claim that moral self-consciousness is constitutively social and not merely socially caused; what becomes of the person whose social mirror is bigoted, servile, or corrupt — does his account have the resources to say the mirror distorts, and where would those resources come from; and whether the mirror metaphor, taken at face value, collapses his theory of conscience into a theory of conformism. Beyond the passage, I want one question on the circularity worry about the impartial spectator (if the spectator's standards are built up by generalizing the reactions of actual spectators, how can conscience ever legitimately judge the crowd to be wrong, as Smith clearly thinks it sometimes is); one question on his distinction between desiring praise and desiring praiseworthiness, and what work that distinction does against the charge that his theory makes virtue a form of servility to public opinion; and one on the tension between sympathy's natural partiality — we sympathize more readily with those who are near, similar, or well disposed to us — and the impartiality the spectator is supposed to supply.

No biographical questions, nothing about the invisible hand, and no question he could answer by reciting the famous opening sentence of the book. Where a question risks drawing a stock lecture, indicate briefly how the follow-up forecloses that.
