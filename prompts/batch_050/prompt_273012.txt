Two friends of mine were arguing after a logic seminar and got stuck. The topic was Carroll's tortoise: the idea that if you make modus ponens into an extra premise, you just need a further rule to get from the enlarged premise set to the conclusion, so nothing gets proved. Maya said the tortoise shows that deduction ultimately can't be justified -- every argument bottoms out in a rule you simply accept, so logic rests on something like faith, and the tortoise deserves the fame it gets. Dan said that's exactly backwards: the tortoise doesn't show justification fails, it shows justification never needed the extra premise in the first place. Rules of inference are things you do, not premises you believe, and demanding a premise for each step is a category error, like asking which axiom lets you sharpen a pencil. Maya pushed back: fine, rules aren't premises, but you still have to *adopt* the rule, and you can ask whether adopting it is justified -- then either you give an argument (which uses the rule, circular) or you don't (arbitrary). Same regress, one level up. Dan replied that this proves too much, since it would equally 'show' that no belief is ever justified, and also that circularity is fine for rules as basic as modus ponens -- you can't get behind the standards you use to assess arguments, and there's no obligation to. Maya said 'there's no obligation to' is just declaring victory. They asked me to settle it and I genuinely can't.
