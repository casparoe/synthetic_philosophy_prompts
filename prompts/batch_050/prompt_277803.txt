Both my roommates are 1Ls and last night they got into a genuinely heated argument over a torts hypothetical, and since I was sitting there they made me referee even though I studied linguistics. The setup is two cases. Case one: a woman gets sideswiped by a bus at night, never saw it, but she can prove that one company, Blue Bus, runs 80% of the buses on that stretch of road. Case two: a rodeo has 1000 attendees and the organizer can only account for 400 paid tickets, so he picks one attendee at random and sues him for the ticket price. Civil cases only need more-likely-than-not, and in both cases the math is well over 50%. The puzzle is that courts, and most people's gut, say the plaintiff should probably lose anyway in at least one of these, even though the same plaintiffs would win if the identical numbers came packaged as an eyewitness who is right 80% of the time.

Dana's position was that this is pure innumeracy: a probability is a probability, and the legal system already runs on witness reliability statistics, it just launders them through a human face, so refusing the base rate is sentimentality that lets likely-liable defendants walk and systematically underdeters. Marcus said there's a real difference between evidence that this defendant did it and evidence that people-like-him usually do: if Blue Bus loses every case like this, it ends up paying for 100% of the accidents on that road, including the 20% the other company caused, while that company pays nothing, so liability gets completely decoupled from actual wrongdoing and the incentives are a mess. Dana replied that that's just an argument for apportioning damages by probability, the way courts handle market-share liability, not an argument for ignoring statistics altogether. Marcus then fell back on what I think is his real point: a system that openly tells a defendant we have no idea whether you personally did anything, but people in your situation usually do, can't produce verdicts the loser can accept, and a legal system needs verdicts that at least purport to be about what actually happened in this particular case, even if that costs some accuracy. Dana said if we're going to sacrifice accuracy for the losing party's feelings we should at least admit trials are theater, and somewhere around there it definately stopped being productive.

So please adjudicate this move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly right to clearly mistaken, with a sentence or two of justification each, and tell me who is more right overall and what both of them are missing. And the thing I actually care about most: are the two cases even the same puzzle? In the bus case there's at least the case-specific fact that she was hit on that road, at that time, while the rodeo defendant has literally nothing connecting him to the non-payment except the base rate. If those are different puzzles, say so and say whether it changes your verdicts on the individual moves.
