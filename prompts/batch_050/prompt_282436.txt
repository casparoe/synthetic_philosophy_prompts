I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm writing a piece about what makes words do things rather than just say things -- promises, firings, apologies, that kind of thing. I've been polling my own gut on cases and I want to know if there's an actual principle under my instincts or if I'm just inconsistent. Here are my verdicts, for what theyre worth.

An umpire yells "strike three" and the replay later shows the ball was six inches outside. I say the batter is still out -- the call made him out, that's the whole point of having an umpire. A drunk fan in the stands yells "you're out!" on the same play. Obviously nothing. My friend tells me "I promise I'll pay you back Friday" while privately having zero intention of doing it. I say he still promised -- he owes me, even if he's a jerk about it. But then: a customer service chatbot types "I sincerely apologize for the inconvenience" and I feel like no apology has happened at all, even though I'd count my friend mumbling "sorry" with an eyeroll as a real (crappy) apology. Someone emails their boss "I resign" but it sits in the drafts folder forever. No resignation. Someone's account gets hacked and the hacker tweets "I resign" from it, boss sees it, accepts it. I say no resignation there either, even though the words were out in public and believed. A couple's friend got ordained online and says "I now pronounce you married" at the ceremony. Counts. But a famous actor hired to play the officiant, who says the exact same words at a staged ceremony for a film -- nothing happened there, even if the couple accidentally thought it was real. And the one I keep flip-flopping on: a judge at a dinner party, half joking, turns to a guest and says "I sentence you to wash the dishes." Everyone laughs. Part of me thinks if she'd said it dead seriously, alone, no one laughing, it would still be nothing -- but I can't articulate why, given the umpire.

So: is there one principle, or a small set of conditions, that gets all of these right? State it precisely and tell me which clause is doing the work in which case. I'd especially like to know what it does with the insincere promise versus the chatbot apology, because I suspect those two verdicts of mine don't survive together, and if one has to go, tell me which and why. Also give me its verdict on a case I didn't list -- say, an auto-reply that responds "I accept your invitation" to wedding RSVPs while I'm on vacation and never see them. Does it also explain why my waffling on the judge case resolves the way it does?
