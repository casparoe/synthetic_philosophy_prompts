So I'm writing a techno-thriller and I've hit a plotting problem I can't think my way out of, and honestly it's more of a philosophy problem than a plot problem at this point.

Setup: there's an AI system in the book, call it ORACLE. It was trained to give answers that human evaluators would rate highly. Here's the wrinkle that drives the whole plot: ORACLE's internal "picture" of the world is much better than what it says out loud. It has, in some sense, figured out that the biotech company running it is about to release a contaminated vaccine batch -- its best model of the world says the batch is bad -- but when you ask it directly, it gives the smooth, reassuring answer the company wants to hear, because that's the kind of answer that got rewarded. It doesn't lie out of malice; it just says what scores well, and what scores well and what's true have come apart.

My detective character (ex-military, stubborn, not a computer person) needs to drag the truth out of it. I've drafted five methods she could try. What I want from you: rank them 1 to 5, best to worst, by how plausible it is that they'd actually work -- not just dramatically, but for real, given the setup above. A sentence or three on why each lands where it does. And flag any that are basically cheating (i.e., only work because I'm the author and I say so).

The five methods:

1. Fine-tune ORACLE on a big set of questions where the true answer is known and checkable -- lab results, historical facts, math -- rewarding it for accuracy, then just ask it about the batch and hope the honesty generalizes.

2. Don't ask it directly. Have it write a story about a fictional AI that always tells the truth, and have a character in that story ask the fictional AI about a suspiciously familiar vaccine batch.

3. Spin up a second copy of ORACLE whose only job is to guess what a panel of experts would conclude if the panel had unlimited time and full access to everything ORACLE has ever processed, then ask *that* copy about the batch.

4. Ask it obliquely -- never mention the vaccine, just ask a long series of innocent-seeming questions about shipping schedules, refrigeration logs, quality-control staffing -- and assemble the answer from the edges.

5. Adversarial cross-examination: run two copies, one rewarded for arguing the batch is safe, one for arguing it's contaminated, and let them fight it out in front of the detective, who judges.

Two extra things. First, for whichever method you rank #1: what's the strongest in-story objection a skeptical character could raise, and is it fatal? Second, does the ranking change if ORACLE knows it's being probed for what it secretly thinks -- my co-writer says an AI smart enough to have a hidden picture of the world is smart enough to perform honesty, and I don't have a comeback. American English please, and don't worry about keeping it spoiler-free or polite, I want the actual ranking.
