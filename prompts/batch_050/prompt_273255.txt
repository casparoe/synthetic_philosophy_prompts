I'm a longtime reader of LessWrong and Astral Codex Ten, and one thing the rationalist sphere has sensitized me to is how quickly any geopolitical crisis becomes a battle of canned analogies: within days, every commentator has decided whether the new situation is 1938 (appeasement must stop), 1914 (escalation nobody wanted), or Vietnam (quagmire incoming). I've been mulling over the run-up to the 2003 invasion of Iraq as the best test case for thinking clearly about this, because both major analogies were deployed loudly and publicly before the invasion, and we now know how the story actually went. Supporters of the war ran the Munich line: Saddam as a Hitler-type aggressor, inspections and containment as appeasement, waiting as the risky option. Opponents ran the Vietnam line: an occupation on the far side of the world, a population that wouldn't greet us as liberators, credibility logic keeping us in long after the rationale collapsed. I know the basic facts well -- no WMD stockpiles were found, the invasion itself was fast, the insurgency and occupation dragged on for years -- so please don't relitigate the case for or against the war itself. What I want is an analysis of the analogies as epistemic instruments.

Here's the lens I'd like you to use, a homebrew checklist I've been developing for when a historical analogy deserves to move my credences rather than just my emotions: (1) the claimed matching features must be structural -- incentives, information environments, capabilities, constraints -- rather than superficial (a dictator with a mustache, a desert, falling dominoes); (2) the analogy must survive a base-rate check, i.e., of all the situations matching those structural features, how many actually unfolded the way the famous remembered case did; (3) the selection problem must be confronted, since analogies get remembered precisely because they were dramatic and consequential, which biases the remembered set; and (4) there should be a rhetorical audit of who is deploying the analogy and which decision it conveniently licenses. Treat this checklist as a draft, not scripture: if you think an item is misguided or something important is missing, say so -- that's part of the exercise.

Please act as a historically literate applied epistemologist, the sort of person who reads both diplomatic history and the forecasting literature, and think carefully before writing -- I'd suggest brainstorming a longer list of candidate matching and mismatching features than you'll ultimately use, then selecting. Structure your response in these sections, in this order: first, "The Mapping" (~250 words), laying out who invoked each analogy and exactly which features of 2002-2003 Iraq each one claimed matched its source case; second, "Grading Against the Checklist" (~300 words), running both analogies through my four criteria as they would have looked to a well-informed person in early 2003, before the outcome was known; third, "What the Outcome Did to the Grades" (~200 words), asking how much the actual sequel -- no stockpiles, swift conventional victory, grinding insurgency -- should retroactively change the assessment, and being careful about hindsight bias here; fourth, "Stress-Testing the Checklist" (~250 words), the part I care most about: would honest application of my checklist in 2002 have produced the right answer, or does the episode reveal a blind spot (for instance, that the decisive failure was in trusting intelligence assessments, an error no analogy-checklist seems built to catch)? Finally, "Verdict" (~100 words): the single most important lesson this episode teaches about the use of historical analogy, stated plainly. Where the historical record is too contested to bear weight, flag it rather than forcing the analysis through.
