I chair the graduate admissions committee in my philosophy department. Each cycle we read roughly 150 files, spending perhaps twenty minutes apiece on the serious contenders, and the four of us then argue to a ranked list of about fifteen offers. Over the years I have become convinced that our file-reading is riddled with what the epistemic injustice literature would recognize as credibility deficits and excesses: letters of recommendation are discounted or inflated depending on the writer's idiom and the applicant's identity, nonstandard academic English in a writing sample gets read as unrigorous thinking, an applicant's description of some adversity gets read as excuse-making in one file and as resilience in another, and I suspect our sense of who sounds "brilliant" tracks pedigree and polish more than we admit. I have taught Fricker and the subsequent literature on testimonial smothering and hermeneutical gaps, but teaching a concept and running a fair admissions meeting are different things.

What I want is a procedure I can actually put in front of my committee: an ordered sequence of questions to ask of each file and of each of our own reactions to it, with an account of what would count as an adequate answer at each step, where the procedure should stop with a determinate instruction (for instance, a rule that certain kinds of evidence must be re-read or set aside), and where it must instead hand the question back to us because it is a judgment call that no checklist can settle. The translation is not straightforward, and I want the answer to be honest about that: this literature was developed largely for interpersonal testimony, and I am asking it to govern an institutional practice of reading documents written by people I will never meet. Which distinctions from the theory must survive that translation, and which can be safely dropped at the level of a twenty-minute file read? I also want the procedure to guard against overcorrection — a mechanical credibility bonus for some writers would itself be a distortion of the evidence, not a remedy — so please say where the line falls between a corrective virtue and a compensatory error.

Please then run the procedure on three cases I have constructed from experience. First: a glowing letter from a very prominent philosopher describes a woman's work as diligent, thorough, and careful, language that has appeared in letters for her male peers as brilliant and original. Second: an applicant's statement describes, in vague and somewhat evasive terms, an experience of mistreatment at their prior institution that they seem to lack the vocabulary to name, and one committee member reads the vagueness as a red flag about the applicant's forthrightness. Third: a writing sample in fluent but nonstandard academic English from an applicant at an institution none of us knows, whose file the committee is initially inclined to set aside in favor of a polished file from a famous program. One of these should, if the procedure is working, yield an answer my committee will not like. Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do — which failure modes of our current practice it will not catch, and where I should expect it to misfire.
