I'm writing a piece about the well-being-science literature and keep running into the dictum that each of us is the final authority on our own mind -- invoked, in particular, to argue that a person's self-reported happiness should override any behavioral or physiological measure a researcher might prefer. I'd like to get precise about what that slogan could actually be claiming. Please lay out the distinct theses it might be expressing -- for instance, that sincere first-person reports cannot be mistaken, that nothing in one's mind can fail to register with its owner, that we each possess a distinctive method of access to our own states that outsiders lack, that the authority is a social or normative convention about who gets the last word rather than an epistemic fact at all, or that first-person reports are merely defeasible evidence of a kind others cannot obtain. For each thesis: say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or straightforwardly false, and whether it would actually license the use the slogan was put to, i.e. discounting external measures of a person's well-being. Where the readings come apart on a concrete case -- someone who sincerely reports contentment while every outward indicator suggests otherwise, or someone who comes to see, in therapy, that a long-standing motive was never what they took it to be -- say so explicitly rather than letting the menu blur the difference.
