So I'm a phd student in political theory and I've been stuck on the Grand Inquisitor chapter in The Brothers Karamazov for weeks — you know, Ivan's poem where Christ comes back during the Inquisition and the Inquisitor explains, at length, that the Church has "corrected" his work, because people can't actually bear freedom: they'll happily trade it for bread, miracle, mystery, and authority, and they'll be *happier* for the trade, which the Inquisitor takes to justify the whole arrangement.

Write me a satirical internal memo, about 500-600 words, from the Grand Inquisitor's administrative office to regional staff, announcing this quarter's policy updates in the ongoing project of relieving humanity of the burden of its freedom. Keep it deadpan bureaucratic-corporate: KPIs for bread distribution, targets for voluntary freedom-surrender rates, a note about the mystery pipeline, maybe an awkward incident report about a recently detained visitor who had to be released after an unexplained kissing incident, which caused a small but worrying spike in unlicensed freedom. The humor has to come from taking the Inquisitor's actual argument seriously and operationalizing it — e.g., frustration that a stubborn minority keeps exercising choice and tanking the aggregate happiness metrics, or concern that too much visible comfort makes people nostalgic for suffering. A reader who knows the chapter should recognize every joke as a fair exaggeration of something the Inquisitor actually argues or Dostoevsky actually shows, not a strawman — that matters to me, since half the point is that the Inquisitor's position is disturbingly reasonable. Don't wink at the reader, no overt moralizing, and don't let the memo's author accidentally concede that freedom might be worth having.

No need for a formal verdict, but end with a final line giving a one-sentence summary of the memo, still in the corporate voice.
