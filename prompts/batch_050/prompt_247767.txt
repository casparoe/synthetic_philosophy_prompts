I'm a grad student in anthropology and I'm putting together a prospectus for my dissertation (planning on about 18 months of work). Here's my half-baked idea and I need you to turn it into a proper proposal.

The rough thought: costly signaling / handicap principle explanations get thrown around constantly in my field -- the big feast is a signal of generosity, the impractical ritual knowledge is a signal of commitment, whatever. But I've noticed that basically ANY expensive behavior can be redescribed as a signal after the fact, and I can't find anyone who states clearly what evidence would show that some behavior is NOT a costly signal. If the theory is compatible with everything, it explains nothing. So my project would be: develop explicit criteria for when a costly signaling explanation of some human behavior is actually testable, versus when it's just a just-so story wearing formal clothes.

Turn that into a prospectus with:

- A precise statement of the question. I want it sharpened beyond "is costly signaling unfalsifiable" -- maybe something like: under what conditions does a signaling explanation of a practice make predictions that a pure prestige/competition or functional explanation of the same practice doesn't?
- Why the standard responses don't settle this. People usually say "just measure whether the signal is honest" (i.e. does the cost correlate with the hidden quality) but I think that misses the problem, because you can also ask whether the behavior exists BECAUSE of its signaling function at all, and that's a harder question that honesty-measurement doesn't touch. Work this out properly.
- The method, concretely. I'm imagining a mix of (a) conceptual work distinguishing different claims the theory makes (the signal is honest vs. the signal is why the trait persists vs. the audience actually uses it to make decisions), and (b) developing what would count as disconfirming evidence for each claim, ideally illustrated with one or two worked examples from ethnography-type settings (feasting, initiation rites, conspicuous ritual skill) -- but hypothetical/stylized examples are fine, don't invent real ethnographies.
- What a successful deliverable looks like: probably a kind of checklist or decision procedure an ethnographer could apply in the field to figure out if "it's a costly signal" is even the right kind of claim to be testing about a given practice.
- What would count as the project failing. E.g., one way it fails is if it turns out the criteria can't be stated without smuggling in the conclusion.
- An honest note on scope: is 18 months realistic, and what part would I cut first if I had to?

Don't include citations or a lit review, I'll add the references myself -- just refer to positions and arguments by description.
