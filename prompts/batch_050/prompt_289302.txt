I am a retired experimental physicist who, in my working years, built and programmed a good deal of laboratory automation, and who in retirement has fallen in with a small philosophy reading group. We have been working through the frame problem in artificial intelligence, prompted by the familiar thought experiment of the robot that must reason about whether the battery it is hauling sits on a wagon loaded with a bomb. I confess the problem resonated with me more viscerally than most things in our reading: when I automated my own greenhouse years ago, I discovered that the genuinely hard part of the programming was never telling the system what an action changes, but everything it leaves alone — that opening a vent does not alter the pH of the irrigation reservoir, does not move the seed trays, does not reset the clock. It has struck me that our group keeps talking past one another because some of us treat the frame problem as a narrow technical puzzle about logical notation, while others treat it as a deep problem about how any finite creature, machine or animal, knows what is relevant. I would like an instrument that forces us to locate where we actually disagree.

Please construct a decision tree that takes a reader from the shared starting commitment — roughly, that an agent which represents the world must somehow avoid re-deriving, after every action, the whole of what remains unchanged — to a named position on what the frame problem fundamentally is and what would count as solving it. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: each branch node should be a single diagnostic question on which the positions genuinely come apart, phrased concretely enough that someone could answer it without knowing any of the labels — for instance, questions about whether the difficulty would vanish in a system with unlimited computing time, whether it would arise for a creature that never represents anything symbolically at all, or whether it resurfaces, in different dress, for a language-model agent that must decide what to retain in its finite context window when acting over many steps. The leaves should include at least: the classical logicist solution (frame axioms or successor-state-style completions of the situation calculus); the Dreyfus-style view that the problem concerns significance rather than inference and is insoluble for rule-following systems; the embodied or enactive dissolution; the claim that learned statistical world models of the modern machine-learning kind dissolve the problem (or merely relocate it); and a deflationary leaf holding that the frame problem was never one problem but a conflation of several. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, note it and say briefly why — incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored.

A few further constraints. Aim for eight to twelve branch nodes. Wherever useful, anchor the diagnostic questions in cases rather than terminology: my greenhouse controller, the robot-and-wagon scenario, or a household robot asked to fetch milk from a refrigerator that may have been emptied since it last looked — feel free to construct variants of these. Every node must be answerable by an intelligent amateur who has not read the literature; nothing may hinge on already knowing which camp a question leads to. At each leaf, give the position its name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate — I suspect the embodied position and the learned-world-model position may collapse into one another, and I would rather have that admitted than concealed by a spurious branch.
