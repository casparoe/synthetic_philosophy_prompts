I'm a journalist who has covered robotics and cognitive science for about fifteen years, and I've been asked — to my surprise — to give the keynote toast at the retirement dinner of a scientist I've profiled twice. Please write the speech for me. Here's the situation. Dr. Elena Varga spent the first half of her career as a committed representationalist: her early lab built robots that navigated by painstakingly constructing and updating internal models of their environments, and she was famous in the field for arguing that a creature without an inner map of the world was, in her phrase, a very expensive thermostat. Then, in her fifties, she underwent the most public conversion in modern cognitive science. After a visiting year in an embodied-cognition lab, she became an enactivist: she spent the second half of her career building systems with no central model at all, arguing that intelligence lives in the ongoing loop between perception and action, and that the world itself does most of the work her old maps were trying to do. She published a celebrated mea culpa retracting the thermostat line. The audience will be roughly half colleagues from both of her eras — including several who never forgave the conversion and several who never respected the early work — and half family and old friends who know her mostly as the woman who is bad at giving directions.

There's one complication that I think should be the spine of the speech. Her final project, which ran for six years and never quite worked, was an attempt at reconciliation: a robot with two architectures, a deliberative module that built an internal representation of its surroundings, and a reactive enactivist layer that responded directly to the world without one. The joke around the lab — affectionate, I should stress — was that the two modules kept sabotaging each other: the planner would confidently steer the robot toward where the map said the door was, and the reactive layer would veer away from where the door actually was, and the robot ended up celebrated mainly as a metaphor. I want the speech to take that failure seriously rather than treating it as a punchline: to ask, through the story of her career, whether the representationalist and enactivist pictures are rivals at all, or whether they're describing different timescales or different jobs — and whether Elena's real achievement was to have lived the whole debate in one working life, which most of us only get to referee. The speech shouldn't declare a winner, but it also shouldn't retreat into mushy both-sides-ism; it should have an actual thesis about what her career shows.

The speech should run about six minutes aloud. It needs one running image or metaphor that the family can follow from start to finish, and at least two or three details the specialists will recognize as precise references to the enactivism–representationalism dispute (the knowledge-in-the-head-versus-in-the-world issue, the role of sensorimotor coupling, the frame problem's shadow over her early work) without stopping to explain them. It should be warm, a little funny at her expense in the way she permits, and land on something true about intellectual courage rather than on sentimentality. Please write it in American English.
