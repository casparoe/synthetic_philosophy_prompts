I am designing the opening session of an integrity-training workshop for an international NGO whose staff come from two quite different backgrounds: some from Western compliance and audit cultures, others from local administrations in West Africa and Southeast Asia, where gift-giving, favor networks, and obligations to kin and community are woven into ordinary professional life. I want the session to begin with a diagnostic classification exercise: participants respond to a set of concrete cases, and their pattern of judgments is mapped onto the main philosophical and social-scientific positions on what corruption is. The point is to show participants that they already hold an implicit theory, and that reasonable theories genuinely disagree, before we ever discuss policy.

Please construct this instrument as a decision tree or taxonomy, meeting the following requirements.

**Positions that must appear as leaves.** Describe each in plain language; do not assume participants know any labels. (1) Legal formalism: an act is corrupt if and only if it violates applicable law or explicit institutional rules. (2) The transactional public-office view: corruption is the misuse of entrusted public authority for private gain within a specific exchange, with the bribe as paradigm. (3) The principal-agent view: corruption is an agent's betrayal of a principal's interests for an inducement, and so occurs in the private sector just as in government. (4) The institutional or systemic view: an institution, and not merely a transaction, is corrupt when money or favor networks bend it away from its purpose, even if every individual act within it is legal. (5) The relational-cultural view: whether a transfer is gift, tribute, or bribe depends on local norms of reciprocity and relationship, so the same envelope may be courtesy in one setting and corruption in another. (6) The universalist harm view: some practices, notably the sale of decisions that ought to be made on the merits, are corrupt everywhere because of what they do to those they exclude, regardless of local acceptance.

**Diagnostic cases.** Provide about eight concrete scenarios, each phrased so that someone can react to it without knowing any theory. They must include at least: a clerk who processes paperwork faster in return for a small payment that is expected but never demanded; an official who hires a qualified nephew over marginally better-qualified strangers; a firm that donates generously to a regulator's favorite charity; a valuable gift given strictly after a favor, where both parties understand a continuing pattern is being established; a practice fully accepted locally that predictably excludes the poor from a public service; a lobbyist whose influence over legislation is entirely legal and disclosed; and a private-sector purchasing agent accepting supplier kickbacks. For each case, record what verdict each leaf position delivers, and flag any case on which a single leaf position splits internally because the case underdetermines its verdict.

**Tree construction.** Build the tree from the questions that actually separate the positions (e.g., whether public office is essential, whether a rule must be broken, whether legality settles the matter, whether local norms can change an act's status, whether institutions as well as persons can be the subject). Label each branch with the claim that splits it. Flag any pair of leaf positions that the eight cases cannot separate, and explain what further case would be needed. Identify any coherent combination of answers that no position on my list occupies, and say briefly whether that cell is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

**Scope note.** Conclude with a short paragraph on what the instrument cannot do: in particular, whether Western anti-corruption standards should be applied universally is a normative question the taxonomy maps but does not settle, and the note should say which branch of the tree that disagreement lives on.

**Output format.** Return the deliverable as a single JSON object with the following fields: "tree", an array of nodes, each with "question", "branches" (each branch labeled with its splitting claim and pointing to a child node or leaf), and where applicable "leaf_position"; "positions", an array of objects with "name", "plain_language_definition", and "most_awkward_commitment" (one sentence); "cases", an array of objects with "id", "scenario", and "verdicts" (an object mapping position names to verdicts, using "splits" where a position is internally divided); "inseparable_pairs", an array of objects with "positions" and "distinguishing_case_needed"; and "empty_cells", an array of objects with "answer_pattern" and "why_unoccupied".
