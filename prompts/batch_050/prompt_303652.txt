I'm on the scientific advisory board of a small foundation that funds conceptual work at the boundary of philosophy and the sciences. We have budget for three two-year projects on the question of whether the brain is a computer, and I'm drafting the call for proposals. I need help deciding which open questions the call should prioritize, and in what order.

To fix terms, I find it useful to split "computationalism" into three claims that are often run together: (1) descriptive computationalism -- brain activity can be modeled, to some approximation, as a computation; (2) explanatory computationalism -- computation is the right level of description for explaining cognition, in the way that thermodynamics rather than particle mechanics explains a gas; and (3) constitutive computationalism -- cognition literally is computation, such that any physical system implementing the right organization would have the same cognitive states, whatever it is made of. A project could bear on one of these without bearing on the others, and I want the call to be explicit about which claim each funded project targets.

The candidate open questions I have in mind, though you should feel free to add or remove some:

- The implementation problem: under what conditions does a physical system implement a computation, rather than merely being interpretable as one under a sufficiently clever mapping? Some proposed answers seem to make nearly everything compute everything, which would trivialize (3); others look tailored to get brains in and rocks out, which invites the charge of gerrymandering.
- The analog/digital question: neurons are noisy, spiky, chemically modulated, and continuous in ways that digital models abstract away. Is there a principled account of which of these details are mere implementation and which are computationally relevant, or is that judgment always relative to a pre-chosen explanatory target -- and if so, does that undercut (3)?
- The explanatory-payoff question: what has the computational framing actually explained in neuroscience that a purely dynamical or mechanistic description would not? If the honest answer is "not much yet, but it will," what would success even look like, and on what timescale?
- Falsifiability: is there any discovery about the brain that would show (2) or (3) to be false? If not, what kind of claims are they -- empirical hypotheses, methodological commitments, or something else?
- Consciousness as a test case: some treat subjective experience as the make-or-break phenomenon for (3); others hold that consciousness is a separable problem and that computationalism should be assessed on perception, memory, and motor control. Which stance one takes changes how much weight the first four questions get.

Which of these questions are upstream of which -- that is, which answers would settle or reshape the others -- and which two or three should the call prioritize? Defend the ordering with explicit criteria: how much downstream work depends on each question, how tractable it is for philosophers working with rather than against practicing neuroscientists over a two-year window, and how likely the answer is to actually change minds rather than entrench existing camps. Also tell me which question on my list you would cut entirely as overrated or ill-posed, and what evidence or result in the next few years would move something up or down your ranking. The deliverable I want is a defended agenda for the call, not a survey of the debate.
