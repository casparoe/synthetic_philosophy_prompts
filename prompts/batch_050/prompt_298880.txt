My friend thinks this basically sinks the whole idea of a radically improved world. I think it's a 19th century guy vibing. But honestly I can't tell exactly where I disagree, because the passage bundles like four different claims into one sentence.

So here's what I want. Give me your actual credence that the core claim is right — that if people's material needs and ordinary desires were reliably met, humans would end up generating comparable or worse suffering for themselves (through boredom, self-destruction, or conflict) than what nature currently imposes. Candidates I'd suggest, but use your own if these are wrong: whether boredom is a genuine and severe form of suffering rather than a trivial inconvenience; whether the "need for struggle" is a fixed fact about human psychology or something malleable that a different upbringing/culture could remove; whether status and positional competition means a paradise of satisfied desire is incoherent in the first place, since some desires are inherently about beating others; and whether hedonic adaptation means any achievable paradise gets renormalized to neutral anyway. Put a rough probability on each sub-claim, say how they combine into your final number (do they multiply? is it a disjunction? spell it out), and tell me which single sub-question my friend and I most likely disagree on given he's at maybe 0.85 and I'm at maybe 0.2.
