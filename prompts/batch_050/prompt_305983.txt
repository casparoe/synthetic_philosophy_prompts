I was reading a chapter on Wittgenstein's On Certainty in a book about skepticism, and it summarized his view like this: "Wittgenstein's answer to the skeptic is that some propositions — like that I have two hands, or that the earth existed long before I was born — are so obviously true that doubting them is unreasonable. He calls these 'hinges': they are the rock-bottom, self-evident certainties on which all our other knowledge rests. Because hinges are certain, we don't need to justify them, and since every rational person accepts the same hinges, skepticism is just a refusal to be reasonable. So in the end Wittgenstein gives us a foundation of indubitable truths, much like Descartes wanted, except the foundation is common sense rather than pure reason."

I've only read summaries of Wittgenstein myself, but this feels off to me in a way I can't pin down — I thought part of his point was that hinges aren't really the kind of thing that's straightforwardly true or justified at all? Could you go through the summary and tell me which parts a careful reader of On Certainty would accept, which are distortions, and whether the final claim about him offering a Cartesian-style foundation survives fixing them?
