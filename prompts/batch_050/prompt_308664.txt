Something I keep running into in the rationalist-adjacent corners of the internet (LessWrong, ACX comment sections, a couple of econ blogs) is the Kelly criterion getting promoted from a bet-sizing formula into a general philosophy of life. The move goes: your career, your savings rate, whether to start a startup, whether to move cities, maybe even who you date — these are all "bets," you should size them to maximize the long-run geometric growth of your "bankroll," and people who blow up did so because they were betting above full Kelly. Sometimes Ole Peters' ergodicity economics stuff gets cited to upgrade this from a heuristic to a theorem: maximize your time-average growth rate, not the ensemble-average expectation.

I'm suspicious the analogy is doing more work than it can carry, but I can't pin down exactly where it snaps. Can you audit it?

What I want:

1. The correspondence table. In the casino or portfolio case you have a bankroll, win probabilities, payoff odds, repeated roughly-independent trials, and a growth rate to maximize. When someone says "going to grad school is a bet, size it Kelly-style," what plays each of those roles? What even is the bankroll when the stake is three years of your twenties? What are the "odds" when the payoff is a different life rather than a multiple of your stake? If a correspondent has no good analog, say so rather than forcing one.

2. Where it breaks. My candidate list, which you should add to or veto from: you get one life, not an ensemble and not infinite repetitions, so it's unclear which average is even defined for a single-shot decision; lots of life decisions have absorbing states (death, ruin, permanent reputational damage) past which no growth rate matters; outcomes across your life are correlated *through you* (burnout from your career bet tanks your marriage bet); the formula is notoriously sensitive to overestimating your edge, and in life your edge is always self-reported with no house posting the true odds; and "maximize log wealth" smuggles in one particular risk attitude and then calls it rationality rather than a preference.

3. Whether the advice survives anyway. Even if the ergodicity framing is philosophically shaky for one-shot lives, is "never take a risk whose worst case ends your game, and do take cheap positive-expectation gambles" still basically right under a less grand name? Or is full-vs-fractional-Kelly talk actively misleading once you leave finance, because it lends fake precision to decisions where the inputs are made up?

One epistemic request: please keep a clean line between what's settled math and what's genuinely contested. The Kelly result under its assumptions isn't controversial. Whether Peters' time-average-vs-ensemble-average critique actually dethrones expected utility theory is, I gather, disputed by working economists, and whether any of it transfers to careers and relationships is further out still. Don't present the contested layers as established just because they get upvoted — flag which rung of the ladder each claim sits on.
