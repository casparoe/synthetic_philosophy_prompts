So I've been reading a bunch about Nyāya epistemology lately (long story, started from a "what would a non-western epistemology of instrumental rationality look like" rabbit hole) and I want to actually USE the pramāṇa framework instead of just nodding along to it. Here's my situation: I'm one of the rotating moderators of a small "claim court" channel in a rationalist-adjacent discord, where people bring claims they saw somewhere and we rate them once a week. I want a decision procedure, built out of the four pramāṇas, that any of us can run on a claim in like 30-40 minutes.

Concretely: perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. Write the procedure as an ordered set of questions with stopping points. Some specific things I want it to wrestle with:

1. Perception as the foundation. As I understand it, the Nyāya take is that the other pramāṇas all bottom out in perception somehow — inference needs perceived regularities, testimony needs a speaker who's in a position to have perceived (or otherwise known) the thing. So the procedure should have a step that basically asks "whose actual eyeballs or instruments are at the bottom of this claim, and how many removes are we from them?" and treats that as a first-class rating input, not a vibe.

2. Inference: the procedure needs the actual machinery, not just "does this follow." I'm thinking of the pervasion requirement — the hetu has to be invariably accompanied by the conclusion, like smoke and fire — so the checklist should force the reviewer to state the pervasion claim explicitly ("every case of X is a case of Y") and then go hunting for even one counterexample, and say what to do when you can't find one but also can't be sure you looked (which is most of the time, honestly).

3. Comparison/upamāna is the weird one. It seems to mostly be about learning what a word refers to ("a gavaya is like a cow"), not about evidence for claims. Should it even be in the procedure? If yes, I want the step to do real work — maybe as a check on whether the claim is even using a concept we all point at the same thing with. If no, tell me why it's fine to drop it.

4. Testimony: encode the āpta conditions properly — the speaker has to actually know the thing, intend to communicate it without distortion, and be competent to express it. The procedure should make us assess each condition separately, because in practice "trusted person said it" smushes all three together and that's how we get laundered hearsay from popular bloggers.

Also: what does the procedure do when pramāṇas conflict — e.g., your own perception says one thing and an expert's testimony says another? And mark the points where judgment can't be eliminated and it just hands off to the reviewer.

Then run it on three claims: (a) "moderate alcohol consumption extends lifespan," (b) a specific anecdote a well-liked community figure told about their meditation retreat changing their personality, where the whole room wants to believe it, and (c) "this startup's product will fail because their last one did." I want at least one worked case where following the procedure honestly gives an answer the room won't like — my guess is (b), where there's exactly one perceiver, no checkable pervasion, and the testimony passes the āpta conditions on sincerity but maybe not on the "actually knows" condition, since self-reports of personality change are notoriously unreliable. But run it properly rather than taking my word for it.

Finally: one paragraph at the end on what the procedure structurally cannot catch — i.e., what kinds of bad claims sail through a pramāṇa-based checklist, since presumably the framework was built for a world without p-hacking and engagement-optimized media.
