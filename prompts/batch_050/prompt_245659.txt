My flatmates Mara and Dev got into it over the colour phi demo after I showed it to them, and I was just washing up so I didn't really back either side. In the demo you flash a red dot on the left, then a green dot on the right, and people swear they see one dot moving across and changing colour part-way along — apparently before the green dot has even appeared. Mara said this is basically Stalinesque: the brain waits until it has both flashes, cooks up the whole moving-and-changing show afterwards, then presents it as if that was what was experienced all along. So there is no earlier conscious moment that gets corrected; there is only the final constructed percept, with the timing smudged.

Dev said no, that's too theatrical. He went more Orwellian: first you really do have a brief experience of the red dot just sitting or starting to move, then when the green dot lands the system rewrites the short-term record so you later report a smooth colour change, like an editor altering the minutes after the meeting. The experience happened, but the official memory is doctored.

Mara replied that this still smuggles in a little cinema: an original showing of red-not-yet-green that no subject can ever catch, followed by a cover-up. If nobody can ever attend to the first draft, saying it was conscious sounds like positing a ghost to save a theory. Dev said that's unfair, because lots of ordinary perception is postdictive — later input changes what you say about earlier input, like when a beep changes how many flashes you report — and it needn't prove there was a full private film before the beep, only that the brain doesn't freeze experience instantly.

Mara said that proves her point more than his: postdiction messes with reports and access, not necessarily with some already-final phenomenal fact, so Orwellian talk quietly trades on a confusion between what was phenomenally there and what can be remembered or said. Dev answered that maybe the confusion is deeper: once you give up the idea of one exact publication moment when content becomes conscious, and think instead of many local drafts competing, the Stalinesque versus Orwellian choice may be a false binary built on a newspaper-office model of the mind. Mara said that was a clever dodge but also a concession, because it admits his clean Orwellian story was too simple. Dev said not a concession, just the grown-up version of his view.
