So I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training at all, and I keep running into something at work that apparently philosophers have their own version of, the causal exclusion argument about mental causation (Jaegwon Kim and all that). Here's the thing that bugs me. Last week I told my team "the retry storm caused the outage." But obviously the outage also has a totally complete explanation at the level of individual packets and CPU instructions and electrons doing their thing. Nobody thinks the packets left some causal gap for the "retry storm" to fill. Yet the retry-storm explanation is clearly the useful one — it generalizes to other systems, it tells you what to fix, the packet-level story tells you nothing. So which one *really* caused the outage? Both? That's overdetermination and feels weird. Just the packets? Then "retry storm" is just a way of talking, and by analogy my headache doesn't really cause me to take an aspirin either.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers hashing out exactly this. One of them defends an exclusionist, Kim-flavored position: every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, so if mental (or higher-level) properties are distinct from physical ones and not identical to them, they're causally idle, and the comfortable talk about retry storms and headaches is epiphenomenal all the way down. The other defends higher-level causation — maybe via causal powers being inherited from the realizer, maybe via some proportionality/invariance idea that the *right* causal level is the one whose counterfactuals actually hold up — take your pick, but commit them to a definite view, don't let them just gesture at "functionalism."

Some requirements:

- Both of them should be equally sharp. I don't want a strawman on either side, and they have to actually respond to each other's arguments instead of delivering monologues.
- They should keep coming back to the engineering case and stress-test it. E.g., the exclusionist should press on whether the analogy is disanalogous because "retry storm" is just a description of a pattern in one physical system, whereas defenders of mental causation want something stronger. The other side should press back on whether the exclusionist's own notion of "physical cause" survives the same scrutiny.
- At some point one of them should genuinely concede a sub-point. Real arguments have those.
- By the end they should have pinned down at least one precise crux — a specific claim where they can state exactly what would have to be true for each of them to change their mind. Don't let it end in a vague "agree to disagree."
- Roughly 800–1200 words, dialogue format with names, no narrator summarizing who won.

Don't dumb it down for me btw, I can look stuff up if I need to.
