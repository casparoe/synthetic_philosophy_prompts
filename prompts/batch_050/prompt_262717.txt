Next semester I have agreed to give one ninety-minute guest session in a colleague's interdisciplinary graduate seminar on formal models of belief. The enrollment is split between philosophy students, who have some modal logic but mostly at the level of 'box means necessity', and computer science students, who know Kripke structures from program verification but have never thought of them as models of knowledge. My assigned topic is the problem of logical omniscience: the fact that under the standard possible-worlds modeling of knowledge, agents know every logical truth and every logical consequence of what they know. The seminar's organizer asked me not to present my own favored solution but to give the students a working understanding of why the problem arises with such force, why the obvious escapes fail, and what the live design trade-offs are. What I need from you is not the lecture itself but a plan for the lecture: the explanatory architecture.

Here is what I am hoping the plan will settle. First, the order of ideas. My instinct is that one must begin with the possible-worlds picture as a genuinely attractive model of information (what an agent's evidence rules out), then show omniscience falling out as a theorem rather than a stipulation, and only then survey responses. But I am unsure whether agents-as-automata intuitions from the CS half of the room should be activated earlier or deliberately held back. Second, the carrying example. The familiar devices are the agent who knows the axioms of arithmetic but not whether some particular open conjecture follows, and the agent who cannot tell whether two descriptions co-refer. Tell me which single scenario should bear the weight of the session, what it must contain and what it must avoid containing (I suspect any example framed in terms of 'hasn't bothered to compute it yet' invites the wrong objection), and when it should first appear. Third, the misconception map: I expect students to insist the problem is merely about bounded computation and so irrelevant to a logic of idealized knowledge; to think that dropping the rule of necessitation or moving to a non-normal modal logic simply dissolves everything; and to confuse logical omniscience with ordinary closure of belief under known entailment. For each misconception I anticipate, tell me at which point in the sequence it will naturally arise and where and how I should pre-empt it, including any misconceptions you expect that I have not listed. Fourth, how I should close: one question or exercise whose answer would show the students have understood why the problem is hard rather than merely that it exists.

Please structure your response with the following sections, in this order: Session Shape (roughly two sentences on the overall arc and its rationale); Dependency Order (about 150 words on what must precede what, and why the theorem-before-solutions ordering matters or does not); The Carrying Example (about 150 words on the chosen scenario and its required and forbidden features); Misconception Map (about 250 words, organized misconception by misconception, each with its expected point of emergence and the planned countermeasure); Closing Check (about 100 words on the final question and what a good answer sounds like); and Deliberate Omissions (about 80 words on which responses to the problem — for instance, awareness structures, impossible worlds, or algorithmic knowledge — I should mention only by name or exclude outright at this level, and why).
