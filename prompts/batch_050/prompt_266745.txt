A colleague and I have been arguing about Parfit's hitchhiker case (from Reasons and Persons), and she has produced an argument that I cannot refute but also cannot accept, because it seems to prove that rationality is useless across almost all of cooperative life. Here is her argument, as fairly as I can state it.

The setup: a stranger is stranded in the desert and will die without a ride. A driver will pick him up only if she is confident he will pay her $100 on arrival in town, and she is an excellent judge of character. The stranger knows all this.

(1) An act is rational for an agent at a time if and only if, among the acts available to him at that time, it maximizes his expected payoff.
(2) Once safely in town, both paying the $100 and not paying are available to the stranger, and not paying has the higher expected payoff — he already has the ride, and nothing further hinges on the payment.
(3) Therefore, paying in town is irrational by the standard in (1).
(4) The driver can foresee (3), so she will leave the stranger in the desert unless she believes he is the sort of person who would pay even when paying is irrational.
(5) So the stranger does better, by the lights of (1), if he is disposed to perform an act that (1) condemns as irrational.
(6) But a disposition that reliably produces irrational acts is itself an irrational disposition to acquire and maintain.
(7) So rationality requires the stranger to cultivate an irrational disposition: rationality is self-undermining.
(8) Nothing in steps (1)–(7) is special to deserts and drivers. The same structure recurs wherever present payoffs diverge from what one must be expected to do in order to secure others' cooperation — keeping promises, honoring threats, exacting revenge, repaying debts, punishing defectors.
(9) Since nearly all social cooperation has this structure, rationality is self-undermining in nearly all of human life, and cannot serve as a general guide to conduct.

I balk at (9), but every time I press a step, she shows me it follows from the previous one. I would most like to keep (1), since it seems like the minimal core of any means-end notion of rationality, and I would also like to keep the commonsense thought that promise-keeping and the like are not irrational. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost? In particular, I'd like to understand why the step I should reject looked so plausible, whether the trouble is with the standard in (1), with the move in (6) from acts to dispositions, or with the generalization in (8), and how the main responses one finds in the literature — Parfit's own talk of rational irrationality, the idea of resolute choice, and Gauthier-style constrained maximization — each locate the fault differently and what each has to give up.
