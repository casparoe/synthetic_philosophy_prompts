So last night I got into this stupidly long argument with a friend (we'd had a few beers) about the whole torture vs. dust specks thing. You know the setup: would you rather one person be horribly tortured for decades, or some astronomically huge number of people each get a single dust speck in the eye. I said the specks can't aggregate up to the torture, he kept insisting they must, and then around 2am he came out with this weird compromise view that I couldn't immediately refute, which annoyed me.

His idea, roughly: tiny harms like dust specks DO aggregate, so a billion specks are worse than a thousand specks, but they aggregate at a kind of diminishing exchange rate -- like each additional speck adds less total badness than the one before, asymptoting toward some cap that stays well below the badness of torture. So specks matter, more specks matter more, but no finite pile of specks ever outbadens the torture. He was very proud of this and called it a principled rejection of both "pure aggregation" and "threshold" views.

Anyway, I teach a second-year normative ethics course and I want to weaponize this on the midterm, because I suspect his view is actually broken in an interesting way and I want to see if students can find the break. Write me the assessment. Specifically, in this order:

1. The exam question (as I'd print it, ~100-150 words). It should present my friend's diminishing-exchange-rate view and ask students to evaluate it -- but the question itself shouldn't hint at whether the view is coherent or not, and it should force them to engage with the aggregation problem generally, not just with his specific proposal. Answerable in about 45 minutes.

2. A model answer (~500-600 words). The crucial thing I want it to land on: his view seems to smuggle in an inconsistency, because if specks genuinely aggregate against each other at a constant rate (so speck comparisons work), then you can build a chain of comparisons that either gets you past the torture (aggregation wins after all) or requires the discounting to kick in arbitrarily depending on what you're comparing the specks to -- and a good answer should pin down exactly where that tension lives rather than just gesturing at it. Also whether his view even deserves to count as a middle position, or whether it collapses into one of the two horns it was meant to avoid.

3. A marking scheme, 20 marks total, broken into 4 or 5 named must-hit points with the marks for each, plus for each point one line on the typical way a mediocre answer fumbles it (e.g. students who just recite the two standard positions and never notice the internal tension in his proposal, which is honestly what half of them will do).

Keep the tone of the exam question neutral/professional even though the origin story is me losing an arguement at 2am.
