I recently joined the safety team at an AI lab, and my first assignment is to write the internal case for treating chain-of-thought monitoring as a deployment safeguard -- that is, flagging models whose visible reasoning contains planning, deception, or reward-seeking before those models are given more autonomy. Before I present the proposal, I want my working assumptions audited for coherence, because I assembled them from different corners of the literature and have never checked them as a set. Here is where I currently stand: (a) a model's visible chain-of-thought is a meaningful safety signal, so the absence of scheming in the stated reasoning substantially raises confidence that no scheming is occurring; (b) models routinely fail to report what actually drives their outputs -- when an answer is demonstrably shifted by a cue in the prompt, the model typically omits the cue entirely and offers a plausible post-hoc rationale instead; (c) faithfulness is best defined causally, such that a chain of thought is faithful insofar as the final answer counterfactually depends on the steps it states, which can be tested by intervening on the reasoning and observing whether the answer changes; (d) scaling outcome-based reinforcement learning on chains of thought will tend to erode faithfulness, since the reasoning is optimized to produce rewarded answers rather than to expose the computation that produced them; (e) the right remedy is to make faithfulness itself a training target, using causal interventions to detect divergence between stated reasoning and actual drivers and penalizing that divergence. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision? I am particularly unsure whether the causal definition in (c) is strong enough to underwrite the monitoring claim in (a) given what (b) says, but I do not want to assume in advance that this is where the real problem lies. Present the audit as exactly five numbered items, each no more than two sentences: the first four should take the tensions and apparent tensions in descending order of severity, stating for each what would have to give, and the fifth should name the single cheapest revision and what it costs, with no introduction or conclusion.
