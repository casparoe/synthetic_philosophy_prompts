At a philosophy reading group last week, two members got into a running argument about origin essentialism -- the thesis, associated with Kripke, that a person could not have originated from a different sperm and egg than the ones they actually came from. Neither would concede, and eventually they turned to me to settle it, which I could not do.

Dana defended the thesis. Her opening move was intuitive: consider any world in which your parents conceive a child from a different sperm and egg. That child, she said, would at most be a genetic near-duplicate of you -- a sibling-like counterpart, not you. Treating it as you conflates qualitative similarity with numerical identity.

Marcus replied that the intuition is doing less work than it appears. We happily say a table could have been made from slightly different wood, and the reason the gamete case feels different, he argued, is that when we imagine the alternative child, we imagine meeting it, and no one we could meet could be us. But that is a fact about imagination, not about metaphysics. He then offered what he called the token problem: it is metaphysically possible for two numerically distinct sperm cells from the same father to be genetically identical. If the possible child from a genetically identical but numerically different sperm still wouldn't be you, then your identity depends not on anything about you -- your genome, your origins' character -- but on the bare thisness of a particular cell. That, he said, is metaphysically extravagant.

Dana answered that Marcus had mislocated the thesis. Origin essentialism is about causal history, not genetics: you are essentially the product of that particular conception event, and a different sperm means a different event, full stop. This is no more extravagant than saying this war could not have been fought by different armies, and it blocks a slippery slope -- if a slightly different origin is allowed, then by iteration a radically different one is too, and you end up able to have been anyone at all.

Marcus's last move was the one that stuck with me. Identical twins, he said, come from the very same sperm and egg. So the actual origin cannot by itself fix which person you are: your twin shares your entire origin yet is not you. If origins are not sufficient for identity even in the actual world, it is obscure why they should be necessary for it across worlds -- the theory, he claimed, takes on a necessity it cannot cash out.

Dana said this conflates sufficiency with necessity, but Marcus insisted the twin case shows origins were never tracking personhood in the first place.

Please adjudicate. For each of the specific points above -- the counterpart intuition, the token problem, the causal-history reply and the slippery slope, and the twin objection -- say who is right and why. Then give an overall verdict on who is more right, and identify anything both parties are missing.
