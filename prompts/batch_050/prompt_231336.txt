I'm working on a grant proposal about AI welfare and I keep hitting the same wall: everyone has an opinion on whether an RL agent can suffer, but nobody states what would even settle it. Before I look at anyone's theory of machine valence, I want the checklist. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of whether reward signals can constitute suffering (or pleasure) in artificial agents has to deliver -- which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must respect, which bullets it must either bite or explicitly dodge -- with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and mark which desiderata you think are genuine and which are just one camp's prejudice dressed up as common sense.

Some constraints I'd impose on the list, but push back on any of these if you think they're wrong:

- It has to respect the reward/feeling gap. In RL the reward is a scalar defined by the training setup, and in animals the dopamine literature already pulled "wanting" and "liking" apart, so a theory that just reads valence straight off the reward function is doing a pun, not an argument. The theory must say what has to be true of a system *in addition to* being reward-shaped.
- It has to handle wireheading without embarrassment. An agent that grabs its own reward channel and pins it at maximum -- is that bliss, or has the whole signal gone semantically dead at that point? Whichever answer the theory gives has to be a consequence of the theory, not an ad hoc patch.
- It has to deal with the training/deployment asymmetry: a frozen policy at inference never sees reward at all. If suffering requires the reward signal, does the agent stop mattering morally the moment training ends? That seems crazy, but the theory should make the answer fall out rather than leaving it as a loose end.
- It has to get the trivial cases right in both directions -- not count a tabular Q-learner or a thermostat as miserable, not count humans out -- and if it can't avoid one of those, it should at least be honest about which side it fails on.
- It should say something about the asymmetry between suffering and pleasure (why "must escape this now" feels different from "more of this please") and whether its account of valence even has the resources for that distinction.
- It should be as architecture-neutral as possible: whatever criterion it gives shouldn't secretly assume a particular setup (value-based vs policy gradient vs model-based), or if it does, it should own that.

The thing I most want flagged: which pairs of these desiderata are in tension with each other. My suspicion is that demanding an empirically checkable criterion from the outside pulls against respecting the reward/feeling gap, because any functional marker you add can in principle be reimplemented without anything being felt -- but maybe that's just the zombie problem in a trench coat and it's fine to live with it for policy purposes. Say so either way.

Then briefly -- a short paragraph each, don't make a big table of it -- how the obvious contenders do against the list: naive reward-as-pleasure, a global-workspace-flavored account, a higher-order account, and a functionalist account that defines valence as imperative motivational role. Fix the purpose of the theory as "good enough to make defensible triage decisions about which systems warrant moral caution," not "metaphysically certain," since I think the desiderata actually change depending on which of those you pick.
