I work as a systems engineer, and the vocabulary I think in natively is that of distributed computing: processes, message passing, shared state, consensus protocols, two-phase commit, fault models, network partitions, and the split between safety and liveness properties. I have been trying to make sense of the analytic literature on collective intentionality and joint action -- roughly, the dispute over what makes it the case that two people are doing something together, as opposed to two people happening to do similar things in parallel, and over whether an adequate account needs anything more than interlocking individual intentions and beliefs (the reductive family associated with Bratman) or instead requires an irreducibly plural element (the non-reductive family associated with Searle's we-intentions and Gilbert's joint commitments and plural subjects).

Restate this dispute in the vocabulary of distributed systems, as faithfully as you can. Concretely: give the best rendering of what it is for two nodes to jointly intend an outcome -- is a shared goal reachable from individually held intentions plus common knowledge of each other's intentions (as Bratman-style accounts suggest), and does common knowledge itself reduce to unbounded chains of message acknowledgment, which is provably unattainable under an unreliable channel? Render Gilbert's claim that joint commitment is not decomposable into personal commitments, and her further claim that participants acquire standing to rebuke each other for defection -- the natural candidate translation is a protocol with penalties or an atomic commit with obligations on abort, but say whether that captures it or merely simulates it. Render Searle's claim that we-intention is a primitive mode of mind rather than a data structure assembled from I-intentions.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most. Which elements of the debate map cleanly onto things like consensus, shared state, and coordinated atomic action; which translate only with distortion; and which have no expression at all in this vocabulary. My suspicion is that the normative layer -- obligations, rebuke, betrayal -- is where the translation fails, because protocols specify behavior but do not generate entitlements, but I want that tested rather than assumed. For each piece of residue, say whether it exposes a genuine limitation of the systems vocabulary (something the framework cannot say) or whether it suggests the philosophical dispute is tracking a difference that no mechanism could realize (something that looked substantive only in the original vocabulary).

Finally, one stress test I have not seen treated anywhere: a network partition splits a group of agents mid-coordination, and each side continues acting under the assumption that the joint project persists. In distributed systems terms, is the joint intention during the partition like a split-brain cluster -- two nodes each believing they hold the lock -- and if so, what does the correct failure semantics for a shared intention look like? Should joint intentions have the equivalent of a quorum requirement, a timeout, or a fencing mechanism, and would the major positions in the literature disagree about which failure model is the right one? If the philosophical accounts are silent or indeterminate on this, that itself seems to me like a finding worth reporting.
