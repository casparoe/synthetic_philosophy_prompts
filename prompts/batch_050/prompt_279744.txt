I am drafting an essay for a philosophy of mathematics reading group, and I would like detailed comments on the argument before I revise it. The essay's thesis is that the dialectical pattern Imre Lakatos reconstructs in Proofs and Refutations — the back-and-forth between a conjecture (his central case being the Euler formula relating vertices, edges, and faces of polyhedra), putative proofs, counterexamples, and the successive refinement of the concepts involved — recurs, almost structurally unchanged, in contemporary machine learning practice. My claim is not the trivial one that machine learning is empirical; it is that it is quasi-empirical in Lakatos's specific sense, with the same repertoire of defensive manoeuvres he catalogued.

Here is the sketch of the essay as it stands. I begin by summarising Lakatos's taxonomy: monster-barring (redefining the key concept so that the counterexample no longer counts as an instance of it), exception-barring (restricting the conjecture's scope to exclude the troublesome cases), and lemma incorporation (turning the counterexample into a condition explicitly built into an improved proof, thereby generating new, proof-generated concepts). I then argue that each manoeuvre has a close analogue in how practitioners respond when a trained model fails. Monster-barring corresponds to dismissing failures as adversarial or out-of-distribution inputs — the failure cases are declared not to be genuine instances of the task the model was meant to solve. Exception-barring corresponds to restricting performance claims to in-distribution behaviour. Lemma incorporation corresponds to folding failure modes back into the training pipeline — data augmentation, hard-example mining, and the proliferation of redefined notions such as robustness, which I treat as a proof-generated concept in something like Lakatos's sense. The essay's more contentious second half argues that this analogy cuts against a common way of talking about learned models: if the target concept is continually being revised in response to counterexamples, then it is misleading to say the model discovers a pre-existing concept; rather, concept and criterion of success co-evolve, just as polyhedron and the Euler conjecture did in Lakatos's narrative. I close by suggesting that the quasi-empirical character Lakatos attributed to mathematics is therefore not a peculiarity of mathematics but a feature of any practice in which formal or formalised claims are tested against recalcitrant cases.

What I would like from you: first, comments on accuracy — do I misrepresent any of Lakatos's categories, and is my gloss on proof-generated concepts faithful to how he actually uses the notion? Second, comments on whether the analogy genuinely illuminates machine learning or merely decorates it: where do you think the disanalogies bite hardest (for instance, the absence of anything like a deductive proof structure in model training, or the fact that in machine learning the concept revisions are made by the practitioners rather than the artefact)? Third, please identify the single weakest step in the argument as sketched and suggest how it might be repaired or, if it cannot be, which conclusion I should retreat to. Finally, raise one serious objection I have not anticipated. I would rather have blunt criticism than encouragement; the reading group is unforgiving.
