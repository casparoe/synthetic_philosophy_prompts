I am a novelist, and I have spent the last year circling a book I cannot seem to start: a counterfactual story in which Adolf Hitler is killed in the trenches in 1916, so that I can find out whether the Germany of my novel still arrives at something like the Third Reich. In trying to settle this for plotting purposes I fell into the old quarrel between the great-man account and the structural account of history, and I have been struck by how thoroughly stalled it is. The structuralists point to Versailles, reparations, the collapse of the mark, the fragility of the Weimar constitution, and a hundred other conditions, and conclude that some authoritarian, revanchist, antisemitic regime was more or less waiting to happen. The great-man side answers that no one but Hitler combines the oratory, the will, and the particular murderous obsessions, and that without him you get a reactionary dictatorship of a more ordinary sort -- ugly, but not Auschwitz. Both sides marshal the same facts; neither has budged in decades.

While researching, I came across this passage in Tolstoy's second epilogue to War and Peace: "Instead of men endowed with divine authority and directly guided by the will of God, modern history has given us either heroes endowed with extraordinary, superhuman capacities, or simply men of very various kinds, from monarchs to journalists, who lead the masses." Tolstoy's complaint, as I read him, is that modern historiography threw out Providence but kept the shape of the old explanation, merely substituting the hero for the deity -- and that this is why the debate over individuals in history never resolves: the hero is doing the same unexplained explanatory work the gods used to do.

My question is not which side is right; it is what kind of disagreement this is. Is it substantive -- a genuinely empirical dispute about the causal weight of one man that happens to be untestable because history runs once? Is it verbal -- the two sides using "cause" in different senses, one meaning the spark, the other the tinder? Are they answering different questions under one heading -- whether some catastrophe was likely versus whether this catastrophe, in this form, with these victims, was likely? And is Tolstoy's diagnosis in the passage above correct -- does the debate stall because the great man is a placeholder for an explanation nobody has, or is the stall better explained some other way? Please ground the diagnosis in what the two positions actually say, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction to be drawn, a class of comparable cases to examine, or a reason to expect no resolution at all. Keep your answer to no more than 350 words.
