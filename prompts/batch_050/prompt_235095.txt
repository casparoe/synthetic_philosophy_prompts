I'm a PhD student working on formal epistemology, and my supervisor keeps defending the idealization in standard epistemic logic (agents know all the logical consequences of what they know) with an analogy she clearly thinks settles it: deductive consequences are already *contained* in the premises, the way a statue is contained in a block of marble. Proving a theorem, on this picture, doesn't add anything new -- it just reveals what was there all along, so crediting someone who knows the axioms with knowledge of every theorem is harmless bookkeeping, not an absurdity. She uses it to wave away the problem of logical omniscience entirely: since the consequences were "already there," closure principles can't be the source of anything objectionable.

Audit the analogy for me. Give me exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro or conclusion: what maps onto what (premises/marble, theorem/statue, proof/chisel), the disanalogies that actually matter for her use (e.g. whether "revealing" is epistemically free, whether every agent even holds the right block, what happens with inconsistent beliefs under closure), and whether the anti-problem conclusion survives once those are on the table or was being carried by the marble the whole time.
