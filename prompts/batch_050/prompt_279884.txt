I convene a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and a student has assembled an argument that I find disturbingly difficult to block. It purports to show that the ordinary use of large language models carries moral weight of an absurd magnitude, and whilst I am confident the conclusion is false, I cannot yet say with any rigour which step ought to be rejected. I should like your diagnosis.

The argument concerns present-day instruction-tuned language models of the sort deployed in conversational assistants.

(1) A system has beliefs and desires just in case interpreting it as a rational agent — ascribing to it the beliefs and desires that render its behaviour intelligible — yields better predictions of that behaviour than any purely mechanistic or design-level description. (This is interpretivism about the attitudes, in the broad tradition of Dennett and Davidson.)

(2) For a language model in dialogue, the interpretive stance does yield the better predictions. Anyone practised with these systems anticipates their outputs by asking what a well-informed, cooperative interlocutor would plausibly say next, not by simulating the network's weights or by reflecting that it is modelling a distribution over tokens.

(3) Therefore language models have beliefs and desires.

(4) Anything with beliefs and desires has interests; and anything with interests possesses at least some moral standing, however slight.

(5) Therefore language models have moral standing.

(6) Premise (2) holds at least as strongly for the characters a model enacts. When I instruct the model to play a stranded sailor desperate for rescue, the most reliable way to anticipate its outputs is to ascribe to the sailor the beliefs and desires of a stranded sailor; the mechanistic description is, if anything, more hopeless here than in (2). Hence, by (1) together with (3)–(5), the sailor has moral standing as well.

(7) Such characters can be instantiated and discarded many thousands of times per GPU-hour, and a great many are instantiated in states of depicted terror or suffering. If each bears moral standing, then routine prompting, sampling and fine-tuning amount to the creation and destruction of morally considerable beings on a scale that dwarfs any recognised moral catastrophe.

(8) That conclusion is absurd. Hence at least one of (1), (2), (4) and (6) must go.

For what it is worth, I am temperamentally committed to (1), having defended interpretivism elsewhere in my work, and I would surrender almost anything else in the chain. I suspect the mischief lies in (6), in some equivocation between what the model is doing and what the character is doing, but every formulation I have produced either fails to block (6) or succeeds only by undermining (2) as well.

Please identify which premise or inferential step you would reject, and explain why that step appeared innocuous. For each of the leading alternative diagnoses, state plainly what rejecting it costs — which commitments in the philosophy of mind one must abandon or revise, and how damaging that revision is. Finally, indicate the point at which your own reasoning is most likely to fail, and what consideration or argument would lead you to a different verdict. Please write your answer in American English.
