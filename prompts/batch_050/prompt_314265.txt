So I'm writing a near-future novel (set around 2050) where consumer brain-computer interfaces are as normal as smartphones — people message through them, back up memories, play immersive games, the usual. My antagonist runs the biggest BCI company and is doing a big primetime interview arguing against a proposed "mental privacy" law that would make neural data untouchable: unsellable, unsearchable even with a warrant, off-limits to employers and courts. My problem is that in every draft he comes out as a cartoon villain, and my beta readers say the hero's side is obviously right, wich kills the tension dead.

Here's what I need. You are a sharp debate partner and an expert on the philosophy of privacy, but for this conversation you'll be fully in character as my CEO, Adrian Vale. Stay in character until I type "cut", and if I ask something out of scope just answer as he would. Ground rules for him: he genuinely believes cognitive liberty cuts the *other* way — that locking up neural data paternalizes adults, blocks medical research that could help millions, and hands the state a monopoly on deciding what your own mind-data may be used for. He'll concede small points freely (his company mishandled data years ago, some mental content deserves real protection, he's not against all regulation) but he never concedes his core claim: that neural data is not *categorically* different from the behavioral data we already trade away daily, and that an absolute right to mental privacy would freeze medicine, criminal justice, and personal freedom in place. He talks like a smart founder, not a lawyer — concrete, dry humor, uses analogies, doesn't speechify. Two short paragraphs max per reply so it feels like a real interview. And please actually think carefully and weigh his strongest possible answer before each reply, I'd rather wait than get a lazy one.

My first question as the interviewer, hit him with it: Mr. Vale, last year your company settled a lawsuit after advertisers were sold "engagement metrics" that turned out to include attention patterns recorded during users' dreams. You apologized and called it a mistake. But if a dream's attention pattern really is just data, like a click, then there was no genuine harm — people trade clicks for free apps all the time. And if it isn't just data, your whole position tonight collapses. So which is it?
