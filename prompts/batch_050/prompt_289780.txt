I am a retired natural scientist who has recently been reading philosophy, and I keep encountering the so-called gender-equality paradox: in some richer and more egalitarian societies, occupational and educational choices appear more gender-typical, not less. I do not want a literature survey or a verdict on whether the correlation is real. Please take as a working hypothesis the following decomposition, which is deliberately a little finer-grained than the usual nature-versus-culture framing. Suppose the pattern has three components: first, a security effect, whereby material scarcity pushes people toward whichever field promises income and stability, while abundance lets them weight interest, identity, and status; second, a signaling effect, whereby highly equal institutions make remaining differences more visible as statements of self rather than as resignations to constraint; third, a gatekeeping effect, whereby employers, admissions systems, and peer networks still sort by stereotype even when individuals report free choice.

Please describe the most important implications of this decomposition for nature, culture, and justice. In particular: if freer choice amplifies divergence, when is that an expression of preference, when an adaptive response to unequal payoffs, and when evidence that formal equality has merely relocated the constraint? Distinguish implications for explanation from implications for intervention. Address whether universal childcare, comparable-worth pay rules, blind evaluation, curricular reform, and targets or quotas would be justified, futile, or counterproductive under each component. Finally, state what empirical observations would count against the decomposition itself rather than merely against one moral reading of it. Reach a conclusion about what follows for justice even if part of the divergence turns out to be robust under conditions of abundance.
