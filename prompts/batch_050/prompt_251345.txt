I administer a small fellowship that awards grants from a fixed annual fund. This cycle our committee has three finalists whose applications, after long deliberation, we genuinely cannot rank. I suggested we settle it by lottery and be done. A colleague objected that "deciding by lottery" is not one thing but many. We could draw one name and give that person the entire fund. We could instead divide the fund among all three by drawing the shares at random. We could give each finalist a guaranteed equal share and randomize only a bonus. Each scheme treats the finalists symmetrically, so each is arguably "fair," yet they distribute risk and money very differently, and each finalist might reasonably prefer a different one. My colleague says there is no neutral default here, because calling a procedure random just means calling some quantity uniform, and one can always ask: uniform over what?

Is he right that symmetry alone cannot select a procedure? If he is, must the choice among lotteries be settled by something further -- the applicants' attitudes toward risk, the value of partial versus full funding, our own preferences? And if it must, in what sense was the lottery ever a way of avoiding judgment rather than a way of disguising it?
