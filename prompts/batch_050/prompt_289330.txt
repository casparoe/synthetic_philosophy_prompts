I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I keep running into a thing on math stories that I don't have a good word for, and I want to get it right in a piece I'm drafting.

So: there are theorems out there that the mathematical community treats as settled, proven, done -- but no human being has ever read the proof, and arguably no human being could. The four colour theorem is the classic case, the actual checking got farmed out to a computer grinding through a huge pile of configurations. The Kepler conjecture is an even weirder one for my purposes, because it went through two stages: first there was a proof with a giant computer component where the referees basically said they were almost but not totally sure they'd verified everything, and then years later the guy went back and got the whole thing formally machine-checked, and everyone relaxed. Two different flavors of "a person didn't really do this" and people reacted differently to them.

And then there's a third case that I think might be the same phenomenon wearing a disguise: the classification of finite simple groups. No computers involved, but it's spread across hundreds of papers by dozens of authors over decades, tens of thousands of pages, and I've seen people question whether any single mathematician ever had the whole thing in their head at once. So is that the same kind of thing, or not?

Here's what I want to know. Is there an established name for this -- knowledge a community possesses that no individual member does or even could possess by checking the reasoning themselves? And if there is, I don't just want the label, I want the carving. Specifically I want to know how this thing differs from three neighbours it seems easy to confuse with:

1. Ordinary trust in testimony. When I write about a physics result I'm trusting the physicists, when a mathematician cites a lemma from someone else's paper she's trusting them. Everyone already accepts that science runs on trust. So why did the four colour theorem cause a fuss if it's the same thing?

2. Trusting instruments. Nobody accuses an astronomer of not really knowing there's an exoplanet because she "only" saw it through a telescope and a pipeline of code she didn't write. Is a computer proof different from that in kind, or is the fuss just status anxiety?

3. Formal verification of the Kepler-conjecture second act type. That seems like it should be the *most* trustworthy case, since the checking is itself checkable, but it feels like it should land in the same bucket as the others by my definition, since still no human surveys it. Does it?

If the classification-of-simple-groups case belongs in the bucket and the computer cases don't (or vice versa), tell me why, because my current draft lumps them. And if there's genuinely no name for this, coin one and define it with a couple of boundary cases -- something that ISN'T the phenomenon but looks like it -- so I don't overextend it in the article. Honestly the label matters less to me than knowing where the edges are.
