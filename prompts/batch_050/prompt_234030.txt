So I'm a novelist -- mostly lit-fic, three books out, currently stuck on the fourth -- and I've been rereading Crime and Punishment for the third time because my draft is basically stealing its structure and I want to understand what I'm stealing.

Here's my actual question though. Everyone reads the book as Christian providence: Raskolnikov sins, guilt grinds him down, confession and Sonia, epilogue in Siberia, redemption. Fine. But I've been reading a bit about karma as a theory of moral desert -- not the pop version where karma is the universe keeping score, but the more careful idea where it's a law-like mechanism: actions have consequences fitted to the agent, no judge required, desert is baked into causation itself rather than handed down. And it struck me that the novel is weirdly closer to THAT than to a judge-model. Nobody punishes Raskolnikov. The law barely matters; Porfiry is basically waiting for the mechanism to finish. The punishment is internal, automatic, proportionate-ish, and it starts before anyone suspects him. Dostoevsky even kills off the actual investigation plot as a threat pretty early.

So: read the novel as a karma story rather than a providence story, and take a stand on whether the reading works. Where does it genuinely fit -- I'm thinking the fever, the compulsive returns to the scene, the way his theory collapses through his body rather than through argument -- and where does it break? Two places I suspect it breaks: one, karma is supposed to be impersonal and the book has Sonya, who is very much a person doing the work a mechanism can't; two, the epilogue. Karmic accounts have this bookkeeping problem -- if desert is carried across a transformed self (rebirth, or in his case a man who no longer believes the murder was wrong), in what sense is the same person paying? Siberia looks like exactly that problem staged in eight pages.

Don't give me a balanced survey of interpretations. Pick the strongest version of the karma reading, defend it as the right way to teach/read the book, concede the one place it genuinely fails and say so plainly, and tell me what the novel adds to the desert debate that philosophy papers on karma don't have. If at the end you think the karma frame is actually the WRONG frame and providence wins, say that instead -- but commit to one or the other. I'm going to build a novel on whichever answer is better, so hedging costs me a book.
