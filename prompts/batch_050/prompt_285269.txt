A guy in my medieval philosophy reading group (he's a grad student, so everyone treats him as the authority) summarized Abelard's ethics of intention like this, and I want to know how much to trust it before I repeat it in a paper:

"Abelard's whole moral theory boils down to one idea: the sin is in the intention, not the act. The outward deed is morally neutral -- it neither adds to the sin nor subtracts from it. So if two people perform the exact same action, say they both hang a condemned man, one can be sinning and the other not, purely because of their different intentions. Likewise, if you fully intend to kill someone and get interrupted before you can, your sin is exactly as great as if you'd gone through with it. And it goes even further: for Abelard, sin is already present at the level of desire. A flash of lust, or taking pleasure in the thought of revenge, is itself a sin, even if you fight the urge and would never act on it. None of this was especially controversial at the time -- he was basically spelling out what medieval Christians already believed about morality. One consequence is that his view makes ordinary criminal law hard to justify, since courts can only ever punish actions, not intentions."

I know basically nothing about Abelard beyond this, but two things make me suspicious. First, the desire bit seems off -- I vaguely remember something about Abelard using an example of someone who experiences temptation or pleasure without endorsing it, and treating them as blameless, maybe even praiseworthy for resisting. Second, the claim that this was uncontroversial common sense sits oddly with what little I've heard about Abelard constantly getting in trouble with the church.

Could you go through the summary and separate out (1) what an Abelard scholar would accept as accurate, (2) what's a defensible simplification, and (3) what's an actual misrepresentation of his position? I'm especially interested in whether he really located sin in "intention" in the everyday sense of that word, or in something more specific, and whether that distinction changes anything in the summary. And does the final consequence about criminal law actually follow from the accurate parts, or would Abelard himself have rejected it?
