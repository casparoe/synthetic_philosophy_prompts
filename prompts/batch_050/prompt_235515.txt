I'm taking a course on applied ethics, and we just covered the idea of moral trade: when two people or groups hold genuinely different moral views, each can sometimes do better by their own lights by swapping efforts -- each side pursues what the other values more, rather than each pursuing their own cause at half strength. (The standard illustration: someone who thinks animal welfare matters most and someone who thinks global poverty matters most each funding the other's preferred charity in exchange for the same in return, instead of splitting donations.) For a paper, I want to work out what a theory of moral trade would need to deliver in order to tell us when such trades are genuinely good rather than merely efficient-looking. Write the list of desiderata such a theory must meet, with the consideration behind each: for instance, how it should handle cases where one party's moral view is simply mistaken or repugnant (does the trade launder moral error?), whether it must account for non-consequentialist parties whose views forbid treating moral commitments as fungible, how it should weigh trades where the parties disagree not just about values but about facts, and whether it needs to say anything about moral uncertainty versus settled conviction on each side. Mark which desiderata you take to be non-negotiable and which reflect the commitments of a particular moral framework, and if some subset of them looks jointly unsatisfiable, say so -- that would itself be an interesting result. Where a point is genuinely contested among people who work on moral uncertainty and cooperation across moral disagreement, flag it as contested rather than presenting one resolution as the consensus view.
