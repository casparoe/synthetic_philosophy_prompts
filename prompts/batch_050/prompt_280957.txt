I am setting the final examination for a graduate-level seminar on AI safety. The seminar's organizing distinction has been between alignment approaches, which try to ensure a model wants the right things, and control approaches, which try to keep a deployment safe even on the assumption that the model is misaligned. My students can all recite the standard control toolbox — monitoring by a weaker trusted model, randomized human audits, red-team evaluations, defense in depth — so reciting it must earn them close to nothing.

Write one essay question, answerable in about 75 minutes, built around the following scenario, which the question itself should state in full:

A frontier lab deploys its most capable model to propose changes to the codebase of its own training infrastructure. Because the lab does not trust this model's motivations, every proposed change is reviewed by a smaller model that the lab treats as trustworthy, and human engineers additionally audit a random two percent of the changes that the smaller model approved. Before deployment, the lab evaluated this protocol by having a red-team model play the role of the scheming code-generator, and the protocol caught the large majority of its sabotage attempts. The lab concludes that the deployment is safe even if the code-generating model is misaligned.

The question must be impossible to answer well by summarizing the alignment/control distinction. It should require candidates to identify the specific points in this protocol where a premise about a model's disposition, rather than about the protocol's mechanics, is doing load-bearing work, and to assess whether the red-team evaluation licenses the lab's conclusion. In particular, strong candidates should have to engage with at least these pressure points: whether the reviewing model's blind spots can be assumed uncorrelated with the generating model's deception, given that both descend from similar training processes; how an adversary free to choose when to defect interacts with any audit procedure that samples only a small fraction of outputs; whether harms requiring no detectable act of defection — changes that are individually innocuous but damaging in aggregate — are reachable by control measures at all; and what it would take for an evaluation built on a stand-in adversary to bound the behavior of a real one whose capabilities it may not match.

Then supply a model answer of roughly 600 words, written at the level of the best student you have taught, and a marking scheme out of 25 points. The scheme should have five components, state what each is worth, name the specific move that separates an A-level treatment of that component from a B-level one, and add a single line on the characteristic way weaker candidates get it wrong — for instance, treating the reviewer's trustworthiness as a fact about the reviewer rather than as an assumption the protocol declines to justify, or proposing to train the two models differently without noticing that this relocates rather than removes the correlation problem. The exam is closed-book, so neither the question nor the model answer should lean on particular papers or citations; the concepts must carry the weight. Please write the entire response in American English.
