So I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, and this came up at work of all places. Our team lead told a client a feature was "basically done" when it wasn't, and in the retro someone said it wasn't technically a lie because we did have a demo branch that mostly worked. That kicked off a whole argument about lying vs. misleading and whether one is morally worse than the other, and I realized I hold a bunch of positions on this that I picked up from different places and never checked against each other. Since I spend my days finding inconsistencies in other people's specs, I figured I'd run the same check on myself before I keep arguing in Slack.

Here's where I've landed: (1) Lying is intrinsically worse than merely misleading someone, even when the effect on the listener is identical -- saying something you believe is false is a different kind of wrong than saying something technically true that you know will create a false belief. (2) What makes deception wrong, when it's wrong, is the harm it does to the deceived person -- bad decisions made on bad information, wasted effort, that kind of thing. I'm pretty outcome-focused generally; I don't think there's a separate offense against truth itself beyond the consequences. (3) In negotiations it's totally fine to mislead by strategic omission, like not volunteering that you'd accept a lower salary, but outright lying about having another offer is over the line. (4) The reason honesty matters socially is trust -- deception corrodes the reliability of communication, and that's the real cost even when no individual is harmed in a particular case. (5) I'm fine with white lies to spare feelings ("your talk was great!" when it was mediocre) but I'd judge someone harshly for giving a technically-true-but-misleading answer in the same situation ("people seemed really engaged!") -- the second one feels slimier to me, more calculated.
