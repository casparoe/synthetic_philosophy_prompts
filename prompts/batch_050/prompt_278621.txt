Last night a friend and I spent several hours arguing about the quantum suicide thought experiment, and I have been unable to locate where his reasoning fails, even though I refuse to accept where it ends. Assume the many-worlds interpretation throughout, and assume a device that couples a quantum measurement to a mechanism that either kills the subject instantly and painlessly or leaves them entirely unharmed. His argument ran as follows:

(1) On many-worlds, every outcome with nonzero amplitude is realized in some branch.
(2) However many times the device fires, there is always some branch, however small its measure, in which the subject survives.
(3) A branch in which the subject is dead contains no experiences of that subject; all of the subject's experiences, if any, occur in branches where the subject is alive.
(4) Therefore the subject's stream of experience continues with certainty: no matter how often the device fires, the subject will only ever find themselves among the survivors, since there is no "finding themselves" anywhere else.
(5) Rational choice should be guided by what one will experience, and branches containing no experiences of mine are irrelevant to what I will experience.
(6) So the subject should accept the following wager, and repeat it indefinitely: a large fortune in every branch where the device spares them, instant death in the others. From the inside, this is gain with no possible experienced loss.
(7) The reasoning is perfectly general. Whatever the mortal danger -- disease, accident, old age -- there is always some branch in which some continuant of the subject persists, and so everyone's experience simply never ends. We are each, from the first-person point of view, immortal.

I am happy to grant (1) through (3), and I suspect the trouble begins at (4), but every time I try to say why, I find myself either denying that the survivor in the low-measure branch is really me -- which seems to threaten ordinary talk of surviving anything chancy at all -- or conceding (4) and getting dragged to (6). And yet (6) and (7) seem to me plainly false: if the wager were sound, someone who believed this argument should rationally stake everything on it, and if (7) were true, the absence of visibly ancient people among us would be a puzzling coincidence rather than an expected observation.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look compelling in the first place? I would like the alternatives priced honestly. In particular, if I reject (5), what account of rational choice under branching am I thereby committed to, and what does it say I should care about besides what I will observe? If instead I reject the move from (3) to (4), what am I saying about personal identity across branches, and does that commitment force me to revise anything I want to keep about survival in ordinary, non-quantum cases? If I had to choose, I would rather abandon (5) than (2), but I want to know what each retreat actually costs before picking one.
