Write a dialogue between two political philosophers, Dana and Ravi, arguing about a made-up proposal I'll call juried epistocracy. The setup: in this system, any citizen can choose to sit an exam on basic civic and policy-relevant facts (roughly how the budget is structured, what the central bank actually does, which powers belong to which branch of goverment, etc.). Pass it, and your vote counts double. The twist that distinguishes it from textbook epistocracy: the exam questions aren't written by experts, civil servants, or academics, but by a panel of randomly selected ordinary citizens, convened like a jury, who also decide what counts as a pass.

Dana thinks this threads the needle: it respects the intuition that better-informed voters should carry more weight, while the jury mechanism blocks the usual objection that epistocracy just lets a credentialed elite define what knowledge matters. Ravi thinks it fails on its own terms, though he shouldn't just reach for the most obvious slogans. Let them actually engage each others points — e.g. Dana pressing Ravi on whether his view implies that voter ignorance literally doesn't matter at all, Ravi pressing Dana on who in practice ends up sitting for and passing such an exam and what that does to the perceived legitimacy of election results, and both of them getting into whether the jury design really neutralizes the who-decides-what-counts-as-knowledge problem or just relocates it one level down.

Both should be sharp and charitable — no strawmen, no talking past each other. By the end they don't need to agree, but they should have identified the actual crux of their disagreement (one specific premise they can't both hold), and stated it clearly. Around 800-1200 words.
