I'm a site reliability engineer at a mid-sized company, and I sit on the incident review board that writes the causal analysis section of our postmortem for every severity-1 outage (roughly one a month, ninety minutes of meeting time, with the final document read by engineering leadership and occasionally by lawyers). I've recently learned that philosophers of causation broadly divide into two camps: counterfactual theories, in the tradition associated with David Lewis, which analyse causation in terms of what would have happened had the candidate cause been absent; and interventionist theories, associated with James Woodward and with Judea Pearl's work on the do-operator, which analyse it in terms of what would change under a hypothetical manipulation. Each maps onto a habit my colleagues already have: the 'but for this bug, no outage' instinct versus the 'what would we actually change' instinct. What I want is neither a defence of one camp nor a history of the debate, but the two theories translated into a procedure I can actually run.

So: turn the counterfactual and interventionist accounts of causation into a single decision procedure for causal attribution in incident review -- a flowchart in words, or a numbered set of tests to run in order, that takes the raw timeline of an incident and returns a defensible set of causes, and that a reviewer with no philosophy background could apply inside one meeting. Where the two theories genuinely diverge in verdict rather than merely in vocabulary, the procedure should say which test to run first and why, rather than pretending they always agree.

The procedure must be stress-tested against the cases that break naive attribution, and I'd like each one handled explicitly:

- Overdetermination: the deploy was buggy *and* the configuration change was wrong, and either alone would have taken the site down. But-for reasoning acquits both; say what the procedure should conclude and which theory's machinery rescues it.
- Pre-emption: the primary database failed, triggering the failover, which then failed for an unrelated reason; the primary failure never actually got its chance to cause the outage.
- Causation by omission: the outage lasted four hours because nobody had configured the alerting, and because an on-call engineer ignored a warning email. Absences and non-events are central to real postmortems and notoriously awkward for both theories -- the procedure needs an explicit policy on when a missing thing counts as a cause.
- Contributing condition versus triggering cause: the system had run happily with a fragile retry policy for two years until one Tuesday it didn't. The procedure should force a principled distinction here rather than leaving it to whoever argues loudest.

Wherever the procedure cannot eliminate judgement -- for instance, in fixing the 'normal conditions' against which an intervention is defined -- mark that step plainly as a judgement call rather than dressing it up as a test, and note what discipline keeps the judgement honest (documented assumptions, an adversarial reviewer, and so on).

Then apply the finished procedure to three worked incidents, briefly: the overdetermined deploy above; a case where a monitoring gap, not any code change, is what the procedure identifies as the cause -- an answer I admit I'd find uncomfortable, so argue for it properly; and one case of your choosing where the procedure's verdict diverges from what the loudest engineer in the room would say. Close by stating what the procedure cannot do: I assume, for example, that settling causation settles neither blame nor remediation priority, and I'd like that boundary drawn sharply rather than assumed.
