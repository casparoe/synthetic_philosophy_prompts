My closest friend is getting married next month, and I am giving the best-man speech at the reception. His family is Dutch and treats directness as a form of respect: they say what they think at the table, consider tact a species of evasion, and were genuinely puzzled when an earlier family dinner went badly. Her family is Japanese and treats indirectness as a form of respect: a refusal is delivered as a hesitation, criticism arrives wrapped in praise, and the well-chosen polite fiction is how you show you care about someone. Both families will be in the room, both still a little raw from that dinner, and each half will hear the toast through its own theory of what honesty owes to kindness.

Please write a five-minute toast that takes an actual position in this debate rather than splitting the difference into mush. It should argue, in terms a wedding can bear, whether white lies and strategic tact are a betrayal of the Dutch half's ideal or the very thing that makes the Japanese half's ideal coherent — and, more importantly, the speech itself has to enact whatever answer it gives: it cannot say that tact is kindness while being blunt at the bride's family's expense, nor praise frankness while hiding behind euphemism. Keep it warm and funny enough for a reception, but let the philosophy do real work in deciding which stories about the couple I can tell and how to tell them.

After the toast, add a short note flagging where it leans on a contested reading of the honesty-versus-politeness debate, and identify the single consideration that, if you came to accept it, would most change the toast's central move — for instance, what would have to be true about lying or about face-saving for you to rewrite it from the other side's theory of respect.
