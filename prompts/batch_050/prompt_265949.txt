Ok so I've been reading about Wang Yangming and his whole thing about the unity of knowledge and action (知行合一) — the idea that if you genuinely *know* something is right, you'll already be acting on it, so if you "know" you should be good to your parents but you never actually are, then for Wang Yangming you don't really know it yet, you just know the words.

Now here's my question. Say he was alive today and had to reckon with a very modern creature: the self-help addict. You know the type — someone who's read a dozen books on focus and discipline, watches productivity videos at 1.5x speed, can lecture you for an hour about habit formation and dopamine, and meanwhile hasn't finished a single project in three years. Or the person who watches endless cooking videos but eats frozen pizza every night.

What would Wang Yangming say about these people? I can see a few options and I'm not sure which is right:

1. The obvious one: he says they don't *actually* know anything about discipline or cooking at all, they just have a pile of memorized sentences, and real knowledge would show up as behavior automatically.
2. A softer reading: he'd admit they have *some* kind of knowledge (like, they'd probably score well on a quiz about cooking) but say it's a fake or incomplete kind, the verbal shadow of the real thing.
3. Or maybe he'd say something more interesting — that the problem is they've mistaken *learning about* discipline for the thing itself, and that the consuming of all this content is actively making things worse because it lets them feel like they're making progress while avoiding the one thing that would constitute real knowledge.

Which of these best fits his actual view, or is it some fourth option I'm missing? And follow up: does his position imply that stuff like ethics courses, compliance trainings, DEI workshops etc. are basically pointless — because either the knowledge is already there in the doing, or no amount of classroom talk will put it there? Or could he defend a role for them? Genuinely curious how far he'd push this.
