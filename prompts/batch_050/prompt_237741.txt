I am a novelist at work on a book whose narrator, a forensic sketch artist attached to a surveillance unit, spends one crucial chapter watching a suspect through a chain of relays, and I need the philosophy in that chapter to be load-bearing rather than decorative. The text I keep returning to is William Henry Fox Talbot's The Pencil of Nature (1844), the first photographically illustrated book. In its introductory remarks, Talbot insists that his plates were produced by the agency of light alone, with no assistance from the artist's hand, and that they are therefore the sun's own pictures rather than imitations made in engraving. His wager is that the absence of a human hand in the causal chain is what makes the resulting image a piece of the world rather than a report about it. Kendall Walton later gave this idea its modern form: we literally see things through photographs, but not through paintings, because a photograph's dependence on its scene bypasses anyone's beliefs about that scene, while a painting reaches us only through the painter's.

Construct a thought experiment that stress-tests this criterion, to the following specifications:

1. A matched pair of scenarios, using only present-day technology, in which the image reaching my narrator is pixel-for-pixel identical and equally reliable in both, but the causal chain from suspect to screen passes through a human being's beliefs in one case and not the other, so that the Talbot-Walton criterion delivers a verdict of seeing in one case and not seeing in the other. One paragraph per scenario.

2. A third scenario applying maximum pressure: a human intermediary so drilled, habituated, or procedurally constrained that it is genuinely unclear whether her beliefs mediate the image at all. Locate the point where the criterion, applied literally, either bites down or loses its grip.

3. Derive each verdict from the criterion itself rather than stipulating it. Identify the single feature doing the work and say what happens if it is removed. Give the strongest reply available to Walton, then the strongest reply from the rival view, associated with Gregory Currie, that a photograph is a trace of its subject rather than a window onto it.

4. Close with a note I can use in the chapter: does your construction imply that first-person narration could ever be transparent in Talbot's sense, or is the comparison of prose to photographs a category error?

Roughly 700 words.
