I am a novelist, and my forthcoming book is set in a near-future city-state that has constitutionally abolished political parties: candidates stand as individuals on their own stated positions, campaigning on behalf of any organized faction is a criminal offense, and the fiction takes seriously Simone Weil's 1943 essay On the Abolition of All Political Parties — her argument that parties are machines for generating collective passion, that they subordinate the individual conscience to a platform one must swallow whole, and that a party's ultimate aim tends to become its own unlimited growth. The novel is not a polemic; it dramatizes both the appeal and the costs of this arrangement. But I have agreed to appear at a panel event — half literary festival, half academic roundtable — where I will be expected to defend the abolition proposal itself, not merely my use of it.

The audience will be mostly political scientists who work on party systems and democratic representation, plus a handful of working politicians and party organizers, and some engaged readers of the book. None of them will be Weil scholars; nearly all of them will take competitive party democracy to be the only known mechanism for organizing mass politics, and the political scientists in particular will have E. E. Schattschneider's dictum that modern democracy is unthinkable save in terms of parties sitting ready in the back of their minds.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections I will actually receive from this specific audience — not the objections a room of philosophers or of Weil devotees would raise — ordered by how much damage each would do to my position if I handle it badly. Phrase each objection the way this audience would phrase it, in their vocabulary: aggregation of interests, coordination problems, accountability, the collective-action logic of candidates without party labels, what abolition would do to legislative stability, and so on. For each objection, tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold firm on, and give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, together with a note on where that reply is weakest. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks, and say why it is the most dangerous one.

Be committed throughout: for each objection, make a definite call on whether I should concede it, deflect it, or fight it, and give your reasons, rather than presenting me with options to weigh myself. And lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — your single judgment about how I should play the whole evening — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
