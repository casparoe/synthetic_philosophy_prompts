I'm in the final year of a PhD in legal philosophy, and in three weeks I'm presenting a dissertation chapter at our faculty's jurisprudence research seminar. The chapter defends a Fullerian position against what I'd call the Razian orthodoxy in our department. My thesis: the eight desiderata Lon Fuller sets out in The Morality of Law (generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, non-contradiction, not demanding the impossible, relative constancy through time, and congruence between official action and declared rule) are not merely standards of instrumental efficiency, as Joseph Raz holds when he compares the rule of law to the sharpness of a knife, which serves good and wicked purposes equally well. Instead, I argue, they are partly constitutive of law's claim to legitimate authority, so that sufficiently grave failures of congruence or publicity don't just make a legal system morally worse but erode its character as law. My argument runs through Fuller's picture of law as a cooperative enterprise between ruler and subject, resting on reciprocal expectations of fidelity, and I claim that Raz's instrumental reading proves too much: if legality were purely a matter of efficiency, we couldn't explain why even vicious regimes feel the need to simulate compliance with it rather than rule openly by arbitrary decree.

The audience matters here, so let me be precise. Around fifteen people: mostly legal positivists formed by Hart and Raz, two of them Raz's former doctoral students who know The Authority of Law chapter and verse; one sympathetic but demanding natural lawyer working in Finnis's tradition, who will think my concession structure gives away too much; and two political theorists who work on Jeremy Waldron and suspect the entire debate is misframed because it fixates on the properties of rules while ignoring courts, procedures, argument, and participation.
