I am drafting a chapter on mental causation for an introductory philosophy of mind textbook, and I need to decide how much weight to give Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument. As I understand it, the argument runs roughly as follows. Assume that mental properties supervene on physical properties: no mental difference without some physical difference. Assume the causal closure of the physical: whenever a physical event has a cause at a time, it has a sufficient physical cause at that time. And assume a causal exclusion principle: if an event already has a sufficient cause, any further distinct cause is ruled out unless the case is one of genuine causal overdetermination. Now take any case of apparent mental causation, say my belief that it is raining causing my hand to open the umbrella. The umbrella-opening has a sufficient physical cause. My belief is therefore either identical with that physical cause, an additional overdetermining cause alongside it, or excluded from the causal story altogether. Since it seems implausible that every human action is systematically overdetermined, the argument appears to force a choice between identifying mental properties with physical ones and accepting that mental properties do no causal work.

What I want is not a survey of the literature but an account of the argument's most important implications, organized so that I can use it to structure the chapter. In particular, please address the following.

1. Which premises do the argumentative work, and what does it cost to deny each one? For example, what would denying closure do to the textbook's claim that the sciences form a causally complete whole, and what would denying the exclusion principle commit one to saying about ordinary cases in which we happily cite more than one cause of the same event?

2. If the argument succeeds, what exactly is lost? Distinguish implications for the causal efficacy of mental states from implications for the legitimacy of mentalistic explanation. It seems possible that beliefs could fail to cause anything while belief-talk remains genuinely explanatory, and I want to know whether the argument even touches the latter.

3. What are the implications for the positions students typically arrive with, namely non-reductive physicalism, functionalism, and property dualism? Does the argument discriminate among them, or does it strike all of them equally?

4. Does the argument have implications outside the philosophy seminar? Consider how a psychologist should describe the relation between a client's reasons and neural mechanisms, or how to think about a case in which a patient's belief that a pill will work produces a measurable physical effect.

5. Finally, is there a reading of the argument on which it has no distinctive implications at all, because it generalizes to show that no higher-level property whatsoever, whether a hurricane's or a heart's, causes anything? If so, should that generalization be read as a refutation of the argument or as a vindication of it?

Please answer in American English, and be precise throughout about which implications follow from the argument itself and which require further assumptions.
