I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on truth and paradox, and I'm assembling a satirical piece for our department's graduate newsletter. I'd like you to write it.

Write a mock internal memorandum, roughly 550-650 words, from the "Office of Semantic Economy" of a fictional university's philosophy department, announcing that — following the department's adoption of deflationism about truth — the word "true" is to be phased out of all departmental documents as a cost-saving measure. The memo should be deadpan bureaucratic prose throughout: numbered directives, a phased implementation timeline, and a closing FAQ section for concerned faculty.

The humor must come from getting deflationism right, not from mocking a caricature. Specifically:

- The memo should justify the cut by appeal to the equivalence schema (the idea that saying it is true that p adds nothing beyond p itself) and to disquotational or prosentential ways of pressing the point — truth as a mere logical device, an idle cog, not a substantive property requiring departmental upkeep.
- The joke should then escalate through precisely the cases where deflationists themselves insist truth is indispensable: generalizations (certifying that everything on the exam syllabus is true), blind ascriptions (endorsing the external reviewer's report without knowing what it says), and long or infinite conjunctions. The memo should struggle visibly with each case, issuing increasingly contorted administrative workarounds, without the author ever conceding there is a problem.
- Include at least one directive that quietly reintroduces truth under a different name, unnoticed by the memo's author.
- You may gesture at the liar sentence in the FAQ as a "known edge case, under review," but don't let it dominate the piece.

The target is a specific overreading of deflationism — the inference from truth's being thin to truth's being disposable — not deflationism itself. A working deflationist should feel the satire is aimed at a misunderstanding of their view, executed by someone who understands the view. Stay deadpan: no winking, no explicit statement of the moral.

After the memo, add a short note in your own voice (100-150 words, clearly separated from the piece) addressing two things: first, where the satire exaggerates or leans on a contested reading of deflationism; second, which consideration, if developed, would most effectively defuse the implied critique — that is, where the piece's reasoning is most likely to fail against a sophisticated deflationist, and what would have to be true of the generalization and blind-ascription cases for the memo's policy to be defensible after all.
