I'm taking an upper-division course on the ethics of communication, and we spent last week on the manipulation versus persuasion distinction. I thought I had a stable set of intuitions, but after our seminar discussion I wrote down my actual verdicts on a list of cases and I can't find the line they're tracking. Here they are. A friend explains the pros and cons of moving to their city and lets me decide: clearly legitimate. A car salesman mentions that another buyer looked at the car this morning, which is true, knowing it'll pressure me into a quick decision: I want to say this is legitimate, though it leaves a bad taste. A boss who wants an employee to take on extra work frames it as a growth opportunity and never mentions the salary freeze: manipulation, I think, even though everything said is true. A charity fundraiser shows me a photo of one identifiable child rather than statistics about thousands, knowing the photo moves people more: my gut says legitimate, which bothers me because it seems structurally identical to the salesman's case. A partner who sulks and gives the silent treatment until the other person agrees to cancel a trip: obviously manipulation. A doctor who recommends a treatment by citing its 90 percent survival rate rather than its 10 percent mortality rate, because she knows patients respond better to the first framing: I'm genuinely torn, but I lean toward calling it legitimate given her good intent. An advertiser who repeatedly pairs a product with images of attractive, happy people so an association forms without any claim ever being made: manipulation. A debate opponent who asks a series of leading questions that get me to commit to premises whose combination refutes my own view before I've noticed where it's going: legitimate, oddly, since I endorsed every step myself. A parent who lets a tired toddler choose between the blue pajamas and the green ones, when bedtime was never actually up for negotiation: manipulation, but a benign kind I can't bring myself to condemn.

What I'd like is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I can see which of these verdicts it saves and which it forces me to give up. I'm especially interested in whether the distinction tracks something about the audience's rational capacities being bypassed, or about the persuader's honesty or intent, or about whether the influence would survive the audience's scrutiny if made explicit, since each candidate seems to capture some of my cases and wreck others -- the bypass story, for instance, seems to condemn the fundraiser's photo but spare the boss's omission, while my verdicts run the other way. Please also tell me what the best-supported principle says about two cases I haven't listed: a teacher who makes a subject feel thrilling so that students work harder than they otherwise would, and a friend who stops inviting me to restaurants she knows I'll suggest paying for because I always insist, effectively letting me keep more money without ever asking me about it. And if no tidy principle captures all my verdicts, I'd rather know which two or three I should treat as the likeliest mistakes and why, than be handed a gerrymandered theory that accommodates everything at the price of saying nothing.
