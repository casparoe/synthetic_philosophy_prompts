I am preparing the opening seminar for a graduate course on rational choice. Before we assess any particular theory, I want a specification of what such a theory ought to do. Write, in British English, the desiderata that an adequate theory of diachronic rationality must meet if it is to explain what, if anything, is irrational about money-pump arguments.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, distinguish a genuine constraint on any adequate theory from a partisan commitment of one school, and mark the requirement as non-negotiable or defeasible. The list should address at least the following:

1. The standard cycle: an agent prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, and can be induced to pay small amounts to trade successively around the cycle until she is back where she began, but poorer. Must a theory condemn the preferences, the sequence of choices, the underlying disposition, or only the agent’s failure to foresee the sequence?

2. The revolving menu: an agent whose preferences are complete, transitive, and stable is repeatedly offered choices between options whose descriptions and order of presentation vary, although the final outcomes are identical. Small framing effects lead her to pay repeatedly without any cycle in her preferences. Should a theory of diachronic rationality rule this out, or is the fault one of empirical knowledge, attention, or option-individuation rather than rationality?

3. The paid menu-editor: an agent knowingly pays an institution to restrict tomorrow’s menu because she predicts that tomorrow’s unconstrained self will choose badly. Explain when paying for commitment is rational, and specify how a theory can endorse this payment without licensing every form of binding or paternalistic interference.

4. Permissible conversion: an agent’s preferences change over time through learning, reflection, moral development, ageing, or a change in what she values, and the change leaves her predictably worse off by the standards of her earlier self. A theory must distinguish this from exploitation without making rationality require either frozen preferences or unlimited deference to one’s future self.

5. Refusal and incompleteness: in some putative pumps the agent may decline every trade; in others the relevant options are incomparable or on a par rather than strictly ranked. State whether money-pump reasoning requires completeness, whether the possibility of refusing trade changes the diagnosis, and what follows if preference itself is indeterminate.

6. Stakes and thresholds: each individual loss may be trivial even when the cumulative loss is serious. A theory should say whether rational assessment attaches to single choices, whole sequences, plans, dispositions, or expected lifetime outcomes, and it should explain how insignificant steps can compose a significant failure without committing a fallacy of composition.

7. Foresight and vulnerability: distinguish being irrational from merely being predictably exploitable. Specify what epistemic condition the money-pump argument needs: actual knowledge, available knowledge, or knowledge the agent ought to possess. Consider also an agent who foresees the entire sequence but reasonably doubts that resisting at every stage is possible.

8. The object of assessment: determine whether the fundamental bearers of diachronic rationality are preferences, choices, plans, policies, or temporally extended persons. The theory should explain why choosing the wrong object matters and should avoid simply multiplying levels of assessment whenever a problem arises.

Then identify any pairs or triples of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable. In particular, examine whether a theory can simultaneously respect preference change, condemn all guaranteed-loss sequences, permit rational precommitment, and avoid giving earlier selves unlimited authority over later ones. Say which desideratum excludes the largest range of otherwise plausible accounts.

Finally, score the main contenders only briefly against the completed list: a synchronic expected-utility account; a sophisticated-choice account on which the agent plans for her predicted future choices; a resolute-choice account on which she rationally adheres to an adopted plan; and a plan-based account that treats intentions as diachronic commitments rather than as further preferences. Do not provide a bibliography or citations; the conceptual list, not the literature review, is the deliverable.
