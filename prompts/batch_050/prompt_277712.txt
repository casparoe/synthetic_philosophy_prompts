ok so in philosophy club we just did Peter Abelard, the medieval monk guy who said sin isn't in what you do but in the consent of your will -- the outward act is morally indifferent, what matters is whether you actually consented to doing wrong. even wanting something bad isn't sin yet, only consenting is. he's got these examples of two people doing the exact same outward act where one sins and the other doesn't, because their wills differ.

anyway. lets say Abelard got dropped into 2025. i want your best guess at what he'd say about some modern stuff, and for each one tell me HOW sure you are -- like does the answer follow straight from his actual view, or are you extrapolating a lot?

1. AI assistants. say i tell my AI "do whatever it takes to ruin this guy's business" and it goes off and sabotages him. did i sin (consent) the moment i gave the order, even if the AI totally fails? what if i had no idea what it would actually do -- can you consent to something whose content you don't know? this one seems like a genuine hole in his framework since he assumed consent and action live in the same person.

2. intrusive thoughts. people with OCD get horrifying thoughts they hate and would never act on. Abelard distingished desire/temptation from consent so i think he actually handles this better than most people today?? but where exactly is the line between having a thought, entertaining it, and consenting to it, especially when the person themselves can't tell?

3. punishing speech. would the guy who said punishing the outward act misses the point support hate speech laws? or would he say the state can only ever punish acts while God judges consent -- and if so, does that make his ethics useless for actual law?

4. attempted crimes. we punish attempted murder way less than successful murder. Abelard would presumably call that insane, since the consent is identical and only luck differs. but would he really bite the bullet that there's zero moral difference between shooting and missing vs shooting and killing?

5. passive consumption. if i watch cruel videos for fun, or like a post dunking on someone, am i consenting to the cruelty or just standing near it? i vaguely remember he talked about taking pleasure in sin vs sinning itself but i forget the details.

big meta question too: his whole framework is about consent to what's contempt of God. does the view survive translation into secular ethics, or does predicting his modern takes require keeping the theology in? and please be honest about where you're guessing vs where his position is actually clear -- i don't want fake confidence.
