I lead the experimentation review group at a mid-sized software company, and I sign off on about a dozen A/B test and feature-rollout readouts every week. My recurring headache is a fight my analysts keep having: the topline number says one thing, the segment breakdown says another, and nobody has a principled way to decide which one to act on. The team has absorbed the folk maxim that the aggregate can lie and the segments tell the truth, which was a useful corrective two years ago but has started generating its own errors. Last quarter an analyst disaggregated a pricing test by a variable that was only recorded after users had been exposed to the treatment, produced a dramatic reversal in one segment, and talked the team out of shipping a change that had, I believe, genuinely helped. I need something better than both slogans. Reviews are time-boxed to thirty minutes, the reviewers are competent analysts but not statisticians, and the output of a review is a ship/don't-ship recommendation that I have to defend to product leadership.

Acting as a statistician with a serious grounding in the philosophy of causal inference, write me a decision procedure my reviewers can follow when an aggregate result and a segment-level result conflict. Before drafting it, think carefully through the ways such a procedure can misfire, because the failure modes are the point: the procedure must not amount to a fancier version of 'always trust the finer cut'. The core requirement is that it forces the analyst to write down, in plain language, the causal story connecting the proposed splitting variable, the treatment, and the outcome before it tells them which number to trust — and it must give them workable tests, based on what is actually observable in our experiment logs (when the variable was measured, whether it could have been affected by the treatment, whether exposure was randomised), for distinguishing the three structurally different situations: a variable that influences both who gets exposed and what happens to them, a variable that is influenced by both, and a variable that sits downstream of the treatment. My analysts will not know the standard terminology for these, so the procedure has to teach the distinctions through the tests rather than through vocabulary. It should specify what to do when the causal story is contested or unknowable within the thirty minutes, and it should mark honestly which steps are judgement calls wearing the costume of procedure.

Then run the procedure on three disputes drawn from our actual recent history: first, an observational comparison of a new onboarding flow in which the aggregate looks flat but every platform segment looks positive, and the mix of platforms differs sharply between users who received the flow and users who did not; second, a test in which an analyst produced an encouraging result by splitting on users who chose to enable the feature during the test window; third, a properly randomised test of a support tool in which the aggregate is clearly positive, the split is on a pre-treatment account tier, and the enterprise tier — whose account managers are extremely vocal — shows a negative point estimate, where I expect the correct output is one my stakeholders will hate, and I want to see exactly how the procedure defends it. State plainly what the procedure cannot settle and where a human has to take over. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — what the default rule of the procedure is — then give the reasoning and the procedure itself, with no preamble and no restatement of my situation. Please write in British English.
