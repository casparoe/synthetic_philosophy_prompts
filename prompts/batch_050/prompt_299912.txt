I am a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate course on Kant's ethics, and I would like an independent assessment of one student's exam answer before I finalize the marks.

The exam gave students a translated passage from the amphiboly section of Kant's Doctrine of Virtue (Ak. 6:442–443), in which Kant says that even gratitude for the long service of an old horse or dog belongs only indirectly to a human being's duty with regard to animals, and that considered as a direct duty it is always only a duty to oneself. The question read:

"Explain why, on Kant's view, gratitude toward a faithful old dog cannot be a duty owed to the dog, what makes it nonetheless a genuine duty, and what kind of duty it is. Then assess: does Kant's framework capture everything morally troubling about a farmer who has a healthy but no longer useful old dog shot out of convenience? Reconstruct Kant's own position rather than importing a contemporary animal-ethics view."

The student's answer:

"For Kant, all duty is owed either to oneself or to other rational beings, because obligation is possible only where one party can be held accountable by another in a relation of mutual recognition. A dog cannot reciprocate obligations or hold anyone to account, so it cannot be the kind of being to whom a duty is owed; strictly speaking there are no duties to animals, only duties regarding them. This is why Kant treats the gratitude case under what he calls an amphiboly of moral reflection: we are tempted to think the old dog itself has a claim on us, but reflection shows the duty lies elsewhere. The farmer's ingratitude would violate a duty to himself. Kant's reason is that cruelty and callousness toward animals dull our natural sympathy, a feeling nature implanted in us as an aid to morality, and ingratitude coarsens the disposition of gratefulness we owe it to ourselves to cultivate. So the requirement is indirect: it is essentially prudential counsel, a recommendation to protect our moral character, rather than a binding duty of the kind owed to persons. As for the assessment: Kant can condemn the farmer, but only for what the act does to the farmer's character, not for anything about the dog. This captures something real in the case — the smallness of destroying a creature that served you faithfully — but it leaves out any wrong done to the dog itself, and it makes the condemnation contingent: a farmer hardened enough to feel nothing, or skilled at compartmentalizing, would escape censure. That is a gap in the view, though Kant would presumably reply that such feelings cannot be neatly quarantined."

Please give (i) a grade out of 25 with a one-sentence summary justification, and (ii) point-by-point comments keyed to the student's distinct claims, stating for each whether it is correct, imprecise but acceptable, or erroneous — and for the erroneous ones, whether the problem is a conflation, a misattribution of Kant's grounds, or an inference that does not follow. I am not looking for a general impression of the answer; I want comments specific enough to paste into the margin.
