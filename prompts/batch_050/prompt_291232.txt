EXAM QUESTION: State Russell's analysis of sentences of the form "The F is G." Then, for each of the three puzzles Russell uses to motivate his theory of descriptions in "On Denoting" (1905) — the puzzle of informative identities, the puzzle concerning the law of excluded middle, and the puzzle of true negative existentials — explain what the puzzle is and how the analysis is supposed to resolve it. Conclude by explaining what it means to call definite descriptions "incomplete symbols" and what this shows about the relation between grammatical form and logical form.

STUDENT'S ANSWER: Russell analyzes "The F is G" as the conjunction of three claims: there is at least one F; there is at most one F; and whatever is F is G. Definite descriptions are therefore "incomplete symbols": unlike genuine referring expressions, they contribute no object to the proposition expressed by a sentence in which they occur, and they disappear on analysis, being paraphrased away in favor of quantifiers, predicates, and identity. This shows that grammatical form can mislead us about logical form, since a sentence may look as though it is about a particular object while in fact expressing a general, quantificational claim.

(1) Identity. The puzzle is how "Scott is the author of Waverley" can be both true and genuinely informative: if the description merely contributed its referent, the sentence would say no more than "Scott is Scott." On Russell's analysis the sentence is not an identity between two referents at all; it says that there is a unique author of Waverley and that whoever he is, he is identical with Scott. This can be informative because being the unique author of Waverley is a property one might not have known Scott to possess.

(2) Excluded middle. Either the present King of France is bald or he is not bald; yet since there is no King of France, neither disjunct seems true. Russell resolves this by appeal to presupposition: because the existence presupposition carried by the description fails, the negation comes out true. More precisely, "The present King of France is not bald" has a reading on which the description has primary occurrence, so that the negation scopes over the whole existential conjunction — on that reading the sentence is true — and a reading on which the description has secondary occurrence, asserting of a unique King of France that he is non-bald, which is false. Excluded middle is thereby preserved.

(3) Negative existentials. "The present King of France does not exist" is plainly true, yet if the description were a referring expression, the sentence would have to refer to something in order to deny that it exists, which is paradoxical. Russell's solution is that existence is not a predicate of individuals but is expressed by the existential quantifier: the sentence says simply that it is not the case that there is a unique King of France. Since the description disappears on analysis, there is no object of which existence is being self-defeatingly denied.

Finally, because ordinary proper names can also occur in true negative existentials (as in "Sherlock Holmes does not exist"), Russell concludes that ordinary proper names are disguised definite descriptions, and this thesis follows directly from the claim that descriptions are incomplete symbols.
