Okay so quick background: I'm in an undergrad ethics seminar and we just did the unit on what adult kids owe their parents -- the Jane English friendship-model stuff, where the debt/gratitude picture of filial obligation gets dunked on and what kids owe parents turns out to be roughly what friends owe friends. My professor kept saying the debt model "commodifies parental love," and I joked to my study group that someone should just take the debt model 100% seriously and follow it all the way down. Now I actually want that piece for our end-of-semester course zine.

So: write a parody in the form of an itemized invoice, with a short cover letter and an FAQ at the end, that a pair of parents sends their 26-year-old child, billing them for the full cost of being raised, in strict accordance with the debt model of filial obligation. Backstory you can use: the family recently had a fight about how rarely the kid calls, and this invoice is the parents' calm, professional response. Aim for maybe 600-750 words. The tone should be deadpan accounts-receivable -- "please remit within 30 days," itemized ledger, late fees, an optional payment plan. The humor has to come from the model itself, not from making the parents cartoon villains: the line items should force the debt theorist to actually price what the model counts (sacrifices, foregone careers, 3 a.m. feedings) right next to the stuff it's embarassing to bill (love, birthday parties, applause at the school play), there should be a credits/deductions column awkwardly wrestling with mutuality (the joy the child brought, chores, "companionship services rendered 2004–2011"), and the fine print should keep quietly collapsing back into the language of love ("outstanding balances may be reduced at the discretion of the billing department in exchange for phone calls home") -- which is exactly the move the debt model can't make without ceasing to be the debt model.

The FAQ should anticipate the obvious objections ("I never consented to these charges," "wouldn't billing me destroy the very relationship this debt is supposed to honor?") and answer them in ways that stay loyal to the debt model even where that loyalty sounds unhinged. Don't name English or any philosopher in the piece itself -- a reader who knows the debate should be able to feel exactly which moves are being skewered (favors vs. debts, whether gratitude can be demanded, whether the debt can ever be discharged, what happens to a family once it's a creditor-debtor relation) purely from how the invoice handles them. I want to laugh, and I also want to feel a little sick at the clause about repayment via nursing-home selection, which should definitely be in there.
