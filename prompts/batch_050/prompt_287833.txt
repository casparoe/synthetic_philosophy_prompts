Our effective altruism reading group was presented with an argument whose conclusion none of us will accept, but we cannot locate the bad step. (1) The lives of most wild animals contain more suffering than enjoyment — most die young by predation, starvation, exposure, or disease — so the typical wild animal's life has net negative welfare. (2) Destroying a habitat reduces, over time, the number of wild animals that habitat supports, and therefore the number of net-negative lives. (3) Reducing the number of net-negative lives reduces total suffering. (4) So habitat destruction is, other things equal, good, and interventions like draining wetlands or paving meadows should count among the most effective ways of reducing suffering. (5) But if (4) is true, there is presumably also a standing obligation to sterilize or eliminate ecosystems wherever feasible, and none of us will sign up for that. Which step should we reject, and why does that step look so compelling on its face? Price the alternatives honestly: if we reject (1), what does that commit us to about the empirical distribution of wild animal welfare; if we instead keep (1) and attack the move from (2) and (3) to (4), can that be done in a principled way that does not also undermine the ordinary case for preventing suffering in beings who already exist?
