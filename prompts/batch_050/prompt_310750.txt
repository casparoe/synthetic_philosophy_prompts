A classmate argued that the school district's new grading software is deliberately designed to lower certain students' averages -- not just that it's buggy, but that it was set up that way. Our teacher cut him off with "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence," and the discussion ended. I felt the claim was probably wrong but that the slogan did more work than it should have, and I couldn't say why on the spot.

Please break the slogan down into the distinct precise theses it could be expressing -- what makes a claim "extraordinary" (low prior probability? conflict with well-confirmed background knowledge? the sheer number of hidden actors it requires?), what would make evidence "extraordinary" (more of it, a different kind of it, or just enough of it relative to that prior?), and whose priors are doing the work. For each reading, tell me whether it's a trivial restatement of ordinary Bayesian reasoning, a substantive but defensible principle, or actually false -- and, in particular, whether any of them licenses using the slogan to end an argument rather than to assess the evidence offered, or whether it gets applied selectively to unpopular claims while popular claims pass on ordinary evidence. End by telling me which reading my teacher was most likely invoking and whether it supported his use. At most 450 words.
