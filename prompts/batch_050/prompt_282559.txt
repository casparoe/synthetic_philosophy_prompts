Ok so I'm a software engineer, no formal philosophy background, but I fell down a wikipedia rabbit hole last week and now I can't stop thinking about this. Basically: in the mid 20th century, doctors sometimes did prefrontal lobotomies (and similar procedures) on people with severe chronic pain. And the weird part is what patients said afterwards. A lot of them reported that the pain was still there — same pain, they'd say — but that it just didn't bother them anymore. There's also a related condition, I think its called pain asymbolia, where people with certain brain injuries still feel pain, still pull their hand away from a hot stove or whatever, but don't show any of the normal fear or distress or avoidance around it. They'll describe it in this weirdly detached way, like reporting the weather.

Here's my question. It seems to me there are (at least) two stories you could tell about what makes pain bad:

1. The badness is in the feeling itself — pain hurts, and that hurting is just intrinsically awful, so anywhere you have the pain feeling you have something bad.
2. The badness comes from something extra, like the aversion or the not-caring-about-it part — the raw sensation is one thing, and the mind's resistance to it is another, and only the second thing is bad.

My gut reaction was that the lobotomy/asymbolia cases basically refute view 1, since the patients keep the feeling but lose the badness. But the more I think about it the less sure I am. Like, maybe a defender of view 1 could say these patients aren't really feeling pain anymore, they've got some other sensation they just keep calling pain out of habit? Or that the patient reports are unreliable in some way? Or maybe view 2 has its own problems with the case that I'm not seeing — like is it really plausible that the sensation alone, totally stripped of aversion, is even worth calling pain, and if not, does that undercut the whole setup?

So: walk me through this properly. Treat the lobotomy/asymbolia stuff as a case study and analyze it from both views. What does each view predict the patients should be like, which features of the case does each one explain well and which does it fumble, and where does the theory just have nothing to say (e.g. about whether the patients' self-reports should be trusted)? And going the other direction — is this case actually good evidence for view 2 over view 1, or is that too quick, given how weird these patients are and how much rides on taking their reports at face value? If the case ends up not being decisive either way, I'd like to know what further fact, if we could somehow establish it, would settle things.
