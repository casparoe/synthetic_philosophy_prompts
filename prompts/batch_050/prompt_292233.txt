Two of my friends from a reading group got into a real argument last week and it's been stuck in my head, partly because I'm about to inherit some money and have to decide between helping my cousin keep her shop open and just donating it somewhere it'd do more measurable good. The argument started over a third friend, Jess, who gives a big chunk of her salary to global health charities while her own brother is drowning in debt, and she won't help him beyond a token amount.

Ana defended Jess using Mozi's impartial care: she said Mozi had this figured out 2400 years ago -- partiality, treating your own family's and your own country's welfare as weightier just because it's yours, is the root source of disorder in the world, and if everyone extended the same concern to others' families and states as their own, most conflict and neglect would just dissolve. The brother's debt is unpleasant but mosquito nets save actual lives, and concern should track need, not blood. Marcus pushed back with Confucian graded love: care naturally begins with your parents and radiates outward, benevolence grows from filial devotion the way a plant grows from its roots, and someone who claims to treat a stranger's child exactly as their own hasn't achieved universal love, they've just amputated the relationships that make love mean anything in the first place. He also brought up that Mencius basically accused Mozi of treating one's father as no different from any old man on the street, and added that impartial care is psychologically unsustainable anyway -- nobody can actually feel it, so preaching it just manufactures hypocrites. Ana said the Mencius thing is a smear: Mozi never demanded you stop loving your father, he demanded you hold yourself to standards you'd accept being applied impartially, and partiality scales terribly -- the exact same "my people first" reasoning that excuses ignoring distant strangers is what excuses nepotism, cronyism, and states attacking other states. Graded love, she said, has no principled stopping point before outright favoritism, and Confucians know it, which is why they tie themselves in knots over cases like Mencius saying the sage-king Shun would have thrown away the whole empire and fled with his father if his father had committed murder. Marcus's last move was that the stopping point is roles: your obligations are specified by the relationship, and family feeling governs private life while an official's public duties are a separate matter -- the Shun case shows Confucians took the tension seriously instead of pretending it away. Whereas Mozi's defense of impartial care was basically consequentialist (it produces order and benefit for all), so if graded care happened to produce better outcomes he'd have to drop the principle, meaning the principle isn't even doing the real work in his own theory.

Can you adjudicate this move by move -- say who's right at each of the steps and why, not just who wins overall? I'm especially interested in (a) whether Mozi actually has the resources to answer the psychological-impossibility charge, (b) whether graded love really does have a principled line before nepotism or if Marcus's "roles" answer just relocates the problem, and (c) whether there's a coherent position that takes Marcus's point about the roots of love while keeping Ana's point about "my people first" scaling into corruption. And then tell me who's more right overall, because I geniunely can't tell and my cousin's shop is kind of riding on it.
