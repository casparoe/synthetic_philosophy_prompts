I am a property lawyer practicing in a common-law jurisdiction, and I want to pressure-test my sense that the public debate over colonial-era restitution is really just property law conducted in an unfamiliar register. When I read arguments for returning the Benin Bronzes, I encounter vocabulary I do not fully command — cultural patrimony, wrongs done to a people rather than to persons, the object as partly constitutive of the community it was taken from. Take the strongest version of the moral case for restitution and translate it, as systematically and honestly as you can, into the vocabulary I actually work in: conversion and detinue, the nemo dat rule, bona fide purchase for value without notice, adverse possession and prescription, laches and limitation periods, unjust enrichment, standing, and the succession of claims across generations.

Structure the translation in three parts. First, the clean mappings: which elements of the restitution case have direct doctrinal counterparts — for instance, the 1897 punitive expedition as dispossession without even a colorable claim of title, and the subsequent dispersal through European markets as a chain of transfers in which, under nemo dat, no buyer could acquire what the sellers did not have. Second, the mappings that require distortion: where you have to bend a doctrine to make it carry the moral claim — say, treating limitation periods and good-faith purchase as doing the real work in the retentionist case even though the moral intuition behind them is about reliance and settled expectations rather than about the objects themselves. Third, the residue: what in the moral case has no legal expression at all. I have in mind things like the claim that the original wrong was suffered by a community rather than by identifiable title-holders, that today's claimant is not the injured party but a successor people whose identity is partly bound up with the objects, and the retentionist rejoinder that a universal museum's custody confers benefits on the world that count against return.

Then give me your verdict on the residue. Is what fails to translate a defect of the legal vocabulary — evidence that property doctrine simply has no concepts for collective and intergenerational claims — or is it evidence that the moral case, once stripped of legal trappings, rests on notions that are murkier than its proponents admit? I am genuinely uncertain, so do not split the difference: argue for whichever verdict you think is right, and state what observation or consideration would change your mind.

Finally, run the same apparatus on the Parthenon Marbles, which are typically argued on different facts: a claimed Ottoman-era authorization, a much longer lapse of time, and a standing nation-state as claimant. Which parts of your translation carry over unchanged, which doctrines end up doing the discriminating work between the two cases, and does your framework deliver the verdicts you actually hold — or does it commit you to treating the two cases as more alike, or more different, than you are comfortable with?
