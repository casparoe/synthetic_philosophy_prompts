I've been draftng a short essay on the colour-phi phenomenon and I'd like some fairly tough comments before I revise it. Please answer in British English.

The phenomenon, briefly: a red spot appears on the left and disappears, then a green spot appears on the right. People often report seeing one spot move smoothly from left to right, changing from red to green partway across. The puzzle is that the subject apparently sees the spot turn green at a location and time before the green spot has actually appeared. One possible explanation is Stalinesque: the brain delays the whole show until it has enough information, then constructs a tidy sequence before anything becomes conscious. Another is Orwellian: the person has a conscious experience immediately, but later information rewrites the record of what it was, so the report and memory are false.

Here's the essay:

## Experience in escrow

The usual colour-phi debate treats the choice as if it were between two kinds of deception. Either consciousness waits for a fabricated final cut, or it first experiences one thing and is then left with a fraudulent memory of it. But this assumes that every experience has one definite birthday: a moment when it either happens or does not. That assumption may be the real problem.

Suppose perceptual experience works more like money held in escrow. Incoming sensory material first enters a live feed: brief, unstable contents that can guide attention but are not yet organised into a scene. The system then holds a very short sequence in an editorial buffer, where later information can alter earlier candidates. Only after this does the sequence get released as the subject's settled perceptual present. Release is not the same thing as remembering, since something can be released and immediately forgotten. Nor is it the same thing as first having phenomenal character, since the live feed might already involve some faint or fragmentary experience.

On this model, the colour-phi case need not be purely Stalinesque or purely Orwellian. When the green spot arrives, the brain revises the buffered sequence before releasing it: that part is Stalinesque. But there may also have been a fleeting live-feed experience corresponding to the red spot continuing or jumping, which is then overwritten and becomes inaccessible: that part is Orwellian. The dispute is therefore not about which single story is correct. It is about which layer of processing we are describing.

This also suggests that there can be different kinds of revision. A shallow revision changes how an already released experience is remembered. A deeper revision changes the sequence before release. There may also be an intermediate case in which an experience affects anticipation, attention or bodily readiness without ever becoming something the subject could recognise as part of their visual field. The question of exactly when the red spot was experienced may have no one answer until we say which of these thresholds matters.

I do not want to say merely that it depends what we mean by experience and leave it at that. The more substantive claim is that there may be several real thresholds rather than one gateway into consciousness. If so, experiments should not look only for the moment of conscious experience. They should try to identify separate transitions: when a content first biases behaviour, when it becomes available for comparison with later content, when it can be reported, and when it enters durable autobiographical memory. Different theories of consciousness might then turn out to be theories of different thresholds.

Still, the escrow model has an obvious danger. If the live feed contains experiences that can never be reported or remembered, the model may posit consciousness so inaccessible that no evidence could detect it. On the other hand, if we say that only released contents are truly experienced, then the model collapses back into a version of the Stalinesque story. The challenge is to explain what would make an unreleased draft genuinely experiential rather than merely a computational precursor to experience.
