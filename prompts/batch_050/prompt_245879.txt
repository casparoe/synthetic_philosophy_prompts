Now that I am retired from forty years in experimental physics, I have finally had time to read properly in moral philosophy, and I keep being struck by how much of it was already, in a sense, my professional life. Lately I have been working through Parfit's hitchhiker case — the traveller stranded in the desert who can only secure a ride by being the sort of person who would pay the driver upon reaching town, even though once safely there it would be irrational to hand over the money. Parfit's suggestion, as I understand it, is that it can be rational to make oneself, or to make oneself disposed to be, irrational. This struck a chord with me, because I spent my twenties and thirties under the doctrine of nuclear deterrence, and I would like to see the parallel worked out properly.

Please analyze Cold War nuclear deterrence — the strategy of threatening massive retaliation in order to prevent an attack one could not defend against — through the lens of the hitchhiker and the theory of rational irrationality. The broad mapping seems plain enough: deterrence requires an adversary to believe you would retaliate even after your cities are destroyed, when retaliation no longer protects anything and looks, at that moment, like pointless vengeance — just as the hitchhiker's payment is pointless once he is safe. But I want the mapping done with discipline. Who corresponds to the driver, and what corresponds to the rescue as opposed to the payment? Deterrence theorists were obsessed with commitment devices — automatic retaliation systems, doomsday machines of the sort Dr. Strangelove lampooned, delegating launch authority, burning one's bridges — precisely because a threat one can reconsider at the decisive moment is not credible. Is the doomsday machine best understood as a way of removing the decision point entirely, so that the irrationality never has to be performed, only credibly pre-installed? And notice the disanalogies, which I suspect are more interesting than the analogies: the hitchhiker's driver can read faces, so to speak, while states must infer dispositions from postures and pronouncements; the hitchhiker makes a promise to a benefactor, deterrence a threat to an adversary who is making the same threat back; and a bluff called in the desert costs one man a ride, while a bluff called between superpowers costs civilizations. Where exactly does the parallel break, and does the hitchhiker framework illuminate or distort the ethics of threatening what it would be wrong to do?

I would also like the analysis to run in the other direction. Does forty years of deterrence without a nuclear exchange count as evidence that rational irrationality is a coherent and even workable strategy — that being genuinely disposed to do the pointless thing achieved its end precisely by never having to be acted on? Or is the historical record too murky to confirm anything, given that we cannot rerun the century without the arsenals? Be frank about how confident you are in each part of the mapping: state your level of confidence in the analysis as a whole, and single out the single claim in it that you are least sure of.
