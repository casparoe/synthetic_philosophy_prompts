I'm a historian teaching a graduate seminar on historiography, and every year the same fight breaks out over periodization, and every year it ends in a shrug. Roughly: one camp treats the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution as real joints in the historical carcass — Burckhardt's account of a new individualism and statecraft in Italy, Butterfield's claim that something decisively transformed natural philosophy in the seventeenth century — while the other camp argues that these are retrospective constructions: that the Renaissance was a propaganda term invented by people celebrating themselves and canonized by nineteenth-century historians, that the twelfth century had its own 'renaissance,' that scholastic natural philosophy flowed continuously into the new science, and that a working Florentine artisan in 1470 would have experienced no rupture whatsoever. The striking thing to me is that the dispute never actually moves: the lumpers concede that periods are constructed and then keep using them; the splitters concede that some things changed and then keep denying the labels. Nobody updates, and nobody's position predicts anything that could embarrass it.

My hypothesis, which I'd like tested rather than accepted, is that this is not one disagreement but at least three wearing the same clothes: a metaphilosophical question (what would make a period 'real' rather than merely useful — cluster structure in events, self-description by contemporaries, discontinuity in some measurable variable?), a set of empirical questions (how abrupt were changes in, say, painting technique, legal institutions, or mathematical practice?), and a pedagogical-political question (what are we doing to students and to the past when we periodize?). The camps talk past each other because the lumper cites discontinuities as if they answered the first question and the splitter cites self-fashioning rhetoric as if it answered the second. As a diagnostic proposal, I'd suggest a test: for a period boundary to be 'real,' it should mark a discontinuity that is detectable in data the boundary's defenders did not use to define it — otherwise every periodization is just a tautology with dates attached. Feel free to reject this test and propose a better one, but engage with the underlying problem it targets.

So: is the stalled debate over whether these three periodizations are 'real things or later inventions' a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about what 'real' licenses here, or a disagreement about which data are admissible — self-descriptions, material culture, institutional structures, rates of change? Diagnose the structure of the dispute using what the two camps actually argue (including their standard concessions), say what would count as progress on your diagnosis — a distinction, a measurable criterion, a test like the one above, or a reframing of the question — and give an honest verdict on whether the impasse signals that the question is ill-posed or merely hard. Please state how confident you are in the diagnosis overall, and single out the specific claim in your answer you are least sure of.
