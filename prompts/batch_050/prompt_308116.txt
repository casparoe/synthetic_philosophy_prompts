My dad has early-stage dementia and his doctors enrolled him in this trial program where they replace failing brain cells with synthetic ones, a few thousand at a time. Each replacement neuron is supposed to take over the exact job of the old one, so his memories and personality get carried over as they go. He's been on it for about eight months now and honestly he's doing great, sharper than he's been in years, remembers my kids' names again, tells the same dumb jokes he always did.

Here's what's eating at me though. The clinic offers two versions of the program. The one he's on replaces cells slowly, over about three years total. But there's an accelerated option where they do the whole thing in one long operation over a weekend, and it ends up with the exact same final result, same synthetic cells in the same places doing the same jobs. The slow version costs way more and our insurance would actually cover the fast one fully, so when I asked about switching, the coordinator looked at me like I was crazy for hesitating. But I couldn't shake the feeling that the slow one is... fine, somehow, and the fast one is basically letting my dad die on a table and sending home a very convincing copy? Which sounds insane when I say it out loud, because he's already like 20 percent replaced and I still hug him and mean it. Where's the line? Is 20 percent fine but 100 percent over a weekend not? Why would the speed matter if the end state is identical?

My sister thinks I'm being ridiculous and says if I'd accept it cell by cell I have to accept it all at once, same thing just faster. Maybe she's right, but then I follow that logic and it seems to lead somewhere really dark about what we're doing to him right now. So my actual question: should I push to switch him to the fast version, keep him on the slow one, or pull him out entirely? And more than the practical answer, I want to understand what the real difference even is between these options, if there is one, because I can't articulate it and it's keeping me up at night.
