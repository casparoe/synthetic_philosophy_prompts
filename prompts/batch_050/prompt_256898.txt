I recently began volunteering with a small conservation charity whose current campaign raises money to protect the Scottish wildcat, and I have found myself in the middle of a dispute I did not expect to care about. The wildcat interbreeds freely with feral domestic cats, and a vocal faction within the group argues that the campaign is therefore misguided. Their reasoning rests on an analogy they treat as decisive: a species, they say, is like a language. Just as there was no precise morning on which Latin became Italian, and just as neighbouring dialects shade into one another so that any line drawn between them is partly a matter of convention, there is no hard fact about where the wildcat ends and the domestic cat begins. Linguistic purists who try to keep a language free of foreign contamination are figures of fun; the campaign, on this view, is the biological equivalent of linguistic prescriptivism — an attempt to freeze and police an arbitrary boundary. The money, they conclude, would be better spent on a species whose boundaries are real.

I would like you to audit this analogy as far as it can be pushed. Specifically:

1. Lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of speakers, of mutual intelligibility, of dialect continua, of loanwords, of language death, and of a standard language imposed by an academy.
2. Identify the respects in which the analogy genuinely holds — including any that cut against my colleagues. For instance, languages absorb loanwords constantly without losing their identity; if hybridization is the biological analogue of borrowing, does introgression actually fail to dissolve a species' identity in the way the faction assumes?
3. Identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion, as opposed to mere points of difference. Candidates I have been turning over: species delimitation, unlike grammar, is supposed to be a descriptive rather than a normative enterprise; reproductive isolation and shared genealogy may have no linguistic counterpart; and the purity framing may misdescribe what conservationists actually value (evolutionary history, ecological function, genetic diversity for future adaptation) rather than a fetish for unmixed ancestry.
4. Say whether the conclusion — defund the wildcat campaign — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If it does not, state the strongest claim the analogy can legitimately support (perhaps something weaker about the conventionality of subspecies labels or about how conservation triage should handle hybridizing populations). And if you think there is a better analogy for what a species is than a language, say what it is and what it gets right that the language analogy does not.

Finally, please indicate how confident you are in each of your main claims, and then single out the one point in your answer where your confidence is lowest, explaining briefly what makes it the shakiest.
