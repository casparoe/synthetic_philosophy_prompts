I'm a game theorist (IO mostly, but I know my way around incomplete information and dynamic games). I keep bumping into the "dark forest" idea from the Three-Body books -- the claim that the rational response to detecting another civilization is to destroy it immediately and silently, so the galaxy looks empty because everyone is either hiding or shooting. People discuss it in this woolly sci-fi way, but it smells to me like it should just be a game, maybe an ugly one.

So: restate the dark forest argument as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use. Players, types, action spaces, signal structures, beliefs, off-path threats, commitment, discounting, equilibrium selection. I want to know things like: is the "shoot first" conclusion a unique equilibrium or just one of several? What assumptions are doing the work -- is it basically a commitment problem, a lemons-market unraveling, an all-pay arms race, or something else? The books lean on a couple of premises that sound like primitives but might just be modeling choices: survival as the sole terminal goal, the impossibility of credible communication across the "chain of suspicion," and the possibility of sudden technological leaps meaning any currently-weak civilization could become dangerous before you can react.

Then the important part: tell me what didn't survive the translation. Are there claims in the original that have no counterpart in standard game-theoretic language at all? For example, the whole thing seems to assume that hiding is essentially free and detection is one-directional, and I can't tell if that's a payoff assumption, an information-structure assumption, or just an unexamined stipulation. And the "chain of suspicion" -- is that just common-priors-failing, or is it something my framework can't represent, like reasoning about minds whose reasoning procedures you can't even model?

For each piece that doesn't survive, tell me whether you think that's because game theory is missing something real (and what would have to be added -- bounded rationality about other agents' models? non-Bayesian uncertainty? something about value change over time?), or because the original claim was doing rhetorical work rather than logical work and evaporates on contact with formalization. I'd rather get a blunt verdict than a diplomatic one.
