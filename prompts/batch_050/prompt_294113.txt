Act as a professional philosopher with experience designing seminar activities. Think carefully, briefly brainstorm three possible mechanics, and then develop the strongest one.

I teach a 75-minute upper-level philosophy seminar with 18 students. Design an activity that makes them feel the pressure generated by Quinn’s self-torturer case: each one-step change offers a clear benefit and a cost small enough to seem negligible, so every step can appear rational, yet the resulting endpoint is plainly worse than the starting point. The activity should also expose how a series of pairwise comparisons can support a cycle even when no participant endorses the cycle as a whole.

Participants should encounter the problem through their choices before hearing its philosophical name. Provide complete rules, group sizes and roles, materials, a payoff scheme using tokens rather than money or actual discomfort, and a way to make adjacent options difficult to distinguish without making the game arbitrary. Include at least two rounds: one in which students act individually, and one in which they may devise a collective policy. Then provide a debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto local rationality, global irrationality, intransitivity, preference change, and dynamic inconsistency, carefully distinguishing these diagnoses. Finally, identify what will predictably go wrong on the first run and which feature of Quinn’s case the game cannot reproduce.
