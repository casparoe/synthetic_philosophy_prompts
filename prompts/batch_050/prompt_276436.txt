So my flatmates have been going at it all week about Borges and I need a referee because they won't let it drop. The essay is his A New Refutation of Time, and the bit they're stuck on is the passage towards the middle where Borges describes being out one night on an unremarkable street -- the low houses, the muddy ground, the feeling of the whole scene -- and says he suddenly had the conviction that this exact moment was not a repetition of some earlier night but literally identical to one, and he extends this to argue that time, as a single continuous series of distinct moments, doesn't really exist. His broader line in the essay is that once you've denied matter with Berkeley and mind with Hume, continuity of time goes too: there are just isolated presents, and memory is itself a present phenomenon, so it can't prove a past ever happened. Anyay. Here's the fight.

Rosa says this is a genuine metaphysical argument and a good one. Her version: if two moments are qualitatively identical in every respect, there is nothing left to distinguish them except a bare "this happened at a different time," and that's exactly the kind of empty label the argument is stripping away. She says Borges has spotted that temporal order is something we construct by stitching moments together, and the stitching is extra -- it isn't in any of the moments themselves. She admits the argument is idealist in flavour but says so is Berkeley's and we still take that seriously.

Dan says Rosa is conflating two completely different things: how we could ever know the past existed, and whether it did. Memory being a present phenomenon shows at most that our evidence for the past is always in the present, which nobody denies and which is true of basically all evidence for everything not currently in front of you. By that standard, he says, you've also refuted the existence of the other side of the moon, since your evidence for it is also a present experience. He calls the identical-moments move a parlour trick: of course two moments you can't tell apart feel like one moment, but "I cannot detect a difference" is not "there is no difference."

Rosa came back with two points. First, she said Dan is smuggling in the very thing under dispute: he assumes moments have a hidden identity beyond all their qualities, but if a moment has no distinguishing feature at all, the claim that it's "really two moments" does no work and explains nothing. Second, she said the moon analogy backfires, because the moon's far side could in principle become a present experience, whereas the past never can -- so the cases aren't parallel and Borges is entitled to treat time differently from space.

Dan's reply: the fact that we can't revisit the past is a fact about us and our position, not about the past. A goldfish can't revisit it either and that tells us nothing. He also said Rosa's "does no work" test would abolish lots of perfectly good things -- two qualitatively identical electrons are still two electrons, and physics keeps track of the difference fine. Rosa said electrons are distinguishable by their spatial positions and histories, so that example actually helps her, and Dan said that's circular because "histories" is what he's defending and she can't help herself to the question of whether history is real while ruling his answer out of bounds.
