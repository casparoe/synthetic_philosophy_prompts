I'm trying to get my intuitions about resentment and hurt feelings in order, Strawson-style — the whole excuse-versus-exemption business — and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures my verdicts on cases. Here they are, with my gut responses:

- My mate Dave lied about missing my wedding; turned out he was hungover and couldn't be bothered. Resentment: fully warranted, months of it.
- A colleague sabotaged my project pitch, but only because our boss told him it was him or me on the redundancy list. I still resent him, maybe two-thirds as much as Dave, and I think that's about right — he could have refused.
- A bloke with severe bipolar, mid-episode, said vicious things to me at a party. I felt only worry, no resentment, and I think that's right too.
- An eight-year-old told me I have a stupid face. No resentment, mild amusement. Fine.
- My gran, early dementia, now says cutting things she never would have before. Intellectually I exempt her completely — but the hurt arrives anyway, every time, and I can't decide whether the hurt is a defect in me or is telling me something.
- A friend with bad social anxiety has cancelled on me at the last minute three times running. I resent him for it, and unlike with the bipolar bloke I don't think the resentment is misplaced — but I also can't say what he did that the bipolar man didn't also do, namely let me down because of a condition.
- Someone accidentally trod on my foot at a gig: flash of anger, gone the moment he apologised. Obviously fine.
- My aunt does genuinely helpful things for me but always in this smug, look-how-much-you-need-me way. I resent the help itself, and I don't think I should stop.

My tentative principle is that resentment fits iff the person showed ill will or a lack of due regard for me, whatever the actual outcome. That handles the foot and the aunt nicely, and maybe the bipolar man (no ill will in there). But it seems to acquit the anxious friend, which I don't want, and it seems to make my hurt at gran a simple mistake. And on the colleague: he did show me disregard — he picked his job over me — so resentment fits, but then what work is the "it was him or me" bit doing in damping it?

So, deliverables: state a principle (or a small set of candidate principles) that gets as many of these as possible, say which case motivates each clause, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to drop — in particular, whether I can coherently keep both "resenting the anxious friend is fitting" and "resenting the bipolar man is not" without the line becoming unprincipled. Then tell me what your best principle says about cases I haven't given you: someone severely sleep-deprived who snaps at me, and someone who knew they had a condition, refused treatment for it, and then hurt me. And one more thing: is there any principled room for the gran case, where the exemption is right but the hurt won't stop, or is any decent principle committed to calling my feelings just wrong?

If I've garbled the excuse/exemption distinction somewhere, or the request for a single principle is hopeless because these feelings are tracking several different things at once, say so plainly rather than smoothing it over to accomodate me.
