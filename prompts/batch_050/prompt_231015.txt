Below is a passage from Part I of Section VIII, "Of Liberty and Necessity," in David Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding:

"From this circumstance alone, that a controversy has been long kept on foot, and remains still undecided, we may presume, that there is some ambiguity in the expression, and that the disputants affix different ideas to the terms employed in the controversy."

Hume immediately applies this diagnostic to the free will debate itself, writing:

"I hope, therefore, to make it appear, that all men have ever agreed in the doctrine both of necessity and of liberty, according to any reasonable sense, which can be put on these terms; and that the whole controversy has hitherto turned merely upon words."

Write a dialogue of roughly 700–900 words (the passages above do not count toward this length) between two equally capable contemporary philosophers: a compatibilist who endorses Hume's diagnostic and its application here, and an incompatibilist who holds that determinism excludes the ability to do otherwise and thereby undermines moral responsibility. The dialogue must engage Hume's methodological claim directly, not just use it as a springboard. The compatibilist should explain why the longevity of the dispute is evidence of a verbal confusion and should put forward a concrete disambiguation (for instance, a conditional or hypothetical reading of "could have done otherwise") intended to show that both sides agreed all along. The incompatibilist should press the strongest available reply: that a controversy can outlive the clarification of its terms when the parties share a concept but disagree about its analysis, or when each side holds an intuition it regards as non-negotiable; and that the proposed disambiguation either changes the subject or smuggles in an ability that determinism, on the incompatibilist's reading, still rules out. Each speaker must respond to the other's actual arguments rather than restate a prepared position; include at least one point at which a speaker concedes ground or qualifies an earlier claim in light of an objection. Do not declare a winner. End the dialogue with each character briefly stating what consideration or result would lead them to abandon their position, so that the reader can judge whether Hume's diagnostic survives the exchange.
