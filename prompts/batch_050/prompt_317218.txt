For a 75-minute meeting of a philosophy reading group (8–12 adults, no prior coursework assumed), design a structured activity that makes participants feel the force of the heterogeneity problem for hedonism — the question of what, if anything, the pleasures of solving a proof, eating chocolate, a warm bath, and the relief of pain have in common that makes them all pleasures. In Chapter 2 of Utilitarianism, J.S. Mill proposes, in effect, that when people who have experienced two pleasures give a decided preference for one, setting aside any sense of moral obligation, that settles which pleasure is more valuable and reveals a difference in quality, not merely quantity. The activity should put this procedure into practice rather than discuss it: give participants a common menu of heterogeneous experiences, stage individual and then pairwise "competent judge" comparisons under Mill-like rules, and then force the group to construct a single common currency (e.g., a fixed budget of points, or trades of minutes-of-experience) so that they themselves run into the problems — preference reversal, intransitivity, smuggled-in considerations of intensity, duration, and moral worth, and judges who simply refuse to compare. Specify the roles and rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief, which should map moments in the activity onto the felt-quality versus attitudinal theories of pleasure and onto the failure modes of Mill's test. State what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run and how to handle it, and be candid about which feature of the heterogeneity problem the activity cannot reproduce — in particular, whether any game built on choices and reports can make participants feel, rather than merely infer, the absence (or presence) of a shared phenomenal quality across pleasures.
