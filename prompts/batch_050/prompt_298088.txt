I'm a retired chemist who's gotten into philosophy over the last year or so, and I've tied myself in knots over an old puzzle. As I understand it, Bradley's regress says that if a proposition were just its constituents plus a relation tying them together, you'd need further relations to tie that relation to the constituents, and off you go. Fine. But I keep wanting to approach it from the other end, the way I'd think about valence: some constituents seem to come with a free bond, others don't.

Play the role of a professional philosopher who knows this literature well (Frege, Russell, Wittgenstein, the unity of the proposition). Think carefully, and start by brainstorming two or three candidate principles before settling on one.

Here are my verdicts on some cases:

1. "Socrates is wise" -- one thought, true or false. Unified.
2. "Socrates, wisdom," said as a bare list -- not unified; there's nothing to agree or disagree with.
3. "Socrates' wisdom" -- hangs together more than the list does, but it doesn't say anything, so I don't count it as a proposition.
4. "Socrates exemplifies wisdom" -- unified again. But notice that on a purely additive picture this should be no better than case 2; it's just three items with one renamed. So whatever glues case 1 must glue this one without any regress.
5. The newspaper headline "Miller guilty" -- no verb, but I read it as a claim, true or false. Unified.
6. The that-clause inside "Jones believes that Socrates is wise" -- on its own it asserts nothing, yet it seems fully unified inside, unlike case 3.

The pair I can't reconcile is 3 and 5: as I naively picture it, neither has a verb doing predicative work, yet I want to call one a proposition and the other not. The contrast between 2 and 4 worries me too.

What I'd like from you: state one principle, precisely, that sorts these six cases the way I do -- or, if that's impossible, tell me which verdict has to go and give the simplest principle covering the rest. For each clause of your principle, say which case forced it. Then tell me what your principle predicts for two cases I haven't listed: a mathematical equation like "2+2=4" (no verb in the usual sense) and a piece of pure word salad that does contain a verb, say "Wisdom is Socrates" read literally. Be honest about which of my verdicts your principle can't save.

End with a last line beginning "Verdict:" followed by one sentence stating your final principle and which of my verdicts, if any, got sacrified.
