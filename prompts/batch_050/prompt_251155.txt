Isaac Newton held theological views that were unusual for an Englishman of his station: he privately rejected the doctrine of the Trinity on scriptural grounds (a heresy he concealed throughout his life), treated the order and mathematical lawlikeness of the cosmos as evidence of an intelligent and continuously active deity, denied that gravitational attraction was inherent or essential to matter and speculated that it might be mediated by an immaterial agent, and devoted decades of labor to biblical prophecy and sacred chronology. Now suppose Newton were brought to the present and given a careful briefing on three developments: (1) Darwinian evolution by natural selection, including its capacity to produce complex functional organization without a designer; (2) the contemporary fine-tuning arguments in cosmology, together with the multiverse hypotheses that some physicists offer as an alternative explanation for the apparently life-permitting values of physical constants; (3) the thoroughgoing methodological naturalism and widespread personal secularism of the modern scientific community. Predict, in each case, how Newton would most plausibly respond, grounding your predictions in his actual commitments as described above rather than in a generic picture of a religious scientist. Which developments would he welcome as vindication, which would he resist, and would any of them force him to revise his views? Address in particular: whether his design argument would survive natural selection or merely retreat to the level of cosmological constants; whether he would regard multiverse reasoning as legitimate physics or as an evasion of the very conclusion he thought the heavens compelled; and whether he would see modern secular science as a triumph of the natural philosophy he founded or as a corruption of it. Where his recorded commitments genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so explicitly instead of resolving the question by fiat.
