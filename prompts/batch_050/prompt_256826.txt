I write about science and philosophy for an online mag, and I keep running into the same problem: when should I just trust what someone tells me? Philosophers fight about whether testimony is knowledge on its own terms (the Reid-ish view that believing people is the default unless something's off) or whether you need independent, non-testimonial reasons before a source earns your belief (the Hume-ish reductionist view). I've been winging it per story, which is bad.

Write me a checklist I can actually run before filing -- the questions in order, what counts as a passing answer, and where the procedure should stop and force a "verify or kill the line" call. It needs to handle the cases that keep biting me: (1) a source passing along what a colleague said at a closed meeting, so my evidence is two hops from the event; (2) three independent-sounding experts who turn out to have all read the same preprint, so it looks like corroboration but isnt; (3) a scientist with a stellar record talking outside their field.

Then run the checklist on these three cases, including one where it tells me to do something I'll hate -- e.g. downgrading a quote from my best, most reliable source because I can't check the chain behind what they're saying. And be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and which of the two philosophical camps the whole thing is secretly assuming, because my editor will ask.
