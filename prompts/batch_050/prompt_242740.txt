I retired last year from 35 years as an experimental physicist and have fallen into philosphy the way some people fall into cold lakes. Our little reading group has been stuck for months on the extended mind thesis, and I would like a diagnosis rather than another round of championing.

The stall looks like this. Side A says cognition is whatever the skull-and-body system does, so notebooks, phones, maps, slide rules, and spreadsheets are tools: inputs and outputs, never parts of the thinker. Side B says if some external store or process is reliably available, automatically trusted, smoothly integrated, and functionally doing the work memory or calculation would otherwise do, then the boundary of the mind should follow the coupled system, not the skin.

Everyone in the room grants the same boring facts: people remember worse without devices, devices sometimes lie, implants feel different from pockets, culture changes habits, and no one wants to say a library becomes literate when I browse it fast. And yet nobody moves.

My physicist hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the fight is secretly about three different dependences that keep getting collapsed. Call them replacement, where the tool does a job the brain could still do badly; transformation, where the agent can only run the process at all in the loop with the tool, like some kinds of large-scale calculation or navigation; and calibration, where the tool changes the agents sense of when they know, including how they catch their own errors. Maybe Side A is tracking replacement and Side B is tracking transformation, while the real bite is calibration: whether failures of the tool are metabolized as my mistakes or as bad weather.

Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it verbal, over the honorific cognitive; substantive metaphysics, about constitution versus causation; a standards fight over which cases count as data, familiar phone life versus rare implants and illusions; or a normative dispute about where responsibility, credit, and selfhood should be assigned once cognition gets leaky? Use the positions as I described them, not a museum tour of the literature, and no citations or reading lists. Then say what progress would even look like given your diagnosis: a distinction, an intervention or lesion-style test, a formal mark of cognition, better examples, a decision to split the question, or an argument that the impasse is the correct result because the question is ill-posed. If my replacement-transformation-calibration idea helps, use it; if it is muddy, say exactly where it fails. I want the structure of the deadlock, not a verdict.
