I help run a student chapter that does policy briefings, and last month we hosted a panel on autonomous weapons where I noticed something maddening: literally everyone on the panel -- the NGO person who wants a pre-emptive ban, the defense contractor guy, and the ex-military lawyer -- claimed to support "meaningful human control." Each of them clearly meant something different by it, and each used it to draw a different conclusion about the same hypothetical weapon (an air defense system that selects and engages incoming drones faster than any operator could review). After the panel a friend argued the phrase is just empty feel-good PR that does no work, and I couldn't really disagree, but I also suspect it doesn't *have* to be empty.

So here's what I want: take "meaningful human control" apart into the different precise theses it could be stating. Control over *what* exactly -- over whether the system is deployed at all, over each individual engagement, over the rules of engagement it follows, over the outcome in retrospect? Control by *whom* -- the operator watching the screen, the commander who sets parameters, the programmer who trained it, a legislature that authorized it? And what makes control *meaningful* rather than nominal: does the human need enough time to actually deliberate, enough understanding of how the system reaches its decisions, the ability to veto mid-action, or just legal responsibility if things go wrong? Some of these readings probably collapse into things nobody denies (someone, somewhere, switched the thing on), others might be so demanding they'd rule out ordinary cruise missiles or even traffic lights with consequences, and a few are presumably where the actual disagreement lives.

For each reading, tell me whether it's trivial, defensible-but-substantive, or actually incoherent, and crucially whether it supports the ban advocate's conclusion, the contractor's "this system is already compliant" conclusion, or neither. Bonus: say which reading you think the people who coined the slogan probably needed it to mean for it to do the work they wanted -- or whether the phrase survives mainly *because* it papers over these disagreements. I want to bring this to our next chapter meeting as an actual menu of options rather than letting "meaningful human control" keep ending arguments by agreeing with everyone.
