For the past year I have served as the philosophical advisor to a small standing committee that reviews proposed additions and revisions to a major diagnostic manual (the precise manual does not matter; imagine the processes by which DSM and ICD entries get proposed, contested, and retired). The other members are clinicians and epidemiologists, and I am consulted only when a proposal seems to turn on the concept of disorder itself rather than on data. My problem is one of triage: I can demand a full conceptual review of at most three or four proposals per revision cycle before my credibility with the committee runs out, so I must choose carefully where philosophy is genuinely doing work and where it would be decorative.

The backdrop is the familiar dispute between naturalism and normativism about health and disease: Boorse-style accounts on which disease consists in statistically subnormal, species-typical functioning; normativist accounts on which classifying something as a disease is ineliminably a value judgment; and hybrid views (harmful dysfunction and its cousins) that require both a dysfunction and a disvalued condition. Over dinner with a colleague on the committee, I proposed a distinction that I now want stress-tested. Call a classification dispute philosophically load-bearing if complete agreement on all the empirical facts would still leave the parties disagreeing, because what divides them is the concept of disease itself; call it fact-bound if agreement on the science would force convergence under any mainstream theory of disease; and call it politics-bound if the real action lies in who bears the costs and benefits of the label, whatever theory of disease one holds. Here is the slate the committee faces in the next cycle: whether ordinary grief after bereavement should ever be classifiable as depression; whether aging itself, or specific age-related declines such as sarcopenia, should count as a disease; whether infertility should retain its disease status; whether deafness should be framed as a disorder at all, given the Deaf community's objections; whether obesity's classification as a disease (as the American Medical Association adopted in 2013) is defensible; and whether severe chronic fatigue belongs in the manual as a discrete entity. Please rank these six by how urgently they deserve one of my scarce conceptual-review slots, using my load-bearing distinction as the primary currency but correcting or refining it where it fails.

For each case, I want to know: which of my three categories it falls into and why; whether the naturalist, the normativist, and the hybrid theorist would actually deliver different verdicts on it (genuine disagreement among the theories being evidence that the case is load-bearing); what would in practice settle the dispute if the conceptual question were simply ignored; and what new evidence or argument would move the case up or down your ranking. Present the ranking itself as a table with exactly these columns: Rank; Contested classification; Dispute type (load-bearing, fact-bound, or politics-bound); Would the three theories diverge on the verdict? (yes or no, with a one-sentence gloss); What actually settles it in practice; What would change its rank. After the table, add one or two paragraphs defending the ordering: the criteria you used beyond my distinction, the trade-offs among them (for instance, stakes for patients versus the committee's real leverage over outcomes), and the single case in which you found my framework least adequate.
