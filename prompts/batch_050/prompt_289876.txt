I'm putting together the reading list for a grad reading group on decision theory, and this term we're doing the whole "can you assign probabilities to your own options while you're still deliberating?" mess. Here's what I've already got pencilled in as the central exhibits, roughly in the order I want to do them:

1. Levi's argument that deliberation and self-prediction can't coexist — if you genuinely assigned a credence to what you'll do, the deliberative question would already be settled for you, so to deliberate at all you have to treat your own act as outside the scope of your beliefs. Deliberation crowds out prediction, basically.
2. Spohn's reversal of that: cases like the creature of habit who knows perfectly well she'll order the vanilla again, or the chess player who can predict he'll take the obvious move — predicting your own act seems perfectly compatible with still deciding it, so maybe it's prediction that crowds out deliberation rather than the other way round.
3. The smoker's lesion and Egan's psychopath button — cases where your choice is evidence about what kind of person you are, so you seemingly have to reason about your own act evidentially mid-deliberation, and flatly refusing to do so gets you the wrong answer.
4. The tickle defense as a general diagnosis of why those cases mislead: the correlations that tempt the evidential reasoning get screened off once you know what you're inclined to do.
5. Newcomb as the stress test — whatever you say about self-prediction has to survive a case where a predictor has already read you, and where "deliberation crowds out prediction" runs headfirst into the prediction crowding out your deliberation.

Is there another argument or case in this debate that I'm missing? It should be genuinely different from these — not just another medical-cause case in the style of the psychopath button, not another habit case, and not a repackaging of Newcomb with different window dressing. It also shouldn't just be the generic point that you can be uncertain about your own future behavior (obviously you can be, when you're not deliberating about it) — the interesting question is about credences *during* deliberation. And then tell me honestly whether your new exhibit actually holds up under scrutiny, because I'd rather not burn a week of the seminar on something that collapses on inspection.
