Bit of background: I've somehow ended up as the "data governor" at my daughter's school (volunteered at the AGM when nobody else put their hand up, instantly regretted it) and I keep having the same argument with the head about whether you trust the big headline figure or the broken-down figures. The annoying thing is my gut gives different answers in different situations and I cannot articulate the rule. So here are my verdicts, please find me the principle, if there is one. British English in your answer please.

Case 1 (the one that started this): our school's pass rate fell from about 72% to 68% this year and a parent is up in arms. But the rate rose within every prior-attainment band; it's just that this year's intake had far more pupils in the lower bands. My verdict: the school didn't get worse, the per-band numbers are the truth, the headline is misleading.

Case 2: a physio trial a friend told me about. Treatment A: 90 of 100 mild cases recover, 450 of 900 severe cases recover. Treatment B: 765 of 900 mild, 40 of 100 severe. So A wins inside both groups but its overall figure (54%) looks dreadful next to B's (~80%), because A got lumbered with the severe patients. My verdict: A is the better treatment, split the data.

Case 3: a painkiller that works by reducing inflammation. Overall it cuts chronic pain substantially. But if you split patients by their inflammation level after treatment, it looks useless within every level. My verdict: do NOT split here, the headline is right, because the inflammation is the mechanism, splitting by it is daft.

Case 4: during an outbreak, among people admitted to hospital, those who'd been masking at home had worse outcomes, because maskers only ended up admitted if they were already quite ill. My verdict: again don't split, the headline is the honest one.

Case 5, the one I keep flip-flopping on: a hiring tool at my actual work. Overall, women pass at a higher rate than men. But within each department women pass at a lower rate, because women apply disproportionately to the most competitive departments. Is the tool biased? I honestly can't settle which number answers that.

My working rule so far has been "when in doubt, split the data by whatever you can measure" but I'm fairly sure cases 3 and 4 break it. So: what principle (stated precisely, not just "it depends on the causality") captures my verdicts in 1-4, where does it leave my working rule, what does it say about case 5, and what does it predict for a case I haven't raised: a gym comparing two training programmes where members chose their own programme, the raw numbers favour the one the younger members picked, and adjusting for age flips the result? If the principle forces me to abandon one of my verdicts above, tell me which one rather than quietly fudging it.
