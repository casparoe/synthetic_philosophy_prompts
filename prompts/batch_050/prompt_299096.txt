For a while now I've been turning over an idea I first ran into in popular writing about AI: that you can work out what a person wants by patiently watching what they do — engineers call a version of this inverse reinforcement learning, and it also seems to be how friends come to know each other. I've grown doubtful of it, since people constantly choose what they don't actually want and perform for whoever happens to be watching. Kazuo Ishiguro's Klara and the Sun strikes me as built on exactly this tension: Klara's entire way of loving Josie is observation, and Josie's mother eventually asks whether a person could be learned from the outside well enough to be continued after death. I'd like a reading of the novel against this idea. Where does watching succeed in the book, and where does it fail — Klara's misreading of the rough games between Josie and Rick, her conviction that the Sun is a someone with wishes who can be bargained with, the parents who have their children genetically edited largely, it seems, because everyone else does, which makes me wonder what their choices even reveal? Does the novel take a position on whether there is something inside a person that no amount of observation could capture, and is Mr. Capaldi its answer to that question? Where does the analogy strain — Klara hardly seems neutral about what she watches, since she arrives already devoted — and what does the novel understand about knowing people through their behavior that the engineering version of the idea is bound to miss?
