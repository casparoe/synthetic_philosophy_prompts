ok background first so the question makes sense. there's a classic psychophysics experiment, the color phi phenomenon: a red dot flashes on the left side of a screen, then a couple hundred ms later a green dot flashes on the right. subjects don't report two dots — they report one dot moving left to right that changes from red to green about halfway along its path. the weird part: you see the change to green at the midpoint, i.e. before the green dot has actually appeared. so how did your visual system "know" what was coming?

dennett, in his writing on the timing of consciousness, says there are two ways to tell the story. stalinesque, like a show trial scripted in advance: the brain buffers everything for a beat and only then assembles the final experience, so the experience you actually had always contained the switch to green. orwellian, like 1984 rewriting the archives: you really did experience a red dot traveling all the way across, and then the instant the green flash arrived your memory of the last fraction of a second was rewritten, so you misremember. his verdict is that once the two stories are packed this close together in time there's no fact of the matter about which one happened, because there's no inner theater where the "real" experience played before any revision got made.

now, my roommate read this and has been insufferable for a week because he thinks it proves way more. his arguement:

(1) the only difference between the orwellian and stalinesque stories is whether the experience happened and then got revised, or got assembled late in the first place.
(2) nothing could ever settle which is right — same verbal reports, same button presses, same brain data down at that resolution, and a subject who swears they remember it clearly is exactly what both stories predict.
(3) when two hypotheses agree on every possible observation, there's no fact of the matter about wich is true.
(4) so there's no fact of the matter about when the color switch was experienced.
(5) but nothing in (1)-(4) depended on the timescale. for ANY experience you can give an orwellian redescription where the experiencing happened and the record was written a moment later. so there is never a fact of the matter about when any experience happens, only facts about when records get written.
(6) so there's no fact of the matter about whether i'm conscious right now, as opposed to a freshly written memory of a "now" that never actually got experienced. the present moment of experience is a postdated fabrication, all the way down.

he says (6) is obviously true and i should just get used to it. i am not getting used to it. something between (4) and (6) has to give, but he's right that i can't just appeal to "what it felt like," since that's the thing under dispute.

which step do you reject? i'd most like to keep (3) if i can, since it seems like the only load-bearing step that's a general principle about evidence rather than a claim about brains, and i'd very much like to keep the difference between experiencing something now and remembering it a second later. what does rejecting your chosen step cost me? and if your diagnosis is that the generalization in (5) is where it breaks, then you owe me an account of what extra fact exists at the one-second timescale that's missing at the 200ms one — without smuggling back in the inner theater dennett is gunning for.

one more thing: be straight about what's actually settled here versus what's contested. the postdiction results themselves seem solid, but i have a feeling dennett's "no fact of the matter" verdict is not exactly the consensus among philosophers of mind, so don't present it as one if it isn't.
