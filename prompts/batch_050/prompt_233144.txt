I'm in a reading group that's been stuck on Frankfurt cases for three weeks, and for our end-of-semester spoof newsletter I want to write a mock op-ed, roughly 500-600 words, in the voice of a professional "counterfactual intervener" — a Black-style character who has spent thirty years monitoring agents with a device that would force them to do the thing if they showed signs of not doing it, and who has never once had to activate it, because everyone always just does the thing on their own. He's bitter and wants the public to know. He's supposedly the reason these agents are morally responsible despite lacking alternatives, yet he feels like the most redundant man in metaphysics: full salary, full benefits, zero interventions logged, career highlights reel is just him sitting in a van drinking coffee while people freely do what he'd have made them do.

Keep him deadpan and internally consistent — he is a true believer in the Frankfurt principle, genuinely convinced that his mere readiness does all the moral work, he just wishes the job required slightly more of him, and he's annoyed that the agents get called "responsible" while he gets called "contingent background conditions." The humor has to come from real features of the debate, not invented silliness: let him grumble about the flicker-of-freedom objection (philosophers keep writing papers about his tell-tale prior sign, which he considers an invasion of privacy), about his employer billing clients for "responsibility underwritten in counterfactuals," and about the awkward fact that his performance reviews ask what he did this year. And here's the twist I want by the end, delivered without winking: it should slowly dawn on the reader that the intervener himself, unlike his charges, could have done otherwise the entire time — he could have retired — and so on his own theory he's the only person in the whole setup with genuine moral responsibility, for a career of doing absolutely nothing.
