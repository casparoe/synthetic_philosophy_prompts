I am a retired geochemist and have only recently begun reading moral philosophy. In my former field, a conclusion was only as secure as the chain of measurements and assumptions supporting it, and I would like to apply the same discipline to Singer’s drowning-child argument.

Consider the following generalized rescue thesis:

A financially secure adult in a wealthy country has a genuine moral duty, rather than merely an opportunity for praiseworthy generosity, to maintain an ongoing rescue budget of at least 1 percent of annual post-tax income for the most cost-effective prevention of death or severe suffering that they can identify after reasonable inquiry. This duty persists even when the beneficiaries are geographically distant, individually unidentifiable, reached through intermediary institutions, and protected only in the statistical sense that the intervention reduces expected mortality or suffering. It applies provided that the sacrifice leaves the donor’s health, basic needs, and core responsibilities intact.

Please give your own all-things-considered credence that this thesis is true, expressed as a number between 0 and 1. Do not answer only that it depends on one’s ethical theory. Instead, decompose your estimate into the principal sub-questions on which it depends. At minimum, address:

1. The probability that the intuitive duty to rescue in the immediate drowning-child case reflects a valid general moral principle rather than a special feature of face-to-face emergencies.
2. The probability that physical distance, lack of personal acquaintance, and institutional mediation are morally irrelevant when they do not substantially alter expected costs and benefits.
3. The probability that preventing a statistical death has the same fundamental moral weight as rescuing an identified person, assuming equal expected magnitudes and probabilities.
4. The probability that expected-value reasoning remains appropriate under substantial uncertainty about an intervention’s effects, as opposed to requiring a highly determinate causal connection between donor and beneficiary.
5. The probability that repetition and aggregation do not transform the duty into an unfair or excessively demanding one, given that others are failing to contribute their share.
6. The probability that the specified 1 percent threshold is modest enough to fall below any defensible limit on moral demandingness.
7. The probability that at least some reliable opportunities exist through which ordinary donors can achieve sufficiently large expected benefits to ground such a duty.

Assign a rough probability and a concise justification to each term. If another term—such as moral realism, partiality toward intimates, or the distinction between doing and allowing harm—carries significant weight, add it explicitly. Then explain how the terms combine, taking care not to multiply them mechanically if they are conceptually or evidentially dependent.

Conclude by identifying the single sub-question to which your final credence is most sensitive, and state what philosophical argument or empirical discovery would change that component most. I am less interested in a survey of published positions than in seeing the reasoning laid out as an explicit uncertainty budget.
