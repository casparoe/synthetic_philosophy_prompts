Novelist here, working on an alternate history set in Song-dynasty China, and I keep tripping over something that might be more your department than mine.

Everytime someone hears the premise they ask some version of: so why didn't China industrialize first? They had coal, printing, iron, a huge internal market. And I've noticed something odd about the question itself. Nobody asks why the Aztecs didn't build steam engines, or why medieval France didn't. The question only seems to get asked about cases that came "close," and the closeness does something sneaky: it turns industrialization into the thing that was supposed to happen, so China becomes a failure that needs explaining, rather than Europe being the freak accident that needs explaining. Same move in my own genre, honestly -- alternate histories almost always branch off from battles and elections, almost never from "what if the anomalous thing that actually happened just... hadn't."

I've poked at the China literature a bit and people do grumble that the question is malformed, but the grumbling always seems welded to China specifically. What I want to know is whether there's a general concept here: a name for the way a "why didn't X happen" question smuggles in the assumption that X was the default trajectory, and picks its targets on that basis. Is this a recognized failure mode of historical questions, or of why-questions in general? If it only half-fits existing notions, tell me where it diverges from each -- a bare label won't help me. And if nobody's carved it out, what would you call it, and where are its edges: when is a "why not" question perfectly fine, and when is it rigged?
