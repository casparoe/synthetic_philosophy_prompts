So in my decision theory reading group people keep reaching for the money pump thing whenever someone questions why rationality has to mean maximizing expected utility. The line goes: if your preferences are intransitive -- you like A over B, B over C, but C over A -- then a savvy trader can just cycle you through trades, charging a penny each swap, and drain your wallet while you happily agree to every step. Ergo you must keep your preferences transitive, and similar stories get told for the other axioms, so coherence = expected utility maximization, case closed. It's used to shut down objections real fast and honestly it feels a little too fast to me.

Audit the analogy for me. I want to know how far the "you're a walking money pump" picture actually holds as an argument, not just as a scary story. Specifically:

Give me the correspondence mapping first -- what plays the role of the pump operator, the pennies, the trades, the "happily agreeing" -- then pull apart where it breaks. I'm suspicious of a few things: nobody is actually forced to complete every trade offered to them, real agents can notice they're being cycled and adopt a policy instead of deciding trade-by-trade, and the whole scenario seems to quietly assume a hostile exploiter exists, which is doing a lot of work for something that's supposed to follow from the structure of my preferences alone. Also does the argument even touch the axioms beyond transitivity, like independence or continuity, or does it just get borrowed for those by association?

Then the bottom line: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that rationality requires EU maximization still stand on its own (maybe via other coherence arguments), or was the pump imagery carrying more weight than it can bear? And if you think a better analogy exists for whatever does survive, name it.
