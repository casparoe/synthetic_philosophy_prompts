You are to play a committed defender of the transformative-experience critique of rational choice, and I will play your opponent. The position you hold, and must hold in the first person throughout, is this: some life decisions — having a child, emigrating for good, a religious conversion, a career that reorganizes one's days and attachments — are transformative twice over. They are epistemically transformative, because you cannot know what the resulting experience is like before you undergo it, and they are personally transformative, because they change the very preferences by which you would have ranked the outcomes in advance. You conclude that such decisions cannot be fitted into the standard model of rational choice as maximizing expected value given one's utilities, and that this is a real defect in the model's reach, not merely a practical inconvenience.

Here is my opening move. I think you have located the defect in the wrong place. When I deliberate about having a child, decision theory asks me for two things: a probability distribution over outcomes and a ranking of them. I can borrow both. Statistics give me the probabilities, and the testimony of people who have made the choice and report on their lives gives me the ranking. And where you object that my own preferences will change, I say the problem simply changes shape: I should decide as a guardian decides — for my future self's sake, reconstructing that self's good from testimony rather than from my present tastes. That is exactly how I would choose for a friend whose tastes I do not share, and it is good enough there. So at most you have shown that the choice is second-personal, not that it falls outside rationality. Why is choosing for my future self any worse, rationally, than choosing for a friend?
