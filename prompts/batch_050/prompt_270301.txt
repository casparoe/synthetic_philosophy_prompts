I work as a software engineer and, as a side project, record interviews for a small series on the philosophy behind how technical teams form and justify beliefs. My next guest is a philosopher who defends William James's ethics of belief, as set out in 'The Will to Believe' (1896), against Clifford-style evidentialism. In the preface to the volume containing that essay, James concedes: "I quite agree that what mankind at large most lacks is criticism and caution, not faith. Its cardinal weakness is to let belief follow recklessly upon lively conception, especially when the conception has instinctive liking at its back." Draft eight interview questions. One should press the guest on how she squares this concession with the essay's licence for passional belief; the rest should probe the 'genuine option' criteria (live, forced, momentous), the charge that the view dignifies wishful thinking, and whether the argument licenses faith-like attitudes in mundane cases an engineer would recognise, such as a founder's confidence in an unproven product. Each question must include the reply she is most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to that reply. Present the output as a table with five columns: No. (question number); Question (as I would ask it); Pressure point (the specific commitment it targets); Anticipated reply (one or two sentences); Follow-up (the question I ask next). Use British English throughout.
