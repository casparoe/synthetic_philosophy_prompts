So I retired two years ago after forty years as a hydrologist, and one of the things I do now is walk every Tuesday with an old friend, a retired contracts lawyer, and argue about philosophy, which I have gotten way too into. Lately we've been going back and forth on whether a company itself can deserve blame, over and above the people in it. I've noticed she's very consistent, and I've started keeping a little notebook of how she calls cases (I know how that sounds, but retirement is long). I want to test whether I've actually got her number.

Here's her track record, as near as I can reconstruct it:

Case 1. A mining company's board, in properly minuted meetings, decides to postpone roof-support work for two quarters to protect the share price. Collapse, injured miners. Her verdict: the company is blameworthy, full stop, not just the directors as individuals. That one seemed easy.

Case 2. A retail bank's head office sets sales targets that anyone in the industry could see would push staff toward opening fake accounts. Nobody orders any fraud. Internal audit reports flagging weird numbers keep landing and getting ignored at regional level. Her verdict: blameworthy, even though you can't point to a single person who knew the whole picture and chose it.

Case 3. A haulage firm has an explicit, well-enforced policy on driver rest breaks. One dispatcher, acting against it and covering his tracks carefully, tells drivers to skip breaks. Crash. Her verdict: the dispatcher is to blame, the company is not — it did roughly what a company can reasonably do. I pushed her on this and she held firm, which surprised me a bit.

Now the new case I want to spring on her next Tuesday, and I want your prediction first so I can compare. A logistics firm installs a routing-and-scheduling software package, the industry standard one, the same one its competitors run without incident. Over eighteen months the software's optimization defaults, combined with drivers' own choices about squeezing in extra jobs, produce chronic severe fatigue across the fleet. The company's telematics dashboard was quietly recording the whole trend the entire time — the data was all there, aggregated, sitting in the system — but nobody's job description included watching that particular screen, and no company policy produced the situation: no one decided anything, it's just what the defaults plus normal human behavior added up to. A driver falls asleep and kills someone.

Will she call the company blameworthy? I genuinely can't tell, and I've noticed my own waffling depends on which general principle I think she's been applying, and her three verdicts seem to fit at least two different principles equally well. So here's what I want, and please be decisive — don't just lay out the menu and tell me it's a matter of taste, actually put your money down somewhere:

1. Her likely verdict — one short paragraph, a straight yes or no on blameworthiness, no hedging.
2. The rules that fit her record — the longest section, maybe three or four paragraphs. Lay out the two (or more) general principles that are each consistent with all three verdicts, show how they diverge on the new case, and note which tempting rules her record rules OUT (I think the case 3 verdict is doing real work there).
3. Which rule I'd back — a paragraph or two saying which principle you think best captures her, with reasons, and therefore what she'll say Tuesday.
4. The case that would settle it — one short paragraph describing a new hypothetical that would cleanly tell your favored rule apart from the runner-up, since my new case apparently can't.

One last thing: I'm not a lawyer or a philosopher, so skip the name-dropping and the citations, I won't be able to check them anyway. Occassionally I can follow the jargon but I'd rather you just say the thing plainly.
