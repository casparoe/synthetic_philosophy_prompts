In a seminar on normative ethics, I argued that it can be permissible to break a promise in order to prevent several other people from breaking theirs, and a classmate shut me down with fiat justitia ruat caelum -- let justice be done though the heavens fall. I am unsure what exactly that slogan commits one to, and I suspect it is doing more work than any single principle could bear. Lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan might be expressing: for example, that constraints bind agents rather than describe states of affairs to be minimized, that the badness of my violating a duty differs in kind from the badness of others' violations, that justice is lexically prior to all other values, or merely that one should not let anticipated consequences figure in one's deliberation at all. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or untenable, and whether it actually delivers my classmate's verdict that I may not break the promise even to reduce total promise-breaking. Finally, identify which reading makes the position most vulnerable to the charge that deontology cares more about the agent's own record than about the values the duties protect, and indicate which version of the slogan, if any, a sophisticated deontologist should actually endorse.
