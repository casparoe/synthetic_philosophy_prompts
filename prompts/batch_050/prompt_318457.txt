"The split-brain experiments carried out on patients who had undergone corpus callosotomy as a treatment for severe epilepsy strongly suggest that consciousness can be divided within one skull. In the classic experimental setup, when a word is flashed to the patient's left visual field, it is processed by the right hemisphere, and the patient cannot say what it was, since speech production is controlled by the left hemisphere. Yet the patient can pick out the correct object with their left hand. This shows there are two streams of consciousness with no informational connection between them. Thomas Nagel, discussing these cases, concluded that the operation creates two minds, since each hemisphere appears to have its own experiences and intentions. This is supported by the patients' everyday behaviour, in which the two halves of the body regularly work against each other, for instance one hand doing up a button while the other undoes it. Derek Parfit drew a similar lesson, arguing that the divided mind cases show there are really two persons present after the operation, which he took to support his view that personal identity is not what matters in survival. The upshot is that the unity of consciousness, which philosophers like Descartes treated as indivisible, turns out to be empirically fragile."
