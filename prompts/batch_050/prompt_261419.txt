I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the history of ethics, and we're in a unit on Augustine's two treatises on lying. My students can all recite the headline view -- that lying is always wrong, even to save an innocent life, and that Augustine ranks lies by their object and circumstance -- but when I press them in discussion it becomes clear they've absorbed the conclusion without the machinery. They treat Augustine as a rule-worshipper who never considered hard cases, which is exactly backwards: the treatises are built around hard cases, including someone hiding in your house whom murderers are hunting, and a lie told to keep a gravely ill patient from despairing. Augustine engages these and bites the bullet, and I want an exam question that forces students to explain why the bullet-biting is motivated by his theory of what speech is for, rather than by squeamishness or legalism.

So: write one essay question, answerable in about 75 minutes, that tests whether students can reconstruct the argument behind the absolute prohibition -- in particular, the claim that lying is a perversion of language's purpose (words instituted as signs of what is in the speaker's mind), which is what makes lying wrong in itself rather than merely wrong because of its effects, and which is also why Augustine has to distinguish lying from joking, from figurative speech, from keeping silent, and from being mistaken. The question should also make students work with the asymmetry in his treatment of the dilemma cases: why he permits refusing to answer, or answering evasively, but not speaking falsely with intent to deceive -- and whether that line survives contact with the obvious objection that the intent to deceive, not the falsehood, is doing the moral work. A student who only says "Augustine thinks lying is always wrong because God forbids it" should fail the question even if every sentence is true.

Along with the question, give me a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme out of 25. The marking scheme should identify five or six specific moves a top answer must make (for instance: stating the signification account of language as the ground of the prohibition; correctly handling at least one dilemma case on Augustine's own terms rather than substituting a Kantian or utilitarian reconstruction; explaining at least one of the distinctions between lying and adjacent speech acts; raising the strongest objection to the position rather than a straw version). For each move, give the marks it's worth and a one-line note on the way students most commonly botch it. End with a short note on what separates an A answer from a B answer in one or two sentences.

Format: begin directly with the exam question itself -- no preface, no restating of this request -- then the model answer under its own heading, then the marking scheme.
