I'm putting together my position statement for a faculty workshop on migration ethics, and before I circulate it I'd like an audit of whether my commitments actually cohere. Here is where I stand:

1. The allocation of life prospects by birthplace is morally indefensible in principle. Citizenship in a wealthy state is a form of inherited privilege, closer to feudal birthright than to any earned entitlement, and no one consents to where they are born.

2. States nonetheless retain a genuine, conditional right to exclude. If very high levels of immigration would seriously erode the institutions that make liberal justice possible at all — democratic self-governance, a redistributive welfare state, generalized civic trust — then restricting entry is permissible on instrumental grounds, not because the territory belongs to the insiders in any deep sense.

3. Empirically, I'm optimistic: the actual fiscal and cultural costs of much freer migration are modest and manageable, so I expect the conditional exclusion right in (2) would rarely, if ever, be triggered in practice. It's a safety valve, not a working part of the view.

4. Distributive justice has global scope. The principles that govern the distribution of resources and opportunities apply to persons as such; borders have no independent moral significance for distributive questions.

5. I'm a disciplinarian about feasibility: a political principle worth holding must be action-guiding under realistic conditions, which means it cannot demand more than democratic publics could durably be brought to support. Principles that require a moral revolution before they can even be implemented are not recommendations; they're wishes.

6. I accept a Pareto constraint: where an arrangement would make some people better off and no one worse off — for instance, admitting migrants on condition of a substantial entry surcharge or restricted access to certain benefits, so that existing residents are net gainers — it should be adopted over any arrangement that leaves those gains on the table.

My question: does this set hang together? I'm not asking for an overview of the open borders literature or a menu of positions I might take. I want a verdict. Go through the commitments, identify which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be, and be explicit about the difference. For each real tension, state how severe it is, what exactly would have to give, and what the cheapest repair costs the overall view. Then commit: rank the tensions, tell me which single revision would restore the most coherence, and defend that choice rather than leaving me to pick among options. If you think two of my commitments are each individually standard but rest on incompatible motivations, say so directly and say which motivation you think is the one I should keep.
