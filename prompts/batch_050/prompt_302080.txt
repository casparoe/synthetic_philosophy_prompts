Earlier this year I was promoted at the asset management firm where I work as a quantitative portfolio manager, and the salary jump pushed me to take charitable giving seriously for the first time. I set up a recurring donation, started reading about how much one ought to give, and promptly ran headlong into what philosophers call the demandingness objection to consequentialism. Singer's drowning-child case and the strong principle it supports seem to imply that I should keep giving -- and keep giving up comforts, career slack, maybe the career itself -- until further sacrifice would cost me as much as it benefits others. My gut says a moral theory that asks this of me has gone wrong somewhere, but I can't tell whether my gut is tracking a real defect in the theory or just my reluctance to part with money.

Here is what I'd like. My working fluency is in optimization under constraints: objective functions, hard constraints versus penalty terms, drawdown limits, risk budgets, Kelly-style position sizing, the difference between a floor and a target, rebalancing rules. Restate the demandingness debate in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. I mean the whole debate, not just the objection: the maximizing consequentialist's original demand; the objection that this demand is unreasonable or leaves no room for a personal life; and the main families of response -- the concessive moves that cap the demand (satisficing versions, fair-share or rule-based versions), the defiant response that morality really is this demanding and our intuitions are what's off, the suggestion that there is no sharp line between required and supererogatory so the demand fades gradually rather than biting at a threshold, and the appeal to an agent-centered prerogative that lets one weight one's own projects more heavily.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the debate translate cleanly into the language of objectives and constraints, and which parts did you have to distort to get them in? Is there anything the philosophers are arguing about that simply has no counterpart in portfolio terms? I'm particularly suspicious of two possible failure modes: first, that treating the cost-to-me as just another term in a single objective function already concedes the consequentialist's point by the back door, and second, that a hard drawdown constraint looks like an agent-centered prerogative but is really justified differently. Where the translation fails, tell me whether the failure shows my vocabulary is missing something, or whether it shows the original philosophical claim was less precise than it looked.

One request about method: don't smuggle my framework's assumptions into the original positions and then present them as translations. If representing some view requires treating moral value as a utility I maximize, flag that as a distortion rather than letting it pass silently. The residue is the part I most want to read.
