Having retired last year from a career in experimental chemistry, I have been reading formal epistemology with a convert's diligence, and I keep returning to the Dutch book argument for probabilism. What troubles me is a premise that is usually left implicit: the step from an agent's credences violating the probability axioms to a bookie's being able to assemble a package of bets the agent will accept and is certain to lose money on. That step plainly requires some principle connecting what an agent believes to how she bets, and I want a pair of thought experiments that drags this bridge principle into the open by showing that neither direction of the standard argument survives without supplementation.

Please construct the following pair of cases.

Case One — incoherent but unbookable. An agent whose credences over a small set of propositions (a coin toss and at most one further event) demonstrably violate the probability calculus — state the numbers explicitly — yet for whom no bookie can assemble a package of bets the agent will accept that guarantees the bookie a profit. Constraints: the agent must not simply refuse to bet; she must follow a stable, psychologically recognizable betting policy, of a sort an ordinary cautious person might actually adopt, under which she does accept some bets; the policy must not be tailor-made for the example, meaning it must be statable without any reference to Dutch books; stakes are small enough that money tracks utility; and the bookie knows the agent's credences and her policy but nothing about how the events will turn out.

Case Two — coherent but bookable. An agent whose credences satisfy the probability calculus at every moment, yet whom a bookie can still relieve of a guaranteed sum. Constraints: the bookie may possess no information the agent lacks (in particular, he does not know the outcomes in advance); the agent's vulnerability must arise solely from something about her that is publicly known — for instance, an announced policy concerning how she will revise her credences upon receiving evidence; and you must exhibit the actual bets, with prices and stakes, and compute the guaranteed loss.

After the two cases, provide the following, in order:

1. A verification for Case One: enumerate the kinds of package the bookie might offer and show that each either contains a bet the agent's policy leads her to decline or leaves the bookie exposed to a possible loss. Identify exactly which feature of the policy does the protective work, and say whether strengthening the agent's commitments — requiring her to accept any finite set of bets each of which she individually judges fair or favorable — restores her bookability.

2. A verification for Case Two: show that the agent is synchronically coherent at each time, exhibit the book, and compute her loss under every relevant combination of outcomes. State which norm she violates; it should not be a synchronic axiom of the calculus.

3. The bridge principle, stated as a single precise sentence: the strongest principle connecting credences to betting behavior under which the classic argument goes through, followed by your assessment of whether that principle is a norm of rationality, part of the definition of credence, or merely a sociological conjecture about how agents behave.

4. The moral, stated plainly: with the bridge principle made explicit and Case One on the table, what exactly does the Dutch book argument establish? Does incoherence emerge as an epistemic defect, as a prudential defect conditional on an enforced betting regime, or merely as the dramatization of a defect the argument itself never locates?

5. The probabilist's best reply, in one paragraph: how a defender of the argument should attack Case One — presumably by insisting that immunity purchased through a restrictive betting policy does nothing to vindicate the credences themselves — and whether retreating to a non-pragmatic vindication (for instance, an argument that incoherent credences are dominated with respect to accuracy by some coherent set) rescues the conclusion or quietly changes the subject.

The purpose of the exercise, so that you know what counts as cheating: I want to determine whether the Dutch book argument, stripped of its implicit premises, establishes anything about the rationality of credence as such, or only about the liabilities of agents with credences placed in contrived betting situations. A case that stipulates its verdicts rather than generating them from the agent's credences and policy is useless to me; every number should be checkable with pen and paper.
