I'm in an undergrad philosophy seminar on decision theory and this week we're covering transformative experiences (like, choices where the outcome changes what you value, so you can't just weigh the outcomes from your current standpoint). After class two people from my section got into it and honestly I don't know who won.

Context: Sam is deciding whether to take a year off to train as a professional chef. He currently has zero interest in cooking as a career, but everyone he knows who's done the program says it completely rewired them — they came out loving things they used to find boring and can't imagine not having done it. Sam's hesitation is that right now, the life that would result doesn't appeal to him at all.

Maya argued the choice is basically irrational however he makes it. Her reasoning: rational choice means picking what best serves your values, but which values? If he uses his current values, he'll decline — but that answer is rigged, because declining is the only option that keeps those values in charge. If he uses his future values, he'll accept — also rigged, for the mirror-image reason. There's no neutral standpoint, so neither choice can be more rational than the other; he might as well flip a coin.

Jonah said that's too pessimistic. The testimony of the graduates gives Sam perfectly good evidence: lots of people, from lots of starting points, end up glad they did it. That's like a consumer rating — you can rationally buy something you've never experienced based on reviews. So Sam can rationally choose the program without needing to access his future values directly.

Maya replied that the testimony is corrupted. People who go through a transformative experience almost always end up endorsing it, because endorsing it is part of what the transformation does to you — you value what you've become. Nobody comes out saying "I became a person who loves this, and it was a mistake." So the reviews are all five stars no matter what the experience was actually like, and a rating system that can't go below five stars carries no information.

Jonah pushed back: by that logic you'd also have to dismiss everyone who says having kids was worth it, or recovering from depression, or converting to a religion — since in each case the person doing the endorsing was changed by the thing they're endorsing. But we clearly do learn from such testimony all the time, and it would be absurd to treat all of it as worthless. His fallback was: maybe the testimony shows something weaker but still useful — not that the future life is good by Sam's current lights, but that Sam is unlikely to end up regretting the choice, and minimizing future regret is a decision standard that doesn't require picking between current and future values.

Maya's last move (before the prof kicked us out of the room) was that "unlikely to regret it" is itself testimony-shaped and inherits the same corruption — a transformed person doesn't regret the transformation because the transformation installed the values that make it look good. Future-regret minimization only works if the future self's verdicts are independent evidence, and that's exactly what's in doubt. Jonah said something like "then by your standard nothing counts as evidence and you've defined rational choice out of existence" but it was getting heated and didn't really get resolved.

So: who's right about which specific points here? Is Maya's "no neutral standpoint" argument sound, or does Jonah's testimony move actually get around it? And does his future-regret fallback survive her corruption objection or is she right that it smuggles the same problem back in? I'd like a verdict on each exchange, plus whatever you think both of them are missing.
