I'm a grad student and next month I'm presenting at a small workshop that's half practicing type designers and half vision/cognitive science people. My talk defends a claim I got obsessed with: what makes something an 'a' has nothing to do with its shape. My thesis is that letter identity is entirely about the role a mark plays in a writing system -- an 'a' is whatever performs the a-function in that script -- so a circle with a stick, a double-story thing, a scribble, a letterform from some constructed alien alphabet, all of them are genuinely 'a's if they do the a-job, and a perfectly rendered classic 'a' shape that some culture uses to mean the /k/ sound is not an 'a' at all, it's a 'k'. I lean on cases like Cyrillic В (looks like our B, is really a V) and the fact that caps A and lowercase a share almost no geometry.

Prepare me for the Q&A. What objections will they raise, phrased the way a type person would actually phrase them (they'll talk about things like x-height, ductus, contrast axis, readability at small sizes)? Which of their objections are cheap shots and which one actually has teeth? For each objection give me a one-or-two-sentence reply I can deliver live, plus a note on where my own position is softest so I know what to concede graciously vs. dig in on.
