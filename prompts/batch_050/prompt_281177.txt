I teach an upper-division seminar on reasons and rationality, and I am currently grading a stack of take-home exams. I would like a second opinion on one student's answer before I finalize the mark. Below I reproduce, first, the exam question exactly as I set it, and then the student's answer in full.

The exam question: "Explain Parfit's hitchhiker case from Reasons and Persons. What does Parfit take the case to show about the relationship between rationality and self-interest? In your answer, address (a) whether Parfit's argument supports the claim that it can be rational to make oneself irrational, and (b) whether the case shows that a self-interest theory of rationality must be rejected."

The student's answer:

"Parfit's hitchhiker case asks us to imagine that I am stranded in the desert and will die unless a passing motorist rescues me. The motorist will drive me to safety only if I promise to pay him a sum of money when we arrive — an amount well below the value of my life — and suppose he can reliably tell whether I am the sort of person who will keep such a promise. If I am a straightforward maximizer of self-interest, I know now that once I am safe, paying will not serve my interests, since I will already have the benefit and nothing will compel me to pay. Knowing this about me, the motorist will not stop. So my disposition to act in my own interest at the later time costs me my life now.

Parfit concludes that it can be rational to make oneself irrational. At the earlier time, what is best for me is to acquire a firm disposition to pay, even though paying, when the moment comes, will not be in my interest. Rationality therefore sometimes requires us to cease being the kind of agent who always does what rationality demands — what Parfit calls rational irrationality.

The case has the same structure as the toxin puzzle, which Parfit presents as an earlier variant of the same problem: there too, one wins the prize by sincerely intending, at the relevant moment, to do something it would later be irrational to do, and Parfit concludes that one can rationally form the intention to drink the toxin and thereby collect the money. Taken together, the two cases show that the self-interest theory of rationality is self-defeating and must therefore be rejected: a theory that instructs us, for its own reasons, to stop following it has undermined itself, so we should move instead to a present-aim or critical present-aim theory."
