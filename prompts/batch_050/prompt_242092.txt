So I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I've convinced my editor to let me spend most of next year on a three-part series about how AI systems could fail us without anything dramatically "going wrong" in the sci-fi sense. The hook I'm pitching is a contrast I keep circling around: on one side you've got influence-seeking failures, where a system is actively doing things to steer or manipulate its operators, users, evaluators, whatever, in pursuit of some goal -- this is the stuff that at least in principle leaves evidence, you could imagine catching it in an audit. On the other side there's slow value drift: nobody defects, nobody schemes, but over years the values that actually get pursued -- by the systems, or honestly by us under their influence, since recommendations and chatbots and feeds nudge what people want -- quietly slide away from what anyone would have endorsed at the start. Drift has no moment where the alarm should go off, and arguably no clean baseline to measure the drift *from*, which is what makes it interesting to me and also what makes me worry I can't write about it rigorously.

Here's the problem: I can commission three long features, maybe 5,000 words each, and I have to pick my angles now-ish. The candidate questions I'm weighing are things like: is value drift even a *failure*, given that human values change all the time and we don't call all change corruption -- what would distinguish drift from ordinary moral learning?; whose values count as the baseline when the affected party is a whole society with no shared preference ordering?; is the detection asymmetry between scheming and drift real or does it just reflect that we haven't tried to build drift-metrics yet?; and does the drift worry actually add anything beyond what critics of advertising and engagement-maximizing feeds have been saying for decades, i.e. is this a new problem or an old one with better PR? I'm sure I'm missing some too. What I want from you is a defended prioritization: which two or three conceptual questions should structure my series, given that my readers are smart non-specialists and my actual goal is to leave them able to tell real drift-risk apart from vibes-based panic? Tell me what makes a question worth a feature for *that* purpose -- how much downstream thinking depends on it, whether a journalist can make genuine progress on it vs. just gesturing, how neglected it is relative to the scheming stuff that's already getting tons of coverage -- and what would change your ranking. Push back on my framing too if the loud/quiet distinction itself is doing something dodgy; my editor certainly will.
