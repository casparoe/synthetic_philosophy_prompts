In the evolutionary psychology course I'm TAing, the textbook introduces its central picture by way of a well-known comparison: the mind is like a Swiss Army knife. Just as the knife is not one all-purpose blade but a set of tools -- a corkscrew for corks, scissors for cutting, a saw for wood, each shaped for a distinct job -- the mind is said to be a bundle of specialized mechanisms, each designed by natural selection to solve a specific recurrent problem our ancestors faced: detecting cheaters, choosing mates, avoiding contaminants, tracking kin, reading intentions. The textbook leans on the analogy hard. It uses it to argue that looking for general-purpose learning mechanisms is a mistake (you wouldn't look for the knife's "general cutting-and-opening faculty"), that apparent failures of reasoning in experiments are really successes when the task is reframed in the terms the relevant tool evolved to handle (a corkscrew is not defective for being bad at sawing), and that critics who complain the theory is unfalsifiable are confused in the way someone would be who demanded a single explanation of what the knife as a whole is for.

Several students pushed back, and one offered a rival image: the mind is more like a hand than a Swiss Army knife -- one extremely flexible structure that grips, throws, types, and gestures, none of which it was separately built for, its usefulness lying precisely in not being a collection of dedicated tools. The textbook's author would presumably reply that a hand is itself one of the knife's tools, not an alternative to the knife.

I'd like a proper audit of both analogies. First, give the correspondence mapping explicitly for each: what in the mind is supposed to map onto the blades, the spring, the handle, the factory that assembled it, the knife's designer, the tasks it was purchased for -- and what in the hand analogy maps onto the fingers, the opposable thumb, the grip. Second, separate the disanalogies that matter for the work the textbook makes the knife analogy do from the ones that don't. For instance: a real Swiss Army knife has a designer with intentions, while selection has none; the knife's tools were made for their tasks, while a trait can be selected for one thing and now do another; the knife contains no general-purpose blade, but does the analogy merely illustrate that point or quietly assert it?; and the knife's tools don't compete or interfere with each other, whereas the putative modules notoriously do. Which of these, if any, undercut the three conclusions above? Third, does the hand analogy fare better, or does it just relocate the dispute -- since the defender of the knife picture can grant a flexible general mechanism and simply add it as one blade among many? Finally: if neither analogy can carry the argumentative weight placed on it, is there a better comparison for what evolutionary psychology actually claims, or is the request for a picture of the mind itself part of the problem?
