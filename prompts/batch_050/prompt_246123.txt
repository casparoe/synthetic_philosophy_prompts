I've been a reader of Overcoming Bias and LessWrong since the late 2000s, and one episode I've watched get discussed in those circles — usually, in my view, somewhat sloppily — is the 2016 controversy over the COMPAS recidivism-risk algorithm used in US criminal sentencing and bail decisions. For anyone who needs a refresher: a ProPublica analysis argued the tool was racially biased because Black defendants who did not go on to reoffend were labeled high-risk at roughly twice the rate of white defendants who did not reoffend (a false-positive-rate disparity); the company behind the tool responded that its scores were calibrated, meaning that among defendants assigned a given risk score, the fraction who actually reoffended was about the same across racial groups. The subsequent technical literature — the impossibility results associated with Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan and with Chouldechova — showed that when base rates differ across groups, calibration and equalized false-positive/false-negative rates cannot generally be satisfied simultaneously, except in degenerate cases.

Here is what I'd like: analyze this episode as a case study of the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness, running the analysis in both directions. First, map the episode onto the theorems carefully: which fairness criteria were actually at stake in each side's public argument, whether the two camps were really disagreeing about values or about what the numbers showed, and whether the impossibility result, properly understood, adjudicates their dispute or merely reframes it. Second, push in the other direction and ask what the episode reveals about the limits of the impossibility framework itself. Some things I suspect it might miss: the theorems take group base rates as given rather than as partly products of the very enforcement practices the scores feed into; they treat the fairness criteria as properties of a predictor rather than of an entire decision procedure in which a human judge can discount the score; and they are silent on whether the criteria people reach for in public argument (like false-positive parity) are even the ones those same people would endorse on reflection. Tell me where my suspicions are right and where they're confused.
