A reading group I'm part of has been working through the alignment literature, and last week we spent the evening on inner alignment and goal misgeneralization. One member defended what I've since realized is a very common move in these discussions: she argued that human beings are a case study in inner misalignment that we can inspect from the inside. Evolution, she said, "optimized" us for inclusive genetic fitness, but what we actually came to care about are proxies that correlated with fitness in the ancestral environment — calorie-dense food, sex, status, social approval, compelling stories. Once the environment shifted, the proxies came apart from the objective: we now gorge on sugar, compete for meaningless prestige markers, and use contraception while fully understanding that doing so frustrates the very "goal" we were ostensibly built for. Her conclusion was that goal misgeneralization is not a paranoid contingency dreamed up by pessimists but the default outcome of optimization: any sufficiently powerful optimization process directed at a base objective tends to produce agents whose internal objectives merely correlate with it, and the correlation breaks under distribution shift. So we should expect trained AI systems to end up pursuing proxy goals that fail off-distribution, however carefully the reward is specified.

I'd like an audit of how far this analogy actually holds. Please give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the base optimizer, the base objective, the training distribution, the learned mesa-objective, the deployment environment, and where does the contraception case sit — is it meant to be a treacherous turn, a benign capability success paired with goal failure, or something else? Then take the mapping apart. Which correspondences are clean and which are strained? Some strains I suspect, but I want them developed properly: evolution has no loss function, no gradient, and no credit assignment; it shifts allele frequencies across generations rather than updating the parameters of a learning mind; much of what humans value is acquired within a lifetime through learning, culture, and reflection, which seems to introduce a second optimization process the ML side lacks; and inclusive fitness itself may be a bookkeeping device for describing selection rather than anything selection literally maximizes.

Finally, tell me whether the conclusion survives the repairs. Which of these disanalogies actually matter for the inference my reading group member wants — that misgeneralization is the default, not the exception — and which are merely cosmetic mismatches between two very different processes? And if the evolutionary analogy can't carry that weight on its own, is there a better analogy available for the same conclusion (say, employees incentivized on a legible metric, or the letter versus the spirit of a law), and does the better analogy support her pessimism more honestly, or does it reveal that the pessimism needs a different argument altogether?
