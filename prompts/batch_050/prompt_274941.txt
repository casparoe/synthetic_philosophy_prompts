I'm a postdoc in a philosophy of cognitive science program, and next month I'm giving a colloquium talk defending a realist thesis about interpretability research on large language models: roughly, that when interpretability researchers decompose a model's activations into sparse directions and label them things like "a feature for Golden Gate Bridge references" or "a feature for deceptive intent," those features are genuine internal representations the model is really using, not artifacts projected onto it by our decomposition tools. My core argument runs through explanatory indispensability: steering a model by clamping a feature direction produces reliable, specific behavioral changes, features recur across independently trained models, and circuits built from features predict behavior on held-out inputs better than any competing description we have, so the features earn the same kind of ontological commitment that neurons or transcription factors do. The audience is the problem: it's a general philosophy colloquium, heavy on philosophers of mind trained on the classic intentionality literature (people who think content requires more than causal covariation), a couple of philosophers of neuroscience who will want to know whether this is just the single-neuron doctrine warmed over, one enactivist, and exactly one person who has actually trained a neural network and will ask the technical questions. Most of the room has never opened an interpretability paper and knows the field only through headlines.
