I run a small alignment discussion group at work, and over the past few months I've found myself defending a position that my colleagues keep treating as obviously doomed. The position is this: "avoid negative side effects" can be operationalized for an AI agent without anything close to a full specification of human values, by penalizing deviations from some counterfactual baseline — say, what the world would have looked like had the agent done nothing, or had it repeatedly taken some inaction-like default — where deviation is measured in a coarse, task-agnostic currency such as the preserved attainability of a wide range of auxiliary goals, or the relative reachability of states. My claim is not that any current proposal is correct in detail, but that this family of approaches — baseline plus coarse, value-agnostic deviation metric — is fundamentally the right shape for the problem, and that its difficulties are engineering difficulties rather than signs that the shape is wrong.

Tell me what would count against this position. I want a map of its exposure, not just a list of standard objections. Which observed or constructible agent behaviors would show that the whole baseline-plus-deviation shape is misguided rather than merely mis-tuned — for instance, the incentive to offset one's own effects by quietly un-doing side effects in ways that are themselves harmful, the incentive to prevent others from changing the world so the baseline stays close (including preventing genuinely good changes), the dependence of "impact" on how states and options are represented, or the fact that an agent can be arbitrarily low-impact and still catastrophic if its task itself requires changing things. For each candidate defeater, say whether it would actually refute the position as I've stated it — the shape is right, the details are wrong — or only show that some particular instance of the family fails. I'd also like to know what a committed defender of impact measures would themselves concede as counting against the view, versus what they would dismiss as attacking a strawman or as a problem every approach shares (including the alternative of trying to specify values directly). Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and note where the position turns out to be so insulated by its vagueness — "impact," "baseline," and "deviation" can each be redefined in response to counterexamples — that nothing could count against it, and whether that should worry me.

Finally, a meta-question: suppose no single behavioral counterexample is decisive, because each can be patched by a cleverer baseline or metric. Is there a cumulative form of evidence — a pattern across many patches, or a formal result about what any such measure must leave out — that would and should move someone in my position? What would the world of impact-measure research have to look like in ten years for my colleagues to have been right?
