I'm a teaching assistant for an undergraduate bioethics seminar, and this week I need to grade midterm essays on advance directives and the authority of the earlier self. The essay question I set was:

"In Life's Dominion, Ronald Dworkin argues that advance directives refusing life-sustaining treatment should govern even after the patient has become happily demented. Explain Dworkin's argument, including the role played by his distinction between experiential and critical interests and by the idea of precedent autonomy. Then present the strongest objection to his view — in particular, the worry that the demented person is no longer the self who wrote the directive — and assess whether Dworkin's argument survives it. Conclude with your own considered position."

Below is one student's answer, which I'd like you to grade out of 20, with specific point-by-point comments justifying the mark. Please identify what the answer gets right, where it is imprecise or mistaken (including any conflations, misattributed views, or inferential steps that don't follow), and whether the student's own conclusion is adequately supported. I care less about stylistic polish than about conceptual accuracy, so please distinguish clearly between genuine errors and legitimate interpretive latitude.

STUDENT ANSWER:

Dworkin's argument rests on his distinction between experiential and critical interests. Experiential interests are the ones we have in things we enjoy doing — the pleasure of eating ice cream or watching a sunset. Critical interests, by contrast, are preferences that would survive a process of idealized rational reflection, i.e., the desires we would have if we were fully informed and thinking clearly. Because critical interests are more fundamental to a person's life than experiential ones, Dworkin holds that they should take priority when the two conflict.

From this he develops the idea of precedent autonomy: a competent person's autonomous decisions about their own future should be respected even when that person is no longer competent. A patient in the early stages of Alzheimer's who signs a directive refusing life-prolonging treatment once severely demented is expressing her critical interests, and those interests should govern her care later. Dworkin illustrates his view with the well-known case of Margo, a patient with Alzheimer's who remained content reading novels and listening to music. Since the demented person's experiential interests are, on Dworkin's view, of no moral weight compared to critical interests, the earlier directive should control.

The strongest objection is what we might call the different-self problem, most famously pressed by Derek Parfit in his Russian nobleman case, where Parfit argues that a person's later self should remain bound by the commitments of the earlier self. Applied here, the worry is that the happily demented patient is no longer psychologically continuous enough with the person who signed the directive for that person's authority to extend over her. If the current patient enjoys her life, honoring the directive seems to sacrifice a real, existing person's welfare for the sake of someone who no longer exists.

Dworkin's argument ultimately survives this objection, in my view. Since critical interests are more fundamental than experiential ones, and since the directive expresses the patient's critical interests, it follows that advance directives should always be enforced. To refuse to do so would be to treat the competent self's deepest convictions as revocable merely because time has passed.
