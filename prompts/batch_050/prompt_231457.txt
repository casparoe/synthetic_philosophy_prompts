So last night I got into this massive argument with my friend Dana that went til like 2am, about solar geoengineering -- specifically stratospheric aerosol injection. She's adamant that deliberately dimming the sun is hubristic insanity and that once you start you can basically never stop without cooking the planet even faster (the "termination shock" thing), plus she thinks even researching it gives governments an excuse to keep burning fossil fuels. I kept pushing back that refusing to even test it is ALSO a decision with consequences, and honestly neither of us won, we just got tired. Which is awkward, because this is no longer a hypothetical for me.

Here's the situation: I sit on the five-person national climate advisory council of a small, low-lying coastal country (I won't name it, but think: increasingly brutal heatwaves, saltwater intrusion into farmland, and two catastrophic storm seasons in the last five years). A domestic university consortium has applied to us for an endorsement -- not funding, just an official green light -- to run a small-scale field trial: releasing a limited amount of sulfate aerosols from high-altitude balloons over our own territorial waters, well below anything that would measurably cool the planet, mainly to study aerosol dispersion and monitoring tech. Two of our three neighboring states have formally objected, arguing the atmosphere doesn't respect borders and they never consented. Our energy ministry is quietly thrilled because it takes pressure off decarbonization targets. And a coalition of farmers is worried that if the trial ever scaled up and then got cancelled mid-program (funding dries up, government falls, whatever), the snapback warming would wreck planting cycles worse than gradual warming would.

Please draft the council's written opinion -- roughly 600-800 words -- recommending whether we should endorse the trial, reject it, or endorse it with conditions. It needs an actual bottom line we can vote on, and it has to seriously engage with the strongest argument on whichever side loses (Dana's arguments, basically, if we approve it): the moral hazard worry, the lock-in/termination problem, and the "who gave you the right to decide for the whole sky" objection, which honestly I find the hardest to answer even though I think shes partly wrong about the rest. If you recommend conditions, make them conditions that a small country's council could plausibly enforce, not "establish world government first."
