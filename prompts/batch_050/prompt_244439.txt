Longtime LW/ACX reader here, and I've noticed that whenever resolutions, addiction, or commitment devices come up, someone inevitably says some version of "just tie yourself to the mast." It happened again yesterday in a comment thread under a post about Beeminder, where someone used it to argue that the rational move with an unreliable future self is always to restrict your own options -- and someone else replied that no, the lesson of Ulysses is that you *don't* have to restrict your options if you arrange things right, you get to hear the sirens anyway. So now I'm realizing I have no idea what the slogan is even supposed to mean, because those two people were apparently drawing opposite lessons from the same story.

Can you lay out the different precise claims "tie yourself to the mast" could be making? Some candidates I can half-see: (1) a purely instrumental point that when you expect your preferences at time t2 to diverge from your preferences at t1, it's rational at t1 to constrain your t2 self (but then who decides which self is "you" and gets to do the tying?); (2) a Schelling-ish point that making defection *impossible* rather than merely costly is strategically different, e.g. against temptation or against other agents who'd exploit your weakness of will; (3) a stronger claim that the t1 self is *epistemically privileged* because it's calm and the t2 self is in a "hot" state, so binding is basically protecting your real values from a corrupted future judgment; (4) maybe a reading where the mast-tying isn't about the future self at all but about signaling or credibility to third parties. For each reading, tell me whether it's close to trivial, genuinely substantive, or actually false, and what the reading implies about edge cases -- e.g., if your t1 self is the one that's confused or impulsive, does the slogan tell you anything? Does it license binding someone *else* for their own good?

And then the part I actually care about: which reading, if any, supports the commenter's use, i.e. the general rule "with an unreliable future self, restrict your options"? Because it seems to me that reading might prove way too much -- taken literally it would tell you to never leave yourself any freedom at all, since your future self is always somewhat unpredictable -- and the second commenter's point (Ulysses kept his ears unplugged, only the sailors got the wax) suggests the slogan might actually be about keeping experience while removing *only* the capacity to act on it, which is a much narrower and weirder claim. I'd love the readings as a list I can refer back to, with verdicts, and a note on which ones people in these threads are probably actually gesturing at.
