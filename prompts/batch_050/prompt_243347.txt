Whenever a hard decision comes up -- which job to take, whether to move cities, which of two grant applicants to fund -- everyone around me reaches for the same picture: you "weigh up" the options, reasons "stack up" on each side, one side eventually "tips the scales". I've started leaning on it a lot myself, e.g. telling my brother to weigh up staying in academia against going into industry. But a friend who did some philosophy said the whole weighing metaphor is quietly doing the argumentative work and that hard choices are precisely the cases where there's no scale.

So: audit the weighing analogy properly. Give me the explicit mapping first -- what are the weights (reasons? values?), what is the pan each option sits in, what is the balance point, what is the common unit that lets two very different considerations both register as "weight". Then tell me where it breaks. I'm especially interested in whether the metaphor can even represent cases like this: two careers seem neither better nor worse than each other overall, you sweeten one of them slightly (a bit more money, a nicer office), and it *still* doesn't come out clearly better. On the scale picture that should be impossible -- if they were balanced, adding weight to one side must tip it -- so either they were never balanced (and the analogy forces you into saying one was always slightly better, we just couldn't tell) or the analogy can't express what's going on. Some philosophers (I think Ruth Chang is the name) argue two options can be "on a par" -- related but neither equal nor ranked -- and I want to know if that's just the scale metaphor with extra steps or a genuinely different structure the metaphor hides.

Also: say which features of physical weighing the analogy smuggles in that practical reason may lack -- cardinality, additivity, a single dimension, an objective reading anyone can check -- and which of those smuggled features actually matter for the conclusions people draw with it (like "the reasons clearly favour X" or "it's irrational not to take the better option"). If weighing is the wrong picture, what would be a better one, and does the everyday advice to "weigh it up" survive once the disanalogies are on the table?

Please answer in British English.
