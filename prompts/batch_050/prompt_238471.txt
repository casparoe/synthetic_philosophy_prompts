I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'd like a disciplined analysis of an episode from my own field through the lens of a philosophical text I've been reading. The episode is the abolition of the leap second. The text is Book XI of Augustine's Confessions.

First, the episode, with the facts as I understand them (please treat these as given rather than something to verify). Since 1972, civil time (UTC) has been defined by atomic clocks but kept within 0.9 seconds of UT1, a measure of Earth's actual rotation, by inserting or occasionally removing leap seconds. Because Earth's rotation is irregular and slightly slowing, leap seconds are unpredictable more than about six months out; they cannot be scheduled in advance the way leap days can. This has been a persistent source of software failures: the June 2012 leap second triggered a Linux kernel bug that knocked out major services, and several large internet companies responded by "smearing" the extra second across many hours rather than letting a 61-second minute exist, meaning their clocks silently disagreed with official UTC during the smear. After years of lobbying by tech firms and metrologists, the international bodies responsible agreed in 2022–2023 to let UTC and UT1 drift apart by more than 0.9 seconds starting by 2035, effectively suspending leap seconds for roughly a century. In other words, the world's official civil time is being formally decoupled from the rotation of the planet.

Second, the text. Midway through Book XI of the Confessions, Augustine considers the proposal that time just is the motion of the heavenly bodies, and rejects it: he argues that if the sun stood still, as in the biblical episode where Joshua's battle continued while the sun halted, time would plainly still pass, and that if the lights of heaven stopped moving but a potter's wheel kept turning, we could still measure the wheel's revolutions — so motion can be measured by time but time is not itself any body's motion. He then pushes further: the past no longer exists and the future does not yet exist, so when we "measure time" we cannot be measuring either; what we actually measure is a present impression or distension of the mind — memory of what has happened, attention to what is happening, expectation of what is coming.

Here is what I want. Treat the leap-second controversy as a case study and run the analysis in both directions. On the case-through-theory side: Augustine's Joshua argument looks strikingly like the metrologists' position — Earth's rotation, like the sun's motion, is a lousy and halting clock, so timekeeping should stop slavishly tracking it. Work out, rigorously, what Augustine's reasoning would commit him to say about (a) the pre-2035 system that chained atomic time to a wobbling planet, (b) the leap smear as a private, conventional time, and (c) the final decoupling. Be disciplined about the mapping: say what plays the role of the sun's motion, what plays the role of the potter's wheel, and what plays the role of the mind that measures. On the theory-through-the-case side: Augustine's positive account is first-personal — time is measured in an individual mind, in a present stretched between memory and expectation. But a distributed network of machines is precisely a system with no shared present and no single mind, and the entire leap-second mess arises because we need one public, intersubjective standard. Does the episode expose a gap in Augustine — that his account can explain what a person is doing when she measures an interval, but not what it takes to coordinate billions of clocks — or does it vindicate him, by showing that "civil time" was a constructed convention all along rather than a discovered natural magnitude? Finally, say plainly where the theory is silent and the case resists the mapping, rather than forcing Augustine to have a position on everything. I'd rather have three sentences honestly labeled "the text has nothing to say about this" than a paragraph of strained allegory.
