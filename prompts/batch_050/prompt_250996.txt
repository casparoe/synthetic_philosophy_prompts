Here is the student's answer, verbatim: "The boundary problem is the problem of deciding who gets to be part of the demos, the group entitled to take part in collective self-government. The difficulty is circular: settling membership by a vote requires already knowing who is entitled to vote, which is the very question at issue. Any democratic procedure for fixing the boundaries therefore presupposes a demos, so the boundaries can never themselves be democratically authorized; at best they are settled by historical accident, force, or an undemocratic founding decision. It follows that every actually existing democracy rests on an arbitrary foundation, which means that none of its subsequent decisions can claim full legitimacy either, since the body making them was never legitimately constituted. As for proposed solutions, the all-affected interests principle holds that everyone whose interests are affected by a decision should have a say in it. This principle escapes the regress, because who is affected by a given decision is a straightforward empirical matter that can be determined before and independently of any political choice. Its weakness is over-inclusion: since the effects of decisions spill across borders and generations, the principle seems to enfranchise foreigners and future persons in nearly every polity's affairs, pointing toward a single global demos. The second proposal is the coercion or all-subjected principle, under which everyone subject to a state's coercively enforced laws should be enfranchised. Unlike the all-affected principle, this one confines the demos to those physically within the state's territory, since coercive law is territorially bounded, so it roughly vindicates existing state boundaries. The standard objection is that it over-includes transient subjects, such as tourists, who are briefly subject to a state's laws but plausibly deserve no voice in its long-term decisions."
