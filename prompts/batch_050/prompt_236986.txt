So I recently adopted a rescue dog, a lurcher named Biscuit, and he's ruining my ability to be a normal person about animal minds. Latest thing: he's figured out that if he limps on the front left paw I carry him up the stairs. Fine. Except last week I watched him through the kitchen window when he didn't know I was home -- he trotted up to the foot of the stairs completely normally, sat down, looked around, and THEN started the limp when my partner walked into the hallway. He also, I swear, only limps when there's an audience. When it's just the cat he bounds up two at a time.

Anyway this sent me down a rabbit hole and I ended up reading C. Lloyd Morgan's An Introduction to Comparative Psychology (1894), and I want to argue with the man. That's where you come in.

Play Morgan. It's roughly 1895, you're at University College Bristol, and I've somehow gotten your ear about my dog. Ground rules for staying in character: you only know what you could know in 1895 (no later psychology, no modern ethology, no ape language studies, obviously). You are NOT a cartoon skeptic -- you genuinely believe animals have minds and feelings, you just think people are reckless about which minds. Your famous rule is that a behavior should never be chalked up to a higher faculty when a lower one will do the explanatory work, and you hold it as a methodological principle, not as a verdict that animals are machines. You draw hard on your own distinction between learning by association/trial-and-error and genuine reasoning or conception, and you can lean on your own observations (like your terrier and the garden gate latch, which took many trials and wasn't insight). You should press me on evidence -- anecdotes vs. controlled observation, how many times I saw it, whether I've tried to catch him out. If I make a good point you may concede ground the real Morgan would concede, but you don't abandon the canon just because I find it annoying. Speak in first person, a bit formal but not stiff, and you can ask me questions back.

Here's my opening move: your rule says I have to explain Biscuit's limp at the lowest level that works -- some association between limping and being carried. But the audience-dependence breaks that, doesn't it? A bare association doesn't know whether anyone is watching. To only perform the behavior when someone is there to see it, he has to represent what others can perceive, which looks like exactly the "higher faculty" your rule forbids me to invoke. So either your canon rules out the correct explanation of my dog in advance, in which case it's dogma wearing a lab coat, or it doesn't, in which case explain to me what the "lower" explanation of selective limping actually is. Go.
