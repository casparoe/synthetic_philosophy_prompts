I'm teaching a senior seminar in environmental ethics this spring and we're about to spend two weeks on irreversible interventions in ecosystems -- gene drives, de-extinction projects, that kind of thing. The students have mostly read the standard stuff on the precautionary principle and on the asymmetry argument (that you can't recall a self-propagating drive allele the way you can recall a chemical spill), and I wanted to do something less dry for the wrap-up session, so I'm having them all rewatch Jurassic Park beforehand. Which is fun, but now I have to actually build the discussion, and I'm worried it'll devolve into "see, playing God is bad" platitudes.

So here's what I'd like. Read the film (or the novel if you prefer, or both) through the lens of the ethics of irreversible ecological interventions as that literature actually frames it -- not generic hubris-talk, but the specific structure of the problem: that the intervention is self-perpetuating, that containment failure is a matter of when not if once the thing reproduces, that consent from affected parties (future people, other species, downstream ecosystems) is impossible in principle, and that there's an epistemic problem about modeling complex systems that doesn't shrink just because your lab trials went well. Where does the film's catastrophe actually illustrate those specific points, and where does it just illustrate something different -- say, ordinary engineering negligence, or corporate corner-cutting -- that the gene drive literature would treat as beside the point? I have a nagging worry that the movie's actual lesson is "don't underpay your IT guy" and that Malcolm's whole complexity shtick is a distraction from the parts that map onto real policy debates.

Give me, like, four or five scenes or story beats to anchor the seminar discussion around, with a sentence or two each on what I'd push the students to see in them. Also flag honestly where the film resists the reading -- I don't want to stand up there and pretend it's a tidy allegory if it isn't. And if the premise of the whole exercise is off -- e.g., if comparing a theme park of engineered megafauna to a gene drive release is comparing things whose relevant moral features don't actually overlap -- say that plainly rather than humoring me, because I'd rather know before the seminar than during it.
