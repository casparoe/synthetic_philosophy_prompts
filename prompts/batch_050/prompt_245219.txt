In the philosophy department where I did my master's, the utility monster objection to utilitarianism comes up roughly once a term, and the exchange is always the same. Someone raises Nozick's case from Anarchy, State, and Utopia: a being who derives so much more utility from any given stock of resources than the rest of us that aggregative utilitarianism hands everything to it, leaving everyone else with nothing. The utilitarians in the room respond in one of three ways -- they bite the bullet and say that if the monster's gains really are that vast, the conclusion is correct and our revulsion is just weak imagination; they deny the scenario's coherence, arguing that no single being could actually convert resources into utility at that rate given plausible facts about psychological saturation; or they concede the point but rebrand, moving to prioritarianism or some other view and insisting the core of utilitarianism survives. The anti-utilitarians find all three responses evasive, and neither side has ever moved.

What strikes me is that the same three moves have been available for fifty years and everyone knows them, yet the objection keeps being raised as though it were fresh and keeps being answered as though the answer were decisive. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about the monster at all. It is about whether a sufficiently extreme hypothetical -- one whose stipulations may be psychologically or even conceptually impossible -- can serve as data against a normative theory, or whether intuition pumps built on impossible stipulations tell us only about the limits of our imagination. In other words, I suspect this is a disagreement about philosophical method wearing the costume of a disagreement about distribution.

Please give me your diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is: verbal or substantive, and if substantive, whether it turns on the coherence of unbounded utility, on the distinction between pleasure and preference satisfaction, on aggregation as such, or on something methodological like my hypothesis. Engage with what each of the standard responses actually claims rather than surveying them, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis -- a distinction nobody has drawn, an empirical result about the structure of pleasure, an argument about the evidential weight of impossible cases, or a reason to think no progress should be expected because the dispute reduces to intuitions neither side can argue for.

Finally, please be explicit about the status of your own diagnosis: identify the point in your reasoning where you are least confident, and say what argument or consideration, if it were made successfully, would lead you to a different verdict about what this disagreement really is.
