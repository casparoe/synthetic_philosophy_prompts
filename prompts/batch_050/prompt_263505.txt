I'm writing an essay for a political theory reading group and want to use The Dark Knight as my case study. Read the film through the problem of dirty hands in politics -- Walzer's idea that the good politician may have to do wrong for the public and should carry the guilt rather than wash it off, with Machiavelli lurking in the background. The movie practically begs for it: Batman beats the Joker in the interrogation room, and at the end he and Gordon decide to pin Harvey Dent's murders on Batman so the city keeps its hero and Dent's prosecutions hold. Does the film endorse the Walzer line -- that Batman does the right thing and is right to feel soiled by it, taking on the guilt publically -- or does it actually chicken out, since the lie is costless to Gotham and Batman gets to be a romantic martyr rather than a genuinely compromised man? And where does the film push back on the whole framework: the ferry scene seems engineered to refute the dirty hands logic by having ordinary people refuse the utilitarian trade, and nobody ever pays a real price for the Dent cover-up in the film itself. I want a reading that's faithful in both directions -- not the film as an illustration of Walzer, and not Walzer bent to fit the movie -- and that ends by saying what, if anything, Nolan's version adds to the philosophical debate that the standard trolley-and-ticking-bomb arguments miss. Also tell me honestly which parts of the film resist the dirty hands reading and shouldn't be forced.
