After a seminar on verification and truth, a classmate and I reconstructed an argument we had seen sketched on the whiteboard, and our version seems to prove far too much, though we cannot locate the faulty step. (1) Some truths are unknown: for instance, it is either true or false that the number of leaves on the oak outside my window is even, and as it happens no one knows which. (2) Every truth is knowable: if p is true, then it is possible that someone comes to know p. (3) Whatever is known is true. (4) Whoever knows a conjunction knows each conjunct. Now take the unknown truth from (1) and call it C: the leaf-count is even and no one knows that it is. (5) By (2), C could be known; but by (4), knowing C would require knowing both that the count is even and that no one knows it is even; and by (3), knowing that second conjunct would make it true that no one knows the first, contradicting the knowledge of the first conjunct we just supposed. (6) So C could not be known after all, and the same reasoning shows that no truth of that form could be known. (7) But the only assumption that generated the contradiction was that some truth is unknown; so (1) is false and every truth is in fact known — someone, somewhere, knows whether the leaf-count is even. That conclusion is absurd, so something among (1)–(4) or in the transitions between them has to give, yet each premise seemed obvious when we granted it. Which step should I reject, why did it look harmless, and what do the main ways out — restricting (2) to a tamer class of truths, rejecting (4), weakening the background logic, or biting the bullet and adopting an anti-realism about truth — each cost? I would most like to keep (2) in some form, since abandoning it wholesale seems to let truth outrun not just what we know but what we could ever have reason to believe; but I want to know what that preference commits me to.
