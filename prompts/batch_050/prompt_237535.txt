A friend and I spent last night arguing about Sen's liberal paradox and neither of us budged. The result, as we understand it: given unrestricted domain, no collective decision rule can satisfy both weak Pareto and even minimal liberalism — each person being socially decisive over at least one pair of purely personal alternatives — as in Sen's case of the prude and the lewd, where the conflict arises because each has preferences about what the other reads. I'll set out our exchange without saying which position was mine.

(1) One of us argued that the paradox is really about meddlesome preferences: the prude's desire that the lewd not read a book is none of the prude's business, so the correct response is to launder or exclude other-regarding preferences from the aggregation. Pareto then applies only to self-regarding preferences, and the conflict disappears.

(2) The other replied that "meddlesome" cannot be defined without circularity: any line between self-regarding and other-regarding preferences already presupposes the rights assignment the theory is supposed to justify, so laundering smuggles liberalism in rather than deriving it. And since most real preferences are partly other-regarding — people care about neighbors' religion, dress, speech — the laundered Pareto principle would be nearly empty.

(3) The first then fell back on the procedural move: treat rights not as inputs into social choice but as constraints each person simply exercises, so there is no social state ranking left for the paradox to bite on. Each person reads or doesn't, and that's the end of it.

(4) The second countered that this relocates rather than dissolves the conflict: later formal work recasting rights as game forms showed that free exercise of rights can still yield Pareto-inferior equilibria — situations where both parties would prefer an outcome neither can reach while each holds onto their protected sphere. A liberal who accepts Pareto at all must then either accept that rights-respecting outcomes can be ones everyone wants to escape, or abandon Pareto's claim on them.

(5) The first closed by conceding the premise but denying the sting: if taking rights seriously means sometimes blocking deals both parties would take, that is a price liberals already pay elsewhere, and a "paradox" that merely shows rights constrain efficiency is a feature, not a refutation.

Please adjudicate each of the five moves individually — for each, say whether it succeeds, fails, or half-succeeds, and why — then identify the strongest steelmanned version of each side, say what both of us are missing about what Sen's result actually establishes, and state who is more right overall. End with a final line beginning Verdict: followed by which side is more right and the reason in one sentence.
