I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last two years reading moral philosophy, and I have become preoccupied with the debate between virtue ethicists and their situationist critics. What draws me to it is that the dispute looks, to my outsider's eye, like a measurement problem: the psychologists ran experiments and reported that broad character traits fail to predict behavior across situations; the philosophers replied, in effect, that the instruments were being misread. I would like to turn this into a working classification instrument rather than a summary.

Please write me a decision tree that sorts a reader's position on the situationist critique of virtue ethics, suitable for a reading group of educated non-specialists. The tree should begin from the following starting commitment, which every reader accepts at the root: the famous mid-century experiments on obedience, helping behavior under time pressure, and small changes to a person's immediate circumstances show that situational variables influence behavior far more than a naive picture of fixed character would predict. From there, each branch node should be a single question a reader can answer without knowing any of the position labels -- questions about what attributing a trait to someone commits one to, what counts as a disconfirming instance of a disposition, and whether the experiments measured the right kind of variable at the right grain.

Where I think I can contribute something from my own training, I would like it built into the tree rather than left out: a distinction between two standards for what a trait attribution predicts. On the strict standard, which I will call instance-first attribution, possessing a trait commits one to behaving accordingly in each suitably specified occasion, so a few failures sink the attribution. On the lenient standard, distribution-first attribution, a trait is a statistical propensity over a long run of occasions, so occasional failures are expected and the experiments cannot touch the attribution at all. One branch of your tree should turn on which of these two standards the reader thinks ordinary character language presupposes, and your key should note that the lenient standard risks making virtue claims empirically empty.

The leaves should include at least: the reply that genuine virtue is rare and the experiments mostly measured the non-virtuous; the view that local, situation-specific traits survive even if global ones do not; the fragmentation view on which the self simply lacks the unity virtue ethics assumes; the eliminativist conclusion that we should abandon character talk and do ethics without it; and a conservative leaf holding that disposition terms in ethics work like disposition terms in physics, where "soluble" is not refuted by an undissolved sample. Note any logically available combination of answers that no actual position occupies, and say briefly whether it is empty because incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate. Finally, please state how confident you are in your mapping of answer patterns to named positions, and single out the branch or attribution you are least sure of.
