In The Logic of Chance (1866; 3rd ed. 1888), John Venn argues that probability is at bottom a property of indefinitely extended series of events — that to speak of the probability of a single occurrence is only elliptical for the limiting proportion of occurrences of that type within some appropriately chosen series, and that treating probability as a degree of rational belief confuses the measurement of our ignorance with a feature of the world. Take Venn's series conception as the starting point, and consider the open problems it either creates or leaves untouched: the single-case problem, the reference-class problem, the legitimacy of credences as probabilities, the interpretation of probabilities in quantum mechanics and statistical physics, and whether propensity theories genuinely answer Venn's challenge or merely rename it. Imagine you are advising a philosopher of science with roughly three years to spend who wants to work on the interpretation of probability with an eye to what the sciences actually need. Which open questions should they prioritize, and in what order? Give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences: the questions in your recommended order, each with the reason for its rank (how many downstream questions it is upstream of, how tractable it is for a philosopher rather than a physicist, and whether settling it would change practice or only notation), with no introduction or conclusion.
