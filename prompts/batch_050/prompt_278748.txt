I make my living writing literary fiction, and something a reader said to me last month has been nagging at me. She told me, at a book event, that she wept for an hour over the death of one of my characters -- a man she knows perfectly well I invented, whose world exists nowhere but in two hundred-odd pages. She wasn't confused about this; nobody is. Yet the grief was, by her account and by everything I know of my own reading life, entirely real. Chasing this led me to what philosophers call the paradox of fiction, which I understand as a three-way tension: we seem to feel genuine emotion toward characters we know don't exist; we don't believe those characters exist; and genuine emotion directed at something seems to require believing that it exists. I've since read a bit about the standard escapes -- that our responses are a kind of make-believe or pretense rather than true emotion, that emotion can be triggered by mere thoughts without any belief in existence, that the inconsistency should simply be accepted as human irrationality. The debate interests me, but the philosophical vocabulary keeps sliding away from the thing I actually know.

What I'd like is a systematic translation. Take the paradox itself and each of the main positions on it, and restate them as precisely as you can in the vocabulary I work in every day: identification and distance, interiority versus externality, point of view, suspension of disbelief, earned versus unearned feeling, dramatic irony, the line between sentiment and sentimentality, what a workshop means when it says a scene "manipulated" a reader. I'm fluent in that vocabulary -- I've taught it -- so don't dilute it. For each philosophical position, give me its craft-vocabulary equivalent: for instance, does the make-believe theory amount to saying that a reader's grief is really the playing of grief, something closer to what an actor does than to what a mourner does? Does the thought-based account map onto something like the claim that a well-built scene produces real feeling by presenting the shape of a loss, with the question of whether anyone actually suffered being craft-irrelevant? Be honest in the translation -- don't quietly import a philosopher's assumptions and call the result workshop talk.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually want most. Which claims in this debate translate cleanly into craft vocabulary? Which survive only with distortion, and what exactly gets bent in the bending? And which have no expression at all in the way writers and critics talk -- I'm guessing the philosophers' concern with the rationality of emotion may be one of these, since I can barely formulate in workshop terms what it would mean for a reader's tears to be irrational rather than merely unearned. For that last category, tell me which way the lesson cuts: is the residue a blind spot in how my profession talks about its own effects, or is it a sign that the philosophical dispute is less substantive than it looks? I'm not asking for citations or a reading list -- I want the translation, an honest accounting of what it fails to carry, and your judgment about where the failure belongs. Please write in American English.
