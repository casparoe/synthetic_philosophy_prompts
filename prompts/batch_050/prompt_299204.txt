I work as a backend engineer, and a running argument among my colleagues — about when copying something is actually wrong, as opposed to merely unglamorous — has made me realize I don't know what I think about the ownership of ideas. So here are my gut verdicts on a set of cases, and I'd like you to find the principle behind them, if there is one. A competitor reimplements my service's public API from scratch, purely from reading the documentation, so their product is a drop-in replacement for mine: annoying, but fine. A colleague takes a clever algorithm I described at a whiteboard over lunch, writes the actual implementation himself that weekend, and ships it under his own name: not fine, even though every line of code is his. Someone copies fifty lines of my code verbatim into their open-source project without attribution: not fine, and worse than the whiteboard case, though I can't fully say why, since less intellectual work was taken. A company independently patents a technique my team had already been using internally for years but never published, then demands we license it: outrageous, even though they genuinely invented it on their own. Someone reverse-engineers a proprietary binary to make an interoperable tool: fine. Someone buys a product, tears it down, and sells a knockoff made by following the same schematic: fine too, I think, though this one makes me more uneasy than the binary case, and I'm not sure that's consistent. An author spends a decade developing a fantasy world with an intricate magic system, and another writer lifts the magic system wholesale but changes every name and plot detail: not fine, somehow worse than the API case despite being structurally similar. A pharma company sells a drug at a huge markup after its patent funded the clinical trials, and a generics manufacturer copies the molecule the day the patent expires: completely fine.

Is there a single coherent account of when ideas and their expressions can legitimately be owned that captures most of these verdicts — some combination of the idea/expression distinction, labor-based desert, and independent-invention defenses, or something else entirely? Please state the best candidate principle (or two, if the cases split cleanly) precisely enough that I could apply it mechanically, note which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one you think I should abandon, and flag wherever I'm drawing a distinction that only tracks my feelings about the copier rather than anything about what was copied. Finally, tell me what your principle says about the case I genuinely can't decide: an engineer who trains a code-generation model on millions of public repositories, mine included, so that the model now reproduces the style and sometimes the substance of my solutions without ever storing them.
