I run a team of about a dozen engineers responsible for a large, long-lived software platform, and we are about to embark on a multi-year effort to consolidate and, in many cases, outright delete legacy subsystems. While preparing for this, a colleague pointed me to two texts that she claims are deeply relevant to our situation. The first is Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790), in which Burke argues, in essence, that the accumulated arrangements of a society embody far more practical intelligence than any single generation's private stock of reason, that the science of government is experimental and cannot be deduced a priori, and that reformers who tear down inherited institutions typically discover too late what functions those institutions were silently performing. The second is G. K. Chesterton's The Thing (1929), with its well-known fence parable: a reformer who cannot see the use of a fence should not be permitted to remove it, though a reformer who has discovered why it was erected may be allowed to take it away.

Taking these two lines of thought together as a single framework, please describe the most important implications for how my team should actually conduct this consolidation effort. I am not asking whether the framework counsels against the project entirely (we already intend to proceed, and Chesterton himself allows fences to be removed by those who understand them). Rather, I want the concrete, second-order consequences: what does the framework imply about how we should investigate a subsystem before deprecating it, about whose testimony counts as evidence of a subsystem's purpose, about how we should interpret the fact that nobody can currently explain why something exists, about sequencing and reversibility, and about the epistemic posture we should adopt toward our own design judgments? Where the framework has recognizable failure modes or can be abused to rationalize never changing anything, please flag those as part of the implications as well.
