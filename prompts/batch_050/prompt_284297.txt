I'm a novelist by trade, and outside of my fiction I occasionally write short essays for a small literary review that a friend of mine edits. My current book project concerns a protagonist who lives almost entirely for the approval of others, and in the course of researching it I fell down a rabbit hole in the literature on AI alignment, where a distinction is sometimes drawn between goal-directed agents -- systems that steer toward outcomes in the world -- and approval-directed agents -- systems that simply take whatever action they expect to be rated most highly by an overseer. I found the distinction illuminating for thinking about people, not just machines, and I've drafted a short essay exploring that. Before I submit it, I'd like your comments on the draft, which I've reproduced below in full.

---

On Applause-Shaped Lives

When we ask why a person did something, we usually assume there is a goal somewhere at the bottom of the answer: she wanted the promotion, the money, the child, the truth. But I have come to think that many people, perhaps most, are not goal-directed in this way at all. They are approval-directed. They do not steer toward states of the world; they steer toward the anticipated reaction of whoever is watching -- and in an age of feeds and metrics, someone is always watching. The approval-directed person is like a chess player who has no interest in checkmate but only in the spectators murmuring "well played" after every move.

I want to propose a further distinction that I have not seen made elsewhere, between what I call act-level and self-level approval direction. The act-level approval-seeker wants each individual action to be approved: the clever remark, the tasteful gift, the well-timed outrage. The self-level approval-seeker has gone a step further and assembled an entire persona designed to be approvable, so that approval accrues to the character rather than to any deed. This second kind of person, I claim, is both more stable and more dangerous: more stable, because a persona survives the failure of any single act; more dangerous, because once a whole self is optimized for approval, there is nothing left inside that could notice the optimization happening and object to it.

Finally, I suggest that institutions can be approval-directed in exactly this sense. A social media platform does not want anything to be the case in the world; it wants to be engaged with, which is approval at industrial scale. My worry is that approval-directed institutions breed approval-directed people, who in turn demand more approval-directed institutions, and that this loop quietly displaces goals altogether -- that a society of applause-seekers may eventually contain no one who remembers what the applause was originally for.

---

Here is the kind of feedback I'm looking for. First, accuracy: is my rendering of the goal-directed versus approval-directed distinction faithful to how it is understood in the AI alignment context, or have I distorted it in importing it into moral psychology? Second, the act-level versus self-level distinction is my own invention; please assess whether it is coherent and does real work, or whether it collapses on inspection -- for instance, is self-level approval direction just act-level approval direction with a longer time horizon, and if so, does that matter? Third, I'd welcome comments on the essay's weakest link: my sense is that the final paragraph moves too quickly from an analogy to a causal claim about institutions shaping persons, and I'd like either confirmation of that suspicion or a defense of the move. Feel free to comment on anything else as well, but I'd rather have three or four substantive, specific observations than a long list of minor notes.
