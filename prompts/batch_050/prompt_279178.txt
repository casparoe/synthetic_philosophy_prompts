A colleague in our effective-altruism reading group has been pressing an argument that, if sound, would show that the importance-tractability-neglectedness framework for cause prioritization forbids ever doing any direct work at all. I think the conclusion is absurd, but I cannot locate the mistake. Here is the argument as he gives it:

(1) When choosing between causes to work on, one should prefer whichever cause scores best on three factors: the scale of the problem (importance), the fraction of the problem that additional effort will solve (tractability), and how little attention the problem already receives (neglectedness).

(2) Work on cause prioritization itself -- figuring out which causes are best -- has enormous scale: its potential effect spans all the resources it might redirect, which is the sum of the good achievable across every object-level cause, and so exceeds the scale of any single object-level cause.

(3) Such research is also tractable, since people demonstrably revise their cause rankings in response to new arguments and evidence, and it is neglected, since only a tiny fraction of altruistic effort goes toward it.

(4) By (1), then, cause-prioritization research is a better use of one's marginal hour than work on any object-level cause.

(5) But the reasoning of (2)-(4) reapplies at the next level up: research into how to do cause-prioritization research governs the allocation of all prioritization effort, so by the same scale argument it outranks first-order prioritization research; and so on, with each meta-level inheriting the total scale of everything beneath it.

(6) Therefore the framework recommends spending one's entire career at some indefinitely high meta-level, and recommends that no one ever do any object-level work -- no one distributes bednets, no one runs the animal-welfare campaigns, no one builds the pandemic early-warning systems.

(7) But if no one ever does object-level work, there are no interventions to evaluate, no track records to learn from, and nothing for prioritization research to be research about; so the framework, applied consistently, destroys the very activity it is meant to guide. It refutes itself.

Something has clearly gone wrong, since the framework was designed to rank object-level causes against one another and plainly does license working on them. My own suspicion is that something happens to the three factors when the cause in question is meta-level, but I cannot say exactly what, and my colleague insists the framework treats meta-causes just like any other cause. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to give something up, I would rather keep (1) intact than rescue (6), but I want to know the price of each option -- including the option of biting the bullet and accepting that some amount of meta-work really does dominate. Please also indicate how confident you are in your diagnosis, and flag the single point in your answer where you are least sure you have it right.
