I am a novelist, roughly a book every eighteen months, and my work keeps returning to characters whose relationship to pain has been altered: a protagonist left with pain asymbolia after a stroke, who reports the sensation of a burn with complete indifference; a character on an invented drug that leaves the feeling of pain fully intact but strips out everything aversive about it; and, in my current manuscript, a machine intelligence whose damage reports I want to function as pain without my knowing whether they deserve the name. Each time I invent such a case I improvise, and my improvisations are inconsistent across books: sometimes I treat the suffering as undiminished because the sensation is still there, sometimes as emptied because nobody minds it, and my choice seems to track what the plot needs rather than anything I could defend.

What I want is a procedure I can actually run, grounded in what philosophers have said about why pain is bad and what the asymbolia cases show about that. Not an essay on the literature, but an ordered sequence of questions I can put to any fictional alteration of pain -- in about half an hour per case, without philosophical training -- such that each question has a clear standard for what counts as an answer, the sequence terminates in a usable verdict about how much moral weight the character's suffering should carry on the page, and the points where the procedure must hand the decision back to me as a matter of unresolved values are flagged rather than disguised as further steps. In designing it, please be explicit about which philosophical distinctions have to survive the simplification (I suspect the distinction between the sensory character of pain and its unpleasantness is one, but I want you to tell me) and which can be safely dropped at the level a novelist needs. I would also like the procedure to say honestly what it cannot settle: where the philosophy itself runs out and I am simply choosing.

Then run the finished procedure on my three cases above -- the asymbolic stroke patient, the character on the indifference drug, and the machine intelligence -- and include at least one verdict you expect I will dislike, since part of the point is to stop my plotting convenience from doing the reasoning. For the machine case, say specifically where the procedure starts to strain, since it was presumably built for creatures like us.

Two requests about how you write this. First, no formatting at all: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables; continuous prose throughout, laid out the way one would write a letter, with the steps of the procedure given in numbered sentences within the paragraphs if need be. Second, since I will be leaning on this for years, tell me plainly how confident you are in the procedure's central claims, and single out the one point -- whether a premise, a step, or a worked verdict -- where you are least sure you have it right.
