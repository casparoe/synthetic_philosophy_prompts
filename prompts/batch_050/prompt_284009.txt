I TA'd an intro metaphysics course and the week on the identity of indiscernibles went badly in a depressingly familiar way. Leibniz's principle says objects that share all their properties are identical; Black's two perfectly identical iron spheres alone in a void are supposed to refute it; defenders of the principle say the scenario isn't coherently imaginable (you're just counting one sphere twice, or you're smuggling in a non-qualitative fact about which sphere is which), and the other side says the scenario is plainly conceivable and the principle is just a stipulation about what we'll count as a property. Then someone always brings up identical electrons and quantum statistics as the empirical tiebreaker, and it turns out that depends on how you read quantum mechanics, so it settles nothing. Decades of this and as far as I can tell nobody has moved an inch. My hypothesis is that the real disagreement isn't about spheres or objects at all -- it's about whether there can be facts that aren't grounded in purely qualitative facts, i.e. a second-order dispute about what metaphysical explanation has to deliver, with the spheres as the occasion. Diagnose it using what each side actually says (not the strawmen each side attributes to the other): is my hypothesis right, or is this a genuine first-order disagreement, or is it verbal -- two notions of 'property' talking past each other? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress: a principled criterion for which properties are admissible, a settled interpretation of quantum mechanics, or is the impasse itself evidence the question needs reformulating?
