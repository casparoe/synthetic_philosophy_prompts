A colleague of mine argues that the Bertrand-style paradoxes establish something far stronger than they are usually taken to: namely, that questions about unknown physical magnitudes almost never have objectively correct probabilistic answers. Her argument runs as follows, and I cannot locate the mistake, if there is one.

Consider a factory that produces cubes, all of whose side lengths lie between 1 cm and 2 cm. We know nothing whatsoever about the manufacturing process beyond this. What is the probability that the next cube produced has a side length of at most 1.5 cm?

(1) If this question has a definite, objectively correct answer, then that answer must be determined by the description of the setup: two descriptions containing exactly the same information must yield the same answer.

(2) Describing the situation in terms of side length, indifference over the interval [1, 2] yields P(side ≤ 1.5) = 1/2.

(3) Describing it in terms of surface area, indifference over the interval [6, 24] yields P(side ≤ 1.5) = P(area ≤ 13.5) = 7.5/18 ≈ 0.417.

(4) Describing it in terms of volume, indifference over the interval [1, 8] yields P(side ≤ 1.5) = P(volume ≤ 3.375) = 2.375/7 ≈ 0.339.

(5) These three descriptions contain exactly the same information: each is interderivable with the others, and none adds or removes any fact about the world.

(6) By (1), then, 1/2 = 0.417 = 0.339, which is absurd. So the question has no objectively correct answer.

(7) The same construction is available for any unknown continuous magnitude: the length of a chord in a circle, the temperature of a newly observed star, the bias of a bent coin. Hence no question about an unknown continuous quantity has an objective probabilistic answer, except where long-run frequencies happen to be available to fix one.

(8) So either objective chance exists only where frequencies settle it, or all probability is ultimately subjective.

I would very much like to resist (7) and (8) — it seems to me that the factory's actual production process does make some answer true, whatever we can know of it — but I also find (5) hard to deny, and indifference reasoning feels indispensable in cases where frequencies are unavailable. Which step should I reject? Explain why the rejected step looked compelling, and what it costs to reject it. In doing so, please address how the standard responses divide the bill: restricting indifference to the symmetry or transformation group of the problem, retreating to maximum-entropy or reference priors, and denying that probability assignments answer to our ignorance rather than to the physical setup. If any of these responses saves objectivity only by conceding more than my colleague conceded, say so.
