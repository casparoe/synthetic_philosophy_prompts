So this is a bit of a weird mashup question but bear with me. A few months ago my therapist put me on this whole happiness protocol — gratitude journal, a meditation app, morning sunlight, cutting my screen time — and honestly it mostly feels like homework. Around the same time I fell down a wikipedia hole and found Émilie du Châtelet, the 18th century philospher-physicist who translated Newton into French, and it turns out she wrote a short Discourse on Happiness near the end of her life. From what I can piece together her recipe was roughly: shake off your prejudices, let yourself have real passions and strong tastes, stay open to being a little charmed or fooled by pleasures instead of being suspicious of everything, and above all cultivate a love of serious study, because it's the one source of happiness that doesn't fade with age or depend on other people's whims. Also apparently she wrote it while pregnant and quite possibly aware it might kill her (it did), which makes it land differently.

So here's what I actually want to know. If du Châtelet got dropped into 2025 for a month, what do you think she'd make of the modern happiness industry — the meditation apps, dopamine detoxes, positive psychology as an actual academic discipline, influencers flogging morning routines? Where would she say we've basically reinvented her own advice, and where would she say we've gotten it badly wrong? And the part I keep circling: a lot of modern happiness advice treats strong desires and passions as the problem — something to detach from, observe, manage, dampen — whereas she seemed to think being passionate was pretty much a precondition for happiness at all. Who's got the better of that disagreement, in your view? Please reason from her actual stated views as I've laid them out, not just generic "historical thinker would obviously hate smartphones" vibes.
