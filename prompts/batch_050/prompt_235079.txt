I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or economics, and I'm designing the arbitration module for a system in which two autonomous agents must repeatedly agree on how to split a shared, divisible resource (think of two services negotiating over a fixed monthly cloud budget). If negotiations fail, both agents fall back to a known, worse "disagreement" allocation. Each agent has a utility function over allocations, so the set of achievable utility pairs forms a feasible region, and I need to hard-code a rule that picks one point in that region.

Two candidate rules are on the table:

1. The Nash bargaining solution: pick the feasible utility pair that maximizes the product of the two agents' utility gains over the disagreement point. It is known to be the unique rule satisfying Pareto efficiency, symmetry, invariance under independent positive affine rescalings of each agent's utility, and "independence of irrelevant alternatives" (IIA): deleting feasible options that weren't chosen never changes the outcome.

2. The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution: compute each agent's "ideal" utility (the most it could get anywhere in the feasible region), then pick the Pareto-efficient point where both agents receive the same fraction of their possible gain over the disagreement point. It is known to be the unique rule satisfying the first three axioms above but with IIA replaced by "individual monotonicity": if the feasible region expands in a way that leaves one agent's ideal utility unchanged while raising the other's, the latter agent must not end up worse off.

Please compare the two solutions as candidates for my module. In particular: (a) construct a concrete numeric example in my budget-splitting setting where IIA and individual monotonicity pull in opposite directions, and compute what each rule does; (b) explain how each rule behaves under practical stresses I care about — an agent exaggerating or misreporting its utility function, and the feasible region expanding when finance raises our budget; (c) argue for which rule I should implement, and state clearly what consideration, if resolved the other way, would flip your recommendation.
