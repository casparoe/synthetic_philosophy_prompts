I lead a machine learning team at a company that deploys predictive models into production, and I keep hitting the same situation: after validation, two or more candidate models are empirically equivalent — same accuracy on the held-out data, same calibration, same cost — yet they differ in structure and will behave differently in cases we haven't tested. My engineers resolve these ties by taste, and I want to replace that with a decision procedure grounded in what philosophy of science says about underdetermination of theory by evidence: the distinction between a tie that more data could actually break and one it could not, the status of tiebreakers like simplicity and unification as something other than evidence, and the worry that our validation data only samples the deployment distribution imperfectly. Write the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions with, for each, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure should stop and defer to more data collection instead, and where judgment genuinely cannot be proceduralized. Apply it to two cases: two credit-risk classifiers with identical test performance that rely on different features, and two demand-forecasting models that agree within the historical range but diverge sharply outside it. Also state what the procedure cannot settle at all.
