Yesterday evening a friend and I argued late into the night about the Grand Inquisitor chapter of The Brothers Karamazov, and rather than keep circling we have agreed to produce, jointly, the document an honest adversarial collaboration between our two positions would yield. Draft it for us.

My position: the Inquisitor is substantially right. His case rests on a defensible view of human nature — most people, most of the time, will exchange freedom for bread, security, and absolution, and are happier for doing so — and a regime that accepts this, carries the burden of conscience on behalf of the many, and governs through consolation and managed belief is more humane than one that hands people a freedom they cannot bear. My friend's position: the chapter refutes the Inquisitor on his own terms. What he offers is sedation rather than happiness; the deception at the heart of his project corrodes the regime from within; and freedom, understood as the capacity for active and uncoerced love, is constitutive of the human good rather than merely one good among others.

The joint statement should contain the following.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. We want formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted: for instance, that the Inquisitor's argument inverts the three temptations; that his case contains a genuinely empirical premise about the many as against the few, and not merely a moral one; that any adequate reading must account for Christ's silence and for the kiss; and that neither of us may win by stipulating what happiness or freedom means.

Second, a table of the disagreements that survive, each phrased so that the side it describes would accept the wording as its own. Include at least: whether the good the Inquisitor promises is a genuine form of happiness or a counterfeit; whether freedom's value is instrumental or constitutive; and whether the weakness of the many is a fixed fact about human beings or is partly produced by the very regime that claims to relieve it.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of us. For each crux, state the evidence or argument we both accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. We expect these to include the empirical premise (we agree that patterns of authoritarian nostalgia, of dissent and emigration under paternalistic regimes, and of people's actual behaviour when burdened with choice would be relevant; write down what each of us predicts such evidence shows), the coherence of benevolent deception at scale (my friend predicts such regimes decay into cynicism and stagnation; I predict that competent paternalism can remain stable; identify what would count as evidence of each), and the conceptual crux of whether satisfaction without agency counts as happiness at all (we agree that experience-machine-style thought experiments bear on this; record how each of us reads them and where that leaves the disagreement).

Finally, tell us which part of our quarrel you judge to be verbal. We suspect the word freedom is doing different work for each of us — freedom from want and fear on my side, freedom for responsibility and love on hers — and we want to know which disagreements remain once that is made explicit, and whether the Inquisitor's case survives the disambiguation.

Write in a tone we could both sign our names to, with the residual disagreement made exact.
