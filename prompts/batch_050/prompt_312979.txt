I'm preparing a lecture series on the philosophy of humour for a third-year aesthetics module, and before I assign any of the classic positions I want to be clear in my own mind about what we're even entitled to demand of a theory in this area. The literature seems to me to suffer from a recurring problem: each camp quietly builds its favoured cases into its definition of adequacy, so that superiority theorists test rival accounts against status-laden mockery, incongruity theorists against absurdism and wordplay, and so on, and everyone "wins". I'd rather settle the test cases first and then see who survives them.

Please write the list of desiderata an adequate general theory of humour must meet, with the consideration or case motivating each one. I mean desiderata in the strict sense: phenomena a theory must explain or at least be consistent with, cases it must classify correctly, and distinctions it must respect. Among the cases I'd want covered are: failed jokes and jokes that land (a theory that can't distinguish them seems dead on arrival); the fact that the same remark can be funny from one speaker and offensive from another, which suggests humour is not a purely semantic property of the utterance; amusement without laughter and laughter without amusement (nervous laughter, tickling, social laughter), so whatever the theory says about humour it had better not over-generate claims about laughter; children's humour and its development; the way humour can deflate when explained, and whether a good theory should predict that or be embarrassed by it; and the moral dimension — why some jokes seem to be made worse *as jokes* by their cruelty, while others seem to be funnier *because* of their transgression. Please mark which desiderata you take to be genuinely non-negotiable and which are partisan demands of one tradition that a rival could reasonably refuse. If any subset of the desiderata looks jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, if explaining both the intimacy of shared laughter and the universality of incongruity judgements pulls a theory in opposite directions — say so explicitly, because that would itself be the most interesting deliverable.

Once the list is in place, score the four main contenders against it briefly and in this order: superiority theory, incongruity theory, relief or release theory, and the benign violation account. Keep the scoring compact — a paragraph each at most — since the criteria are where I want the thinking to happen and I can run the detailed evaluation in the lecture myself. If you think one of the four isn't really a theory of humour at all but rather a theory of something adjacent (say, of why we laugh, or of the social function of joking), note that rather than forcing it through the list. Please write in British English throughout.
