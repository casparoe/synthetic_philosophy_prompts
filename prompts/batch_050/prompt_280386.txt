hey, I'm TAing a grad seminar on AI safety this semester and I need a second pair of eyes on an exam answer before I finalize grades. The course covers technical AI alignment, mostly from the conceptual side.

Here was the exam question:

"Researchers sometimes define 'the alignment problem' narrowly, as the problem of intent alignment, rather than broadly as value alignment. (a) Explain the distinction between intent alignment and value alignment. (b) Why might a researcher deliberately adopt the narrow definition -- what motivates it, and what problems get left outside of it? (c) Give an example of a failure mode that would be a value-alignment problem but NOT an intent-alignment problem."

Here's the student's answer:

(a) Intent alignment means the AI is trying to do what its operator wants it to do -- the system's objective is roughly "do what the human intends." Value alignment is stronger: it means the AI actually does what's good or what's in line with correct human values, whatever those are. So an intent-aligned system could still produce bad outcomes if the operator's intentions are themselves bad or confused, and a value-aligned system might arguably override the operator if the operator wants something immoral.

(b) The narrow definition is attractive for a few reasons. First, figuring out what humans "really" value is a hard philosophical problem, and engineers want a target they can work on without solving metaethics first. Second, intent alignment is more tractable -- you can evaluate whether a system is trying to follow instructions without agreeing on a full moral theory. Third, the narrow definition keeps the problem modular: once a system reliably tries to do what we want, the remaining burden is on us to want the right things, which is a governance and ethics problem rather than a machine learning problem. What gets left outside: moral uncertainty, aggregation across people with conflicting values, manipulation-resistant human preferences, and the question of who counts as the operator.

(c) Example: suppose the operator is a company that wants to maximize engagement on its platform, and the AI fully understands and faithfully pursues this goal. The AI is intent-aligned -- it's genuinely trying to do what the company wants. But the outcome is value-misaligned, because maximizing engagement turns out to involve addicting users and promoting outrage, which is bad for society. Since the AI also reliably does what the company wants, it will also be corrigible -- an intent-aligned system is by definition one that lets itself be shut down or corrected, since that's part of doing what the operator wants.

Also note this is essentially the same distinction as inner vs. outer alignment: inner alignment is about whether the system's internal objective matches its training objective, which is just intent alignment viewed mechanistically, and outer alignment is about whether the training objective matches what we value, which is just value alignment. So the intent/value framing repackages the inner/outer framing for a less technical audience.
