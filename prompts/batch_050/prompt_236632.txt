I sit on the appeals commitee at a mid-size health insurer. Every quarter we get cases arguing that some condition should count as a genuine "disorder" for coverage purposes -- prolonged grief, sex addiction, infertility, being very short, chronic lateness -- and right now we decide by vibes and precedent, and it shows.

I want you to turn the naturalism vs. normativism debate about health and disease (Boorse-style biostatistical accounts, Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction idea, and the normativist position that "disorder" is really a disguised value judgment) into a decision procedure we can actually run in a 20-minute meeting: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure spits out a clear yes/no, and where it has to punt to a value call -- and at the punt points, say explicitly whose values should be doing the work (the patient's? the doctor's? society's? ours?).

Then run it on two cases: grief after bereavement that looks clinically a lot like depression, and low sexual desire in someone who isn't bothered by it but whose partner is. Give me the results as a table with these columns: condition | verdict | step where it got decided (or "punted") | what a pure naturalist would say | what a pure normativist would say.

Two requests. Don't just recapitulate the three theories, I mostly know them already; the work I need is the translation into procedure, including which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which you can drop. And if a case breaks the procedure or lands somewhere my board will hate, leave it there and flag it rather than fixing it -- that's the interesting part. Also, if you think the whole ask is confused (say, because naturalism and normativism aren't really options on a menu you can operationalize side by side), tell me that plainly instead of politely complying.
