I'm putting together the review sheet for my philosophy of science seminar, which this term is organized around failed long-run predictions, with Malthus as the running case. His population-outstrips-subsistence prediction plainly hasn't come true in the two centuries since the Essay, but his defenders have a whole toolkit for explaining how a theory can survive that, and I want the students to see the full toolkit laid out.

The rescue moves I've already got on the sheet:

1. Blame the auxiliaries (Duhem-style). The core mechanism is fine; what failed were background assumptions no one could have foreseen at the time -- the fertility collapse in rich countries, the productivity explosions (Haber-Bosch, the Green Revolution). The test refuted the conjunction, not the core.

2. It was only ever a tendency claim. Malthus described population pressure as a tendency operating unless countervailing checks intervene, and checks (moral restraint, later contraception) intervened. A hedged tendency isn't falsified by its non-occurence. (Yes, I know this one shades into #1 -- I'd still keep them seperate on the sheet.)

3. Domain restriction. The theory correctly describes the pre-industrial regime, where income gains really did get eaten by population growth, and economic historians basically grant that much. It stops applying once growth decouples from fertility. Right theory, expired jurisdiction.

4. Right but early. The mechanism is correct and the timeline was just off; the neo-Malthusians keep pushing the date out, and maybe someday they're right. Deferred, not dead.

5. Self-averting prediction. The warning helped cause the behavior (agricultural research, family-planning policy) that prevented its own fulfillment, so the non-occurrence is evidence the theory worked, not that it failed.

What I want from you: a sixth move for the sheet that is none of these -- not a rewording, not a special case, and not #1 wearing #2's clothes. If the honest answer is that the toolkit is exhausted and there is no sixth, say that instead, but genuinely try first.

Then two follow-ups. First, does your sixth move actually fit Malthus, or is it a rescue that works for other theories but not his? Second, does adopting it leave the theory with any content that a future observation could still refute, or does it push the whole thing over the line into unfalsifiability? That second question is what the exam is really about, so give it the most space.

End your response with a last line beginning "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of the labels right-but-early, refuted, or unfalsifiable -- whichever you think best describes Malthus's theory once your sixth move is on the table.
