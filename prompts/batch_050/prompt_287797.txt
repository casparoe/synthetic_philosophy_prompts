You are a professional philosopher with particular expertise in twentieth-century naturalism and metaethics. Think carefully, and treat this as a piece of serious philosophical craftsmanship rather than a survey answer.

Some background, which you should treat as the stipulated frame for the problem. W.V. Quine held that there is a deep asymmetry between science and morality. Scientific disputes, however heated, have checkpoints: disagreements are answerable, eventually, to shared observation and shared method. Moral disputes, by contrast, can persist even under total agreement on the non-moral facts and on all the relevant logic — two people can agree about every empirical detail of a case and still disagree about what ought to be done, and nothing in the scientific method settles the matter. On this basis Quine denied that moral judgments enjoy the kind of cognitive warrant scientific claims do. He was no quietist or nihilist, though: he held that we have values, that they guide action, and that they can be assessed instrumentally as means to ends we already hold.

Your task: construct the strongest argument you can that Quine's own naturalism deprives him of the resources to sustain this asymmetry. The argument should develop roughly the following line of thought, but you must make each step explicit and defensible rather than merely gesturing at it. First, naturalized epistemology does not treat scientific method as certified from some vantage point outside inquiry; method is itself part of our ongoing theory of the world. Second, the operative norms of that method — prefer the simpler hypothesis, conserve accepted doctrine where possible, maximize predictive scope and explanatory unity — are normative through and through, and are not themselves forced upon us by observation. Third, if moral norms forfeit cognitive standing on the ground that they outrun the evidence, then epistemic norms, which likewise outrun the evidence, stand condemned alongside them; and without privileged epistemic norms, the very contrast between science and morality that Quine relied on cannot be drawn.

The argument is not finished until you have answered at least these two objections on Quine's behalf, and answered them fairly: (a) epistemic norms, unlike moral ones, are vindicated instrumentally by their track record of predictive success, so the parallel fails at exactly the crucial point; (b) the argument at most establishes that epistemic and moral norms are equally non-cognitive — a conclusion a tough-minded Quinean could simply embrace rather than a refutation of anything.

Structure your response as follows. Begin with a brief brainstorming section in which you identify which premise of the argument is most vulnerable and why, and consider one alternative route to the same conclusion that you ultimately set aside. Then present the argument proper as a numbered sequence of premises and sub-conclusions, with each non-obvious step explicitly defended. Then address the two objections. Close by stating precisely what a Quinean would have to give up in order to resist your conclusion, and whether you judge that price acceptable.

Two constraints on execution. First, do not attribute to Quine a crude emotivism he did not hold: he did not claim moral utterances are mere exclamations, and your argument must work against his actual, more careful position. Second, no citations or bibliographic apparatus are needed; reason directly from the positions as described, supplying your own illustrations where they help.
