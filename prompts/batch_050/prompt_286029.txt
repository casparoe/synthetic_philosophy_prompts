So I've just become the first (and only) research hire at a small democracy-reform foundation, and my job for the next couple of months is to put together an agenda we can actually act on: we can fund maybe 4 or 5 pieces of work a year, mix of commissioned essays, workshops, and one grad fellowship. The board is sortition-pilled -- they've read the obvious stuff, they're convinced citizens' assemblies and randomly selected bodies are promising, and a few of them want to go straight to lobbying for a randomly selected second chamber. My worry is that the conceptual groundwork is way thinner than the enthusiasm. When I push people past the bumper-sticker version (ancient Athens did it, juries kind of work, elections are aristocratic anyway) the open questions pile up fast: what legitimacy a body has when nobody consented to *its* authority in particular; whether descriptive representativeness even survives self-selection, since lots of summoned people decline; how you get accountability without re-election when rotation means nobody faces consequences; whether the epistemic case depends on conditions (good facilitation, balanced briefing, time to deliberate) that powerful actors will quietly control; whether sortition can ever handle executive functions or only advisory/review roles; and whether a hybrid with elections inherits the strengths or just the pathologies of both.

What I'd like from you is help turning that pile into a defensible priority list for a funder, not a literature survey. Which three or four open questions about sortition are most worth putting money behind in the next two or three years, and in what order? For each one, tell me why it's upstream of other work (or of any sane policy push), how tractable it is for political theorists and philosophers rather than empirical political scientists -- since empirical pilots are slow and we mostly can't fund them anyway -- and how neglected it seems relative to how loud it is in the public debate (my sense is descriptive representativeness gets endless airtime while accountability-without-reelection is under-theorized, but maybe I'm wrong). Also flag which popular question you think is *overrated* -- one that the board will expect to see on the list and that I should be ready to argue down -- and explain what doesn't actually hinge on it.

Finally, for your top-ranked question: what would an actually good answer even look like? Like, what's the shape of a resolution -- a framework, a taxonomy, a proof-of-concept design -- and what would make me update toward demoting it in a year's time? I'd rather have sharp reasoning about three questions than a fair-minded tour of ten, and don't bother suggesting specific books or papers, just characterize the work itself.
