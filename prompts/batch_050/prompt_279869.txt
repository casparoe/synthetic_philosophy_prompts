So I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I've been assigned a long essay on online shaming -- the classic pile-on dynamic. My editor suggested I anchor it in the Justine Sacco case from 2013, which I figure most readers still remember: PR exec with under 200 followers posts a tasteless AIDS joke tweet before boarding an 11-hour flight to Cape Town, lands to find herself the number one trending topic worldwide, loses her job, basically becomes the template for everything that came after. Fine. But I don't want to write the standard "mob bad, nuance good" take, and I got it into my head to run the whole thing through Kierkegaard's account of the press and the public from Two Ages / The Present Age, because his diagnosis is weirdly specific: the public isn't a real group of people at all, it's a phantom conjured up by the press, an abstraction that lets actual individuals participate in events without ever taking responsibility for anything, and the whole thing runs on leveling -- the passionless flattening of everyone to the same status, where no one can stand out except by being destroyed. Here's what I need from you, since I want to stress-test the framing before I commit to it: do a careful mapping of the Sacco episode onto that theory. Who exactly plays the role of "the public" here -- the tweeters, the trending algorithm, the journalists covering the trend? Where does the phantom-public idea genuinely explain something (e.g., the sense that "everyone" was outraged when it may have been a few thousand accounts amplified into looking like millions)? And then the harder part: where does the 1846 theory actually break? Two candidate breaks I want you to evaluate: first, Kierkegaard's leveling makes everyone anonymous and equal, but pile-ons work by singling out ONE identifiable person and destroying her specifically -- is that leveling, its opposite, or something his framework can't see? Second, he thought the press lets individuals hide behind the phantom, but on modern platforms the pile-on leaders are highly identifiable and get rewarded with followers for it -- does that refute him, or update him? Finally, run it in reverse: what does the episode show about the theory itself -- confirmed, refuted, or just never imagined a case like this? Don't force him to speak where he's silent; if some feature of the case (the permanence, the global reach, the monetization of outrage) has no Kierkegaardian hook, say so plainly rather than stretching a concept to fit. Keep the whole thing under 800 words, definately no longer -- I need it as a memo I can actually use, not a dissertation.
