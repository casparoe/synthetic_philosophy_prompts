I work in logistics, not research, but I've spent the last couple of years reading everything I can find about AI development, and there's one idea I keep turning over: the claim that safety work on advanced AI has to pay for itself, so to speak. The reasoning, as I understand it, is that any technique for making a system more reliable or better behaved that also makes it meaningfully slower, weaker, or more expensive to run will simply be skipped over, because some developer somewhere is under pressure to ship and won't accept the handicap. So the safety measures that actually get used are the ones that come free or close to free. I've started calling this, in my notes, the discipline of the market on safety, and I've become convinced that most proposals I read fail it without anyone checking.

Before I read another proposal in this space -- whether it's a training technique, a monitoring scheme, an international agreement, a voluntary industry pledge, or a hardware-level control -- I want a specification to test it against. Write me the list of requirements that a proposal for making advanced AI safe must meet if the competitive-pressure problem is real, with the case or reasoning behind each requirement. I'd like you to include, at minimum: what it means for a safety measure to impose a cost small enough to survive competition (and how you'd even measure the size of that handicap when capabilities are hard to compare), who has to comply for the measure to work (one firm, all frontier firms, all countries, all future hardware generations), what happens to the proposal if the most safety-conscious developer is not the most capable one, and whether the measure degrades gracefully or collapses entirely when one party defects. For each requirement, tell me whether it's genuinely forced by the competitive logic or whether it's a preference of one particular camp -- say, the people who think coordination between governments is realistic versus the people who think only technical measures that don't require trust have a chance.

Two further requests. First, if some subset of these requirements looks jointly unsatisfiable -- for instance, if being cheap enough to adopt voluntarily, robust to defection, and effective against a system more capable than its operators can't all hold at once -- say so plainly, because that seems to me like the most important thing a list like this could reveal. Second, at the end, briefly run two or three representative proposals against the list (a purely technical alignment technique, a voluntary pause or slowdown, and a compute-governance scheme would do), but keep the scoring short: it's the requirements and their justifications I care about, since I can apply them myself once I trust them.
