I'm preparing a written statement of my positions for a philosophy of biology qualifying exam, and before I submit it I'd like an audit of whether the package actually holds together, since I've assembled it piecemeal over two years of coursework and never stress-tested the joints. Here is where I've landed. (1) I think Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique is basically right: adaptationist storytelling is a real methodological vice, traits get atomized and assigned functions they may never have been selected for, and developmental and architectural byproducts are systematically under-appreciated. (2) At the same time, I accept the Williams-style inference from Adaptation and Natural Selection: when a trait shows complex functional design — a camera eye, a bat's echolocation — natural selection is the only remotely adequate explanation, and we don't need a population-genetic demonstration in each case before concluding the trait is an adaptation; the improbability of drift producing such design does the work. (3) I hold the standard exaptation point that current utility is not evidence about historical origin — a structure now used for flight may have been assembled under selection for thermoregulation, or for nothing at all. (4) I think optimality models (foraging theory, sex-ratio models, and so on) are the rigorous way to test adaptation hypotheses and are clearly preferable to verbal plausibility arguments. (5) I'm persuaded by the Lynch-style drift-first picture: drift is the null hypothesis, selection must be demonstrated rather than assumed, and much of genome and trait evolution is near-neutral.

What I'd like from you is a consistency check, not an overview of the debate. Go through the commitments pairwise where it matters and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each real tension is — I'd rather have them ranked than treated as equally problematic. For each genuine tension, tell me concretely what would have to give: which commitment gets revised, and what it costs me to revise it (for instance, does weakening one of them undermine the reason I originally held another?). Two things I specifically want you to avoid: first, don't just tell me that (1) and (2) conflict because "Gould versus the adaptationists" is a famous fight — I want to know whether the actual content of my commitments conflicts, given how I've stated them. Second, don't resolve anything by redefining my terms into triviality; if two of my views can be reconciled, show me the reconciliation costs something or nothing, and say which. Finally, if there's a single revision that buys the most overall coherence, identify it and tell me what kind of evolutionary thinker I become after making it.
