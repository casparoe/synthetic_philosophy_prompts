I'm a math major taking a philosophy of mathematics seminar, and a certain way of selling structuralism has now come up twice — once in my textbook and once from a classmate giving a presentation — and I want to know how much argumentative weight it can actually bear. The pitch goes like this: mathematical objects are like positions in a game or an organization. The number 2 is like the position of shortstop in baseball, or white's king's bishop in chess. Nothing about what fills the position matters; what makes 2 the number 2 is exhausted by its place in a network of relations — it succeeds 1 and is succeeded by 3, it is the only even prime, and so on — just as what makes the shortstop the shortstop is a role defined by the rulebook, whoever happens to be standing there on a given afternoon. This picture is then used to dissolve the classic puzzle, going back to Benacerraf, about whether 2 is an element of 4. On the usual von Neumann construction of the naturals inside set theory it is; on Zermelo's older construction it isn't; and both constructions deliver everything arithmetic needs. The structuralist concludes there is no fact of the matter either way, because asking about the internal composition or membership profile of 2 is like asking what the shortstop position is made of — a category error rather than an unsolved problem.

Here is my worry, stated crudely. Positions in a game are positions *for* things: somebody stands at shortstop, a wooden piece occupies the bishop's square. So the analogy seems to smuggle objects back in through the side door — unless we say there are no fillers at all, only pure positions, in which case the positions themselves start to look like objects of a strange new kind, complete with their own identity puzzles (is the 2-position in the natural-number structure the very same position as the 2-position in the rationals, or in the reals?). I'd like a proper audit rather than a verdict for or against structuralism itself. First, lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the players or pieces, the rulebook, the positions, and the "whoever is standing there." Second, identify where the mapping breaks down, and — this is the part I care most about — sort the disanalogies into those that genuinely threaten the intended use (dissolving the 2-is-an-element-of-4 question) and those that are merely cosmetic. Third, tell me whether the "no fact of the matter" conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy merely relocates the identity problem, trading "what is the number 2?" for "what is a position, and under what conditions are positions in distinct structures identical?" Finally, if the shortstop analogy can't carry the dissolution on its own, is there a better one in the vicinity — roles in a play whose script exists independently of any performance, slots in a tournament bracket, something else — or should we conclude that structuralism is the sort of view that analogies can gesture toward but never argue for?
