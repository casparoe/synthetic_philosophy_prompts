I work at a mid-sized research funder and once a year I sit on a panel that decides whether to renew big multi-lab "programmes" -- not single projects, but 5-10 year bets with a shared theoretical core, dozens of PIs, and budgets in the tens of millions. At the moment our renewal criteria are basically citation counts plus vibes, and it shows: we've axed programmes right before they paid off, and we've kept zombie programmes alive for years because the senior people are charming and the field is fashionable.

I want to rebuild our renewal process around Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes, since that's the closest thing philosophy of science has to a theory of when a research tradition is thriving vs coasting. So: write me a decision procedure the panel can actually run. Constraints on the design:

- Panel members are working scientists, not historians or philosophers, and we get about two hours per programme, mostly from a written dossier plus a one-hour interview with the programme leadership.
- It has to operationalize the progressive/degenerating distinction in a way you can assess from documents: what evidence in a dossier counts as a programme making progressive problem shifts vs just patching its protective belt to absorb failures. In particular, give us tests for telling a genuinely novel prediction or content-increasing move apart from an accommodation cooked up after the fact, and say what to do when the panel can't agree on whether a prediction was really novel.
- It has to build in Lakatos's own warnings: no instant rationality, so a string of anomalies by itself can't be grounds for termination; programmes go through slumps and come back; and a degenerating programme only really dies when there's a rival to shift to. So the procedure needs a "what if there's no successor programme in sight" branch, and some notion of a probationary status rather than a binary fund/kill.
- It must say explicitly where the procedure hands off to human judgment -- e.g., deciding what even counts as the programme's hard core, since that's often only visible in retrospect -- rather than pretending those are checklist items.
- Say what the procedure cannot do. Lakatos himself never gave a knock-down kill criterion, so don't let the procedure smuggle one in.

Then run it on three hypothetical dossiers (make them up, but make them realistic): (1) a programme with a great track record a decade ago that's spent five years explaining away failed predictions, currently headed by famous people; (2) a scrappy young programme with two striking confirmed predictions but a messy, unstable theoretical core; (3) a programme with no anomalies it can't handle but also nothing new predicted in six years, and no rival on the horizon. I want at least one verdict I'd find uncomfortable -- my instinct would be to kill (3) and fund (1) forever -- so dont soften the conclusions to match.
