A friend ran the Condorcet jury theorem on me last week and I still can't find the exit. His argument, roughly: (1) Suppose people are even slightly better than a coin flip at answering some yes/no question — say 51% reliable. (2) If their judgments are independant, the majority verdict of a large enough group is correct with probability arbitrarily close to 1. That's just the jury theorem plus the law of large numbers. (3) On most factual questions people bother having opinions about, they're at least slightly better than chance. (4) So we could settle almost any factual question — whether the suspect did it, whether a recession is coming, whether some drug works — just by polling a million ordinary people and going with the majority, no trials, labs, or experts needed. (5) That's obviously absurd, so something in (1)–(3) has to give. He thinks the culprit is (3), i.e. majority opinion tracks truth far less than we assume. I suspect the independence condition in (2) is doing all the real work, but every way I try to explain why independence fails for mass polling seems to also make it fail for actual juries and expert panels, where majority verdicts do seem to carry real epistemic weight. Which step should I actually reject, why does it look so plausible, and what does rejecting it cost me in the cases — juries, forecast aggregation, wisdom-of-crowds stuff — where majority voting genuinely does seem to work?
