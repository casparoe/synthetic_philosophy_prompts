I'm a junior in an upper-level philosophy of mind course, and our professor has asked each student to draft one practice essay question for the midterm study sessions, complete with a model answer and a marking scheme. My assigned topic is Dennett's discussion of the color phi phenomenon and the rivalry between what he calls Orwellian and Stalinesque theories of revision in consciousness.

Here is the background I'll circulate to the study group, which the question can presuppose. In the color phi experiment, subjects see a red dot flash at one location and then, after a brief interval, a green dot flash at a nearby location. Subjects report seeing a single dot moving from the first location to the second and changing color partway along the path — which generates the puzzle that the color change is experienced at a point on the trajectory before the second dot has even appeared. An Orwellian theory says the experience unfolded one way (the dot moved, then turned green at the end) and memory of it was doctored afterward, so that the subject misremembers the change as occurring mid-path. A Stalinesque theory says the conscious experience was staged late, assembled only after the brain received the second stimulus, with the mid-path change written into the show before it ever ran. Dennett argues that the contest between these two accounts is not substantive — that once both sides have been given enough latitude in describing the subpersonal processing, there is no fact of the matter left for them to disagree about.

The question I want should be unanswerable by reciting this summary. I want it to push students on whether the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction marks a real difference at all: whether there could in principle be evidence — behavioral, introspective, or neural — that would settle which revision occurred, and what would follow for the very notion of "the time at which an experience occurred" if no such evidence could exist. To make this concrete rather than purely abstract, the question should incorporate the following hypothetical variant: on some trials, subjects are told in advance what color the second dot will be, and on other trials no second dot is ever presented. Students should have to say what the Orwellian and Stalinesque accounts would each predict in these variant trials, and what it would show about the rivalry between them if the predictions turned out identical, or if they diverged. I'd also like the question to force students to address the objection that Dennett's conclusion is just a disguised skepticism about memory or about the precision of timing measurements, and to explain why it is (or is not) something stronger.

Please write: (1) one essay question, answerable in about 75 minutes, that builds in the variant-trials setup and forces students to engage with these issues rather than summarize them; (2) a model answer of roughly 600 words that takes a defensible position — it may side with Dennett or against him, but it must state explicitly what the two accounts would have to predict in the variant trials in order to count as genuinely rival hypotheses, and it must draw the right conclusion from whichever way that goes; (3) a marking scheme out of 20 points, organized around five required moves with the points each move is worth, and for each move a one-sentence note on the way students typically go wrong (for instance, treating the two theories as disagreeing about the fact of when the color change was "really" experienced, rather than about whether that question has a determinate answer).
