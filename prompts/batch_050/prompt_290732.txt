A friend of mine from the cognitive science program and I have been arguing about functionalism since a reading group last month, and he has backed me into a corner I can't reason my way out of. He cheerfully concedes the key move isn't original with him, but his version of it is the one I can't answer, and it runs like this:

(1) Functionalism about the mind is true: whether a system has a mind, and which mental states it has, depends entirely on whether it realizes the right functional organization — the right pattern of relations among inputs, outputs, and internal states. What the system is made of is irrelevant; that is the whole point of multiple realizability.

(2) Any sufficiently complex hunk of matter can be described as realizing any finite functional organization you like. Consider the wall of my office. It contains astronomical numbers of molecules in constant motion. For any particular stretch of actual computation — say, the five minutes I spent yesterday deliberating about whether to skip a committee meeting — there exists a mapping from the wall's molecular states during those five minutes onto the states of that deliberation, such that the wall's state sequence mirrors the deliberation's state sequence step for step. The mapping is hideously gerrymandered, but nothing in functionalism forbids gerrymandered mappings: the whole doctrine is that the details of the medium don't matter.

(3) So by (1) and (2), my office wall was, during those five minutes, realizing my deliberation — it was weighing the meeting, feeling vaguely guilty about wanting to skip it, and so on.

(4) But the wall wasn't doing any of that. Nothing was.

(5) So either functionalism is false, or multiple realizability must be restricted after all — and the medium does matter.

My friend takes this to refute the standard computationalist picture and has lately become enthusiastic about the view that minds depend essentially on the right kind of biological stuff. I, meanwhile, find functionalism deeply attractive and would very much like to keep it — unrestricted multiple realizability included, if possible. What I can't do is accept (3), and I can't see where the argument slips. Each premise looks defensible on its own: (1) is just the view itself, (2) strikes me as a sober observation about the abundance of mappings, and (3) and (5) seem to follow. Which step do you reject? Why did that step look fine when it isn't — if it isn't? And what does rejecting it cost, in particular for someone who wants to keep the thought that a mind could in principle be built out of silicon, neurons, or beer cans indifferently? If you think the argument is in fact valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender and what the surrender does to the rest of the functionalist picture. One request: don't just reply that the conclusion is implausible. I know it's implausible. That's why I'm asking.
