I administer grants for a mid-sized regional arts council, and my most contentious recurring task is choosing the volunteer panelists who decide which artists, ensembles, and small museums get funded. We run four panels a year, drawing from a pool of perhaps sixty applicants — working artists, retired critics, teachers, passionate amateurs — and the board signs off on my slate each cycle. Lately we have had complaints from two directions at once: that the panels reward polished conformity, and that their judgments are mere personal preference wearing official dress. In casting about for a defensible basis on which to select judges, I went back to David Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste," which I last read in college. Hume concedes early on how hopeless the problem looks — "It is natural for us to seek a Standard of Taste; a rule, by which the various sentiments of men may be reconciled; at least, a decision, afforded, confirming one sentiment, and condemning another" — and then famously argues that the standard lies in the joint verdict of ideally qualified critics: those with sound reasoning, a delicate sensitivity to fine distinctions, extensive practice with the art form, wide experience of comparisons across works and traditions, and freedom from prejudice.

Write me a vetting procedure I can actually run each quarter: for each of Hume's five qualifications, say what observable evidence in an application or interview could serve as a proxy for it, what proxy is illegitimate (does twenty years of reviewing one genre count as practice or as narrowed vision?), and how to weight the qualifications when they come apart. Then address the two difficulties I suspect are fatal if ignored. First, the apparent circle: Hume seems to identify good judges by their agreement on works already agreed to be great, which suggests my procedure can only ever reproduce the existing canon — say whether that circle is real and, if so, how a procedure should handle it honestly rather than disguise it. Second, Hume himself admits that some disagreements are blameless and beyond any standard, tracing them to differences in people's temperaments and to the customs of their age and culture; our council funds work from several distinct communities and traditions, so tell me where exactly my procedure must stop and simply refuse to rank.

Run the finished procedure on three of my actual recurring applicant types: the veteran critic of a single art form who is dismissive of everything outside it; the young reviewer with dazzling range and no track record; and the beloved elder who champions her own community's tradition and whose judgment I suspect is prejudice in Hume's strict sense rather than expertise. Include at least one case where the procedure hands me an answer I will not want to give the board, and mark explicitly the points in the process where no procedure can substitute for my judgment and I will simply have to decide.
