I am grading a short-answer response for an undergraduate course on probability and philosophical methods. The exam question was: A chord of a unit circle is chosen at random. What is the probability that its length exceeds the side length of an equilateral triangle inscribed in the circle, namely √3? Derive the three standard Bertrand-paradox answers of 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4, explaining the randomization procedure behind each. Then assess whether any procedure is privileged merely because its parameterization represents each chord exactly once, what the principle of indifference contributes here, and what additional information would make the original question well-posed. Please grade the response below out of 20 points—9 for the calculations, 6 for the diagnosis of the paradox, and 5 for the discussion of parameterization and indifference. Give the numerical grade and specific comments identifying what deserves credit, what is mistaken or insufficiently justified, and how many points you would assign in each category.

The student wrote: Take the radius to be 1. First, fix one endpoint of the chord and choose the other uniformly around the circumference. If their angular separation is θ, the chord is longer than √3 exactly when θ lies between 2π/3 and 4π/3, so the probability is 1/3. Second, choose a diameter uniformly and then choose a point uniformly along it as the chord’s midpoint; the chord perpendicular to the diameter at distance d from the center has length 2√(1 − d²), which exceeds √3 when d < 1/2. Since d is uniform on [0,1], the probability is 1/2. Third, choose the midpoint uniformly over the disk. The favorable midpoints lie in the concentric disk of radius 1/2, whose area is one fourth of the original disk, so the probability is 1/4. The answers differ because random chord does not by itself specify a probability measure over the set of chords. The principle of indifference cannot select a measure without first being told which variables or outcomes should count as equally likely; a physical selection procedure or an independently justified invariance requirement would be needed. If a purely geometric default is required, however, the third method has the strongest claim: every chord has exactly one midpoint, whereas the endpoint construction represents each chord twice and the diameter construction gives a special ambiguous representation to diameters. Choosing the midpoint uniformly over the disk therefore avoids double-counting and makes 1/4 the most natural answer.
