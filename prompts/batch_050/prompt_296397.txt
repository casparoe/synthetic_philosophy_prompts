In the philosophy of science module I help teach, the raven paradox comes up every year, and every year my co-teacher dispatches it with the same analogy. Confirming "All ravens are black" by inspecting a white shoe, he tells the students, is like checking a conditional by checking its contrapositive: "All non-black things are non-ravens" is logically the same claim, so an observation that supports the one supports the other for exactly the same reason and with exactly the same force, and anyone who balks at "indoor ornithology" has simply failed to notice that the two investigations are one investigation. He backs the move up by reading out this passage from Carl Hempel's "Studies in the Logic of Confirmation" (1945), where Hempel is discussing the hypothesis that all sodium salts burn yellow and the case of holding a piece of pure ice in a flame that does not turn yellow:

This result would confirm the assertion, "Whatever does not burn yellow is not sodium salt", and consequently, by virtue of the equivalence condition, it would confirm the original formulation. Why does this impress us as paradoxical? The reason becomes clear when we compare the previous situation with the case of an experiment where an object whose chemical constitution is as yet unknown to us is held into a flame and fails to turn it yellow, and where subsequent analysis reveals it to contain no sodium salt. This outcome, we should no doubt agree, is what was to be expected on the basis of the hypothesis ... thus the data here obtained constitute confirming evidence for the hypothesis. ... In the seemingly paradoxical cases of confirmation, we are often not actually judging the relation of the given evidence, E alone to the hypothesis H ... we tacitly introduce a comparison of H with a body of evidence which consists of E in conjunction with an additional amount of information which we happen to have at our disposal; in our illustration, this information includes the knowledge (1) that the substance used in the experiment is ice, and (2) that ice contains no sodium salt. If we assume this additional information as given, then, of course, the outcome of the experiment can add no strength to the hypothesis under consideration.

I suspect my colleague's contrapositive analogy is doing more work than the passage licenses, since the passage seems to say that whether the same observation confirms the hypothesis depends on what else the observer already knows, which is not something the bare logical equivalence of a conditional and its contrapositive can see. Could you audit the analogy: set out explicitly what maps onto what (logical equivalence, evidential support, the equivalence condition); identify where the comparison breaks down and which of the disanalogies actually matter for his dismissive conclusion, as opposed to being mere imperfections; say whether his conclusion -- that the white shoe confirms the raven hypothesis in just the way a black raven does, so the "paradox" is nothing but a failure to notice an equivalence -- survives Hempel's own diagnosis; and, if not, propose a better analogy for the point the passage is really making.
