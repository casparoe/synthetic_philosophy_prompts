I co-teach a seminar for law students and a few philosophy graduate students on proof in the courtroom, and next week I'm responsible for a ninety-minute session on naked statistical evidence -- the puzzle posed by cases like the blue bus (a bus company runs four-fifths of the buses on the road where a hit-and-run occurred, yet a court won't find it liable on that evidence alone) and the gatecrasher (a rodeo or stadium knows, on solid evidence, that most attendees entered without tickets, yet that fact alone seems an unacceptable basis for finding any particular attendee liable). Don't teach the material to me; design how I should teach it. The session must move the students from finding these verdicts intuitive to understanding why the law resists them, without collapsing into the lazy answer that courts simply distrust numbers.

Structure your plan in these sections, in this order:

**The dependency sequence** (about 250 words): the order in which the components must be introduced and what each presupposes. I suspect the students must first see that the statistics in these cases are perfectly sound -- the posterior probability of liability really is high -- before any puzzle exists; then that the verdicts nonetheless feel right; and only then that a principled account is needed. Correct my ordering if it's wrong and explain what depends on what.

**The carrying example** (about 150 words): choose either the blue bus or the gatecrasher as the case the whole session is built around, say why that one, and specify exactly what features the example must have and must lack to do its work (e.g., no possibility of acquiring better evidence, no negligence in the statistics themselves) -- and what role the rejected example should play as a contrast case later.

**The misconception map** (about 250 words): the errors the students will predictably form, each flagged with the point in your sequence where it should be headed off. I anticipate at least these: that the problem is unreliable or soft statistics; that a sufficient probability threshold (say, 95%) fixes everything; that this is just base-rate neglect in disguise; and that the difference is merely evidential strength rather than kind. Add any I haven't anticipated, particularly ones the law students will bring that the philosophers won't, and vice versa.

**Candidate explanations, staged** (about 200 words): which live accounts (the demand for particularized or case-specific evidence, practical rather than mathematical probability, worries about what a statistics-based verdict does to the standing of the individual before the court) should be presented, in what order, and which one the session should openly decline to settle.

**The comprehension check** (about 100 words): one question or variant case to pose at the end whose answer would show a student grasped the distinction between having high probability of liability and having the kind of evidence a court accepts -- not merely that they can recite the examples.

Total around 950 words.
