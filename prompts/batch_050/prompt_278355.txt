So I've been a LW/AF reader for years and I keep coming back to the old threads about heuristic arguments and the "presumption of independence" -- the move where you estimate how often something happens by pretending the relevant events are independent, even though you know full well they aren't. I want to push my understanding past the standard examples, so here's the situation.

The cases where this move shows up that I already know, and don't need re-explained:

1. Cramér's random model: treat each n as "prime with probability 1/log n", independently, and use it to predict prime gap statistics.
2. Twin primes: p and p+2 both prime, with the local-factor correction giving the twin prime constant.
3. Goldbach: count representations of even n as p + (n-p) under independence plus local corrections, get a plausible density of representations.
4. Riemann hypothesis vibes: the Möbius function behaves like a random ±1 sequence, so its partial sums grow like a random walk, roughly sqrt(n), which is basically what RH says.
5. The classic failure modes: Maier's matrix method showing Cramér's model breaks down for primes in short intervals, and the trivial point that n and n+1 are never both prime, i.e. actual dependence at small scales.

What I want: one more application of the presumption of independence as a heuristic, NOT in analytic number theory (so nothing about primes, zeros of L-functions, etc.), and not a re-skin of anything above. Ideally from some other area -- combinatorics, CS, statistical physics, whatever. It should be a case where someone (or you, hypothetically) could run the "pretend these are independent and see what density you get" argument and learn something. Then tell me honestly whether the independence presumption there is actually defensible or whether it's the kind of place where hidden correlations will eat you alive, and what a formalization of the presumption (like, stating precisely when the heuristic is licensed) would have to say about your example.

Give me the answer as a JSON object with these fields:

- "application": name of the problem/area, one line
- "heuristic_argument": the actual independence-based estimate, spelled out enough that I could redo the calculation, ~100-150 words
- "known_result_or_prediction": what the heuristic concludes, and whether the conclusion is a known theorem, a conjecture, or just folklore
- "not_a_reskin_because": why this is structurally different from items 1-5, 2-3 sentences
- "dependence_risks": where hidden correlations could break the heuristic in this case, and how badly
- "verdict": one of "trustworthy heuristic", "useful but fragile", or "independence assumption is doing all the work", plus a one-sentence justification
- "formalization_note": what a general criterion for when the presumption of independence is legitimate would have to require in this case, 2-3 sentences

Feel free to editoralize in the verdict field if you think the whole genre of argument is shakier than people let on.
