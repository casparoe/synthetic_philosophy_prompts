A colleague in the mathematics department recently served as an alternate juror and has been pressing the following argument on me ever since. Consider two stock cases. First, a plaintiff is struck at night by a bus whose driver did not stop, and the only proof that the bus belonged to the Blue Bus Company is that company's share of traffic on the road. Second, the same case, except that the proof is a single eyewitness who identifies the bus as blue. His reasoning:

(1) Whether a body of evidence warrants a verdict depends solely on how probable it renders the claim at issue; nothing else about evidence — its source, its form, its particularity — is relevant.

(2) The probability a piece of evidence confers is a function of its reliability, and reliability can only ever be established statistically: a witness's accuracy is a frequency estimated from the past performance of witnesses of her kind, just as a company's traffic share is a frequency estimated from past counts of buses.

(3) Suppose the witness's errors are symmetric and the prior odds even, so that her testimony raises the probability that the bus was blue to exactly 0.8 — precisely the figure the traffic-share record yields on its own.

(4) Then the two bodies of evidence are epistemically on a par: the same probability, supported by credentials of the same, statistical, kind.

(5) Courts, however, will send the eyewitness case to the jury while dismissing the traffic-share case — and would dismiss it even if the company's share were 0.98.

(6) Therefore either courts should be willing to enter verdicts on naked statistics — holding every patron of a rodeo liable for the gate fee once it is shown that 980 of 1,000 climbed the fence — or the statistical credentials underpinning eyewitness testimony are insufficient, which would sweep away most of what passes for evidence anywhere, since every reliability claim is known only through group frequencies.

Each disjunct strikes me as intolerable: the first licenses verdicts we know to be mistaken in an exactly known number of instances, and the second discredits nearly all testimony, fingerprint and DNA evidence included. My colleague concludes that the law of evidence is simply incoherent. I would like to retain (1), or something close to it, and I am unwilling to write the courts off as superstitious; but I cannot locate the step where the argument goes wrong — or, if it goes wrong nowhere, which premise is cheapest to surrender. Which step do you reject, why did it look acceptable, and what does rejecting it cost?
