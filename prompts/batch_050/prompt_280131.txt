A coworker of mine has this habit of explaining every drawn-out conflict with the dollar auction, that game where an auctioneer sells a one-dollar bill with the twist that the runner-up bidder also has to pay what they bid, so people bid past a dollar just to avoid losing what they've already put in. He used it last week to argue that our company should kill a product line we've sunk three years into (we're "bidding past the dollar," his words), and then the same evening I saw a pundit deploy the same analogy about a country that keeps escalating a war it can't win, as if that settles that withdrawal is obviously correct and staying in is just a cognitive glitch. I get the basic shape -- costs already sunk, each side keeps paying to not be the one who paid for nothing -- but I suspect the analogy is doing a lot of quiet work, because real escalations don't have a fixed prize worth exactly one dollar, the rules aren't announced in advance, and "cutting your losses" in a war or a career or a marriage isn't a free action the way folding in an auction is.

Can you audit how far the dollar auction analogy actually holds when it's used this way? Give me your answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro or wrap-up: (1) the correspondence mapping, what plays the role of the prize, the bids, the auctioneer, and the runner-up rule in a case like a prolonged war or a failing project; (2) where the mapping breaks, i.e., the features of the auction that real escalation lacks (known fixed stakes, exogenous rules, no learning about the prize mid-game, etc.); (3) what the analogy smuggles in -- especially the assumption that continuing is irrational since you "should have known" the trap, and whether that survives once escalation might be rational ex ante or the prize can change in value; (4) whether the punchline people use it for (withdraw early, anyone who stays is escalation-trapped) still follows once the disanalogies are on the table; (5) if the analogy can't carry that punchline, what comparison would do better.
