As a novelist, I am assembling a companion story for a novella built around Borges's meditations on time, and I want the story itself rather than an essay about it. Write me a short story in exactly five scenes, with no prologue and no epilogue, on the following design.

Each scene takes place on the same veranda, at the same hour of the evening, in years separated by decades. In every scene the same things happen, as nearly as you can manage: the same guest arrives, the same exchange of words, the same rain beginning, the same object noticed on the table. The design is meant to dramatize a specific argument Borges presses in his writing on time: that two moments between which no difference can be discerned are not two moments at all but numerically one and the same, so that an exactly repeated evening does not recur -- it simply is, once, however many times we are inclined to count it. The story should leave a careful reader genuinely unable to say how many evenings it contains.

One character in the story holds this view and one resists it, but their disagreement must be carried by the events and by the architecture of the scenes, never by dialogue that lectures. Across the five scenes, introduce one small variation each time -- the guest addressed by a different name, the rain arriving a breath earlier, a sentence spoken in a different order -- and arrange the variations so the reader is forced to judge, scene by scene, which alteration is enough to constitute a new moment and which leaves the single original moment intact. In the final scene, let the resisting character appear to win the argument in dramatic terms while the Borgesian character wins it in the reader's judgment -- or the reverse; choose one and commit fully.

The prose should be calm, exact, and unhurried, after the manner of the essay-fictions it answers to. Roughly 2,000 words in total, with the five scenes of approximately equal length.
