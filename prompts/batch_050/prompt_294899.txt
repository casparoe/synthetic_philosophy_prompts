There's a long-running dispute among people who work on Buddhist ethics about whether it's basically a form of consequentialism. One side points to things like the huge emphasis on compassion and reducing suffering for all sentient beings, and to stories like the one where the Buddha in a past life kills a man who is about to murder five hundred merchants, willingly taking the karmic hit himself because the outcome is so much better. The other side says karma is exactly the sort of thing consequentialism can't accomodate: killing corrupts the killer's own mind regardless of how the outcomes shake out, intention matters more than results, and the whole path is about gradually cultivating states of character, which sounds much more like virtue ethics. Both sides have been making roughly these moves for thirty years and as far as I can tell nobody has budged.

My suspicion is the debate is partly fake: the classical texts don't contain a systematic ethical theory in the Western sense at all, so both camps are squeezing the material into a taxonomy (consequentialist / deontological / virtue) that was built for other theories, and the real disagreement is about which box distorts the material least, not about anything actually in the texts. Is that right, or is there a substantive disagreement underneath -- about what the texts commit a practitioner to, or about what a view has to look like to count as consequentialist? I want a diagnosis with reasons, engaging what each side's best arguments actually are rather than a survey, plus an honest answer about what would count as progress here: a sharper distinction, a closer reading of particular texts, or just agreeing the question is ill-posed.
