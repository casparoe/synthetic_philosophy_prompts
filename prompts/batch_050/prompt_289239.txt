Since Hume first pressed the point, philosophers have tried to explain how a promise — a mere utterance — can generate a moral obligation where none existed before, and the debate seems genuinely stuck. Expectation-based accounts are told they confuse disappointing someone with wronging them; assurance accounts are told they misclassify promises made without any reliance; conventional-practice accounts are told that an immoral promise can still wrong someone even where no useful convention exists; and views positing a normative power to bind oneself are accused of simply asserting the very thing everyone else finds mysterious. Each side knows the other's best objections and keeps going anyway. My suspicion is that this is not one disagreement but at least two: some parties are asking how a voluntary act can create a duty, while others are asking what, exactly, is wrong with breaking a promise — and these need not have the same answer. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a single substantive dispute underneath, concerning whether obligations can ever be created at will, what counts as an explanation in ethics, or which cases should be treated as fixed data? Identify what kind of disagreement this really is, support the diagnosis with what each position actually claims rather than with summaries, and state what would count as progress — a sharper distinction, a decisive case, an account of obligation-creation that both sides could test against their commitments — or else argue that no resolution should be expected, and why.
