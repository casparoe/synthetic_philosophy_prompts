I am a doctoral candidate in law, and in three weeks I will present the central chapter of my dissertation at our faculty workshop. The chapter argues that the so-called paradox of self-amendment — the question whether a constitution's amendment clause can validly authorize its own repeal, restriction, or entrenchment — is not a paradox of legal self-reference at all. My claim is that the puzzle is an artifact of treating constitutional rules as timeless propositions that somehow act on themselves. If amendment clauses are instead understood as temporally indexed instructions issued by a constitution-making authority whose power is exhausted at promulgation, then an amendment clause amending itself is no more logically problematic than a legislature repealing its own enabling statute: institutionally dramatic, perhaps, but logically tame. The genuinely hard question, I argue, is not whether such amendments are coherent but which institution gets to decide their validity; provisions widely described as unamendable or eternity clauses are best analyzed not as logical limits on amendment but as reallocations of that decision to courts. The audience will be about a dozen constitutional law scholars — several originalists, several doctrinalists — plus one comparative constitutionalist and one legal philosopher who works on the logic of rules and is familiar with the game of Nomic and the philosophical literature on self-amendment. None of them do formal logic professionally except the philosopher.
