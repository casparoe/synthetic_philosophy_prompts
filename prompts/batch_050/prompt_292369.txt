So I write a column on science and big ideas, and my editor has finally had it with the translation-understanding food fight. You know the one. On one side you've got the Hofstadter-style skeptics: machine translation, they say, shuffles symbols into other symbols without ever latching onto what the sentences are about, and the fluency of the output just makes the hollowness harder to notice. On the other side you've got the modern understanding camp: today's large neural systems, they say, build internal representations rich enough that calling it "not real understanding" is just moving the goalposts, and a translation engine that handles metaphor, ambiguity, and register is doing whatever understanding turns out to be, even if it does it in an alien way.

For a long feature, I want to imagine these two camps sat down and did an adversarial collaboration, and I want you to draft the joint statement they'd come out with. Not a debate transcript, not a verdict on who's right -- the actual document both sides could sign.

Here's what it needs to contain:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign. These should be substantive, not mush -- e.g., things about what the systems demonstrably do, about what the skeptics' best arguments do and don't establish, about the failure of purely behavioral criteria to settle the question either way. If either camp would want to add a caveat before signing, rewrite the claim until neither needs one.

2. A disagreements table. Each row: one disagreement, with each side's position phrased in words that side would accept for itself (no strawman summaries like "they think it's just autocomplete"). At least one row should be flagged by you as largely verbal -- a place where the two camps are using "understanding" differently rather than disagreeing about the facts -- and say which row and why you think so.

3. The cruxes. For each one, a concrete test, case, or observation both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Here are three candidate cruxes I want included, plus any you think I'm missing:
   - Error profiles: does genuine understanding predict characteristic *kinds* of failure (sense-preserving mistakes, hesitation at wordplay) versus the brittle or absurd failures skeptics point to? What error pattern would each camp's view forbid?
   - Grounding: is perceptual or embodied contact with the world a necessary ingredient of understanding, or only the usual route humans take to it? Both camps need to say what case would show the other one is wrong about this, since neither side can just appeal to intuition here.
   - The "noticing" question: suppose a system translates a pun in a way that preserves the joke across languages, or flags that no equivalent exists and explains why. Does the skeptic have to concede anything, or can they redescribe every such case as pattern-matching? Is there any observation that would be *impossible* to redescribe that way?

Two ground rules from my editor. First, no appeals to "you just know it when you see it" -- every crux has to bottom out in something checkable, even if only in principle. Second, if by the end it turns out the remaining disagreement is about whether understanding comes in degrees rather than being binary, say so plainly and say what that does to the whole dispute, because honestly that might be the story.

Don't adjudicate a winner -- the point of the piece is what a good-faith collaboration can and cannot settle.
