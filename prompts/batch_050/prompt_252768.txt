Read Anton Chigurh in No Country for Old Men (the Coen brothers' film mainly, but bring in McCarthy's novel if it helps) as a test case in the debate over whether psychopaths actually know that what they do is wrong. The standard worry about the moral-knowledge view is that psychopaths can parrot moral judgments without really grasping them, and there's empirical work suggesting they blur the moral/conventional distinction, treating wrongness as just rule-violation. Chigurh is interesting because he seems almost the opposite: he talks constantly about rules, promises, and obligation, keeps his word even when it's costly, and seems genuinely contemptuous of people who act on mere convenience. So does he know killing is wrong, in the sense of knowing that, while lacking any grip on why (no moral understanding)? Or is his whole ethic of fate and the coin a way of treating murder as something more like breaking a private code — i.e., conventional wrongness dressed up in cosmic language? Pay attention to the coin-flip scenes and the final confrontation with Carla Jean, where she points out that the coin itself has no say in the matter and his answer falls apart in an interesting way. Also say where the character resists the psychopath reading entirely, because I don't want an essay that flattens him into a diagnosis. I'm writing this up for a moral psychology seminar and want a reading that's honest in both directions.
