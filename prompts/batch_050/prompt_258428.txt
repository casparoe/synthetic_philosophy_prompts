So my roommate and her friend from the philosophy deparment got into it at dinner last night and it's still bugging me, I keep flip flopping on who was actually right. The topic was basically: are there such things as rationally required priors? More specifically, whether maximum entropy gives you an objective starting point for Bayesian inference. From memory the argument went something like this:

Mara (the stats one): if all you know about a quantity is its mean, the only rational distribution over it is the maximum entropy distribution with that mean. Anything else encodes information you don't actually have. Entropy is the unique reasonable measure of uncertainty, so maximizing it is just what "don't assume stuff" means, made precise. Jaynes worked all this out decades ago.

Theo: "don't assume stuff" isn't well defined until you've picked your variables. Take the classic Bertrand paradox — pick a random chord of a circle, what's the probability it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? Three equally natural ways of randomizing give three different answers: a half, a third, a quarter. Indifference over one parameterization contradicts indifference over another. Same with the old wine-and-water puzzle. So max entropy's answer depends on a background measure, and the choice of measure is exactly where the assumptions hide.

Mara: Jaynes answered the Bertrand thing ages ago. Require your distribution to be invariant under the symmetries of the problem — translation, rotation, scaling — and the chord problem gets a unique answer (a half, as it happens). The naive answers only conflict because they ignore the problem's invariance structure. So the principle isn't "be indifferent," it's "respect the symmetries," and that's not arbitrary, it's forced by the setup.

Theo: but deciding which symmetries count is itself extra input. In the chord case the invariance argument works because there's an actual physical procedure generating the chords and the symmetries are facts about that procedure — fine, but then you're not deriving a prior from ignorance, you're deriving it from substantive knowledge about the setup. In cases where we genuinely have no setup — the probability this new drug helps patients, before any trial data — there's no transformation group to lean on, and max entropy on a continuous parameter is measure-dependent and arbitrary.

Mara: compare the alternative. Pure subjective Bayesianism, de Finetti style, says any coherent prior is as rational as any other, since all coherence requires is not being Dutch-bookable. That means a prior that says the sun almost certainly won't rise tomorrow is perfectly rational, which is absurd. So there have to be constraints on priors beyond coherence, and max entropy is the only principled candidate anyone's produced.

Theo: two things. First, nothing in the subjectivist view says crazy priors survive contact with data — conditionalize on enough sunrises and the crazy prior washes out. Second, washing out only happens when the priors agree on what's even possible — if my prior assigns zero to your hypothesis, no amount of evidence moves me. So the convergence results don't vindicate any prior, they just relocate where the dogmatism lives.

Mara: you're making my point for me. Assigning zero is exactly the kind of thing rationality should rule out, and subjectivism can't rule it out. And look at the track record: max entropy gives you the Gibbs distribution in stat mech, which is spectacularly successful, and it drives a bunch of image reconstruction methods too. If it were an arbitrary convention it wouldn't keep working.

Theo: that last move is a non sequitur. The Gibbs distribution works because of physical facts about huge collections of particles, not because rational agents are required to maximize entropy. Statistical mechanics is evidence about the world, not about epistemology. You're conflating "max entropy succeeds as physics" with "max entropy is a norm of rational belief."
