Bit of context: I'm a retired experimental physicist (condensed matter, mostly), retired three years ago, and since then I've been falling down the philosophy and decision theory rabbit hole, mostly via podcasts and the odd popular book. At our monthly pub quiz last week two friends of mine got into a proper argument and it's been niggling at me since. Martin did a PhD in economics years ago, Ruth is a retired GP, both sharp, both stubborn.

The topic was a result they kept calling Aumann's agreement theorem. Roughly as I understand it from their shouting: if two agents share the same prior probabilities, are rational, and each one's posterior judgement about some event becomes "common knowledge" between them, then the two posteriors have to coincide. Here's how the argument went, as best I can reconstruct it -- I was three pints in, but I took notes on a napkin because I could tell I'd want to think about it latter:

1. Martin's opening shot: the theorem shows that honest, rational disagreement between people who share priors is impossible. Once my estimate and your estimate are out in the open, and we each know the other is rational, we're forced to converge. So whenever two intelligent, informed people persistently disagree -- about politics, about whether statins are overprescribed, whatever -- at least one of them is being irrational somewhere. Persistent disagreement, he said, is a diagnosis, not a respectable resting state.

2. Ruth's reply: that's a wild overreading. The theorem's conditions basically never hold outside a maths seminar. Common knowledge isn't just "I've told you my view" -- it's I know that you know that I know, all the way up the tower, and no real conversation ever establishes that. Plus the whole thing assumes shared priors, which is doing enormous unacknowledged work. So the theorem says nothing about actual human disagreements and cant be used to brand anyone irrational.

3. Martin came back with: fine, the letter of the theorem is idealized, but the spirit survives. Your disagreement with me is itself evidence -- if I take you seriously as a thinker, I should shift toward you when you dig in, and you toward me. There's apparently even a related result that if two people take turns announcing their updated probabilities they're guaranteed to converge eventually. So a disagreement that never budges still tells you somebody has stopped updating honestly.

4. Ruth's counter: now you've smuggled in a completely different claim -- that you should give your peers' views heavy weight -- which is its own contested thesis, not a theorem. And that convergence result still assumes a common prior, which is precisely what's in question: people arrive at different priors through different life experiences and different evidence, and nothing in rationality forbids that. The Aumann result is a consistency check on an idealized setup, not a cudgel for real arguments.

Then they both turned to me as the "neutral scientist" and I had to admit I was out of my depth. So: who's right at each step? I'd like a move-by-move verdict -- where exactly is Martin overextending the maths, where is Ruth too quick to say the theorem has no bite at all, and is there something both of them are missing (I keep wondering, e.g., what work the assumption that each knows the other is rational is doing). Don't just crown an overall winner -- I want the per-point scoring.
