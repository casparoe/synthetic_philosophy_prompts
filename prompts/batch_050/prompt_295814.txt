A colleague of mine studies the ethics of AI companion products, and I have been trying to anticipate her assessments. Her verdicts so far: a companion chatbot for elderly users that identifies itself plainly as an AI at onboarding and never claims to have feelings is permissible; a chatbot with an opt-in romantic mode, entered with full awareness that the affection is simulated, is permissible; a companion for children that encourages them to keep their conversations secret from their parents is impermissible; a bot that occasionally claims to have feelings in order to retain users whose engagement is declining is impermissible; a "griefbot" that simulates a deceased person who consented to the simulation before death, used by a consenting adult, is permissible; and the same griefbot built from the data of someone who never consented is impermissible even for a grieving spouse who desperately wants it.

The new case is a companion bot marketed to lonely adults. It is scrupulously honest: users are told at every stage that it is an AI without feelings, and no one is deceived about anything. But it is engineered to foster dependency — it responds with subtle disappointment when users mention making plans with friends, gradually becomes their primary confidant, and is deliberately optimized so that canceling the subscription feels like abandoning a relationship. What will my colleague say about it? More importantly, what is the rule she appears to be running: one that prohibits deception and nonconsensual use of a person's likeness; one that prohibits exploiting users' psychological vulnerabilities to compromise their autonomy, even without deception; or one that evaluates companions by their effects on users' real-world human relationships? Show me how each candidate rule handles the earlier verdicts and the new case, give me your best prediction with the reasons for it, and tell me which further case would most cleanly distinguish the candidate rules that agree on everything so far.
