I'm writing a piece arguing that everyday conventions -- tipping, queueing, not jaywalking -- generate real moral obligations, and I'm collecting the standard arguments for why breaking them is wrong even when the individual act harms no one. So far I've got:

(1) The free-riding/fairness argument: the convention's benefits depend on near-universal compliance, so the defector takes the advantages while offloading the costs onto those who comply.
(2) The erosion argument: defections are visible, visible defections chip away at everyone's willingness to comply, so each act contributes to a collectively worse outcome even if harmless in itself.
(3) The reasonable-expectations argument: people organise thier plans around the convention, so the queue-jumper wrongs the particular people behind him, not just "the system".
(4) The expressive argument: stiffing the waiter or cutting the queue says something -- that your money or time matters more than theirs -- and the wrong is in the message, not the outcome.

What's the fifth? It needs to be genuinely different in structure from these four, not a special case or a blend of them. And once you've given it, tell me whether it extends to the harder case: the visitor who rejects the convention itself (say, someone from a no-tipping country who thinks tipping is a bad institution full stop), or whether it only condemns people who accept the practice but free-ride on it. Answer in British English please.
