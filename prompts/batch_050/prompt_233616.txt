Since retiring from experimental physics I have been attending a philosophy reading group, and last week two members fell into an argument about Zeno's arrow paradox that I would like adjudicated. Martin presented the paradox as decisive: at each instant the arrow occupies a space exactly equal to itself, nothing can move during a single instant, and since time is composed of instants the arrow never moves at all. Catherine answered that motion just is occupying different positions at different times -- there is nothing further for motion to be -- so Zeno's demand that movement be happening *within* an instant is a category mistake. Martin replied that this makes motion a cinematographic illusion: each instant's state is intrinsically identical to that of a stationary arrow, so the theory explains motion away rather than explaining it. Catherine countered that instantaneous velocity, defined as a limit over intervals, is exactly what velocity is; given continuous time, the positions-at-times determine the entire trajectory, and Martin was confusing the psychology of perceiving motion with the metaphysics of it. Martin's final move was to note that if no instantaneous state distinguishes motion from rest, then motion is a property only of the whole four-dimensional block of time, not of the world at any moment -- a steep metaphysical price for disarming a paradox. Catherine said she was happy to pay it.

Please begin with your overall verdict in one sentence, with no preamble and no restating of the question, and then adjudicate round by round: for each of the five exchanges, say who has the better of that specific point and why, identify any conflations either party is committing, and finish by stating what both are missing about what the arrow paradox actually targets.
