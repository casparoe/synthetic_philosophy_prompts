I sit on the review board of a small private foundation whose charter directs us to fund heterodox science — proposals that mainstream agencies will not touch — while excluding pseudoscience. Each year we receive roughly two hundred proposals spanning anomalous cognition, alternative medicine, unconventional energy, and heterodox psychology. Initial screening is done by generalist staff, not specialists, who have about twenty minutes per proposal before deciding whether to reject, flag for expert review, or advance. Several board members want to adopt Popper's falsifiability criterion as our bright-line screen, and I have been asked to determine whether it can be turned into a workable procedure.

My first attempt was a single rule: reject any proposal whose central claim could not, even in principle, be shown false by some conceivable observation. It failed three times in one funding cycle. First, it rejected a proposal to reconstruct the transmission route of a particular nineteenth-century epidemic from archival records — a one-off historical claim that no observation could falsify in the way a universal law can be, yet plainly empirical. Second, it stalled on a parapsychology proposal predicting that subjects would guess concealed cards at a rate slightly but reliably above chance: no finite run of outcomes strictly falsifies a probabilistic hypothesis, so the rule said reject, yet this looked like the most conventionally empirical proposal in the pile. Third, it passed a rigorously designed, pre-registered, double-blind trial of homeopathic dilutions, since the trial's hypothesis is eminently falsifiable — and several board members consider funding exactly that kind of proposal a misuse of the charter, whatever its testability.

Please write the decision procedure we should actually use: an ordered sequence of tests a generalist screener can run in twenty minutes that preserves what is right in falsificationism — the contrast between claims that take empirical risks and claims that accommodate every outcome — while surviving the three failures above. For each step, state the question the screener asks, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates in a rejection, and where it hands off to a specialist or to the board because the question is one of values or expertise rather than screening. The procedure must say explicitly how to handle probabilistic hypotheses, singular historical or existential claims, and auxiliary hypotheses — that is, give the screener a usable rule for distinguishing a legitimate appeal to a confound from an immunizing maneuver that protects a theory from any result. It should also state which distinctions from the philosophical literature had to survive the simplification and which could be dropped without loss.

Then run the finished procedure on three proposals: (a) the homeopathy trial described above; (b) a pre-registered study testing whether dream content predicts next-day mood better than a chance baseline, with an explicit analysis plan; and (c) a proposal to develop a comprehensive theory of a biological "vital energy," whose adherents have historically explained every null result as the measuring instrument disturbing the field. At least one of these should produce a verdict the board will not like; identify which, and say whether that is a defect of the procedure or of the board's expectations. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do — in particular, whether it can mark any difference between "testable and almost certainly false" and "pseudoscientific," and what a funder should do with proposals that fall in the gap between those verdicts.
