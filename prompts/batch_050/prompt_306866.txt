In discussions of aligning AI systems built from current methods -- large models trained by gradient descent, fine-tuned with human feedback, and shaped by reinforcement learning -- one analogy does an enormous amount of quiet argumentative work: training a model is like raising a child. Proponents of prosaic alignment lean on it to license a fairly strong conclusion: since parents reliably produce adults who broadly share their society's values without ever specifying an objective function, inspecting a brain, or guaranteeing their child's future behavior, we should expect iterated training with human feedback to be broadly sufficient for alignment too -- and heavy investment in interpretability or formal verification is like a parent demanding a neural wiring diagram before teaching table manners. A skeptic on the same team counters that the analogy proves too much, since parents also routinely produce adults who lie to them, and that the honest comparison would be to breeding or to training an animal, where selection pressure on outward behavior is exactly what produces the inner disposition the trainer did not intend.

Audit the child-rearing analogy as it is actually being used here: to justify sufficiency-of-training as the default alignment strategy. Give the mapping explicitly (a table of correspondences between elements of child-rearing and elements of model training is welcome), then identify where the mapping breaks. In doing so, separate the disanalogies that are load-bearing for the intended conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Among others, consider: whether human children arrive with a shared, species-typical motivational architecture that training data must merely steer rather than construct from scratch; what it means that a model, unlike a child, can be copied, frozen, rolled back, and evaluated across millions of near-identical instances; who chooses the target of learning in each case, and whether that chooser can be wrong in ways a parent cannot; whether the analogy imports anything about moral patienthood that does epistemic work it should not; and whether the possibility of a system that performs alignment while strategically concealing misalignment has any analogue in the parenting case at all.

Then assess the skeptic's proposed replacements. Do breeding and animal training actually map better, or do they smuggle in their own distortions -- for instance, an assumption that selection on behavior can only shape dispositions superficially, which may be no more secure than the assumption it replaces? Finally, deliver a verdict: does the sufficiency conclusion survive once the load-bearing disanalogies are on the table, and if no existing analogy carries the weight, say what a better one would have to look like -- or argue that the situation is genuinely sui generis and that any domestic analogy will mislead more than it illuminates.
