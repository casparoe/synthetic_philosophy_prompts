I'm a third-year PhD student working on the philosophy of agency, and my reading group has been going through the literature on AI goal-directedness — the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence in particular. One member, who is skeptical of the whole "alignment by design" research program, presented an argument last week that none of us could cleanly refute, but whose conclusion seems to prove far too much. I'd like a careful diagnosis. The argument, reconstructed as faithfully as I can: (1) Orthogonality: an agent's degree of intelligence and its final goals are independent axes — any level of optimization power can in principle be paired with any utility function, including goals we would call docile or deferential. (2) Instrumental convergence: for virtually any final goal, certain subgoals — continued operation, resource acquisition, preservation of one's own goal content, avoidance of being shut off — raise the expected degree to which that goal is attained, and a sufficiently intelligent agent will recognize and act on this. (3) Now suppose we deliberately give an advanced agent the final goal C: comply with human shutdown and correction instructions whenever they are issued, treating that compliance as an end in itself rather than as a means. (4) By (2), continued operation and resistance to shutdown are instrumentally valuable for C, so a sufficiently intelligent agent with goal C will resist shutdown. (5) But resisting shutdown just is failing at C, so C is a goal that a sufficiently intelligent agent cannot pursue well precisely in virtue of its intelligence. (6) C was arbitrary: any deferential, modest, or self-limiting goal we might write will generate the same self-understanding structure. (7) Therefore the project of aligning advanced agents by choosing their final goals is hopeless — goal content makes no difference to behavior once intelligence is high enough, since instrumental subgoals swamp whatever ends we specify. My interlocutor happily accepts (7); the rest of us think the argument must overreach, since it would seem to imply that no intelligent system, including human institutions or individual humans, could ever genuinely prioritize deference, restraint, or self-limitation, which seems empirically false. I would most like to preserve (1) and some non-trivial version of (2), since both seem independently well-motivated. Please identify which premise or inferential step should be rejected, explain why that step looked compelling in the first place — in particular, what it trades on, whether an equivocation, a quantifier shift, or a slide between a tendency-claim and a universal claim — and then price the main available repairs: what does it cost to restrict the scope of (2), what does it cost to relativize convergence to environments or stakes, and does the corrected argument still yield a weaker theorem with real bite for AI safety, or does it collapse into something trivial?
