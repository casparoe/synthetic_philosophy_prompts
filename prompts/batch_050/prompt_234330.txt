So my weather app says stuff like "70% chance of rain tomorrow" and I've been going down a rabbit hole about how the people who make these forecasts actually get graded. From what I can tell, meteorologists don't just check whether it rained or not -- they use a scoring system where you get penalized by how far your number was from what happened. Like if you said 70% and it rained, that's a small penalty, but if you'd said 95% your penalty would be even smaller, and if you said 10% you get hammered. And apparently the scoring systems are designed very specifically so that a forecaster does best, on average, by reporting their genuine belief rather than hedging -- if you actually think it's 60% but you say 80% to look more confident, the math works out so you lose on average. Meanwhile my friend who plays poker says the only real test of a probability is whether you'd bet on it at those odds, and that anyone whose probabilities are inconsistent in certain ways can be bled dry by a clever bookie no matter what actually happens, which I gather is an old argument.

Here's what's bugging me. A single "70%" forecast can't really be checked -- it either rains or it doesn't, and nobody can say after the fact whether 70% was "the right number." Yet the whole scoring practice seems to treat each individual probability as something that can be more or less accurate, like there's a true probability out there that the forecaster is trying to hit. What does this practice presuppose about what a probability even is? Does it only make sense if probabilities are real features of the world (or of the forecaster's information) that can be closer to or further from the truth, rather than just descriptions of what bets you'd accept? And is the scoring practice actually consistent with itself -- the "you can't check a single forecast" idea and the "every forecast gets its own score" idea seem to be in tension, since if single forecasts are uncheckable, what's a score on one forecast even measuring?

Also, how different would the practice look if we swapped in my poker friend's view? If probabilities are really just betting odds you're willing to post, would we grade forecasters by letting people bet against them and seeing if anyone can systematically take their money, and would that punish different mistakes than the penalty-score system does -- like punishing internal inconsistency rather than distance-from-what-happened? And one more thing: under the scoring system, someone who always says "50%" on everything seems like they should be punishable for being useless, but is the system actually set up to catch that, or does the practice quietly presuppose some extra notion (informativeness? resolution?) beyond mere accuracy that it never states? I'd like the implicit theory of the practice reconstructed, plus an honest accounting of where the practice is compatible with several theories of probability and doesn't actually choose between them.
