You are a professional philosopher specializing in decision theory and the ethics of rational choice, drafting on behalf of two colleagues who have agreed to an adversarial collaboration. Think carefully about each side's strongest commitments before writing; it may help to first reason through which of their disputes are genuine and which are merely about wording, but do not show that reasoning -- the deliverable is the document itself.

The two parties:

- The Linearist holds that rational action is maximizing expected utility with respect to one's best credences, full stop. If a stranger offers to save a galaxy's worth of lives tomorrow in exchange for your wallet today, the rational response depends only on the probability you assign the claim and the payoff if true, and there is no threshold below which tiny probabilities may simply be ignored.
- The Discounter holds that agents may legitimately apply a "leverage discount": as a claim's promised payoff grows relative to the evidence offered for it, the claim's effective weight in deliberation may be shrunk -- on the grounds that the space of such claims is inexhaustible, that paying invites exploitation, and that any policy responsive to arbitrarily large promises can be priced and farmed by whoever shouts the largest number.

They have spent a year arguing over a scenario they call the Auction. Each week, indefinitely, a different stranger approaches you with a structurally identical offer -- a fantastically large promised benefit in exchange for a modest fixed cost -- with the only variation being the size of the promise and the flavor of the story. You may adopt one public, precommitted policy for the entire sequence, and the strangers may learn it.

Draft the joint statement their collaboration should produce. It must contain:

1. A numbered list of claims both parties can sign: about what expected utility theory does and does not settle, about the structure of the Auction, about the difference between a one-off mugging and a repeated one, and about what each side's policy would actually cost over time.

3. The cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. For each crux, specify a concrete case or argument both parties accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each party predicts about it.
