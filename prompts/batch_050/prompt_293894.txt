For a graduate seminar on forgiveness, I want the specification settled before any particular theory is read. Write the desiderata that an account of forgiveness must meet in order to count as adequate for practical-ethical purposes — that is, a theory good enough to adjudicate whether someone ought to forgive, not merely a lexical analysis of how the word is used.

Organize the requirements in three groups. First, the distinctions the account cannot collapse: forgiveness versus excusing, versus justifying, versus condoning, versus forgetting, versus reconciliation, versus legal or institutional pardon — with, for each pair, the case that shows the two come apart (e.g., a wrong done under duress invites excuse rather than forgiveness, which suggests forgiveness presupposes culpable wrongdoing in a way excuse does not). Second, the cases the account must deliver a verdict on: forgiving the unrepentant; forgiving the dead or unreachable; third-party forgiveness, where someone unharmed claims to forgive a wrong done to another; self-forgiveness; and forgiving a wrong that is still ongoing. State for each case what constraint it imposes — for instance, if third-party forgiveness is incoherent, the theory must explain what standing is and why victims alone possess it. Third, the normative questions the account must take a position on rather than evade: whether forgiveness can ever be obligatory, and if so, to whom it is owed (the wrongdoer, the victim herself, third parties, God); whether it can be unconditional, granted prior to and independently of repentance; and whether genuine forgiveness can later be revoked.

For each requirement, give the consideration or case motivating it, and mark it as either a genuine adequacy condition or the prejudice of a particular camp — I suspect several common demands are merely inherited from religious frameworks in which forgiveness is a duty, and I want those flagged rather than smuggled in.

The part I care most about: identify any subset of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied, and prove the tension rather than asserting it. Two candidate tensions to evaluate, though you may find others: (a) treating forgiveness as a gratuitous gift, beyond what duty requires, while also holding that it is sometimes obligatory; and (b) holding forgiveness to be rationally evaluable — apt only where there is genuine culpable wrong and proper standing — while also holding that it may be unconditional. If either pair is truly unsatisfiable, the desiderata list itself is a philosophical result, and say which requirement should be dropped and at what cost.

Conclude by briefly scoring three families of accounts against the list: the view, descending from Bishop Butler, that forgiveness is the forswearing or overcoming of resentment; the view that it is a performative commitment, akin to a promise, to treat the wrongdoer henceforth as one not wronged by them; and transactional views on which forgiveness completes a moral exchange initiated by repentance. Keep the scoring short — a few lines per family — and identify the single desideratum that knocks out the most contenders.
