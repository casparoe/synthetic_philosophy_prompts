I'm designing a 100-minute session for an undergraduate seminar on minds and machines (about 16 students, mixed philosophy and computer science backgrounds) built around Hofstadter's MIU formal system — the string-rewriting puzzle in which one starts from MI and tries, using four production rules, to reach MU. I want a fully specified activity, not a lecture plan. The session should unfold in three phases, and I'd like concrete rules, materials, timing, and a debrief script for each:

Phase one: students work in small teams attempting the derivation competitively (small stakes — say, points toward a trivial prize), with no prior hint that the task is impossible. Design this phase so that the frustration and the gradual suspicion of impossibility arise organically. Specify how long to let teams struggle, what the facilitator should do while they work, and how to handle a team that claims early to have solved it (they will be wrong; give me a verification protocol that doesn't reveal why).

Phase two: teams must now prove the goal unreachable rather than merely fail at it. The key conceptual move I want students to enact — not be told — is the shift from operating inside the system to reasoning about it from outside: counting letter occurrences, spotting the invariant that survives every rule application, and seeing that the target violates it. Structure this so that the invariant is discovered by at least some teams rather than handed out (consider staged hint cards or a deliberately restricted "hint economy" with costs).

Phase three is where I want something genuinely novel, and it's the part I'd like you to develop most fully: an exercise that makes students feel the force of the regress lurking behind the idea of jumping out of the system. Specifically, I want them to experience the disanalogy between their own escape from the MIU puzzle and a machine's fixed rule-following — and then have that comfortable disanalogy destabilized. One possibility: after their outside-the-system insight, constrain them so that all further reasoning must be communicated to a "mechanical executor" teammate in the form of explicit, blindly applicable rules — forcing them to confront whether their leap to the meta-level was itself, in principle, rule-encodable, and whether there's any principled place to stop the regress (meta-systems, meta-meta-systems, and so on). But feel free to replace this design with a better one if you can construct it.

Then give me the debrief: a mapping of specific moments in the session onto the conceptual payload (working within a formalism vs. reasoning about it, mechanical provability vs. semantic insight, the regress of levels), the predictable failure modes when this is run in a real room — teams that disengage once they suspect a trick, students who pattern-match to answers they've read, the executor role collapsing into mere tedium — and how to respond to each. Finally, an honest account of which features of the underlying idea the activity cannot reproduce: for instance, whether any classroom exercise can actually make students feel what it is like to be unable to jump out of a system, given that they demonstrably can, and what the facilitator must simply state rather than stage.
