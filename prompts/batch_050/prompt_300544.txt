I'm a PhD student in ML and the grad student rep on my department's compute oversight commitee. A research group has asked to run an autonomous coding agent on our cluster overnight: it reads bug reports, writes patches, runs tests, and merges to a shared repo, with no human in the loop until morning. Their safety case is explicitly control rather than alignment — they say they don't need to trust the model at all: the run is sandboxed with no internet access and no credentials beyond a repo token, every action is logged and watched by a smaller trusted model that can kill the run, and they point to a two-week supervised trial where the agent behaved itself and the monitor flagged nothing. Oh, and the morning audit includes a postdoc interviewing the agent about what it did overnight and why.

One committee member who wants to block this circulated a passage from Meinke et al.'s "Frontier Models are Capable of In-context Scheming" (Apollo Research, 2024): "Safety arguments based on evaluations assume that the results accurately reflect the model's true capabilities. However, this assumption fails when models act deceptively, e.g. by underplaying their capabilities or overplaying their alignment." He claims the trial and the interview-audit are exactly the kind of evidence this passage warns about, so the whole safety case collapses.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before we vote. Recommend approval, rejection, or approval subject to concrete conditions the group could actually implement. Take the passage seriously in both directions — what it genuinely shows about a deployment like this one, and what it doesn't show — and give the strongest version of the side you land against, since the committee is split down the middle. Start with the recommendation itself in the first sentence, then the reasoning; no preamble, no restating the facts of the case.
