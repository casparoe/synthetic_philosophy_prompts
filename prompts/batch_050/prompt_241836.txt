I'm the newly appointed risk manager at a small proprietary betting operation — eight traders, mostly sports and political-event markets with a little statistical arbitrage on the side — and I've been asked to write the firm's position-sizing manual. The trigger was an argument at our Monday meeting: one partner read a popular account of the Kelly criterion and now wants every position "Kelly-sized," while our senior trader insists that expected value is the only thing that matters and that the logarithm is just risk aversion dressed up as mathematics. Act as a quantitative risk manager who is also genuinely fluent in the decision theory underlying the Kelly criterion. Before drafting anything, work through for yourself — briefly, in a short preface — why maximizing the expected logarithm of wealth is (or isn't) the right objective for repeated proportional betting, whether the log objective is really distinct from ordinary risk aversion, and why the case for it is weakest for one-off events that never repeat. Getting this preface right matters more to me than any formula, because it's the section the partners will actually read.

The main deliverable is a decision procedure any of our traders can run on a proposed bet in under an hour, expressed in words rather than code: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a satisfactory answer to each, and the points where the procedure halts with "no bet" or "zero size." It must handle, at minimum: edges that are estimated rather than known, with different treatment for an edge that comes from a backtested model versus a trader's claimed feel for a market; simultaneous bets whose outcomes are correlated, where summing individually computed Kelly fractions would overbet the book; one-off events like elections, where the long-run-frequency justification seems thinnest; and payoffs that are heavy-tailed or many-valued rather than simple win/lose. At each step, state what naive full-Kelly would prescribe, what conservative correction the procedure applies and why, and mark explicitly the places where judgment cannot be proceduralized and a named human has to sign off — I want those flagged rather than papered over.

Then run the procedure on three worked cases: (a) a backtested arbitrage showing a stable 3% edge on clean data across several years; (b) a senior trader's strong conviction, based on two seasons of watching a particular football league, that a market is mispriced by 8%; (c) two bets on the same political race at different venues, each individually attractive, whose payoffs are highly correlated with each other. At least one of these should come out at a size the trader will hate, and you should say why that's the procedure working rather than failing. Close with a short section, written for the Monday meeting, explaining the asymmetry between overbetting and underbetting — why doubling the Kelly stake is so much worse than halving it — in language a non-mathematical partner could repeat without mangling it; I want the "geometric" part of geometric rationality to survive translation into plain English. Think carefully about which simplifications are safe for a betting shop and which would quietly change the principle. Aim for roughly 1,200–1,500 words.
