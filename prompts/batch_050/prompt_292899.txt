I retired from a career in condensed matter physics two years ago and have since fallen rather badly down the philosophy rabbit hole, as retirees do. At the moment I'm chewing on the old torture-versus-dust-specks problem: would you rather one person suffer horribly, or an astronomically large number of people each get a dust speck in the eye? In physics, if you pile up enough negligible contributions you genuinely get a large effect -- that's just integration. So my gut says enough dust specks must eventually outweigh the torture, but every philosopher I read seems to squirm at that conclusion, and I've seen half a dozen different escape routes: lexical thresholds (harms below some line just never aggregate), denying that the comparisons stay transitive across long chains of tiny steps, discounting very small harms to literally zero, the "harmless torturers" style argument, and simply biting the bullet and saying yes, the specks win.

Here's the wrinkle I haven't seen discussed anywhere, and it's the bit I actually want tested. Suppose the dust specks aren't certain. Each of the enormous population has, say, only a 1% chance of actually receiving a speck, and you don't know in advance how many people are in the population -- it could be a trillion, it could be effectively unbounded. Now my physicist's instinct about expected value starts doing work, but it also starts feeling like I'm smuggling in an assumption. My question: take the standard menu of responses to the original dilemma (threshold views, transitivity denial, discounting, bullet-biting, and any others you'd count as a genuinely distinct position) and rank them in a tier list by how well each one copes with this probabilistic, uncertain-population version -- does the fix still work, or does adding uncertainty quietly break it? A sentence or two of justification per tier is plenty.

One request on the epistemics: please be clear about which parts of your ranking reflect something like settled territory among people who work on aggregation problems and which parts are genuinely contested, and don't dress up a minority view as the consensus answer. I'd rather be told "this is where philosophers genuinely fight" than be handed a tidy story. And British English throughout, please -- colour with a u, if you don't mind humouring an old man.
