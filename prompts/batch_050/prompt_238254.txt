So I've been chewing on this for a while and I want to figure out what I actually believe about people acting alone on stuff that affects everyone, where once one person does it, it can't be undone. Going case by case on gut feeling:

- A virologist's gain-of-function paper gets rejected for publication on safety grounds, so she posts the full methods on a preprint server, figuring the information does more good out than locked up. Bad move. The fact that she thinks the risk is overblown shouldn't settle it for the rest of us.
- A mid-level analyst leaks documents proving an illegal mass surveillance program, after the agency's own internal review cleared it. Honestly? Good, maybe even heroic. One person, no authorization, irreversible, affects millions -- and I'm still glad he did it.
- A small island nation, after years of international talks stalling, unilaterally runs a solar geoengineering trial over its own waters. Four other countries were positioned to do the same thing and held off because they couldn't get consensus. Bad, and it doesn't seem to matter much that the science was reasonable.
- A city engineer, during a once-in-a-century flood, opens a spillway without authorization to save downtown, flooding farmland upriver. Justified, I'd say, even though it's the same shape as the others: one person, irreversible, huge stakes, no consensus.
- A pharma exec ships an unapproved drug to a poor country where it saves thousands, knowing regulators there would have said no. I keep flip-flopping on this one but mostly land on "wrong process, right outcome, and the process mattered."
- An astronomer with weekend access to a big transmitter beams a message at a nearby star system. Her colleagues mostly think the risk is tiny but nonzero, and nobody agreed to it. Feels wrong to me out of proportion to the actual risk, which bugs me.

The pair I can't reconcile: the analyst and the geoengineering nation. Both defied a review process, both acted on their own judgment about what's good for everyone, both were arguably more optimistic about their own case than the average informed person. If anything the analyst had *less* expertise in what he was overriding. And yet.

The case I keep going back and forth on: an engineer at an AI lab who, after leadership decides not to release a model's weights, leaks them onto the internet, genuinely believing openness is safer.

Is there a principle that gets all or most of these? I want it stated precisely enough that I can tell what it says about the AI case, and I want to know which of my verdicts it has to give up to stay simple. My only tentative thought is something about whether the actor is exposing information versus imposing a physical change on people who never consented, but the flood engineer blows that up, so it's probably wrong. Tell me where it breaks and what to replace it with.
