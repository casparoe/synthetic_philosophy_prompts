I'm a high school student in a philosophy club, and we've spent three meetings stuck on a problem that I later found out has a name in political theory: the boundary problem, or the problem of constituting the demos. It started with an actual case at our school. The student council administers a budget jointly with the neighboring middle school to fund a shared theater program, and someone proposed that the middle schoolers should get to vote on how that money is spent. The council's leadership answered that this question would have to be decided by a vote of the student council. Several of us objected that this settles the question by assuming it: if the question is who belongs to the group that gets to decide, you can't answer it by holding a vote among the people you started with, because that just builds your preferred answer into the procedure.

From the reading I've managed since (a borrowed book and some summaries), the academic debate seems to be stuck in the same place we are, and has been for a long time. As far as I can tell there are roughly three positions. First, the all-affected-interests principle: everyone whose interests are affected by a decision should have a say in it. The standard objection is that almost every decision affects almost everyone somehow — foreign policy affects foreigners, a dam upstream affects everyone downstream — so the principle either hands the whole world a vote on everything or has to quietly draw a line about which effects count, which is the original question all over again. Second, the all-subjected principle: everyone subject to a state's coercive laws should have a say. But who is subject to a state's laws depends on where its borders and jurisdiction run, and somebody decided that, so this seems to move the bump in the rug rather than remove it; and tourists are subject to the law for two weeks without anyone thinking that earns them a vote. Third, membership views, where the demos is whoever shares a nation, a culture, or a history. These avoid the regress, but as far as I can tell only by taking the boundary as already given, which is precisely the thing the problem says can't be justified from inside.

So my question is not which principle is correct. My question is what kind of disagreement this actually is, because after three meetings it doesn't feel like a factual one. Is it a verbal disagreement hiding inside words like affected and subjected, where each principle comes out true under its own preferred reading? Is it that the three camps are answering different questions — one about who is morally owed a say, one about who may legitimately be coerced, one about which groups already exist as a matter of social fact — under the single label of who belongs? Or is it a disagreement about what an answer is even allowed to look like: one side thinks a legitimate demos must be constituted by a principle that doesn't presuppose it, and the other thinks democratic legitimacy can only ever begin from a group that simply finds itself together, so the first side is demanding something impossible and then treating the impossibility as a scandal?

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the debate has stalled because it treats the question who are we as if it had the same shape as the question what should we do — as if there were a procedure-independent fact of the matter that the right principle could discover, when in fact every candidate procedure already contains its own answer, so the real argument is about which prior commitment (to affected interests, to subjection, to nations) each side is unwilling to surrender. Please test that hypothesis against what the three positions actually claim and against where the standard objections actually bite, and replace it with a better diagnosis if mine is wrong. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress here: a distinction that dissolves part of the dispute, a hidden empirical question that could in principle be investigated, a new form of argument, or a reason to think the problem has no solution and that democratic theory should simply say so. And if some part of it comes down to bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for, I'd like that stated plainly rather than disguised as a premise.
