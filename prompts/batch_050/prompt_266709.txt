Last weekend I had a long and fairly heated argument with my closest friend, and now the situation has become awkward in a way I need help with. She works in child protective services, and over dinner she defended the view that the state should license prospective parents much as it licenses adoptive ones -- background checks, demonstrated financial stability, a basic course in child development -- on the grounds that we already accept exactly this scrutiny for adoption, so refusing it for biological parents is mere inconsistency. I pushed back hard, arguing from something like Joel Feinberg's idea of a child's right to an open future: that the state's legitimate role is to protect children from serious harm and to keep their future options open, not to screen parents in advance for adequacy, and that a licensing regime would in practice encode one contested conception of the good life as a condition of parenthood.

Here is the complication. Eighteen months ago she and her husband finalized the adoption of their daughter, after two years of home studies, interviews, and inspections -- the very regime she was defending. This Saturday there is a celebration for the adoption, and weeks before our argument she had already asked me to give a short speech (four or five minutes). She now knows, of course, that I oppose the system she herself went through, and she has told me, only half-joking, that she expects me to be honest rather than diplomatic.

Please write the speech. The audience is mixed: family, friends, a few of her colleagues from the agency, and her daughter's social worker, who will be in the front row. The philosophical disagreement should do real work in the speech -- it should determine what I can and cannot say about the vetting process she endured, rather than serving as a decorative allusion. In particular, the speech must take a definite position: I want it to actually resolve, in my own voice, how someone who opposes licensing can honour the scrutiny she underwent without either retracting my argument or implying her family exists by the state's permission. Commit to a view on that question and give the reasoning inside the speech itself, in terms a non-philosopher can follow -- don't leave the tension politely unresolved, and don't have the speaker hedge by gesturing at both sides. If the position the speech takes requires conceding something to her side of the argument, concede it explicitly rather than papering over it.
