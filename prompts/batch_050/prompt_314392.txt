I've been volunteering at an animal sanctuary and ended up in a long argument with one of the other volunteers about which animals "count", morally speaking, and it got me thinking about how inconsistent our actual practices are. Most countries ban or heavily restrict experiments on great apes but allow essentially routine experiments on mice. The UK recently extended its animal welfare legislation to cover octopuses and crabs, so boiling a lobster alive is now frowned upon, while swatting flies remains completely unremarkable. And there are odd wrinkles: a pig is widely considered smarter than a dog, but we eat one and keep the other as family; a whale has a much bigger brain than a chimpanzee, yet it seems to be the chimp that gets the special protections.

What I want to know is: what theory of moral status does this whole patchwork of practices actually presuppose? The implicit rule seems to be something like "the bigger or more complex the brain, the more moral consideration you're owed" — but I can't tell whether the practice treats brain size as morally relevant *in itself*, or whether brain size is just being used as a rough proxy for something else (capacity to suffer, self-awareness, having plans for the future, whatever). If it's a proxy, then presumably the practice is hostage to neuroscience: if we found out tomorrow that insects have rich experiences, everything should change. If it's not a proxy, that seems frankly bizarre — why would raw neural tissue matter intrinsically?

So please: reconstruct the implicit view (or views — maybe the practice isn't even consistent with itself), say which rival theories it quietly rules out (a pure sentience-based utilitarianism? a Kantian line about rationality? speciesism plain and simple?), and identify where the practice is underdetermined, i.e. where several different theories would license exactly what we do. Then tell me what would concretely have to change if we swapped the implicit brain-size view for a strict suffering-only criterion. Please answer in British English.
