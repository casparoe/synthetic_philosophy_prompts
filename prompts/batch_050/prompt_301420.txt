I've been chewing on this since a Kant reading group and nobody there gave me a satisfying answer. The setup: in Perpetual Peace (1795), Kant proposes a free federation of states -- a voluntary league nations can join and leave, with no coercive power over them -- and explicitly rejects a world state. But a friend reconstructed Kant's own arguement like this, and it looks like it runs straight through that conclusion:

(1) Individuals in a state of nature are each justified in coercing the others into a civil condition, because without a common authority backed by force, nobody's rights are actually secured -- every claim of right remains provisional.
(2) States, in their external relations, stand to one another exactly as pre-civil individuals do: no common judge, no common law, each relying on its own force.
(3) So states are obligated to exit the international state of nature and enter a juridical condition with one another, for the same reason as in (1).
(4) But a juridical condition just is one with public law backed by a common coercive authority. A voluntary league whose members can withdraw whenever they like leaves every state's right provisional again -- it's a treaty, not a civil condition. So the only genuine exit from the international state of nature is a world republic with enforcement power.
(5) Therefore Kant's federation isn't a half-measure on the way to his own ideal; it's inconsistent with his premises. Perpetual peace, on Kantian grounds, requires world government whether Kant liked it or not.

I really don't want to accept (5) -- I think the league-of-states reading of Kant is right and world government has its own problems he'd be the first to name. But I can't find the step to attack. (2) felt fishy at first (states aren't like individuals in some ways), but when I pressed on it I couldn't say why the differences matter for the argument rather than just being true. Which step do you reject, why did it look plausible, and what does rejecting it cost the rest of Kant's setup? Please answer in American English.
