I am writing an essay for a philosophy-and-literature seminar and would like help working out the reading before I draft it. Read Tolstoy's The Death of Ivan Ilyich through Zhuangzi's treatment of death: the passage where Zhuangzi is found drumming on a basin and singing after his wife's death and explains his changed attitude through the transformation of qi; the story in chapter six of the four masters, with the hunchbacked Master Yu greeting his deformity with equanimity and Master Lai comparing his dying to a smith casting metal wherever he will; the account of Mengsun Cai, who went through the forms of mourning without inward grief; and the butterfly dream that closes chapter two with its lesson of the transformation of things.

The reading I want should take three points of friction seriously rather than harmonizing the two texts:

First, Ivan's final hour is narrated as a breakthrough: he sees that his whole life has been wrong, that the mistake can still be repaired, and he dies in a state of release. Would Zhuangzi recognize this as genuine transformation, or does the novella's structure — a life judged retrospectively as wasted, a final insight that redeems it — still rest on ranking lives and deaths as better and worse in exactly the way Zhuangzi's equalizing of life and death is meant to dissolve? In other words, is Ivan's last judgment about his life itself one more attachment, and would Zhuangzi count the breakthrough as liberation or as a more refined clinging?

Second, set the three days of screaming against Mengsun Cai and against Qin Shi's refusal to grieve conventionally at Lao Dan's death. Tolstoy gives the screaming enormous dramatic weight, almost as a purgation; Zhuangzi's mourners achieve composure through a kind of comprehension of process. Are these rival accounts of what a good dying looks like, or descriptions of different stages of the same one?

Third, Gerasim. The peasant servant holds Ivan's legs on his shoulders night after night without resentment and is the only character at ease with the fact of dying. Is Gerasim best read as a picture of something like effortless, un-self-conscious responsiveness to what is needed — the closest figure in the novella to a Zhuangzian ideal — or is his care essentially Christian charity, and does the difference matter for what each text is trying to teach its reader about how to face death?

Where the novella resists the reading, say so plainly. And close by telling me what each text can show about death that the other's form cannot: what does a realist narrative of one particular bureaucrat's dying dramatize that Zhuangzi's parables and dialogues leave out, and what does Zhuangzi's refusal of narrative consolation expose about what Tolstoy is still doing in the final pages?
