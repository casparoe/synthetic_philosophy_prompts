I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I keep running into the same structure in stories I report: a research team sits on a potentially dangerous paper, a security engineer knows about a vulnerability, a company can unilaterally deploy a product feature no regulator has reviewed — and in each case the outcome depends not on a collective decision but on whichever single party acts first. Philosophers call this the unilateralist's curse: when an action needs only one agent out of many to go ahead, and each agent makes an honest but imperfect judgment about whether it is a good idea, the action becomes increasingly likely as the number of potential actors grows, even if it would be a mistake on the merits. The more labs could publish, the more someone publishes.

I want to turn this idea into a reporting tool I can reuse: a diagnostic procedure I run on any story with this shape, so that my coverage is driven by the structure of the situation rather than by whoever gives me the best quote. Write me that procedure. I file roughly one such story a month, I usually have two or three days of reporting time, I am not a technical expert in the fields involved, and my editors will ask me to justify any claim that a party "shouldn't" have acted.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

**The Procedure** (roughly 500 words): the questions to ask in sequence when I encounter a candidate story — how to tell whether the unilateralist structure is genuinely present (as opposed to a one-off rogue actor, or a genuinely collective decision dressed up as fragmented), how to estimate the relevant number of potential actors, how to probe the quality and independence of their judgments, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each question before moving on. Mark clearly the points where the procedure must hand the question to someone else — an editor, an outside expert — because it turns on values or specialized knowledge rather than on anything my reporting can settle.

**Complications the Procedure Must Handle** (roughly 350 words): at minimum, cases where the potential actors are not equally competent or equally well-informed (does the curse still bind if one party is demonstrably the best judge?); cases where the action is reversible versus irreversible; cases where abstention is itself costly and the cost falls on the abstainers; and "partial" versions where the action requires not one actor but, say, three of ten to coordinate. For each, say whether the procedure's output changes and how.

**Worked Application I: The Dual-Use Paper** (roughly 300 words): run the full procedure on a story in which one of several virology labs, all holding similar results with plausible misuse potential, is about to publish while the others have held back pending a governance process.

**Worked Application II: The Security Tip** (roughly 300 words): run it on a case closer to home — I have received a tip about a serious, unpatched software vulnerability, several other journalists likely have the same information, and publishing could pressure the vendor into fixing it faster but could also tip off attackers. I want this one to be the application where the procedure delivers an answer I will not like; tell me honestly what it tells me to do and why.

**What This Procedure Cannot Do** (roughly 200 words): the failure modes — the kinds of stories where it will mislead me, and the questions about the curse itself (e.g., whether awareness of the structure actually helps agents correct for it) that a reporting procedure cannot settle.

One request: keep the procedure usable by a generalist with no philosophy background. Where you must keep a technical distinction alive, give it a plain-language name I could put in a notebook.
