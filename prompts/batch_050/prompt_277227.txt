I'm drafting a grant proposal on mental privacy and keep going in circles: every account I sketch either rules in way too much (if inferring my mood from my face already counts as mind reading, the concept does no work) or too little (if only literal neural decoding counts, then a camera system inferring emotions from micro-expressions across an entire workforce is perfectly fine). So before I commit to a theory, I want the desiderata. What must any adequate account of mental privacy deliver, if the goal is a theory good enough to ground regulation of consumer neurotech? Which cases does it have to classify correctly, which distinctions must it draw -- reading vs. altering the brain, mental states you're aware of vs. ones decoded without or before your awareness, inference from neural data vs. inference from ordinary behavior (and is that distinction principled or just a technological accident of our current devices?) -- and which facts does it have to stay consistent with? Give the case motivating each requirement, mark which ones you'd treat as negotiable and which not, and flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, since I want to know if the list itself is already a result. Then stress-test it on two hard cases: a gaming headset that picks up your intended move a few hundred miliseconds before you feel like you've decided, and an insurer that never touches your brain at all but infers things about you from the pooled neural data of thousands of people statistically like you. Briefly at the end: how do the two broad camps -- mental privacy as just ordinary information privacy applied to a new data source, vs. cognitive liberty as a genuinely new standalone right -- score against your list?
