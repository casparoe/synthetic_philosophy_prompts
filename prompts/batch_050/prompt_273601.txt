One of the durable obsessions of rationalist blogs is self-binding: precommitment devices, Ulysses pacts, commitment contracts, and the lurking suspicion that the person who sets the alarm for 6 a.m. and the person who swipes it off at 6 a.m. are not quite the same agent. The standard justification for honoring advance directives runs on the Ulysses model: the earlier self rationally anticipates that the later self will be biased, akratic, or panicked, so overriding the directive is exactly the failure the earlier self was guarding against. I'm writing a piece about value drift in long-term commitments, and I want to stress-test whether that justification is really doing all the work we assume, or whether our intuitions about these cases track something else entirely.

Construct a matched pair of thought experiments about an advance directive or self-binding commitment, built to the following specifications:

1. In both scenarios, the later self is fully competent. No dementia, no delusion, no depression-clouded judgment, no intoxication, no coercion. Both later selves have changed their minds through a process that is, by their lights and by ours, rational: they have considered the relevant evidence, reflected calmly over time, and are not being manipulated.

2. The two scenarios differ in exactly one psychological variable, and that difference should flip the intuitive verdict: in one scenario the directive clearly deserves to be honored, in the other the later self should plainly be free to override it. The variable may not be the later self's competence, third parties relying on the commitment, or the stakes of the decision.

3. Realistic settings only: medical consent, career choices, marriage, money, organ donation. No memory wipes, no uploads, no predictors, no science fiction. One paragraph per scenario.

Then do the analytical work. State the verdict each of the following frameworks delivers for each scenario, and why: (a) the Ulysses/precommitment model; (b) a Parfit-style psychological-connectedness view, on which a later self who has drifted far enough from the drafter is, for moral purposes, closer to a different person than to the same one; (c) a view on which the earlier self's authority rests not on identity at all but on its having deliberated under structurally better conditions — more time, less pressure, further from the moment of decision. Identify which single variable in your pair is doing the work, and say honestly whether that variable can bear theoretical weight or whether, on inspection, it smuggles one of the excluded factors back in. Finally: if someone who thinks directives should be honored in both cases wanted to attack your second scenario, where would they push, and does the push work?
