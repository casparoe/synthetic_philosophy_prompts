I am a novelist, and I have spent the last three years writing a book set in a small fishing town on a northern coast, loosely modeled on several real places. The town's defining story, repeated at every public occasion, is this: during a great storm in the 1890s, the lighthouse keeper rowed out through the surf, again and again, and saved the crews of three wrecked ships. The keeper's descendants are prominent in the town; the story is on plaques; it is how the place explains to itself what kind of place it is. In the course of my research I found the harbor records and the keeper's own surviving log, and they are unambiguous: the keeper stayed ashore that night, the rescues were carried out by unnamed fishermen, and the keeper's reputation was manufactured decades later by a local newspaper editor who needed a founding hero. My novel, as drafted, tells the traditional version, because that is the version the town lives inside.

At a reading last month, two audience members got into an argument about my choice, and it has kept me up at night. Marta, an archivist from the county records office, said that a novelist who knows the record and prints the legend anyway is doing something worse than the newspaper editor did: he acted in ignorance of some facts, whereas I would be acting against facts I hold in my hand. Fiction, she said, is how most people actually absorb the past, so the novelist's obligation to the record is if anything heavier than the historian's. Henrik, a retired schoolteacher who grew up in such a town, replied that Marta was treating memory as a defective copy of the archive, when it is a different organ altogether: the archive's job is correctness, but memory's job is to carry a community's sense of what it owes and what it is for, and a memory that does that work well is not simply "refuted" by documents. He added that the archive itself is not innocent -- it preserves what the powerful bothered to write down, and the fishermen who did the real rescuing left no logs, so Marta's "record" is itself one family's paperwork elevated into truth.

Marta conceded that archives are partial, but said this case was the clean kind: here the documents directly contradict the story on its central point, and the story moreover credits a specific man with the deeds of specific other men, which is not a benign myth but a theft -- the fishermen's descendants are owed their ancestors back. Henrik answered that the theft was completed a century ago, that no living person can name the fishermen anyway, and that "correcting" the story now, through a novel the town will read as an attack, would not restore the fishermen but only strip the town of its one shared exemplar of courage. He proposed a test: a communal memory should yield to the record only when it is being used to blame or diminish an identifiable group, not when it merely burnishes. By that test, he said, the keeper's story should stand, because it scapegoats no one.

Marta's last move was to say that the test proves too much: on it, any flattering falsehood a community tells about itself becomes untouchable, and the line between "burnishing" and "blaming" is not stable, since crediting the keeper just is, by omission, discrediting the men who actually rowed out. She suggested a different remedy: I should rewrite the novel so that the discrepancy itself is the subject -- the town slowly discovering what its founding story costs. Henrik said that was the archivist's vanity talking: it would turn my novel into an appendix of the records office, destroy the myth for the people who live by it, and purchase a truth that benefits only people who already spend their days in archives.

I have my own stake in this, obviously, but I genuinely could not tell which of them won. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: who is right about whether a novelist who knows the record has a heavier obligation than a historian, who is right about whether memory and the archive are rivals or merely different instruments, whether Henrik's test survives Marta's objection, whether her proposed rewrite is a resolution or an evasion, and what, if anything, both of them are failing to see.
