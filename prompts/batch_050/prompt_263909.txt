You are a professional philosopher of mind with expertise in computational theories of cognition and the philosophy of neuroscience. Think carefully and begin by brainstorming three possible ways to frame the project; briefly explain which is strongest and then draft the proposal.

I am applying for a one-year graduate research grant and need a polished two-page research proposal. My broad question is: In what sense, if any, is the brain a computer? The familiar answers seem unsatisfying. Affirmative answers often treat any information processing as computation; negative answers point to embodiment, analog dynamics, stochasticity, chemical modulation, or the absence of cleanly separable software and hardware; and deflationary answers say that almost any physical system can be described as computing something if we choose the right mapping.

My half-formed idea is to distinguish three claims that are often run together:

1. Descriptive computationalism: neural activity can usefully be modeled as performing computations.
2. Explanatory computationalism: cognitive capacities are explained by transformations over information-bearing states.
3. Constitutive computationalism: systems with the same relevant computational organization instantiate the same cognitive processes, even if their physical materials differ.

I then want to develop a criterion based on implementation-level counterfactuals. Roughly, a computational description is substantive only if it tells us which physical changes would preserve the system’s cognitive behavior and which would alter it. On this view, two systems are computationally equivalent not merely because an observer can map one onto the other, but because specified interventions on their algorithmic variables have systematically similar effects despite changes at a lower physical level. The project would ask whether this criterion can separate genuine computational explanations from merely convenient redescriptions without assuming that cognition must be digital, symbolic, serial, or brain-bound.

Turn this into a viable one-year proposal. Include:

- A precise statement of the central research question and two or three subsidiary questions.
- Why the standard positions leave the question unresolved.
- The project’s central hypothesis, stated clearly enough that it could turn out to be false.
- The method: what I would actually do, including a realistic month-by-month plan.
- Why this method is preferable to obvious alternatives, such as a purely conceptual analysis, a broad survey of neuroscience, or computer simulations.
- The main obstacles and how I would address them.
- What a successful result would look like, and what outcomes would count as failure.
- The broader significance for debates about artificial intelligence, cognitive modeling, and whether computation is discovered in nature or imposed by our descriptions.

Use a small number of manageable cases to anchor the analysis—for example, stochastic neural signaling, recurrent neural activity, or neuromodulation—rather than trying to cover all of neuroscience. Do not include a bibliography, literature review, or citations; I will add references later. The proposal should be intellectually ambitious but plausibly completable in one year, and it should not simply define computation in a way that guarantees the desired conclusion.
