I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about the norm governing flat-out assertion, working from my gut reactions to cases, and I'd like to know what single principle — if any — lies behind them.

Here are my verdicts. First, a colleague holds one ticket in a fair million-ticket lottery whose winning number has been drawn but not announced. I won't flatly tell him his ticket lost, even though I believe it did and my belief is about as close to certain as beliefs get; saying it outright feels defective, not merely impolite. Second, I routinely tell people my flight leaves at 6:05 on the basis of a printed itinerary, even though I know itineraries are occasionally wrong — roughly as often as a million-to-one ticket wins, I suspect — and this feels perfectly proper. Third, I once told a coworker, flat out, that our meeting was at three, relying on my usually reliable memory of an email that in fact said two. What I said was false. My sense is that the assertion was improper but that I was blameless in making it — criticizable and excused, somehow both. Fourth, suppose I glance at a clock that stopped exactly twelve hours ago, form the belief that it's 4:30, and tell someone it's 4:30. It's true, and my belief was reasonable, but the assertion still strikes me as defective. Fifth, anyone who says "It's raining, but I don't believe it is" has said something wrong, even if the first half is true and the second half is true. Sixth, if I assert on a mere hunch that the visiting speaker's train is delayed, and it turns out to be delayed, the assertion still feels improper — the lucky truth doesn't retroactively fix it. Seventh, when I pass along something a trustworthy friend told me — that the bridge is closed — and she genuinely knew it, my assertion seems fine even though I couldn't have established it myself.

The first two cases obviously pull against each other, and the third and fourth seem to pull against whatever resolves them. Is there one precisely statable principle of the form "one may assert that p only if..." that captures all or most of these verdicts? If so, state it, explain which cases motivate each clause of it, and say how it handles the distinction I seem to be drawing in the third case between an assertion's being improper and the speaker's being blameworthy. If no single principle gets everything, tell me which one or two verdicts you'd ask me to give up, and why those rather than others. Finally, tell me what your principle predicts about a case I haven't listed: an engineer asserts that a bridge is safe purely on the basis of a diagnostic instrument she knows to be 99.9% accurate, without inspecting the bridge herself. Is that more like the lottery, more like the itinerary, or neither — and does your answer survive the intuition that we'd want the engineer to check?
