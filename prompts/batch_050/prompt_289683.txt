so a friend of mine keeps defending Singapore and post-78 China with what he calls the company analogy: a state is basically a firm, the government is management, citizens are customers and shareholders at once, elections are just an expensive and noisy way of doing performance reviews, and since nobody demands that Apple or Toyota hold shareholder votes on the CEO every four years, nobody should demand that of Lee Kuan Yew's successors or the CCP either -- as long as GDP goes up, the trains run, and crime stays low, the "board" (whoever that is) should leave management alone. he says Lee Kuan Yew basically ran Singapore Inc. and it worked, and Deng figured out the same thing.

i want to know how far this actually holds. give me the correspondence table first -- what plays the role of the CEO, the board, the shareholders, the customers, the product, the exit option -- and then tell me where it breaks. some disanalogies i can already see nagging at me: you can quit a company or sell your shares but leaving a country costs you everything, the firm has competitors keeping it honest but the state has a monopoly on violence inside its borders, and shareholders only want one thing (returns) while citizens want like fifteen incompatible things. but i don't know if these are fatal or just friction. the real question is whether the conclusion survives -- that delivering prosperity and order is *sufficient* for legitimacy, the way delivering good phones is sufficient for Apple -- or whether the analogy was smuggling that in the whole time. also: the analogy cuts both ways, right? employees can't fire bad management either, and we think that's fine *because* they can leave -- so does the whole thing just reduce to the exit question, and if so what's the actual answer for a state? if there's a better analogy for what Singapore or China are doing (a landlord? a gym membership? a ship's captain mid-storm?), tell me what it is and what conclusion it would license instead.
