I've been arguing with my officemate about Kuhn for weeks now, and I've noticed my own verdicts on the classic cases are kind of a mess. I want to know what principle, if any, I'm actually operating on. Cases:

1. Priestley vs Lavoisier. Priestley kept defending phlogiston long after Lavoisier's work was out. My verdict: Priestley was just wrong. There was a fact of the matter about whether combustion releases a substance or absorbs one, they disagreed about it, and Lavoisier won. No translation problem deep enough to change that.

2. Newton vs Einstein on mass. People tell me "mass" means something different in the two theories, so Newton's claim that mass is conserved can't even contradict anything Einstein said. But I can't shake the feeling that Newton was simply mistaken -- mass isn't conserved, full stop, and I can say that in plain English without picking a paradigm first.

3. Ptolemy vs Copernicus on "planet." For Ptolemy the sun and moon were planets and the earth wasn't; Copernicus moved the earth in and the sun out. Here I do feel like Copernicus was proposing a reclassification rather than refuting anybody. "The earth is not a planet" strikes me as true in Ptolemy's mouth and false in Copernicus's, and no one made a factual error.

4. Aristotle on motion. He counts an acorn ripening into an oak as "motion." When a modern physicist says ripening isn't motion, I think they're talking past each other. But Aristotle also thought heavy bodies naturally come to rest at the center of the universe, and about that I think he's flat-out wrong -- even though it's the same theory, and plausibly the same word doing the work in both claims. This pair bothers me.

5. A made-up one. Two biologists: one defines "fitness" as first-generation offspring count under lab conditions, the other as long-run lineage persistence. They argue for years about whether strain A is fitter than strain B and keep getting opposite answers. My verdict: no disagreement at all, just a shared word. And yet from the inside their argument presumably feels exactly like case 1 felt to Priestley, which bothers me too.

My officemate's proposed principle is "there's real disagreement iff there's a proposition both sides succeed in expressing and have conflicting attitudes toward," but that seems to trivialize everything, since you can always cook up some shared proposition (e.g. both agree combustion happens) and always deny one.

So: is there a better principle, stated precisely, that delivers my five verdicts? Tell me which clause of it each case motivates. What does it predict for a case I haven't listed -- Dalton said atoms are indivisible, and we now split them, so did we refute Dalton or just keep the name while changing the subject? And if your principle can't save all five, which verdict should I surrender, and why that one rather than the others?
