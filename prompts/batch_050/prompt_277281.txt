A colleague of mine has spent the semester arguing about cases in which evidence about one's own reliability bears on what one should believe. I have written down her verdicts, and I would like to predict how she will rule on a case I plan to present to her next week. Her verdicts so far:

(1) Two auditors of equal competence and equal care independently review the same accounts; one concludes there is fraud, the other concludes the books are clean; each fully explains their reasoning to the other, and neither can locate any error in the other's work. Her verdict: each should move substantially toward the other's conclusion.

(2) A diner calculates a tip in his head and feels certain of the result; he then learns that his drink was spiked with a substance that produces arithmetic errors in nine out of ten people who take it, with no felt symptoms. Her verdict: he should abandon confidence in his answer even if it is, as it happens, the correct one.

(3) Same as case (1), except that one auditor turns out to have been drunk while reviewing the accounts. Her verdict: the sober auditor is entitled to stand firm.

(4) A pilot's instruments indicate level flight while her bodily sensations insist she is climbing steeply. Her verdict: she should trust the instruments over her sensations.

(5) A researcher disagrees with a colleague after full disclosure of reasoning, but in each of their several past disagreements the researcher has turned out to be right and the colleague wrong. Her verdict: the researcher may largely stand firm, treating the track record as grounds for discounting the colleague's judgment.

Now the new case. An engineer carefully reviews the evidence bearing on whether a bridge design is safe and judges, with high confidence, that it is safe. A colleague of hers, whom she regards as at least as competent as herself and who has examined the same evidence, judges that the design is unsafe. The engineer believes she can identify the precise step at which her colleague's reasoning goes wrong -- a subtle mishandling of load-distribution statistics -- although the colleague disputes that it is an error, and the engineer has never been able to make the colleague see it. However, the engineer also knows that in structurally similar past situations, when she has been highly confident and has believed she could pinpoint the other party's mistake, her judgments of this kind have been wrong somewhat more often than right.

Please predict her verdict on the new case, and then do the following. First, identify at least two general rules that are each consistent with all five earlier verdicts -- I can see at least a calibration rule, on which confidence should track the expected reliability of whichever faculty or person produced the judgment, and an explanation rule, on which one may stand firm just when one can account for why the dissenting judgment went wrong -- and state precisely what each rule predicts about the bridge case. Second, say which rule you take her to be implicitly running, with reasons, and acknowledge honestly if the track record underdetermines the choice, specifying what further principle she would be committing herself to with each possible verdict. Third, describe one further hypothetical case whose verdict would discriminate between the two leading candidate rules. I am less interested in which rule is correct in absolute terms than in which prediction best coheres with her own prior pattern of judgments.
