I cover science and ideas for a magazine and I'm drafting a piece on the "safe Pareto improvement" idea that came out of Oesterheld and Conitzer's work on game-playing agents -- roughly, a deviation from default behaviour that leaves every player better off no matter what the others turn out to do, which people then got excited about as a tool for AI coordination. For the sceptical section, here's my list of objections so far: (1) strict guarantees are almost never available, so genuine SPIs barely exist outside toy games; (2) they only fix miscoordination, not real conflict of interest, so they say nothing about the hard cases like arms races; (3) "improvement relative to default play" is baseline-sensitive, and if the default is ambiguous the so-called improvement might be worse than what would actually have happened; (4) when several SPIs are available they can be incomparable or even mutually exclusive, so picking one reintroduces the very coordination problem they were meant to solve; (5) the whole setup leans on agents inspecting each other's source code or making credible commitments, which real actors mostly can't. I want one more objection that is genuinely different from these -- not a rewording, special case, or mash-up of them -- and then your honest verdict on whether it actually lands or whether the SPI camp has an easy reply. British English please.
