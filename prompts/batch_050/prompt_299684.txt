Moral and political philosophy constantly lean on a distinction nobody has a proper theory of: the line between a genuine moral preference, which demands respect or at least aggregation, and a bias, which calls for correction or discounting. Every simple test fails in both directions at once. Consistency convicts the person who has genuinely changed their mind. Freedom from framing effects convicts virtually everyone, since trolley judgments shift with wording and order. Calm, informed reflection acquits the lifelong bigot whose prejudice has been thoroughly rationalized and entrenched by years of rehearsal.

Fix the purpose as follows: the theory is meant to guide someone who must decide whether another person's stated moral judgment may be discounted or corrected against that person's protest -- say, in designing deliberative institutions, weighting public input on policy, or evaluating debunking challenges to moral convictions. Given that purpose, write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of the preference/bias distinction in morality must deliver. I want the requirements list itself, with the case or consideration motivating each one. It should cover, at minimum: the particular cases the theory has to get right (moral dumbfounding, adaptive preferences formed under deprivation or oppression, disgust-driven condemnation, judgments that survive debiasing attempts, and cases where what looks like a bias turns out to be a genuine insight); the distinctions it cannot collapse (bias versus mere error versus heuristic shortcut; bias as a statistical deviation versus bias as a normatively defective formation process; having been influenced by something versus being biased by it, given that every judgment has a causal history); and the constraints from empirical moral psychology it cannot contradict (for instance, that no human judgment issues from an emotion-free or cause-free process, so a criterion requiring that is vacuous).

Then do three further things. First, identify any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied -- I have in mind, for example, the tension between requiring sensitivity to a judgment's causal history and requiring that the criterion not debunk the evaluator's own judgments by the same standard -- and say which one you would relax. Second, flag which desiderata strike you as genuine constraints and which look like the smuggled-in prejudices of one camp (coherence requirements are a candidate). Third, tell me which single requirement excludes the most existing contenders, and score the main families -- idealized-reflection or full-information accounts, causal-process or debunking accounts, and coherence-based accounts -- briefly against the full list.

Please write your answer in American English.
