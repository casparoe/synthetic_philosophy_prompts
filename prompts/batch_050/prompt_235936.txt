I am a postdoctoral researcher giving a thirty-minute talk next month at a workshop hosted by a cognitive neuroscience institute whose senior members helped develop and experimentally test global workspace theory. My thesis is unfashionable in that room: I want to argue that the distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness does no explanatory work that global workspace theory cannot already do, and that the standard evidence offered for phenomenal overflow — partial-report superiority in iconic memory tasks, the apparent richness of unattended peripheral vision, the felt sense that we experience more than we can report — is better explained by access to coarse, ensemble-level summaries of a scene than by rich phenomenal content sitting beyond the reach of access. My argument sketch runs as follows. First, any proposed instance of phenomenal consciousness without access must, to enter scientific discussion at all, be inferred from some behavioural or neural marker, and whatever licenses that inference constitutes access in the only sense that does theoretical work; the notion of consciousness that is in principle inaccessible to any system whatsoever is empirically idle. Second, the partial-report paradigm shows that a high-capacity visual buffer outlasts reportable working memory, but such a buffer is exactly the sort of pre-conscious store global workspace theory already posits, so the burden falls on the overflow theorist to show the buffer's contents are conscious rather than merely durable. Third — and this is the part of the talk I care most about — I want to float a speculative proposal that the felt richness of visual experience tracks the brain's estimate of its own representational capacity rather than the presence of fully detailed content, so that the feeling of seeing more than one can report is a cheap, gist-level signal that the overflow theorist mistakes for the thing itself.

The audience matters here. The two senior researchers I mentioned will be present and they know the no-report paradigm literature and the methodological debates around it thoroughly; there will also be one philosopher sympathetic to the access/phenomenal distinction who has written on the overflow question, a clinician who works on residual consciousness in non-communicative patients and is wary of any framework that ties consciousness too tightly to report, and a dozen doctoral students from both camps. I expect the neuroscientists to attack the empirical adequacy of my reading of the partial-report data, the philosopher to press the conceivability and epistemic points, and the clinician to raise cases where report-based criteria seem to fail patients.

Please prepare my Q&A prep sheet, in British English, containing the following: the objections I will actually receive from this specific audience — not the objections a generic philosophy seminar would raise — ordered by how likely they are to come up and how much damage each would do if I answer badly; each objection phrased the way the relevant audience member would actually put it, in their vocabulary; for each, what I should concede up front, a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Include the objection I am quietly hoping nobody raises, say why it is the most dangerous one, and tell me what my best available answer to it is. End with a short section identifying the weakest load-bearing step in my own three-part argument and whether I should defend it head-on or quietly restructure around it.
