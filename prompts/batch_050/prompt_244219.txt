Bit of context first. I'm not an academic or a policy person, I repair bikes for a living, but I spent twelve years in Australia where voting is compulsory, and since moving back to Britain I've got a bit obsessed with how few people bother voting in local elections and what that does to councils. I've been banging on about it to anyone who'll listen, and now the local civic society has invited me to do a 15 minute talk at their monthly meeting, followed by questions. Village hall crowd, tea and biscuits, that sort of thing.

My proposal is a package of three changes to local elections (not Westminster, just local):

1. Compulsory voting, Australian-style. You have to turn up and get your name crossed off or you get a small fine, but once you're in the booth you can spoil your ballot, and I'd add an official "none of the above" box so nobody is forced to endorse anyone.
2. Lower the voting age to 16.
3. Give the local vote to non-citizen residents who've lived here for a qualifying period, say three years, whatever passport they hold. The current rules are a total patchwork depending on where you came from and when you arrived, which nobody in the room will even be able to explain.

The three are meant to hang together: if the state makes voting a duty, like jury service, then everyone the council governs has to be in the electorate, otherwise compulsion just amplifies the usual suspects. My pitch in a nutshell: turnout here is maybe a third, so councillors answer mostly to older homeowners; council decisions hit 16 year olds (who can work and pay tax) and non-citizen neighbours (who pay council tax) just as hard; and Australia shows compulsion is ordinary, not authoritarian.

The audience, roughly 40 people: mostly retired homeowners, including two ex-councillors who still know where the bodies are buried; one current district councillor who's quite party-political and protective of the council; a retired headteacher who I suspect has strong views about 16 year olds; a secondary school politics teacher; and a sprinkling of younger renters who'll probably be on my side but quiet.

- Order: roughly when in the Q&A it will come up, 1 first
- Objection: phrased the way that person would actually say it out loud, not cleaned up

- Damage if I flub it: 1-5

- My reply: 60-90 seconds spoken, plain language, no jargon
- Where the reply is softest

Give me the 8-10 objections this specific room will actually raise, not the ones a politics seminar would raise. Then under the table, two short things: the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and the single reply that has to land or the whole evening dies on me.

Be honest about my soft spots please, I'd definately rather find out now than in front of the headteacher.
