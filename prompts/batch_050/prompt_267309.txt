Last night I got into one of those arguments with my friend Priya that goes until 2am and resolves nothing. We were talking about a decision she actually has to make — a stable, decent-paying job near her family vs. a risky fellowship abroad in a field she loves — and I said it's just a hard empirical problem, she doesn't have enough info yet. She said no, even with perfect info the two options might just not be rankable, and that's the whole point of a hard choice: neither is better, they're not equal either, and no amount of reflection fixes that.

Over the course of the argument we cycled through basically every way we know of dissolving or solving hard choices like this:

1. The epistemic dissolve: the options are comparable, one really is better, we just can't tell which. Hard choices are a knowledge problem, not a metaphysics problem, and the right response is more thinking, more information, maybe a coin flip once you've hit diminishing returns.
2. The small-improvements argument: if you improved one option slightly (say the fellowship pays a bit more) and it still didn't clearly beat the other, then the two can't be merely equal, so there must be a fourth relation beyond better/worse/equal — being "on a par" — and hard choices are choices between items on a par.
3. The vagueness line: "better than" is just indeterminate in these cases, the way "tall" is indeterminate for borderline heights. Nothing metaphysically spooky, just a predicate that runs out before the options do.
4. Bootstrapping: you don't find the reasons, you make them. By throwing yourself behind an option you create new reasons in its favor, so hard choices are occasions where agents get to legislate value rather than discover it.
5. The regret test: a choice is hard when you'd rationally feel regret whichever way you go (or when you wouldn't — we argued about which direction this test is supposed to cut), so pick the life you can whole-heartedly narrate afterward.

Here's what I want: a sixth way of handling hard choices that is genuinely different from all five. Not a rewording, not a special case of one of them, and not just "combine #3 and #4" — though if you think your proposal is close to one of them, say so and defend the difference. Also tell me: does your sixth option even try to *solve* hard choices, or does it change the subject in a productive way? And stress-test it against Priya's actual case — does it tell her anything she couldn't already get from #1 through #5, or does it fall apart as soon as you lean on it? If your proposal ends up collapsing into one of the listed ones when you push on it, be honest about that instead of pretending its new.
