Two of my colleagues have spent a year arguing about the Meno problem -- why knowledge is better than mere true belief -- and I have asked them to stop giving talks past each other and instead produce a joint statement from an adversarial collaboration. Draft that statement for them. Their positions:

- The Stabilist holds that knowledge is true belief produced by a reliable method, and that its extra value is entirely instrumental: reliably produced beliefs persist under new evidence, social pressure, and the passage of time, so they can be counted on in planning and action in a way that luckily true beliefs cannot. On her view, once a belief's stability and practical payoff are fixed, nothing of value is left over.
- The Credit-Theorist holds that knowledge is a cognitive achievement -- a true belief whose correctness is attributable to the believer's competence -- and that achievements have value of their own, independent of usefulness. On her view, this explains why we prize knowledge even when no practical stakes are in play.

Their statement must have four parts:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign, covering at least: what the Meno problem actually demands (an account of the *epistemic* value of knowledge, not merely its practical convenience); the concession that lucky true beliefs guide action just as well in the moment; and a shared verdict on the swamping worry (the concern that, once truth is secured, adding reliability adds no further value -- like an excellent espresso whose excellence is unchanged by coming from a dependable machine).
2. A table of residual disagreements, each row giving the issue in neutral terms and then each side's position in wording that side would itself accept -- neither slanted nor so hedged as to be empty.
3. The cruxes, where each crux is paired with a concrete case both agree bears on it and each side's prediction recorded in advance. They must consider at least these two: the dogmatist, whose belief happens to be true and is every bit as stable as knowledge because he ignores all counterevidence (does the Stabilist's view really entail that his belief is as valuable as knowledge?); and the archivist, who reliably knows thousands of facts -- old phone extensions, discontinued train schedules -- that will never be of any use to anyone (does the Credit-Theorist's view entail that all of it is genuinely valuable?).
4. A final section naming any part of their year-long dispute that, on reflection, was verbal rather than substantive, with a one-paragraph defense of that verdict.

Do not adjudicate who is right; the deliverable is a document both could sign. Keep the entire statement under 850 words.
