So the surprise exam paradox, roughly: a teacher announces on Monday that there will be an exam on exactly one day of the coming week, and that on the morning of the exam the students won't know in advance that it's happening that day. One student runs the backward induction -- it can't be Friday, because come Thursday night they'd know; but then it can't be Thursday either, since Friday's ruled out; and so on down to Monday -- concludes the announcement can't possibly hold, stops worrying, and is then genuinly surprised when the exam lands on Wednesday.

Here are five-ish takes on what's going on:

1. Quine-style: the student's eliminative reasoning is mostly fine, but the last step is where it breaks -- the announcement can still be true, the student just can't *know* it's true. Concluding it's false is the error.
2. Epistemic-logic take: the announcement is a Moore-flavored sentence -- consistent, possibly true, but not knowable by the class, so the iterated "they'd know that they'd know" steps the induction needs secretly fail somewhere in the chain.
3. Equivocation take: "surprise" shifts meaning between the setup and the elimination steps (knowing the night before vs. being able to deduce vs. plain psychological unexpectedness), so the whole thing is a fallacy of ambiguity and nothing deeper.
4. The paradox only really bites in the one- or two-day case; with a full week the announcement is perfectly believable and the induction works fine until the very last step, and only that step is fishy. (I'm least sure I've stated this one right -- if I've mangled it, steelman it before judging.)
5. Deflationary take: it's a pragmatic self-defeat thing -- the announcement undermines itself once believed, roughly like "don't think of an elephant" -- and there's nothing interesting about knowledge here at all.

Rank these five from most to least convincing as resolutions of the original puzzle, with a couple sentences per placement saying what earns it that spot (and say which one, if any, you'd rank below "just shrug and move on").
