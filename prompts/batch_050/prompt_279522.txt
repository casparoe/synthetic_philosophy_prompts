I'm a high school senior in a Theory of Knowledge class, and each student gets to lead one 50-minute session for our group of about sixteen. For mine, I want the class to feel the force of the Frankfurt-style challenge to the principle of alternative possibilities: the claim that you can be fully responsible for something even when you could not have done otherwise. Design an activity in which students make a real choice with small but genuine stakes, and only afterward learn that a hidden arrangement would have produced the same outcome for them had they been about to choose differently — an arrangement that, as it happens, never had to act. Give me the exact rules and setup, then a debrief that pries apart "I chose this myself" from "I could have chosen otherwise" and asks which of the two responsibility actually needs. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it (students suspecting the trick, a case where the mechanism actually has to fire, and so on) and how to handle each problem, and be frank about which feature of a true Frankfurt case a classroom game cannot reproduce. No lecture up front, and no one should hear the name Frankfurt until the very end.
