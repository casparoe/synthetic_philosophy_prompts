A colleague recently pressed the following argument on me, and although I am confident it goes wrong somewhere, I have been unable to locate the fault to my own satisfaction. I would like to know which step you reject, why that step looked compelling in the first place, and what rejecting it costs compared to the alternatives.

The setup: in a recent national election, turnout collapsed, and a candidate widely regarded as dangerous won on a small fraction of the eligible vote. My colleague abstained, and defends herself as follows.

(1) A particular act is morally wrong only if things would have gone better, all things considered, had the agent not performed it. She stresses that this is not full consequentialism; it is merely a necessary condition on wrongness, which nearly any theory should accept — an act that changes nothing for the worse cannot itself be wrong.

(2) The election's outcome would have been exactly the same whether or not she voted. It was not decided by one vote, and the probability of any single ballot being decisive in an electorate of millions is, she says, for practical purposes zero. The same holds, with even greater force, for her individual purchases of meat (given the thresholds at which supermarkets adjust orders) and for the emissions of any single flight she takes.

(3) Therefore her abstention — like her purchases and her flights — was not wrong. This follows from (1) and (2) alone.

(4) The reasoning of (1)–(3) can be applied to each abstainer in turn, one at a time, holding everyone else's conduct fixed. Each application is sound, so no individual abstainer did anything wrong.

(5) An outcome produced entirely by acts, none of which was wrong, is an outcome for which no one is at fault. Wrongness cannot float free of wrongful acts; if the candidate's victory is the sum of individual abstentions and each abstention was permissible, then there is nothing and no one to blame.

(6) So the widespread conviction that the abstainers are collectively at fault — and the parallel convictions about meat-eaters and frequent flyers — is simply a mistake, perhaps explained by our evolved discomfort with free-riding in small groups, misfiring in mass societies.

I find (6) incredible, and I notice the argument has a sorites shape: it would seem to show that no sequence of individually inefficacious acts can ever add up to a wrong, which cannot be right. But I also struggle to concede any premise. (1) seems like the weakest consequentialist claim one could make; (2) is an empirical fact; (3) and (4) look like straightforward inferences; and (5) seems to be a truism about responsibility. Ideally I would keep (1) and (2) intact. Where does the argument break, and what do I have to give up to make it break there?
