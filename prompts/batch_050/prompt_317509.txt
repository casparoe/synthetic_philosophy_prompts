So I've been reading about this position called tranquilism, which basically says that happiness/pleasure don't actually matter in themselves -- the only thing that matters morally is the absence of suffering and craving. Like, a moment where you feel genuine joy isn't better than a moment of complete numbness or dreamless sleep, as long as neither involves suffering. Pleasure only matters insofar as it relieves a craving or distracts from pain, kind of like how scratching an itch feels good but you'd never miss scratching if you never had the itch.

I find this weirdly compelling?? Like when I really examine my good experiences, a lot of them do seem to be relief from some want or discomfort (hungry -> eating feels great, stressed -> relaxing feels great). But I also don't want to just talk myself into a position and never stress-test it. So here's my question: what would actually count AGAINST tranquilism?

Please break it down for me:

1. What kinds of experiences or thought experiments, if they hold up, would show that positive experiences have value beyond just removing suffering? Like is there some category of pleasure that can't plausibly be redescribed as relief-from-craving? (I keep thinking about things like listening to music you weren't craving beforehand, or the enjoyment of learning something new -- do these actually threaten the view or can the tranquilist just absorb them?)

2. What would its defenders themselves admit is the strongest pressure point? I don't want just the lazy objections like "but then we should all be in comas" -- I want to know where a smart defender would say "yeah ok this part is genuinely hard for us."

3. Are there consequences the view is stuck with that might count as reductios? For example does it commit you to saying that a world with zero beings is just as good as a world full of ecstatic beings (as long as nobody suffers in either)? And is that actually as crazy as it sounds or am I just status-quo biased.

4. One thing I specifically wonder about: tranquilism seems to rely on introspection, i.e. looking closely at your own experiences and noticing that "pleasure" is usually relief of some tension. But what if some people's introspection just reports differently -- like some people honestly report value in experiences that involve no prior craving at all? Does the view survive people just... disagreeing about what their own experience is like? Or is that fatal to it since the whole theory is built on phenomenology?

5. Finally: is tranquilism even falsifiable/testable in any meaningful sense, or is it more like a way of interpreting experience that you either buy or don't? And if it's the second one, is that a problem for it or is that just how all theories of wellbeing work?

I'm not asking for a reading list or anything, just the actual arguments. Also feel free to tell me if I've misunderstood the view somewhere lol.
