I am preparing a seminar on the moral status of video-game characters, and I want my students to grasp a distinction that popular discussions routinely blur. Consider two characters in a game. The first is a villager whose pleas for mercy when attacked are entirely scripted: fixed animations and dialogue triggered by a damage threshold. The second is an NPC whose behavior is governed by a learned model, trained on recordings of human gameplay, which has generalized to produce novel, unscripted avoidance and distress behaviors in situations its designers never anticipated -- and whose internal state, when probed, exhibits something like a persistent, self-maintaining aversive representation of the player. Please explain, carefully and without assuming any particular theory of consciousness, why these two cases might differ morally even though both characters are "just code," and why they might not. In particular, I want the explanation to disentangle three questions that are usually run together: whether the character (the fictional person we see on screen) can be a bearer of moral status at all, whether the underlying computational process could instantiate morally relevant properties independently of what it represents, and whether our uncertainty about the second case generates any obligations of caution. Where do the real conceptual fault lines lie, and which moves in this debate are doing the heaviest lifting?
