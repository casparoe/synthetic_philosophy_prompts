I'm in an online journal club for med students and philosophy grads where each week somebody has to bring a puzzle about research ethics, and it's my turn. We've been going back and forth about equipoise -- the idea that a randomized trial is only ethical if there's genuine uncertainty in the medical community about which arm is better. There's the community version (roughly Freedman's: no consensus among competent experts, so offering randomization to patients is fine even if the enrolling doctor personally leans one way) and the older individual-clinician version (the trial is only ethical if the actual treating physician is personally torn between the arms). Everyone in the group keeps saying the community version is just strictly more permissive, i.e. anything fine under individual equipoise is fine under community equipoise plus some extra cases. I suspect that's not quite right, but I can't engineer the counterexample myself.

So: construct a thought experiment, set in a realistic present-day clinical context (a real kind of condition, plausible interventions, no hypothetical miracle drugs, no sci-fi diagnostics), in which a randomized trial comes out ethical under the individual-clinician notion of equipoise but unethical under the community notion -- the opposite of the direction everyone assumes. The case has to generate that verdict from the two definitions, not just stipulate it, and it can't cheat by making the expert community irrational or corrupt; the divergence should come from a structural feature of the case (e.g. who counts as part of the relevant community, how disagreement is distributed across subfields or regions, or how the evidence base looks different from inside versus outside the treating relationship). One scenario, a paragraph or two, and the patient-facing details should be specific enough that I could actually pitch it in the meeting without people saying "that would never happen."

After the scenario, show the work: state what the individual-clinician view says and why, what the community view says and why, and which single feature of the case is doing all the work -- plus what happens to the verdicts if you remove that feature (does it collapse back into a boring case where the two views agree?). Then steelman the obvious objection: how would a defender of community equipoise argue that my case doesn't count, e.g. that I've mis-specified the relevant expert community or that the trial is actually fine on their view too? Keep the whole thing under 700 words.
