You are a political philosopher with expertise in conservative thought and institutional design, advising a university body. Think carefully through the strongest considerations on each side before drafting.

I chair the Graduate Studies Committee of a large philosophy department. A motion before us would abolish our long-standing rule that every PhD student, whatever their specialization, must pass a formal logic proficiency exam before advancing to candidacy. No one on the committee can say why the rule was adopted; the minutes from the relevant era are missing, and the two senior faculty who might remember give conflicting accounts. One recalls it being introduced so that students could serve as competent TAs for our large undergraduate logic courses. The other believes it was meant to guarantee a common technical vocabulary for departmental seminars and dissertation committees -- though neither can point to a document confirming either story. The reformers' case is straightforward: the exam costs students a semester of preparation, it disproportionately delays students from under-resourced undergraduate programs, and no one can articulate what the rule is for. The defenders' case is essentially Burkean: a rule that has survived forty years is probably doing work we cannot see from the inside, and the costs of removing it are precisely the kind that only become visible afterward.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate before our vote. Recommend a concrete course of action -- abolish, retain, or some structured alternative such as a sunset clause or a trial suspension -- and give reasons the losing side could actually answer. In particular: address the asymmetry problem (the costs of keeping the rule are legible, the costs of removing it latent), say what it would even mean to "find out why the fence was built" when the original builders are gone and the fence may serve functions its builders never intended, and specify what, if anything, the committee should observe or measure before or after acting. About 600 words, in American English, written as a formal committee opinion rather than a personal essay.
