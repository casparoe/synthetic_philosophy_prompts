I'm a PhD student in moral and political philosophy with a focus on bioethics and health policy, and I've been invited to present my dissertation chapter to an interdisciplinary workshop next month. The chapter defends a moderately revisionary position: that QALY-maximization is the right default rule for rationing scarce life-saving interventions (I'm thinking of ICU beds during a surge, or expensive cancer drugs under a public formulary), but that its standard critics are wrong to treat the disability and age-discrimination objections as refutations rather than as calls for corrective weights. Roughly, I argue that prioritizing patients by expected QALY gain from the intervention is not discrimination against people with pre-existing disabilities, because what matters is the treatment's marginal benefit, not the patient's baseline quality of life; but I concede that certain cases require departures — I accept a limited "fair innings" age weighting and I argue that QALY reasoning must be supplemented, not replaced, by a rule-of-rescue floor for identified patients in imminent peril.

The audience worries me. It's about half health economists, who will think I'm too soft on the equity critiques and will push me on why my corrective weights aren't just ad hoc patches that smuggle in the very intuitions QALY-maximization was meant to discipline. The other half is disability studies scholars and a couple of clinicians, who will think I'm far too hard — they'll press the double-jeopardy objection (disabled patients score worse on both baseline health and capacity to benefit, so QALY rules disadvantage them twice), the charge that QALYs encode the healthy person's distorted judgments about what disabled lives are like, and the complaint that my "marginal benefit" move fails whenever the disability itself lowers expected treatment success or post-treatment lifespan.
