Got into it with my friend Marcus for like three hours last night about whether philosophy ever actually makes progress. His whole position was that it's the same questions as the ancient Greeks, nothing ever gets settled, everybody just keeps publishing. I pushed back with the best examples I could think of -- I said we clearly know things we didn't before, like Gettier showing justified true belief isn't knowledge, or logic going from Aristotle to Frege to basically the foundations of computing, or that the brain-in-a-vat skeptical stuff got sharpened into something we can actually argue about instead of just vibe about. He shrugged at all of it and then hit me with this line he's obviously picked up somewhere: whenever philosophy starts making progress, it stops being called philosophy. Physics used to be natural philosophy, logic became math, psychology split off, linguistics split off, so all the wins get rebranded and "philosophy" is just the name for the pile of leftovers nobody's cracked yet.

It totally worked as a conversation-ender because I couldn't say on the spot what was wrong with it, but it's been bugging me for two days and I think the slogan might be smuggling together a few completely different claims, and I'm not sure any of them actually do what he needed it to do. So here's what I want: lay out the distinct precise theses that one-liner could be expressing, as a menu I can actually bring back to him. I can gesture at a few -- there's something like a pure bookkeeping claim (fields get renamed, so "philosophy makes no progress" is an artifact of how we count), there's a stronger definitional claim (philosophy is by definition the holding pen for questions we don't have a method for yet, so any question that gets solved leaves the discipline -- progress is real but structurally invisible from inside), and there's a flat empirical claim (no genuinely philosophical problem has ever been resolved, full stop, and every supposed win was secretly science all along). Maybe there are others I'm not seeing.

For each reading, tell me: is it a platitude, is it substantive and genuinely defensible, or is it just false -- and be specific about what's false, since the empirical version seems to me to have real counterexamples but I might be wrong. And the part I care most about: which reading does Marcus actually need for the slogan to support his original conclusion that philosophy as a discipline gets nowhere? My suspicion is the true readings don't get him the win and the reading that gets him the win isn't true, and I'd like that nailed down rather than just asserted. One more thing: does the definitional reading cut against him anyway? If philosophy is defined as what's unsolved, then "philosophy makes no progress" comes out trivially true and boring, and then it seems like he has to pick between the boring version being right and the exciting version being right, and I don't think he'd be happy with either.
