I chair the question-and-answer sessions for our departmental colloquium in formal epistemology and decision theory, and next term we are hosting a visiting researcher who has written a paper defending what she calls evidential cooperation in large worlds. The rough idea is that a rational agent has reason to cooperate with beings she can neither observe nor causally influence — counterparts in other Everett branches, distant alien civilisations, simulated agents — whenever she has evidence that those beings are running deliberative procedures relevantly similar to her own, since her choice is evidence about what they will choose. She presents the view as a way of grounding moral concern at cosmological scales, and although she builds on evidential decision theory, she insists the position survives even if one is not a committed evidentialist.

I should like a sequence of ten interview questions that presses the view where it is genuinely vulnerable rather than inviting her to restate it. For each question, please supply a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. At minimum, I want the sequence to cover the following pressure points:

1. The individuation problem: how similar must a counterpart's deliberation be before my decision counts as evidence about theirs? If she answers in terms of shared algorithms, press on approximate similarity — whether evidential weight comes in degrees, and whether any principled threshold is available rather than a smooth but apparently arbitrary weighting.

2. The measure problem: in a sufficiently large world, for any procedure there may be vast numbers of near-copies cooperating and vast numbers defecting. If both populations exist, what exactly is my decision evidence about?

3. The familiar charge that evidential decision theory commends managing news rather than outcomes: does her view merely inherit this difficulty, or does the appeal to distant counterparts introduce a new version of it? If she retreats to the claim that the relevant reasoning is updateless — that one acts on the policy one would have pre-committed to — ask whether that remains an evidential thesis or has quietly become a causal claim about logical facts.

4. The step from "my decision is evidence about theirs" to "I have reason to benefit them": is the moral premise doing the work the decision theory is advertised as doing, and if so, is the argument circular?

5. What the view adds to Hofstadter's notion of superrationality beyond a change of setting.

6. Whether the view changes anything for earthbound agents, and if not, in what sense it is a theory of cooperation rather than an attitude.

Please conclude the sequence with a question about what could count against the view.
