I'm in an undergraduate philosophy of science course, and we're on the unit about falsificationism. The professor mentioned Popper's worry that Darwinism fails to be genuinely scientific because "survival of the fittest" risks being an empty tautology: the fittest are just defined as whoever survives, so the theory can't be refuted. (I gather Popper later walked this back, but the worry seemed to interest her independently of the history.) She keeps repeating the slogan as if its mere shape proves the problem, and I keep feeling the slogan could be making several very different claims, only some of which are tautologies. For example, it might be definitional (fitness just means actual survival), or it might be a claim about traits (organisms with greater expected reproductive success tend to leave more descendants), or it might be a claim about long-run outcomes, and those seem to differ enormously in whether they're testable.

Could you spell out the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing? I'd like a numbered menu, and for each thesis: whether it is a tautology or an empirical claim, whether it's actually true or plausible, and whether that reading would vindicate the Popper-style worry about unfalsifiability. Also tell me which reading someone who deploys the slogan in a debate about Darwinism's scientific status most likely needs, and whether that reading is one the theory is actually committed to. Please keep your answer under 600 words.
