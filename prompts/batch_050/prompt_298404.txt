I'm a high school student, and for the past year I've been keeping a notebook of something I keep noticing in the news, and I'd like to know whether it has an established name — and if it doesn't, what it should be called.

Here are the kinds of cases I wrote down. First: a caver gets trapped underground, and the government spends the equivalent of several million dollars on the rescue — helicopters, specialist teams flown in from abroad, hundreds of volunteers. Nobody in the press asks whether it was worth the money, and anyone who did would be called heartless. Second: that same year, the same country's transport agency declines to fund a road barrier project costing a similar amount, even though its own models predict the barrier would save roughly one life every year or two. The stated reason is that the project doesn't clear the cost-per-life threshold the agency uses. Third: a hospital moves heaven and earth for one dramatic case — a child who needs a rare transplant — while its cheap preventive programs, which on paper save far more lives per dollar, stay underfunded.

So the pattern I keep seeing is this: one visible, identifiable person in immediate danger justifies almost unlimited spending, while a larger number of faceless, statistically expected deaths doesn't justify modest spending — and this holds even when it's the same decision-maker drawing from the same budget. The same society, in the same year, acts as if a life is priceless and as if a life has a fairly low price tag, depending on whether the person can be photographed.

I've heard the phrase value of a statistical life, and I know there's a known psychological tendency to care more about a specific child in a well than about anonymous masses. But I don't think either of those is exactly what I'm describing. The psychology is about what people feel; the statistical-life concept is just a measurement tool. What I'm after is the inconsistency itself — the fact that an institution can hold both policies at once without apparently noticing, and that we mostly let it. Does this specific configuration have a name of its own? How does it differ from the psychological bias, from whatever moral principle says you must rescue the person drowning in front of you (which some people think is a genuine duty, not a bias), and from the plain fact that future expected deaths feel less real than present ones? If it fits one of those exactly, tell me which and why. If it fits none of them cleanly, please coin a term, define it precisely, and give me the boundary conditions — for instance, does it require the same budget, or does a society-wide split between agencies count? Does it still apply when the rescuers genuinely couldn't have redirected the money?

I'm not looking for a one-word answer; I want the carving. And if my setup is confused — if I've lumped together phenomena that don't actually belong together, or if the apparent inconsistency dissolves once you state the two policies carefully — please say so directly rather than politely answering a badly formed question.
