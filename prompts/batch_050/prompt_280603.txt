I write novels for a living, and an argument has been nagging at me for months. It began as a thought about my characters and has ended somewhere I refuse to stay. I would like you to act as a philosopher specializing in free will and the philosophy of action, think carefully, and tell me where it goes wrong.

The background. In my work I have always traded on a contrast between two kinds of character: those who can surprise me, who push against the plot I designed for them, and the clockwork kind who do exactly what their genre demands no matter what happens around them. Philosophers have an image for the second kind: the digger wasp, which repeats its nesting routine step for step even when an experimenter keeps moving its prey a few inches away, never noticing that the situation has changed — mechanical rigidity, or what is sometimes called sphexishness. I flattered myself that this contrast marked something deep about persons: that free agency consists in the capacity to step back from one's routines and revise them when they have stopped making sense, and that the wasp, like my wooden characters, lacks precisely that capacity.

Then I started paying attention to my own habits of revision — when I notice I am in a rut, what triggers the noticing, how I decide how far to rewrite — and the following argument assembled itself. I give it in numbered steps.

(1) An agent acts freely only insofar as her behavior is not sphexish: only insofar as she can notice when a routine has ceased to serve its point and revise the routine in light of that fact.
(2) Revising a routine is itself a piece of behavior, and like any behavior it is governed by a policy — some standing rule about when to step back, what counts as a reason to revise, and how far the revision should go. I catch myself in ruts only by means of habits of catching myself.
(3) A policy that is simply followed, rather than itself stepped back from and revised, runs whether or not the occasion calls for it; it is, by the standard of (1), sphexish.
(4) So an act of self-revision is free only if the policy governing it is itself freely revised, which requires a further policy, one level up, governing that revision.
(5) Iterating: either the hierarchy of policies bottoms out in some policy the agent simply follows, or it ascends without end. No finite agent contains an infinite hierarchy. So every finite agent's behavior bottoms out in a routine that is simply followed.
(6) By (3), that base-level routine is sphexish; and a façade of flexibility resting on a sphexish base is itself sphexishness. Flexibility all the way up cannot be conjured out of rigidity at the bottom.
(7) Therefore no finite agent is free — or (1) must be abandoned, and with it the only criterion I know for telling my living characters from my clockwork ones.

I cannot accept (7). It seems to prove too much: it would make the difference between my most vivid character and my most mechanical one a matter of decoration only, and it would convict every genuinely reflective person I admire of waspishness. Something must give, but I cannot see what gives, or what the giving costs.

Here is what I would like from you, in this order. First, reconstruct the argument in its strongest form: say which premises you find independently plausible and which step you provisionally suspect, without yet arguing for the suspicion — treat this as your pass at mapping the terrain before digging. Second, name the step you reject — a premise, or the transition between two of them — and explain why it looked innocent: what true thought it trades on. Third, price the repair. Tell me what I must give up to reject that step, and then tell me what the two most obvious alternative rejections would each cost, so that I can compare the three bills side by side rather than simply taking your word for it. If it matters to your answer: I would rather keep (1) than any other premise. I am attached to the idea that freedom has something to do with flexibility, and if that idea is itself the problem, I want to be told so plainly rather than consoled.

End your response with a single final line in the form \answer{...}, naming the premise or transition you reject — for example, \answer{premise (3)} or \answer{the step from (5) to (6)}. If you conclude, against my hopes, that the argument is sound as it stands, write \answer{sound}.
