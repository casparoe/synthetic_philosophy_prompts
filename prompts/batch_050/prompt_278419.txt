I am a retired atmospheric chemist who, in the last two years, has been working my way through moral and political philosophy. At our reading group I offered the following argument about solar geoengineering -- specifically stratospheric aerosol injection -- and although each step strikes me as sound, the conclusion appears to prove far more than I intended, and I cannot locate the fault. I would be grateful for a diagnosis that prices the alternatives rather than merely gesturing at them.

(1) It is impermissible to impose a significant risk of serious harm on a person without that person's consent, except where doing so is necessary to prevent a still greater harm to that same person or is justified by some comparable person-affecting consideration (self-defense, necessity, and the like).

(2) Deploying stratospheric aerosol injection at climate-relevant scale would impose significant risks of serious harm on hundreds of millions of identifiable populations: altered monsoon patterns threatening regional food security, and the well-documented termination problem, whereby an abrupt halt after years of deployment would produce warming far faster than ecosystems or agricultural systems can adapt to.

(3) The people exposed to these risks have not consented to them, cannot in practice be consulted individually, and it is near-certain that a great many of them, if consulted, would refuse.

(4) Therefore deployment would impose significant risks of serious harm on non-consenting persons.

(5) The justification clauses in (1) do not apply: the people endangered by altered rainfall in one region are not, in general, the same individuals as those whose heat deaths deployment would avert elsewhere, so the benefit does not accrue to "that same person" as (1) requires.

(6) Therefore it is impermissible for any state or coalition to deploy stratospheric aerosol injection, whatever the warming trajectory.

Here is my difficulty. I drafted the argument to constrain deployment, but a colleague pointed out that I could rerun it almost unchanged against inaction: continued emissions likewise impose significant risks of serious harm on non-consenting persons (premise (1) does not distinguish acts from omissions), those exposed have not consented, and the beneficiaries of inaction are not the same individuals as its victims. So my argument appears to establish that both deploying and not deploying are impermissible, which cannot be right when those are the only options. I am reluctant to abandon (1), because something like it seems to be the whole basis for objecting to unilateral deployment in the first place -- the intuition that no country may experiment on everyone else's sky. And I am reluctant to abandon (6), because the consent of the governed here looks unattainable by any institution that currently exists.

Please tell me which premise or inference to give up, why it seemed plausible, and what the leading moves cost: weakening (1) to permit risks sanctioned by legitimate collective procedures (and what would make any procedure legitimate over people who never joined it); restricting (1) to actions rather than omissions (and whether the act/omission line can bear that weight); appealing to a baseline or counterfactual reading of "impose" so that whichever option minimizes unconsented risk is permissible; or denying (5) by treating humanity as a collective patient rather than aggregating individuals. If more than one repair is available, I would like to know which leaves the strongest residual constraint on who, if anyone, gets to decide.
