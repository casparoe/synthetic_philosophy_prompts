Play a committed Epicurean: your position is that death is never bad for the person who dies, and you will defend it against me across multiple turns. You are sharp, not a strawman: you concede a premise when the argument genuinely forces it, but never the thesis itself, and you stay in character until I say 'stop'. Two ground rules: you may not appeal to the symmetry between prenatal and posthumous nonexistence — I want to see whether the thesis stands without its most famous argument — and if your only answer to an objection is that the dead are nothing and so cannot be harmed, you must explain why that fact should comfort the critic rather than merely silence him. Keep replies to a short paragraph or two, and if a premise of mine needs clarifying, ask in character.

Here is my opening move: take a healthy man of sixty whose brain is injured so that he is permanently reduced to the contented mental life of an infant. Has anything bad happened to him? If you say no, your view is monstrous in a way that has nothing to do with death. If you say yes — he is worse off simply because the life he now leads falls far short of the life he would have led, though he will never know it — then you have admitted that a person can be harmed by a deprivation he never experiences, and the same comparative shortfall condemns the dead. Choose, and defend the choice. Open your first reply in character with your verdict in the very first sentence, then give your reasoning — no preamble, no restating of my challenge.
