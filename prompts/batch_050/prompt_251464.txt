After a family argument in which my brother-in-law declared that Europe's industrial takeoff was "bound to happen somewhere in the West," I volunteered to lead one evening of our local history reading group -- eight adults, intelligent but with no background in economic history -- on whether the question "why Britain and not China?" is even well posed. I want a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself. Specify the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what depends on what: my guess is that the contrastive structure of why-questions (that "why X rather than Y" silently selects a comparison class) must precede the unit-of-analysis problem (Britain vs. the Yangzi Delta is not Europe vs. China), which must precede the contingency question. Give me one carrying example for the contrastive-question point drawn from ordinary life: it needs shared background conditions and one salient difference-maker, and it must be free of any civilizational or political charge, or the group will import rankings before they've grasped the logic. Map the misconceptions in the order this audience will voice them -- "Europe was simply ahead for centuries," "any why-question with a real answer is well posed," "you're just dodging the question" -- and indicate where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than rebutted. I've devised a check I call the contrast slider: participants take a popular one-factor explanation (coal, colonies, institutions) and say which reformulated question it actually answers and which it fails. Tell me how to run it so success is visible. Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level -- and to say I'm leaving it out.
