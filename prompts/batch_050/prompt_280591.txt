I belong to a small reading group of philosophers and decision theorists that has been circling the same question for several years now: what, if anything, should moral theory say about worlds containing infinitely many people or infinitely great quantities of value? The discussion has settled into a stable three-way standoff, and I no longer want another contribution to it. I want a diagnosis of why it refuses to move.

The positions, as they actually present themselves in our meetings:

First, the formalists. They hold that the correct response to infinite populations is mathematical: extend our orderings so that infinite cases become comparable, whether through hyperreal-valued utilities, overtaking or limit-based criteria for infinite streams, or expected-utility constructions designed to handle infinitesimal probabilities of infinite payoffs. On their view, the standard paradoxes — a world in which adding one more flourishing life leaves the total unchanged, the apparent moral equivalence of wildly different infinite worlds — merely show that we reached for finite-sum intuitions in a domain where they were never warranted, and a well-chosen formalism dissolves them.

Second, the restrictionists. They argue that a sane theory should decline the cases: assume, as a methodological postulate if not an empirical wager, that value-bearers are finite in number, or impose enough discounting and boundedness that the paradoxical configurations never arise. They note that every proposed formal extension carries its own counterintuitive costs, and they suspect cosmology will settle the matter anyway.

Third, the skeptics. They read the impossibility landscape — the apparent fact that no aggregation rule satisfying a short list of weak desiderata handles infinite cases cleanly — as a reductio not of any particular fix but of the aggregative framework itself.

Here is my hypothesis, offered strictly as a hypothesis: the debate is replaying the nineteenth-century fight over infinitesimals. The restrictionists are playing Weierstrass, setting the problematic objects aside by redefining the subject in terms that never mention them; the formalists are playing Robinson, insisting the objects can be rigorously reinstated. If that is right, then nobody actually disagrees about any theorem — all three camps accept the same impossibility results — and the real disagreement is about what counts as a solution to a paradox. Does a formalism that reproduces our pre-theoretic verdicts on an agreed set of test cases constitute progress, or is the point of a paradox precisely to put those verdicts on trial? And I suspect the disanalogy does the real work: calculus had predictive success to arbitrate between rival formalizations, while ethics has nothing comparable — no transfer principle for intuitions.

So: is my diagnosis correct, and if not, what is the actual structure of this disagreement — verbal, factual (a bet about the finitude of the cosmos), meta-normative (about what a moral theory owes us in edge cases), or a collision of starting intuitions neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would progress even look like here: a new impossibility theorem with weaker premises, an agreed-upon suite of intuitive verdicts to serve as a benchmark, an empirical result, or a principled abandonment of the question as ill-posed?
