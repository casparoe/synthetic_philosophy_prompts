I moved to Switzerland last year for work and have now voted in three rounds of referendums, and the whole thing has started to genuinely puzzle me philosophically. What got me thinking was a recent vote where a constitutional amendment squeaked through on something like 50.4% of the popular vote plus the required majority of cantons, and now it's locked into the constitution, basically untouchable except by another referendum. Meanwhile, if parliament had passed the exact same policy as an ordinary law, it could be quietly revised a few years later when circumstances change. So the harder the decision is to reverse, the thinner the majority needed to make it feels like it should be — but in practice it's the same simple majority bar either way, give or take the cantonal bit.

So here's what I'm trying to figure out, maybe for a little essay I want to write for the expat magazine of all things. When a political system treats a one-off binary referendum result as "the people have spoken" and makes it nearly irreversible, what does that presuppose about the will of the people? It seems to assume there IS such a thing — a single coherent preference that millions of individuals collectively hold on a yes/no question — and that a bare majority on one particular Sunday captures it, and that it stays put afterwards. But from what little I've read, if you'd offered three options instead of two the majority might have cycled between them, and turnout and question wording do a lot of work, so the "will" looks suspiciously like an artefact of the menu. Does the practice strictly presuppose the romantic Rousseau-ish picture, or could you defend it on thinner grounds (e.g. it's just a legitimacy-conferring procedure and nobody needs to believe in a literal general will)? And where is it just philosophically underdetermined?

Also: if we dropped the presupposition that the people's will is timeless, what would have to change? Sunset clauses on referendum results? Requiring bigger majorities the more entrenched the outcome is, the way some constitutions make amendment deliberately hard but demand supermajorities for it? I'd love an answer that reconstructs the implicit theory behind the Swiss (and, I guess, Brexit-style) practice, says which parts of that theory the practice actually needs versus which parts are just habit, and then tells me what a redesigned system would look like if you took the "there is no single popular will, only shifting majorities over shifting option sets" view seriously.
