I am preparing a position paper for a seminar on conceptual engineering, and I would like a consistency audit before I submit it. The seminar has focused on the difference between analysing an existing concept and revising or replacing it for theoretical or political purposes. I am sympathetic to the Carnapian idea that revision can sometimes be more useful than faithful analysis, and also to the ameliorative approach associated with Haslanger, on which we ask what legitimate work a concept ought to do rather than merely report how it is currently used. But I do not want revision to become unconstrained stipulation: if an engineer changes a concept so thoroughly that old agreements and disagreements no longer carry over, I want to know why this still counts as improving inquiry rather than simply changing the subject.

My commitments are as follows. First, concepts are primarily individuated by their core inferential and practical roles: two expressions may differ in dictionary definition while deploying the same concept, provided that they support the same central inferences, classifications, and interventions. Second, an ameliorative revision can legitimately abandon both ordinary usage and ordinary speakers’ self-understanding when those are ideologically distorted or practically harmful. Third, for a revision to remain continuous with the old discourse, it must preserve enough of the central cases and live disputes that participants can still be interpreted as agreeing and disagreeing about the same topic. Fourth, because reference is partly fixed by causal and social history rather than by what individual speakers intend, conceptual engineers cannot create continuity merely by announcing that the old and new concepts have the same target. Fifth, the success of an engineered concept should be assessed mainly by its downstream usefulness—whether it improves prediction, explanation, deliberation, or institutional decision-making—rather than by whether competent speakers regard it as intuitively correct. Sixth, it is coherent to evaluate a concept morally or politically without assuming that the concept has a hidden essence waiting to be discovered.

Please determine whether these six commitments hang together. Identify the genuine tensions, including any that arise only from the motivations behind the commitments rather than from their literal wording, and distinguish them from tensions that merely appear to be present. Rank the tensions by severity; for each, explain exactly which commitments conflict, under what interpretation they conflict, and what would have to be revised to resolve the problem. I am especially interested in whether my continuity condition can be reconciled with my role-based account of concepts, semantic externalism, and usefulness criterion. Finally, propose the least costly revision of the overall package: specify which single commitment you would modify or abandon, what the revised position would look like, and what explanatory or normative cost that revision would incur.
