My friend Marta is a condensed matter physicist who moonlights as a philosophy-of-science person, and over many drinks we've been arguing about whether mathematical beauty is actual evidence for a physical theory or just a taste that sometimes gets lucky. She's pretty consistent, I think, so I've started cataloguing her verdicts. On Dirac writing down the relativistic electron equation because the math was too pretty to be wrong: beauty was real evidence, and antimatter proved it. On Maxwell adding the displacement current basically for symmetry reasons: real evidence. On Einstein's general relativity: real evidence, the unification of gravity and inertia under one principle is exactly the kind of beauty that counts. On the SU(5) grand unified theory, which everyone called gorgeous until proton decay didn't show up: not evidence, she says the elegance there was real but it wasn't doing evidential work. On supersymmetry: not evidence -- too many knobs, the beauty is manufactured. On string theory: same verdict, not evidence. And interestingly, on Copernicus circa 1600: she says the heliocentric elegance was NOT evidence at the time, because the theory fit the data worse than the patched-up Ptolemaic system did.

Here's the new case I want to spring on her at our next dinner, and I want you to predict what she'll say. A colleague of hers has a theory that unifies dark matter and dark energy as two faces of a single scalar field, with zero adjustable parameters -- by any aesthetic standard it's the unification-style beauty Marta admires in GR, not the manufactured kind she pans in SUSY. The catch: its distinctive quantitative predictions only kick in at energies we won't reach for at least a century, so it will neither be confirmed nor refuted in her lifetime. Is its beauty evidence, on her view?

The thing is, I can think of at least two rules that fit all her existing verdicts: one where beauty only becomes evidence retroactively once independent empirical confirmation arrives, and one where beauty is evidence right away but only when it's genuine parameter-free unification rather than symmetry-decoration. Tell me which rule she's most likely running, what each rule predicts about the scalar field theory, and give me your best guess for her actual verdict with reasons. And if her track record genuinely underdetermines it, tell me what historical or hypothetical case I could ask her about that would force the two rules apart -- I have some ideas (Weyl's ill-fated 1918 attempt at unifying gravity and electromagnetism comes to mind) but I want to hear yours.
