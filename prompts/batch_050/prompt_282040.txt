I work as a clinical ethics consultant for a mid-sized hospital network, and our reproductive medicine and genetics services refer me the same shape of case several times a month: a prospective parent (or the clinic) is choosing among courses of action where the "worse" option doesn't harm a child who would otherwise exist and be fine, but rather determines which child, or whether any child, comes to exist at all. My consultants keep reaching for ordinary harm-based language -- "this is worse for the baby" -- and then stalling, because in these cases the baby who would be worse off under one option would simply not exist under the alternative, and nobody on the team knows how to reason past that point. I've read enough to know this is the nonidentity problem, but what I know doesn't survive contact with a 24-hour consult turnaround, a hospital policy committee that wants defensible reasoning rather than seminar discussion, and referring physicians who want to know what they may say to patients, not what the literature debates.

Please turn the best available thinking on the nonidentity problem into a decision procedure my team can actually run on a consult: the questions to ask in a fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a recommendation (and in which direction), and -- just as important -- where it must stop and hand the decision back as a matter of values or policy rather than one the framework settles (I suspect the placement of any "life worth living" threshold and the treatment of choices affecting different numbers of future people belong in this category, but tell me if I'm wrong). Be explicit about which distinctions from the philosophical literature have to survive the translation into checklist form because dropping them changes verdicts, and which can be safely dropped as technicalities. Then run the procedure on three cases from my referral log: (a) a couple declining embryo screening who will, if they proceed naturally, have a child with a serious but livable early-onset condition, where the alternative is a different, unaffected child from a screened embryo; (b) a woman whose current medication is teratogenic, who would rather conceive this month and risk moderate impairment than wait three months for the drug to clear, knowing the child conceived later would be a different child; and (c) a couple pursuing IVF with an embryo carrying a gene for a very late-onset, variably penetrant condition, against clinic recommendation. Flag which case produces the verdict my team will find most uncomfortable, and state plainly what the procedure cannot do for us.

Present the response as exactly ten numbered items, each one to three sentences long, covering in order: the steps of the procedure, the points where it must hand off to judgment, the three worked applications, and the honest failure modes -- with no introduction and no concluding paragraph.
