So I've been reading Dedekind's Was sind und was sollen die Zahlen? (1888, in the old Beman translation) and I want to run a thought experiment by you. Dedekind builds up what he calls simply infinite systems and then, around §73, does his famous abstraction move: he says (paraphrasing, I don't have it in front of me) that we should entirely neglect the special character of the elements, keeping only their distinguishability and the relations they stand in to each other via the successor mapping, and that the elements considered this way just ARE the natural numbers.

Okay. Now imagine Dedekind had lived to, say, 1930, and someone sits him down and explains the von Neumann ordinals: 0 is the empty set, each successor is n ∪ {n}, so 2 = {∅, {∅}} and it literally comes out true that 2 ∈ 4. And then someone else shows him Zermelo's rival construction (0 is the empty set, successor is n → {n}), which works just as well but makes 2 ∈ 4 come out false, and presses him with the obvious dilemma: since both sequences do everything arithmetic needs, it seems arbitrary to say either one is REALLY the numbers -- so maybe neither is.

Given that §73 move, what would Dedekind most plausibly say? I'm torn between three readings: (a) he'd accept the von Neumann sets as a legitimate answer to what 2 is; (b) he'd say the ordinals are a fine model or stand-in but 2 itself is the abstracted thing, so "2 ∈ 4" is just false of the genuine numbers; (c) he'd say asking whether 2 ∈ 4 isn't even false but a kind of category mistake -- a question arithmetic doesn't generate and shouldn't answer.

A few subquestions that I think decide between these. First: when Dedekind "neglects the special character of the elements," what actually happens metaphysically -- does abstraction create new objects (the pure numbers), or just redescribe old ones? He hated psychologism, so he presumably can't say it's just us ignoring stuff. Second: if abstraction creates new objects, do those objects have any properties beyond their positions (like, does his 2 have ANY non-structural features?), and if not, is he basically an ante rem structuralist seventy years before the label existed? Third: the sneaky one -- his own construction uses systems, i.e. sets/classes as he understands them. So is there a circle in appealing to his view to explain why numbers aren't sets, when his grounding of arithmetic in §73 runs through set theory itself?

Please actually commit to an answer (a, b, or c, or some mix) rather than just laying out the options, and defend it from the text and the surrounding context of the book, not just from vibes. Also tell me where the analogy between his abstraction and modern structuralism breaks down, because I assume it breaks down somewhere and I'd rather hear it from you than walk into it in a seminar.
