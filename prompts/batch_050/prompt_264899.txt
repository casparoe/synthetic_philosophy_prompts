You are a professional philosopher of science with particular expertise in confirmation theory and the epistemology of explanatory reasoning. Think carefully before answering: begin by brainstorming several candidate principles that might unify the verdicts below, note briefly where each candidate strains, and only then present and defend your best proposal.

I want to know whether my verdicts about when inference to the best explanation is legitimate add up to a coherent principle, and what that principle is. Here are the verdicts, as I actually find myself with them:

1. Wet streets. I wake to find the streets wet and infer that it rained overnight, rather than that a cleaning truck passed. A perfectly legitimate inference.

2. The twins. Security footage shows that exactly one of two identical twins took the laptop, and nothing distinguishes which. Each hypothesis explains the footage flawlessly, yet I refuse to infer either; suspension of judgment is the only correct response.

3. The butler. The evidence points to the butler, and I infer he did it — even while conceding that there are almost certainly hypotheses no one has yet thought of that would fit the evidence just as well.

4. The conspiracy. A theorist offers a hypothesis that explains everything the mundane story explains, plus all the apparent counter-evidence (documents were forged, witnesses were bought). It arguably explains more, yet I refuse to infer it, and I don't think this is mere squeamishness.

5. The lottery. A highly reliable newspaper prints the winning numbers. From the one-in-a-million odds alone, I refuse to infer that my ticket lost; from the newspaper's report of different winning numbers, I do infer it lost — even though, measured purely by fit with the evidence, the odds-based inference looks the stronger of the two.

6. The tailored rival. One colleague predicts a surprising experimental result in advance; another revises her theory after seeing the data so as to accommodate it. Both theories now explain the data equally well, but I count the first as strongly confirmed and the second as barely confirmed at all.

My worry is that no single principle delivers all six. In particular, cases 2 and 3 pull against each other: if a known rival of equal explanatory power blocks inference in case 2, why doesn't the guaranteed existence of unconceived rivals block it in case 3? And cases 1 and 4 seem to require the principle to say something about what makes an explanation good, not merely available, without collapsing into an ad hoc list of virtues.

Please deliver the following. First, a principle stated precisely enough to be applied mechanically, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it — including, if my verdicts demand it, a clause about when the hypothesis must have been formulated. Second, its verdicts on two cases I haven't listed: (a) two witnesses independently report the same unusual false detail, and I must decide between coordinated fabrication and coincidence; (b) the only available explanation of a phenomenon posits a kind of entity never previously observed. Third, an honest statement of which of my six verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should drop the verdict or the principle. Finally, tell me whether what you've produced is genuinely inference to the best explanation, or a probabilistic rule wearing explanatory clothing — and whether that distinction even matters.
