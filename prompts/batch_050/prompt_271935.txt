I have no formal education in philosophy or theology, but over the past year I have been reading summaries of Aquinas and thinking through his views on political violence on my own. As best I understand them, three strands of his thought pull in different directions. First, he holds that the commands of a tyrant do not bind in conscience the way just laws do, so a tyrant forfeits at least some of the protection a legitimate ruler enjoys. Second, he is deeply wary of private individuals killing a tyrant, partly because sedition and civil disorder tend to produce more harm than the tyranny itself, and partly because whoever kills a tyrant often ends up feared by the very people he meant to liberate. Third, he distinguishes sharply between acts done on public authority and acts done on private initiative, and his account of killing in self-defense turns on whether the death is intended or is merely a foreseen side effect of stopping an aggressor.

Suppose Aquinas were transported to the present and briefed on modern conditions: professional standing armies, precision weapons, international bodies that authorize the use of force, and dictators who kill on a scale he never witnessed. What do you think his position would be on each of the following cases, and why, given the strands above?

1. A palace guard who kills the tyrant he serves, on his own private judgment, without consulting anyone.
2. A group of senior military officers who overthrow and kill a tyrant, claiming to act on behalf of the commonwealth rather than for themselves.
3. A foreign government that kills another country's tyrant by a targeted strike, authorized through its own lawful institutions, partly to spare the tyrant's subjects and partly for its own security.
4. A case where the tyrant can almost certainly be removed only by killing him, but the likely successor is a coin flip between a decent ruler and a worse one.

For each case I would like two things. First, the verdict you think Aquinas would reach, with the reasoning reconstructed from his own commitments rather than from generic modern intuitions. Second, an honest note wherever the strands genuinely conflict — where two of his principles pull toward different verdicts and you would have to guess which one he would let win. If in some case you think his framework simply lacks the resources to answer, rather than yielding an answer we cannot predict, say so and explain which resource is missing.

One further question. His worry about sedition seems to rest on an empirical claim about what typically happens when private persons kill their rulers. If modern history showed that claim to be false as often as true, would that weaken only the application of his view, or the principle itself? I am not sure he separates those cleanly, and I would like your judgment on it.
