I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and Popper comes up constantly in those spaces, usually as a kind of mascot: unfalsifiable beliefs are bad, state in advance what would change your mind, and so on. I was recently at a dinner where a friend described her company's new commitment to "pre-mortems" and "kill criteria" for all major initiatives — and then described, without any apparent irony, how every failed quarter since had been explained away: the market hadn't cooperated, the metrics had been mis-specified, the test turned out not to have been a real test of the strategy. It struck me that the corporate world has adopted the vocabulary of falsificationism while quietly perfecting precisely the repertoire of protective moves that Popper catalogued as ways of shielding a theory from refutation, and I'd like a piece of satire that makes that visible.

Write a parody of a management-consulting white paper — title, executive summary, and 500–700 words of body, in the confident, jargon-saturated voice of that genre — introducing a framework with a name like "Falsification-Driven Leadership" or "Refutation Excellence." The white paper should earnestly promise to render every belief in the organization falsifiable: kill criteria for all strategies, a Chief Falsification Officer, quarterly refutation reviews. But its actual recommendations, delivered entirely deadpan, should amount to a systematic catalogue of the immunizing stratagems and conventionalist twists Popper warned against: redefining success conditions after the fact and calling it "agility," demoting failed predictions to "out-of-scope anomalies," blaming auxiliary hypotheses ("the forecast was correct; the conditions were not"), adding ad hoc parameters so the model can never lose, restricting the domain of a claim until nothing can touch it and calling that "focus," and — this is essential — a closing section in which the framework itself is demarcated as foundational and therefore exempt from the falsification requirements it imposes on everything else. The humor should come from getting Popper right: the asymmetry between falsification and verification, what makes a test severe, corroboration versus confirmation, and the standing temptation to save the hypothesis by sacrificing the auxiliaries. A philosopher of science should laugh rather than wince. The piece must never break character or wink at the reader; legibility should come from the reader recognizing, clause by clause, which real feature of naive falsificationism-as-procedure or of corporate "data-driven" discourse is being exaggerated one notch past its actual practice. Avoid mocking any named individual; the targets are the position and the discourse.

After the white paper, include a short author's note (roughly 150 words, out of character) stating where the satire takes liberties with Popper's actual position or leans on a contested reading of him — he was considerably more sophisticated than the white paper's implied target, and I want to know exactly where the caricature begins. In that note, also say what would change your assessment: which consideration or argument, whether about Popper or about how organizations in fact handle disconfirming evidence, would make the satire unfair or misdirected, and where its implied criticism of falsificationism-turned-into-procedure is most likely to fail.
