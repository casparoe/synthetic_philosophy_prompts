Two weeks ago, at the planning dinner for our small film society's winter series, someone proposed a retrospective of a director whose private conduct has lately become notorious, and the room split within minutes. Listening, I realized that my own unease was never really about the man's biography; it was about what his best film asks of its audience. It invites you to savor something no one should savor, and it is so well made that the invitation works. My instinct — that the film is worse and better in the very same place — is either a position or a muddle, and before we vote I want a case built to order that will tell me which.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. Describe a single fictional artwork — a novel or film that could plausibly exist, one paragraph at most — on which three standard positions about morality and aesthetic value are forced to deliver three different verdicts, each verdict generated from the theory rather than stipulated. The positions, in my own working formulations so that we mean the same things: (a) the merited-response view, on which a work's prescribing a response that is morally unmerited is an aesthetic defect in the work, since the work there fails at something it sets out to do; (b) immoralism, on which a work's moral defect can be partly constitutive of its aesthetic achievement, the transgression being what purchases the imaginative or emotional payoff; and (c) autonomism, on which moral assessment does not bear on a work's aesthetic standing at all, however much it bears on other things. The case must yield an aesthetic defect for (a), an aesthetic merit for (b), and aesthetic silence for (c).

Constraints: the artist in your scenario is personally unimpeachable — the moral defect must live inside the work, in the responses it prescribes to its audience, not in anyone's biography, because biography is exactly what I am trying to set aside. The work must not be propaganda or crude villainy; make it the sort of thing an intelligent viewer could love, and make its power over its audience real rather than merely alleged. For each position, show the reasoning step by which the scenario's features produce its verdict; do not let any verdict fall out by definition. Then identify the single structural feature of the case that makes the three positions peel apart, say what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed, and give the strongest available reply on behalf of whichever position your case treats worst. Finally, take my dinner-table instinct — that the film became worse and better at the same time and in the same place — and tell me whether it is coherent, and if so which of the three positions it commits me to.

End with a last line of exactly this form — Verdict: <ethicism, immoralism, autonomism, or none> — naming the position my instinct commits me to, or none if the instinct proves incoherent. Write nothing after that line.
