I am a PhD student in political philosophy working on historical injustice, and I have been developing an argument that I now suspect proves too much, since its conclusion is one I cannot accept. I would like a diagnosis rather than reassurance. The argument is meant to show that reparative duties never survive across generations — not merely that they are hard to calculate, but that the logic of reparation itself dissolves for any injustice whose direct victims are dead.

(1) A reparative duty requires an identifiable victim who is worse off than they would have been had the injustice not occurred, and an identifiable duty-bearer whose connection to that shortfall grounds the obligation to repair it.
(2) Any injustice old enough to be "historical" changed who exists. Had the injustice not occurred — had the victims not been displaced, enslaved, dispossessed, killed — the subsequent pattern of who met whom and which children were conceived would have differed, so the particular people now alive as descendants would not be those people, and indeed would not exist at all.
(3) It follows that no living descendant of the victims is worse off than they would have been absent the injustice: the counterfactual in (1) cannot be evaluated for them, because they are absent from the counterfactual world.
(4) An event that was a necessary condition of a person's existing with a life worth living cannot have wronged that person.
(5) So no living person holds a victim-side claim grounded in the original injustice.
(6) Claims do not float free of claimants: a reparative claim can be inherited only if there is someone positioned to inherit it, and (5) shows there is not; the dead, meanwhile, cannot presently be owed anything, since there is no subject whose interest the duty would serve.
(7) One might relocate the duty on the side of persisting institutions — states, churches, firms — but an institution's present obligation must be grounded either in a wrong to a current claimant, which (5) excludes, or in present holdings traceable to the injustice; and tracing fails, because after a century or more any institution's wealth is the product of innumerable intervening causes, just and unjust, with no principled way to assign fractional responsibility.
(8) Therefore there are no reparative duties for historical injustice, for any such injustice, however grave and however well documented.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd: it entails that no duty of repair survives the death of the last direct victim of any wrong whatsoever, no matter how clearly the wrong explains the present distribution of advantage, and it makes the passage of time do moral work it plainly should not do on its own. I take (2) to be the least negotiable premise — the dependence of present identities on past events seems like plain fact — and I would also prefer to keep (1), since the counterfactual structure feels like the core of what reparation, as opposed to forward-looking redistribution, even is. But I cannot see which step to give up.

Please tell me which premise or inference you reject, explain why it looked acceptable on first pass, and state what rejecting it costs: which commitments I would have to take on, and which intuitions elsewhere I would be giving up. If you think more than one step is load-bearing, say so and rank them by price. I would also like you to indicate how confident you are in your diagnosis overall, and to single out the specific step in your reasoning — whether in the diagnosis itself or in the pricing of the alternatives — where you are least sure of yourself.
