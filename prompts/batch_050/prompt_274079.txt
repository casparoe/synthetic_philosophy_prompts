In Gödel, Escher, Bach, Douglas Hofstadter introduces the toy system Typogenetics, in which strands built from the letters A, C, G and T are copied and modified by so-called typoenzymes, a construction deliberately modelled on the way DNA is transcribed and translated by real enzymes, and presented as an illustration of the isomorphism between genetic coding and Gödel's arithmetisation of syntax. Since Gödel's first incompleteness theorem establishes that any system expressive enough to encode self-referential instructions must contain true statements that the system itself cannot prove, does it not follow that every sufficiently complex self-replicating strand in Typogenetics — and by extension every biological genome — necessarily harbours instructions that its own machinery cannot execute? If this is right, which cellular processes would correspond to the unprovable statements, and might regions such as so-called junk DNA be the biological residue of this inevitable incompleteness?
