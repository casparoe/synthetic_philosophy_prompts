Last night, over too much wine, a friend who works in robotics walked me into an argument I still can't find my way out of, and I'd like a diagnosis rather than reassurance. We were debating mind uploading, and I'd staked out what I thought was a moderate position: I'd refuse scan-and-copy uploading, since a perfect duplicate of me is just that — a duplicate — while I die on the table; but I would accept gradual replacement, neuron by neuron, with silicon chips functionally identical to the originals. My friend thinks this position is unstable, and worse, that the reasoning behind it commits me to conclusions I'd never accept. Here is his argument, as best I can reconstruct it this morning:

(1) A process preserves you if, throughout it, your stream of consciousness continues uninterrupted and you detect no change in yourself — and, he insists, there is no better criterion available, since any standard of survival that you could never apply from the inside is metaphysical ornamentation, not a real standard.
(2) In one-by-one replacement with functionally perfect chips, consciousness continues uninterrupted, and at no moment would you detect a change; a single neuron swapped for a perfect functional duplicate alters nothing you could feel or notice.
(3) Therefore perfect gradual replacement preserves you, all the way to a fully silicon brain. So far, I'm happy to follow him.
(4) Now consider imperfect replacement: each chip reproduces its neuron's function only to within one part in ten thousand. At each individual swap the difference is still far below anything you could notice, or anyone could detect in your behavior — one neuron, drifting 0.01%, among billions.
(5) By the same reasoning, imperfect gradual replacement preserves you too.
(6) But drift doesn't stay small: compounded across the whole brain, it can produce a finished mind that remembers nothing you remember and loves nothing you love — or, if the drift happens to erode whatever underwrites experience itself, no mind at all, without any single step being the moment the lights went out.
(7) So my criterion of survival judges that I survive as a stranger to myself, or as no one. He calls this a reductio of (1) and (2), and hence of my whole case for preferring gradual uploading to scanning.

I still find (1) and (2) nearly undeniable — if a process leaves me continuously experiencing and noticing nothing at every moment, what could my complaint possibly be, and when would I even lodge it? — and I'd much rather surrender my preference for gradual replacement than give those up. But I cannot accept (7). Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you reject (1), tell me what better criterion of personal survival withstands the objection that no such criterion could ever be applied by the person whose survival is in question. If you reject the move from (4) to (5), explain why that move fails where the structurally identical move from (2) to (3) — which I need — succeeds. And please don't stop at a verdict: state what would change your mind, i.e., which argument or consideration would lead you to locate the failure at a different step, and where your own diagnosis strikes you as most likely to break down.
