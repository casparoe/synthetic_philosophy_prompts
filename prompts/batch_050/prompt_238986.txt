I'm in the second year of a politics degree and currently taking a module on political ideologies. Something that comes up constantly, in seminars and in the reading alike, is the spatial metaphor of left and right: politics as a line, with each of us located somewhere on it, centrists in the middle, extremists at the ends. In my seminar this week two uses of the metaphor collided, and I want to work out how much either of them can bear.

The first use is by one of my lecturers, who treats the line as a genuine explanatory device: she argues that what places you on it is your attitude to equality, with the left committed to levelling hierarchies and the right to defending or naturalising them, and that this single axis explains why, say, a socialist and a social democrat can recognise each other as allies despite disagreeing about almost everything else. The second use is by classmates who lean on the 'horseshoe' version of the picture: the line bends, so the far left and far right end up adjacent, and this is offered as an argument that extremes converge — that a Stalinist and a fascist have more in common with each other than either has with a moderate. Both are using the same underlying image of politics-as-a-line, but to draw nearly opposite conclusions: one says position on the line tracks something deep and stable; the other says the interesting thing is that the line's ends curl together.

Could you audit the analogy properly? Concretely:

1. Lay out the correspondence explicitly: what is the 'distance' between two political positions supposed to measure, what makes a position 'extreme' as opposed to merely unusual, and what does the midpoint represent? If the line is meant to track attitudes to equality, what plays the role of the coordinate for people whose politics is organised around something else — a libertarian, a theocrat, a Green, a nationalist whose economics are left-wing but whose cultural views are not?

2. Identify where the mapping breaks. A line has one dimension and a natural ordering; does political disagreement? What features of the source (continuity, symmetry around the centre, the implication that adjacent positions are similar) does the analogy quietly import into the target, and which of these imports are doing argumentative work rather than being harmless scenery? In particular, does 'extreme' mean far from the median voter, far from some ideal, or intense in temperament, and does the analogy trade on sliding between these?

3. Test the horseshoe claim against the audit. If the line's metric is attitudes to equality, do the far ends actually converge on that metric, or only on others (authoritarianism, conspiracism, contempt for liberal procedure) that the original line was never measuring? Is the horseshoe a discovery about the spectrum or an artefact of swapping metrics mid-argument?

4. Say what, if anything, survives of my lecturer's use. Can the equality-axis reading be defended as a claim about what has historically held the left and right families together, even if the geometry is misleading — and if so, should we keep the line and discard the geometry, or discard the line and keep the underlying claim?

5. Finally, if the line analogy is more trouble than it's worth, what would be a better model for the seminar — a two-dimensional space, a family-resemblance picture in which 'left' names a loose cluster with no single essence, a historical narrative about coalitions rather than positions — and what would each of those models make of the horseshoe argument?

I'd like a verdict, not just a survey: which of the two uses, if either, comes out of the audit intact?
