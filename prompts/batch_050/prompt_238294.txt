I have no formal training in philosophy or music theory, but I have been turning a question over for a while and would like to approach it properly before I read what others have said. The question: how can a piece of instrumental music be sad? A melody is a pattern of pitches in time. It has no mind and feels nothing, and unlike a sad face or a sad sentence it does not seem to point at anything beyond itself. Yet describing music as sorrowful is not loose talk; listeners largely agree on which passages are sad and which are triumphant, and we can be wrong about it.

What I want from you is a specification: a list of desiderata that any adequate theory of emotion in instrumental music would have to satisfy, with the consideration or case behind each one. Please include at least the following, and add whatever I have missed:

- The cases a theory must explain. For example: music that sounds sad without making anyone feel sad; sad music that people deliberately seek out and enjoy; disagreements between listeners about a piece's character; the fact that sadness in music seems to come in shades (grief, resignation, tenderness) rather than just a sad/not-sad toggle; and whether a theory must also handle cheerful, angry, and serene music or may legitimately be built around the sad case alone.
- The distinctions a theory must not collapse. The ones I keep stumbling over: music expressing an emotion versus music arousing that emotion in the listener; an emotion belonging to the music itself versus belonging to some imagined persona or performer behind it; and recognizing that a piece is sad versus feeling anything at all. Tell me whether these distinctions are real requirements or artifacts of how I happen to phrase things.
- The facts a theory must be consistent with, stated carefully. I have the impression that the pitch patterns of sad music resemble the slowed, falling contours of sad speech, and that some emotional associations with music vary across cultures while others seem widespread. I am not sure any of this is established rather than folklore, so please say which empirical claims a theory may safely lean on and which it should merely remain compatible with.
- Which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are the signature demands of particular camps -- for instance, someone who thinks the whole phenomenon is learned convention versus someone who thinks music moves us by resembling bodily or vocal expression versus someone who thinks it is about expectation, tension, and release.

Two further requests. First, if some combination of these desiderata appears jointly unsatisfiable -- if, say, any theory that explains why we enjoy sad music must give up some plausible claim about what sadness in music is -- say so explicitly, because that seems like the most interesting thing the exercise could produce. Second, indicate which single requirement eliminates the most candidate theories at a stroke.

If you sketch briefly how the main families of views fare against the final list, that is welcome, but the list itself is the deliverable. No references, please; I will do the reading afterward.
