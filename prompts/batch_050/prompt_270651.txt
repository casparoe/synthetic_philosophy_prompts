Give an original example of a situation involving higher-order evidence—one that does not simply recycle the standard cases from the literature, such as the sedated pilot or the dinner-bill calculation that a respected peer computes differently. In particular, construct the example so that the higher-order evidence suggesting the person's reasoning is unreliable is itself produced or filtered through the very cognitive process whose reliability is in question (for instance, a self-administered alertness test whose results the person must interpret while impaired). Then take a definite position on the epistemological question the example raises: does it show that epistemic akrasia—continuing to believe p while believing that one's evidence does not support believing p—can be rationally permissible, or does rationality always require adjusting the first-order belief in line with the higher-order evidence? Commit to one verdict, give your reasons, and address the strongest consideration on the other side rather than merely laying out both options.
