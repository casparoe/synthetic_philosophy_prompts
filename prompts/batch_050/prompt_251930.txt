For the past few weeks, my philosophy reading group has been stuck on Kavka's toxin puzzle, and our discussions have stalled in a way I find more interesting than the puzzle itself. The setup, as everyone knows, is roughly this: an eccentric billionaire will pay you a fortune if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon -- a toxin that will make you miserably ill for a day but cause no lasting harm. The payment depends solely on your intention at midnight, verified by a perfect intention-detector; once midnight passes, nothing requires or rewards you for actually drinking. Half the group insists that a fully rational person simply cannot win, because at midnight you already know that tomorrow you will have no reason whatsoever to drink, and you cannot now intend to do what you know you will have no reason to do. The other half insists this conflates the rationality of intending with the rationality of acting: intending to drink is the best action available to you tonight, full stop, and whatever tomorrow-you does is tomorrow-you's problem. Both sides accept every stipulation of the case. Nobody disputes any empirical claim. And yet after three sessions nobody has conceded an inch.

Two modified versions, proposed by members of the group, have only deepened the impasse, and I would like the diagnosis to engage with them. In the first, the billionaire additionally offers tomorrow-you a separate modest payment, large enough to make drinking worthwhile on its own but small enough that you'd never accept the toxin without it -- so the intention is trivially formable. The "cannot intend" camp says this variant changes the subject rather than solving the puzzle; the other camp says it reveals that the original puzzle was only ever about how much incentive the act itself carries, which suggests the deep disagreement is about a threshold, not a principle. In the second variant, the prize goes not to you but to a desperately needed charity, and drinking the toxin affects only you. Strangely, several people switched sides on this version without being able to say why, which makes me suspect that judgments about what one "can rationally intend" are quietly tracking judgments about what one would be blameworthy for failing to do.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about the puzzle at all, but a disagreement in the theory of intention wearing the puzzle as a disguise: one side holds that reasons for intending must bottom out in reasons for acting as intended, while the other treats intention as a state that can be rational to occupy for reasons that never become reasons to act. An alternative hypothesis I have considered is that the dispute is partly verbal, hinging on an unnoticed slide between "cannot intend" as a psychological claim and "cannot rationally intend" as a normative one. Please diagnose which of these it is -- substantive, verbal, or two camps answering different questions under one heading -- using what each side actually says, including how they respond to the two variants. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction, a result in decision theory, empirical work on how intentions are in fact formed, or something else. If you think no resolution should be expected because the puzzle just exposes a bedrock divergence in starting intuitions about rationality, argue for that verdict instead rather than splitting the difference.
