On a rationalist forum I have followed for years, two regulars were debating the paradox of deontology through the usual case: may you intentionally kill one innocent person to prevent someone else from intentionally killing five? Lior argued that if rights matter because victims matter, then one violation is preferable to five, and refusing to act privileges the cleanliness of one’s own agency over four additional victims. Mara replied that this treats rights as violation-events to be minimized rather than claims persons hold against agents; it would authorize using one person as material whenever doing so prevented enough wrongdoing by others. Lior then proposed a victim-relative constraint: from the victims’ perspective, one person has a complaint rather than five, so the agent’s special relation to the act should not determine the result. Mara answered that this merely reproduces consequentialism under a different label, though she accepted a threshold constraint under which killing could become permissible to avert a sufficiently catastrophic disparity. Lior objected that the threshold cannot explain why one-for-five is forbidden while one-for-five-million is permitted, and that its location looks arbitrary. Mara responded that even vague boundaries can mark real moral differences, as with emergencies that justify otherwise impermissible property damage. Please adjudicate each move in turn, identify which claims are sound or mistaken, and explain whether either disputant has actually resolved the paradox rather than relocated it.
