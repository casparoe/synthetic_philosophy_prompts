I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine. Several times a month, I edit articles that invoke the public: the public has lost trust in science, people want certainty from experts, readers are outraged by a study, society no longer believes in progress, and so forth. I need a reusable pre-publication procedure for deciding when such language describes a real collective phenomenon and when it manufactures an abstraction for which nobody is answerable.

Please base the procedure on Kierkegaard’s critique of the press and the public, but do not provide an exegesis, bibliography, or quotations. The relevant concerns are that the public can become an abstraction rather than a collection of concrete and responsible persons; that the press can create the appearance of shared conviction by circulating opinions detached from action or accountability; that pressure toward leveling can lead people to replace their own judgments with whatever everyone is presumed to think; and that a journalist may conceal a personal editorial judgment behind alleged public sentiment. I want those concerns translated into something a line editor without philosophical training can apply in about twenty minutes.

The procedure should give the tests in the order an editor should run them. At minimum, address:

- how to identify the grammatical subject of a public-sentence and determine what it is actually claimed to believe, want, feel, know, or do;
- how to classify the sentence as an empirical generalization, an institutional claim, a moral judgment, a prediction, a description of discourse, or an editorial judgment in disguise;
- what evidence would suffice for each classification, including when surveys, behavioral data, organized action, audience metrics, interviews, and observable institutional decisions are or are not probative;
- how to force distinctions among the whole population, an affected group, the magazine’s audience, social-media participants, subject-matter experts, an organized movement, and a small number of politically consequential actors;
- what an editor should require concerning denominator, selection mechanism, intensity, dissent, timing, and the difference between expressed preference and behavior;
- when the sentence should be localized, pluralized, attributed to a named actor, converted into a claim about the journalist’s own judgment, or deleted;
- where the procedure must stop because the issue is irreducibly evaluative rather than factual.

I also want the procedure to test for a failure mode I call agency leakage. Agency leakage occurs when a sentence assigns causal or rational agency to the public, but scrutiny reveals that the effective actor is a platform’s ranking system, a polling instrument, an editorial selection process, a funding body, a small elite, or no identifiable actor at all. Define the test precisely enough that two competent editors would usually apply it alike, while identifying its boundary cases. In particular, distinguish agency leakage from legitimate shorthand for institutions and from genuinely emergent collective behavior.

Present the result as a compact flowchart in words, with explicit pass/fail conditions, required rewrites, escalation points, and the judgments that cannot be eliminated. Then apply it to three examples: a lead claiming that the public has lost trust in science, supported by a nationally representative survey; a lead claiming that readers are demanding stories about artificial intelligence, supported mainly by engagement metrics; and a passage claiming that society now treats longevity as the highest human good, supported by cultural examples selected by the writer. At least one application should produce an editorial decision likely to disappoint the writer. Finally, state what the procedure cannot accomplish and where Kierkegaard’s framework may mislead an editor working with contemporary polling, platforms, and fragmented audiences.
