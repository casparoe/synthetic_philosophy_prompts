Since I retired from the chemistry department last year I've been going along to a philosophy reading circle that meets at the library on Tuesdays, and last month we did the experience machine -- the thought experiment where you're offered the chance to spend the rest of your life floating in a tank with electrodes on your skull, having any experiences you like, and everything feels exactly as real as this does. The facilitator was visibly pleased that almost everyone said they wouldn't plug in, which I gather is the standard result, and we dutifully trotted out the usual reasons: we want to actually do things and not merely have the experience of doing them, we want to be a certain sort of person rather than a sort of blob, and we want some genuine contact with reality rather than a very convincing feed. What struck me, though, sitting there, is that nobody ever talks about the poor sod whose job it would be to sell the thing. In my old life I sat through enough marketing presentations to know that someone, somewhere, would have to write the brochure, and every single objection we raised in the group is precisely the sort of objection a competent copywriter is trained to smother in soothing corporate language. So I want that brochure.

Write the glossy marketing webpage for a consumer experience machine -- give the company a name -- in the deadpan, relentlessly upbeat voice of a Silicon Valley startup or one of those infomercials that used to run at three in the morning. I want a hero pitch at the top, a few glowing testimonials from satisfied floaters (or their families, since the floaters themselves are presumably busy), maybe a pricing or financing plan, and above all a decent FAQ section, because the FAQ is where the real work happens: each question should be one of the actual philosophical objections, translated into customer-anxious language ("Will I still be *really* hiking in the Lakes?"), and each answer should quietly neutralise it with the kind of euphemism that sounds reassuring until you notice what it just conceded. The comedy has to come from the company taking the objections perfectly seriously and absorbing them into sales-speak -- never naming them, never winking at the reader, and never conceding that anything is wrong with the product. It should be funny to someone who knows the Nozick material and why people resist plugging in, but still readable to my neighbour who thinks philosophy is crossword puzzles. Around 700-800 words. And do keep it deadpan; if the piece ever admits it's a joke it has failed.

One more thing, and this is the lab habits talking: after the piece, give me a table with three columns -- "Brochure section", "Objection being defused" (meaning the genuine philosophical worry about well-being or reality lurking underneath), and "Euphemism it uses" (the specific phrase in your copy that does the defusing). I'd rather have that as a proper table than worked into the prose, since I want to pin it up for the reading group next week and occassionally point at it.
