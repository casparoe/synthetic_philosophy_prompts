I'm a high school senior taking an elective in epistemology and philosophical method, and our teacher has us peer-mark each other's practice essays as exam preparation. Each of us receives a classmate's answer, plus the original question, and we have to return a grade out of 20 with written comments. I want comments I could hand back to my classmate without embarrassment, so point-by-point rather than a general impression.

The question was:

"Philosophers often defend thought experiments by comparing them to scientific experiments. Others, following Daniel Dennett, treat many of them as 'intuition pumps' — devices that steer us toward a verdict rather than teach us anything. With reference to at least two thought experiments you have studied, discuss whether philosophical thought experiments are better understood as experiments conducted in the mind or as persuasion devices, and explain how, if at all, one can tell a good one from a misleading one."

My classmate's answer:

Thought experiments sit between experiments and arguments, but on balance they are closer to experiments. Like experiments, they generate new information: we do not know in advance how we will judge a case, so the verdict is a discovery rather than a premise disguised as a conclusion. The trolley problem illustrates this. In Philippa Foot's original case, a bystander must decide whether to pull a switch diverting a runaway tram so that it kills one person instead of five, and the near-universal judgment that switching is permissible was new information about our concept of harm. Judith Thomson later added the fat man variant, where most people refuse to push one person off a bridge to stop the tram, and the contrast between the two verdicts taught us something nobody had deduced from premises beforehand.

Dennett coined the term "intuition pump" for any thought experiment engineered to draw out an intuition, and he suspected that many of them work by framing rather than by insight. This worry is real, but it can be overstated. Galileo's falling-bodies case refuted Aristotle's theory of motion without any new empirical data, since a heavy and a light body chained together would have to fall both faster and slower than the heavy body alone. Similarly, Gettier's cases proved that knowledge is not justified true belief, which shows that a thought experiment can settle a philosophical question conclusively.

The main residual worry is that intuitions are just gut feelings, and gut feelings vary. Since verdicts on trolley cases differ across cultures, such cases cannot reveal anything about the concept of harm itself. Even so, good and bad intuition pumps can be told apart: a good one, like Galileo's, forces an inconsistency, while a bad one merely makes one option vivid. The framing worry therefore applies only to the bad ones, and the experimental model survives for the rest.
