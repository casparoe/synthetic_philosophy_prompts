I sit on the institutional review board of a university hospital, and a protocol now before us has split our reviewers down the middle. The study proposes to randomize adults presenting with a first episode of moderate major depression between two treatment strategies: medication-first (an SSRI, with psychotherapy added later if response is inadequate) and psychotherapy-first (structured weekly therapy, with medication added later if response is inadequate). Both strategies are already used routinely at our hospital; both are consistent with current guidelines; and there is no head-to-head evidence establishing which works better as an initial approach. The investigators point out, accurately, that our own department is divided: some psychiatrists start nearly every such patient on medication and are privately convinced that delaying it is negligent, while others start nearly every such patient in therapy and are privately convinced that medication-first medicalizes ordinary suffering. Outcomes under either strategy are acceptable, and the trial would answer a genuinely important question.

The objections before us are these. First, one reviewer argues that equipoise is a fiction here: since every physician involved holds a firm individual conviction, no participant can be told truthfully that the experts are uncertain — randomization is a coin flip between two options, and whichever arm a patient lands in, her treating psychiatrist believes it is the wrong one. The investigators reply that individual conviction is exactly what the evidence-free zone looks like from the inside, and that the disagreement within the medical community is itself the state of equipoise that justifies the trial. Second, the consent form must disclose that, absent the study, the patient's own psychiatrist would simply have chosen one strategy — and that the psychiatrist's choice may depend on which clinic door the patient happened to walk through. The investigators want to omit this as confusing and irrelevant to the decision at hand.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate to the board before our next meeting. It should open with a clear recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with specified conditions — and then give the reasoning, including: (a) whether community equipoise, understood as genuine unresolved disagreement or uncertainty in the medical community, is the right standard for permitting randomization, or whether it confuses a sociological fact about what clinicians happen to believe with an epistemic fact about what the evidence warrants, and what the right standard implies for this protocol; (b) how the memo should answer the reviewer who thinks an equipoise-based justification sits uneasily with a physician's duty of personal care to the patient in front of her — that is, whether enrolling patients in a trial is a departure from that duty that needs separate justification, or no departure at all; and (c) a ruling on the consent-form dispute. State the strongest consideration on the losing side of your recommendation and say why it does not prevail. Keep the memo under 700 words.
