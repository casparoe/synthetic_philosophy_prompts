Two friends of mine got into a long argument after a campus talk about using a CRISPR gene drive to wipe out the invasive mice on an island where they're eating seabird chicks, and I honestly couldn't settle it. Basicly how it went:

(1) Alex opened with: the standard objection, that a gene drive is irreversible, proves way too much. Eradicating smallpox was irreversible. So is damming a river, and so is dropping rat poison all over an island, which conservation groups already do as a matter of routine. If "you can't take it back" vetoes an action, then doing nothing is vetoed too, because letting the seabird colony collapse is also irreversible. So irreversibility can't be the deciding factor.

(2) Maya said irreversibility was never the real objection — uncontainability is. A dam stays where you pour it. A drive can ride off the island in the hull of a fishing boat, and once it's on the mainland there's no recall and no off switch. And the ecosystems and countries downwind never consented; there's not even anyone who *could* consent on their behalf.

(3) Alex came back that the mice themselves are an uncontained, self-spreading intervention some sailor released centuries ago, so "leave nature alone" is a myth — the choice is between two human-caused states, not between action and innocence. And the newer designs are daisy-chain drives that run out of fuel after a few generations, engineered precisely so they can't establish on a mainland. Judging all gene drives by the cane toad, he said, is like judging all pharmaceuticals by thalidomide.

(4) Maya: the thalidomide line fails because drugs don't reproduce — a bad drug only hurts whoever takes it, while a drive that behaves unexpectedly rewrites a shared environment for everyone. And daisy chains only "fail safe" if nothing mutates or recombines once loose, which is exactly the kind of prediction biology keeps humbling us on. The cane toad wasn't a freak accident, it's the base rate for deliberate releases. She added that the same goes for de-extinction: every dollar and headline for mammoths and passanger pigeons is pulled from species still alive, and it teaches the public that extinction is undoable, so why bother preventing it.

(5) Alex's last move: then your objection isn't about gene drives at all, it's about deliberate ecological intervention as such — including the poison drops you just granted are routine, which are cruder and kill plenty of bystander animals, while a drive at least touches only the target species. And the de-extinction point is a false choice: the cloning and editing tools built for the mammoth are already being used on living endangered species like the black-footed ferret, so the money and expertise flow in both directions.
