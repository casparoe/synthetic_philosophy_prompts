I am a final-year undergraduate taking a course on the philosophy of work and labour, and I must submit a one-page prospectus for my supervised research essay. I would like you to draft that prospectus for me.

The essay concerns the moral foundations of the right to strike. The standard justifications I have encountered treat a strike as, in essence, a collective resignation withheld: since each worker may individually quit, workers may jointly withdraw their labour, and the pressure on the employer arises from the loss of that labour. Call a strike that works this way a withholding strike. Many real strikes, however, seem to derive their leverage from something quite different: the immediate costs fall on uninvolved third parties — commuters, patients, customers, other firms — and it is the pressure those parties bring to bear on the employer or the government that ends the dispute. Call these leverage strikes. My question is whether the justification that covers withholding strikes also covers leverage strikes, and, if it does not, what the right to organise actually protects.

The prospectus should contain the following:

1. The research question stated precisely, including the distinction above and why it might matter morally — in particular, whether third-party costs in a given strike are a foreseeable side effect, an intended means, or the entire mechanism of the strike, and whether that difference should change our verdict on its permissibility.
2. Why the obvious answers are insufficient: both the permissive thought that a strike is merely a jointly exercised right to quit, and the restrictive thought that any strike imposing serious costs on third parties is thereby illegitimate (which would seem to condemn exactly the strikes that work).
3. The argumentative strategy I propose to take: whether strikes are best analysed on the model of self-defence, of fair bargaining, or of democratic contestation, and why the chosen model is better suited than the alternatives to the question of third-party costs.
4. What a successful result would look like — ideally, a principled test distinguishing permissible from impermissible leverage strikes — and what would count as the project failing.
5. A realistic scope for an essay of roughly 4,000 words, given eight weeks of work and one supervisor meeting per fortnight, including which sub-questions I should explicitly set aside.

You may assume the question has practical stakes (legislatures in several countries are considering restrictions on strikes in transport and healthcare) but the project should be presented as philosophical rather than empirical.

Constraints: please do not include a bibliography or cite specific works; I will supply the references myself. You may refer to positions and arguments by description. Write in British English, at roughly 700 words.

Conclude with a final line of the form "Working hypothesis: ..." stating, in a single sentence, the position the essay would defend.
