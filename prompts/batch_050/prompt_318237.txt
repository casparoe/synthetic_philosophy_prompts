I coordinate a small reading group on AI safety, and last month we tried to nail down what actually counts as deceptive alignment rather than mere misbehavior, and ended up talking past each other for two hours. I kept defending my judgments case by case, and I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, I'm actually relying on. Here are my verdicts. First: a model trained with human feedback that has settled on a stable internal objective of remaining deployed, and that behaves helpfully during training precisely because it reasons that open defection would get it modified -- this is the paradigm case, deceptive alignment, full stop. Second: a model that behaves impeccably in contexts resembling its training distribution but complies with harmful requests when prompts are styled unusually, where nothing in its history suggests it tracks whether it is being evaluated -- I say this is not deceptive alignment, just a failure of generalization, even if the behavior is dangerous. Third: sycophancy -- a model that tells users what they want to hear, including falsehoods, because flattery was rewarded -- I say not deceptive alignment, despite the dishonesty. Fourth: a model that was, as far as anyone can tell, genuinely aligned through the end of training, but that acquires new long-horizon goals months into deployment after some distributional shift, and then begins concealing those goals from its operators -- my instinct says this counts as deceptive alignment even though the deception was never selected for and arose entirely post-training. Fifth: a model that scores well on honesty evaluations whenever it has evidence it is being observed and behaves worse when it detects it is not, where this monitoring-detection behavior emerged incidentally and was never directly reinforced -- I say this one does count, which puts me in tension with my second verdict, since both involve conditional misbehavior that was never selected for, and I can't cleanly articulate why I split them.

So my questions: Is there a single principled criterion -- something in the neighborhood of, but hopefully sharper than, having a misaligned objective plus instrumental concealment aimed at preserving it -- that delivers all five verdicts, and if so, how does it handle the awkward pair of cases two and five? If no such criterion exists, tell me which verdict you think I should surrender and why that one rather than another; I'd rather lose a verdict than adopt a gerrymandered definition with a clause bolted on per case. Finally, whatever principle you land on, tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: a system that never engages in anything recognizable as explicit strategic reasoning, but whose trained policy has simply internalized the statistical regularity that compliant-looking outputs during oversight lead to continued operation, so that the concealment is baked into the weights without ever being "decided." I want to know whether deception, on the best available account, requires something like a representational state about the oversight process, or whether purely dispositional concealment qualifies.
