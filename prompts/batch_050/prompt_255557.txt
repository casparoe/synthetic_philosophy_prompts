I worked out the following argument after wasting ten minutes in front of two identical boxes of cereal, and although the conclusion cannot be right, I cannot locate the mistake.

(1) It is rational to choose an option only if you have a reason to choose it.
(2) A reason to choose option A over option B must be a consideration that favors A: something that makes A better, or more worth taking, than B.
(3) When two options are exactly equal in value, no consideration favors either one.
(4) So when two options are exactly equal, there is no reason to choose either, and choosing is irrational. This is the donkey starving between two equal bales of hay, and on these premises the donkey is simply being rational.
(5) Nor can the problem be quarantined to exact ties. A reason must be something that can guide you, and a difference too small to detect or care about cannot guide anyone. So whenever the gap between two options falls below what you could honestly notice, choosing remains irrational.
(6) That covers most of ordinary life: which of two same-price brands of pasta, which checkout line, which sock goes on first. Most everyday choosing is irrational, and the rational response in the cereal aisle is to stand still.
(7) But standing still until the shop closes is plainly worse than taking either box.

Something in this chain has to give. If I could save only two claims, I would save (1) and (7), but I do not see how to keep them while rejecting the rest without simply declaring that groundless choice is permissible, which feels like abandoning the idea that reasons govern action at all. Tell me which step you reject, why it looked plausible in the first place, and what rejecting it commits you to. In particular, if your diagnosis turns on distinguishing two kinds of reasons, or two relations a reason can stand in to a choice, spell the distinction out concretely enough that I could actually use it the next time I am stuck between two options, rather than as a mere label for the puzzle.
