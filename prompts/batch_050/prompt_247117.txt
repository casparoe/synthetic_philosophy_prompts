Before I retired I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and one habit I have carried into my late-blooming interest in philosophy is a respect for the question of what makes a *kind* of evidence trustworthy in the first place. Recently I have been reading about synthetic media — fabricated audio and video that is increasingly difficult to distinguish from genuine recordings — and I keep noticing a phenomenon for which I cannot find a settled name. I would like to know whether one exists.

The phenomenon is not the obvious one. The obvious one is that a person caught on an authentic recording can now dismiss it as fabricated and sometimes get away with the dismissal. What interests me is something quieter. Over the past several years I have watched journalists, courts, and ordinary people raise their standards for *all* recordings — including recordings that nobody has accused of being fake, in contexts where fabrication is neither alleged nor even plausible. A video that fifteen years ago would have settled a question now counts as merely one piece of evidence among others. The striking feature, to my physicist's eye, is that this devaluation requires no actual fakes whatsoever. It is driven entirely by the known *possibility* of fakes, much as a currency depreciates once counterfeiting becomes easy, even in the hands of people who have never held a counterfeit note. The evidential instrument itself has been recalibrated downward, wholesale, in response to a change in what is technologically feasible rather than in response to anything that has actually occurred.

So my question is this: is there an established name for the specific pattern I have described — the possibility-driven, across-the-board erosion of an entire evidence-type's default authority, occurring even where no particular instance of that evidence-type is disputed? I am aware that this sits near two other ideas, and I want to know precisely how it differs from each: first, the tactical exploitation of the possibility of fabrication by particular liars in particular cases; second, the general climate of distrust in information that commentators so often lament. If what I have described reduces to one of those two, please show me the reduction rather than merely asserting it. If it is genuinely a third thing, tell me what the relevant literature calls it — and if the literature has no term, coin one. In that case I would like a precise definition, a treatment of two boundary cases (for instance: does the twentieth-century devaluation of eyewitness testimony, once experimental psychology revealed the unreliability of memory, count as the same phenomenon or a different one?), and an honest assessment of whether the distinction is worth carving at all.
