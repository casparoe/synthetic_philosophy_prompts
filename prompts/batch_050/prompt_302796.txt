I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in social epistemology, and this term we have been working through a unit on photographic and video evidence. Last week, during a discussion of synthetic media, one of my stronger students attempted to close down a line of questioning by remarking that deepfakes had finally put "seeing is believing" out of its misery, and that we should stop treating video footage as possessing any special epistemic standing. The remark landed well in the room, but the more I have turned it over, the less sure I am what was actually pronounced dead. In their response papers, half the class treated the slogan as an obviously false piece of folk naïveté that no serious person ever held, while the other half treated its demise as a genuine catastrophe for public reason — and I suspect the two groups were simply disambiguating the proverb differently without noticing.

My own tentative view, which I have not yet presented to the class, is that whatever "seeing is believing" ever meant, its most defensible content was never a thesis about the direct justificatory force of perception at all. It was rather something like a backstop thesis: the value of photographic and video records lay largely in their mere existence and checkability, so that even people who never inspected the footage were indirectly disciplined by the standing possibility that someone could. On this reading, what synthetic media threatens is not anyone's trust in their own eyes but the background infrastructure that kept public testimony honest — which would make the student's dismissal far too quick, but would also make the slogan's original meaning quite different from what either group in my class assumed.

What I would like is a careful disambiguation I can adapt into a handout. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that "seeing is believing" could be expressing — running from near-trivial readings (for instance, that perception is one source of belief among others) through substantive ones (about perceptual entitlement, about the defeasibility structure of visual evidence, about norms of public justification, about the sociology of verification) up to the strongest readings one might construct, including something like my backstop proposal. For each reading, give a verdict: trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, with a sentence or two of justification. Then, and this is the part I most need, tell me which of these readings the deepfake development genuinely touches and which it leaves entirely alone — and on that basis, which precisification, if any, would actually support my student's dismissive use of the slogan, and which would support treating its erosion as a serious epistemic loss. Assume the students reading the handout know basic epistemology but nothing about generative models beyond what they have absorbed from the news.
