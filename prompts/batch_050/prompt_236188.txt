Quick one for a column I'm drafting on what we can and can't know about our own minds. I keep coming across Timothy Williamson's "anti-luminosity" argument, which supposedly shows that no interesting mental state is luminous -- i.e. there's no state such that whenever you're in it, you're automatically in a position to know you're in it. I roughly get the set-up: you wake up feeling freezing, the room warms over the morning in increments too small to notice, and by midday you feel fine. But I keep getting lost at the step where the arguement actually bites.

Could you explain, at gut level, why the "margin for error" principle does the damage -- why knowing that you feel cold at one moment supposedly requires that you still feel cold an instant later, given your basis for the belief barely changes, and why chaining that requirement across the whole morning ends in absurdity. In particular I want to understand why the defender of luminosity can't just reply: fine, knowledge of the feeling cuts off sharply at some point mid-morning, exactly where the feeling itself ends, so there's no chain to ride down. What's supposed to stop that move working? That's the bit every explainer I've read either skips or fudges.

Also, as a side question: does killing luminosity directly kill the KK principle (that knowing implies knowing that you know), or is there daylight between the two targets? Some summaries treat them as the same casualty and I suspect that's too quick.

Keep it under 400 words, gloss any jargon, and no citations or reading lists -- I just need the intuition sharp enough to paraphrase for a general audience.
