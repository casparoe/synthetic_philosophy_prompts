Last night I spent three hours arguing with an old friend about Frank Ramsey's short 1925 talk usually printed as "Epilogue," in which Ramsey — the Cambridge mathematician and philosopher, dead at twenty-six — responds to the idea that the vastness and age of the universe should make us feel our lives are insignificant. My friend read it last month and has been wielding his interpretation of it against me ever since. Our argument ended without resolution, and I want to know, before I see him again, whether the reading he's been pressing on me is actually fair to Ramsey.

His summary, as near as I can reproduce it, ran like this:

"Ramsey's argument is basically an admission dressed up as a rebuttal. He grants that on any objective scale the universe is immense and we are nothing, and that nothing we do matters cosmically — he's a naturalist and an atheist, so he has no way to deny it. His response is to announce that he personally refuses to feel small: he prefers to picture the world with human beings in the foreground and the stars shrunk down to the background, and he goes so far as to say he doesn't really believe in astronomy, which shows that his whole position rests on simply declining to take science's picture of reality seriously. Value, for Ramsey, is just whatever impresses him — thinking and loving happen to impress him more than size does — so meaning is a matter of subjective taste, not argument. His cheerful conclusion that the world is a pleasant place and that he pities his gloomy friends rather than disagrees with them is really just temperament: he was a happy man, so he didn't feel the problem. The upshot is that Ramsey is a nihilist about objective value who papers over the abyss with good humour — a kind of proto-existentialist who tells us to create our own meaning in defiance of an indifferent cosmos, except that unlike Sartre he doesn't even have the courage to look the absurd in the face. And his one positive claim — that minds matter more than stars because stars can't think or love — is just the assertion that humans are the most important thing in the universe, which begs the entire question against the cosmic-insignificance argument."

I'm hoping you can audit this for accuracy. Specifically:

First, which parts of it would a careful reader of Ramsey accept as a fair statement of what the talk says, and which parts are distortions? I have my suspicions — I don't think Ramsey ever concedes that nothing matters, and I suspect my friend is conflating "human life has no cosmic significance" with "human life has no significance" — but I read the piece years ago and can't trust my memory of where exactly Ramsey plants his feet.

Second, the astronomy remark. My friend treats it as Ramsey admitting he won't take the scientific worldview seriously, which would be an odd thing for a philosopher of mathematics and science to say. Is there a better reading of what Ramsey is doing there — something connected to his broader views about theories, perspective, or our practical relation to the world — and does it survive the charge of simply sticking his head in the sand?

Third, the "stars can't think or love" move. My friend reads it as the claim that human beings are, objectively, the most important things in the cosmos — which would indeed beg the question. Is Ramsey instead refusing the standard of comparison altogether rather than trying to win on it, and if so, is that refusal a philosophically respectable answer to the insignificance argument or just a change of subject?

Fourth, the existentialist framing. Does Ramsey's position have the structure my friend attributes to it — objective meaninglessness plus a subjective act of meaning-creation — or is that an anachronistic template being laid over someone who simply doesn't experience the universe's indifference as a problem in the first place? And does the fact that the piece was a witty talk delivered to an audience of friends, some of them prone to a certain cultivated pessimism, matter to how seriously we should take its tone versus its argument?

Finally: my friend's overall verdict is that Ramsey offers no argument at all — only temperament, irony, and a decision not to think about the problem — and that the talk is therefore charming but philosophically empty. Even where his summary is inaccurate, does that verdict survive the corrections, or is there a genuine and defensible position in Ramsey's few pages that my friend has missed? If there is, I'd like it stated as strongly as possible, because I intend to put it to him next week.
