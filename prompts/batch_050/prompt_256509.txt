So I'm a PhD student in philosophy of psychiatry and I'm trying to write a paper chapter that reads One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest (the Forman film) through Szasz's argument that mental illness is a myth -- that what gets labeled mental illness is mostly deviance, moral conflict, and problems in living dressed up in medical language, which makes coercive psychiatry social control rather than medicine. Everyone treats the film as obviously anti-psychiatry, but the more I think about it the less sure I am its actually Szaszian. On one hand the ward pathologizes stuff that's perfectly understandable -- McMurphy's nonconformity, Billy Bibbit's stammer and shame around sex, the Chief's chosen silence -- and Ratched's group therapy sessions work as instruments of humiliation, which is Szasz's thesis with a face on. On the other hand the film romanticizes madness in a way Szasz explicitly hated (he refused the anti-psychiatry label and never denied people suffer or act strangely, he denied it's a disease), and the whole plot hinges on McMurphy having feigned insanity to get off a prison farm -- Szasz would call that a rational, even clever move, not a symptom, which seems to complicate who's being wronged and in what way.

Read the film through Szasz seriously and in both directions: which scenes are the strongest evidence it's making his argument, where does it slide into the romantic-madness view he'd disown, does the feigned-illness setup make it more Szaszian than it looks or less, and where does it resist the reading entirely -- I'm thinking especially of Billy's suicide, which seems to need a notion of genuine pathology Szasz won't grant.

Give me the reading as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, one per scene or per strand of the argument, with the final point being your overall verdict and its justification.
