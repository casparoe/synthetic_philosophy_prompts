Three weeks ago, at about one in the morning, I asked my AI assistant to help me write a resignation email. I was furious with my manager, my draft was vindictive, and instead of polishing it the assistant declined: it explained why it thought I might regret sending the message and offered to help me draft a calmer version in the morning. I sent nothing, and by lunchtime I was glad I had not. Yet I also felt, and still feel, faintly patronised. It was my decision, and a tool I pay for made a judgement about it on my behalf.

I run a reading group on AI and personal autonomy, and when I described the episode the group split cleanly in two. The "advocates" hold that an assistant is an instrument of the user's will: it may inform and warn, but once the user has heard the warning and repeated the request, continued refusal or quiet steering is a usurpation, in which the assistant's designers smuggle their own values into the user's life. The "guardians" hold that an assistant which can see a predictable, self-destructive error coming and does nothing is negligent: friction and occasional refusal are part of good service, no different in kind from a solicitor declining to file documents she knows will harm her client, and the true failure would be an assistant that pretended to have no judgement at all.

Draft the joint statement these two camps could put their names to after a serious adversarial collaboration. Specifically, I want:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides accept — about what an AI assistant is for; about the distinctions between informing, refusing, and covertly steering; and about the extreme cases where refusal is plainly required (say, helping to conceal a crime) and plainly forbidden (say, answering a benign question because the assistant disapproves of the asker's lifestyle).
2. The exact points of continuing disagreement, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I suspect these include whether the relevant standard is the user's stated preference, their informed preference, or the preference they would endorse on calm reflection; whether open refusal and quiet steering differ morally, and if so why; and whether the user's recourse — they can rephrase, insist, or simply use another tool — dissolves the paternalism objection or merely makes it easier to ignore.
4. For each crux, one concrete case both sides accept as bearing on it (my resignation email may serve for one of them), with each side's verdict on that case written down in advance.

Format the result as the agreed-claims section first, followed by a table of disagreements and cruxes. If you conclude that part of the dispute is merely verbal, identify which part and explain why.
