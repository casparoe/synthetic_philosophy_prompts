I'm doing an MFA thesis that involves translating a cycle of short French poems into English, and the catch is that the originals are insanely constrained: every line is exactly three syllables, the rhymes are couplets, and the last poem in the cycle ends on a pun involving the poet's own name. I got into this whole mess partly from reading Hofstadter's big book about translating one little Marot poem dozens of different ways, and I decided to go all-in on preserving the form -- syllable counts, rhymes, the pun, everything -- and let the literal sense bend where it has to.

At my prospectus meeting one committee member (a literary translator who does faithful prose versions of everything) heard my plan and just shrugged and said "well, traduttore, traditore" and moved on. Fine. Except that my friend who defends the opposite approach -- keep the sense, drop the form -- uses the exact same line, against people like me. According to him I'm the traitor for mangling the meaning to save a rhyme scheme. And when I pushed back that HIS versions betray the poem by throwing away the form, which is arguably half the content, he said... traduttore, traditore. So apparently it's treason all the way down, which makes me suspect the proverb isn't actually doing any argumentative work for either of us.

So what I want is a disambiguation. Lay out the distinct precise claims "the translator is a traitor" could be making, from the most trivial (translations are never perfect? some nuance always gets lost?) up through the radical ones (maybe: in poetry the form IS the meaning, so any translation is strictly impossible? or something about meaning being indeterminate across languages so there's no fact of the matter to be faithful TO?). For each reading: is it a platitude, a real substantive thesis that's defensible, or actually false? And then the part I really need -- which readings would actually support my commitee member using it as an objection to MY project specifically, and which would support my friend using it against form-preserving translation? Because it seems to me that if the only true reading is "every translation loses something," that's not a criticism of any particular approach, it's just a law of nature, and neither of them gets to wield it. And if there's a reading that convicts one of us, does it acquit the other, or can the proverb convict us both at once without contradicting itself?

Give it to me as a list I can bring to the next meeting.
