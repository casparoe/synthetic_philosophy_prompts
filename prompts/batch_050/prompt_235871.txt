You're a poet who also happens to know your way around contemporary metaphysics of material objects -- the statue-and-lump-of-clay literature, coincidence vs. constitution, all that. I want a sestina about the statue and the clay. Here's the hook: a sestina is already six stanzas built by permuting the same six end-words, so the form itself is the problem -- same matter, different arrangement, every stanza. Use that. Pick six end-words that can carry the metaphysics (e.g. clay, statue, shape, survive, crush, name -- or better ones you come up with) and make the poem dramatize the actual puzzle: the lump is shaped into the statue in the morning, admired at noon, squashed back into a ball by evening. Track which one of the two can say "I" in each stanza -- the clay should be able to narrate the whole day, the statue only the middle. The envoy should land the asymmetry: something destroyed, something merely rearranged, and only one history to explain both.

Don't name-drop philosophers or use words like "mereological" -- someone who knows the constitution debate should recognize it from what the poem does, and someone who doesn't should still get a good poem about a sculptor and a bad evening. Think carefully about the end-word scheme before you write (retrogradatio cruciata -- get the permutation order right), and after the poem add four or five sentences of prose noting where the form did philosophical work and where you had to fudge the metaphysics for the sake of the verse.
