Please argue that the Kelly criterion has no genuine normative force: that maximizing expected log wealth is just one utility function among others, and that the famous asymptotic results don't give you a way around this. I'd like the argument to engage with the strongest pro-Kelly considerations rather than a strawman, so please address at least (a) Breiman-style optimality claims (Kelly asymptotically outgrows any "essentially different" strategy and minimizes the expected time to hit a wealth target), and (b) the ergodicity-economics pitch that time-average growth rate is the thing you should care about regardless of your preferences. Explain why the "almost surely, in the long run" framing doesn't settle what a rational agent should do at any actual finite horizon, and why "essentially different" might be doing more work than its defenders admit. Feel free to bring in Samuelson's old complaint that long-run growth dominance is irrelevant to someone whose utility isn't log. Also: state clearly what would change your mind -- i.e., what result or consideration, if it held up, would show that Kelly really is rationally privileged rather than just one taste among many, and where you think your own argument is most likely to have a hole.
