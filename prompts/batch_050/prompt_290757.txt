So I got into this long argument at a work offsite last month (we'd shipped a gnarly outage, blameless postmortem culture and all that) and it made me realize I've been carrying around a set of views about moral luck that I've never actually stress-tested. I'm an engineer, no philosophy training, but I read a bunch of Nagel and Williams-adjacent stuff on my own and I've landed somewhere. Here's roughly where I'm at. First, I think resultant luck is irrelevant to blameworthiness: two equally drunk drivers leave the same bar, one hits a pedestrian on the way home and one doesn't, and to me they are exactly equally blameworthy, full stop, the one who got lucky just got lucky. Second, despite that, I'm totally fine with the legal system giving the killer a drastically harsher sentence, and I don't think that's hypocritical. Third, I lean determinist and I'm basically a compatibilist about responsibility, but specifically in the forward-looking sense: blame and punishment are tools we use because they shape future behavior, not because anyone metaphysically deserves suffering, which I suspect is a confused idea anyway. Fourth, I think nobody is blameworthy for anything that doesn't trace back to some choice they made where they knew or should have known better. Fifth, and this is the one that got me in trouble at the offsite, I think the exhausted nurse who misreads a dosage label and harms a patient is blameworthy even in the version of the case where there's no earlier bad decision in the story -- she didn't skip training, didn't ignore a warning, she was just tired and human and messed up. Sixth, I think people who grew up in genuinely brutal circumstances deserve real mitigation when they end up violent, the constitutive-luck thing cuts deep for me.

Before I go shooting my mouth off about this again I'd like to know whether this package actually hangs together or whether I've got some merge conflicts in here. Which of these are in genuine tension with each other and which only look like they clash but turn out fine on inspection? Rank whatever tensions you find by how severe they are rather than just listing them, and for each one tell me specifically what has to give and what giving it up would cost me. If there's a single revision that would buy the most consistency for the least collateral damage, I want to know what it is. I genuinely don't know where the problem is, if there is one, so don't just pat me on the head. One format thing: please no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables -- just write it as continuous prose, like an email from a friend who happens to know this stuff cold.
