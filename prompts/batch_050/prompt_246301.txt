I have no training in philosophy or economics, but I've spent a while thinking about the so-called money pump argument, because it bothers me and I can't tell whether the disagreement I keep running into is a real one. The setup as I understand it: I prefer apples to oranges, oranges to pears, and pears to apples, each time strictly enough that I'd pay a small fee to swap. If my preferences really work like that, a trader can cycle me around and drain my wallet, and this is supposed to show that cyclic preferences are irrational. But when I read people arguing about this, they seem to be stuck, and stuck in a strange way: nobody disputes how the pump works mechanically, and nobody thinks being pumped is pleasant, yet one side insists the mere possibility of exploitation proves the preferences defective, while the other side replies that an agent could simply foresee the whole sequence and refuse to trade at some point, or adopt a policy of never trading toward something they prefer less than what they started with, and thereby hold genuinely cyclic preferences without ever losing a penny. The first side then answers that the policy is just a disguised admission that the preferences can't stand on their own; the second side answers that choices, not preference orderings, are what rationality governs. Round and round.

My amateur hypothesis is that the two sides are using the word "irrational" to mark different things: one means "your attitudes guarantee bad outcomes in some possible world," the other means "you must make a choice you yourself have reason to reject," and that the pump conflates these because it only bites an agent who is both cyclic and naive. But I'm not confident, and I may be flattening a real dispute about whether rationality evaluates preferences at all, or only acts, or only patterns of acts over time.

So rather than asking who is right, I'm asking for a diagnosis: what kind of disagreement is this — verbal, factual, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about what rational assessment applies to? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a formal result, a new kind of case, or nothing?
