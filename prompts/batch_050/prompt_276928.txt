I'm a novelist, and in the book I'm working on there's a minor character, a retired TV weatherman in the 1970s who gets obsessed early with computer forecasting and then slowly unravels when he realizes the better his models get, the more obvious it becomes that the atmosphere will never let him see past about two weeks out. Late in the novel his daughter finds a poem taped inside his desk drawer. I need to actually write that poem, and I want it to be good enough to carry a little thematic weight, since the book's whole thing is that he isn't crazy, he's just the only one around him who understood what the math was saying.

What I'm after is a villanelle, but with one modification that does the philosophical work: the two refrains should each be allowed to drift by a single word every time they come back, so that by the final stanza they've both mutated into lines that mean something quite different from where they started. The idea is that a tiny change, repeated, compounds -- which is exactly the thing he's spent his life learning. Content-wise it has to get chaos right rather than just vibe on it: the weather isn't random, nothing in his worldview has dice in it, and a hypothetical intelligence with perfect knowledge of the present would have no trouble at all. The problem is specifically that our measurements of the world are always slightly off and the errors grow, and that a trajectory can be bounded and orderly without ever exactly repeating itself. No jargon in the poem itself, no name-dropping Lorenz or butterflies or whatever -- it's a weatherman's private poem, not a textbook -- but someone who actually knows dynamical systems should be able to nod along, and everyone else should just read it as a sad, exact poem about watching the sky.

Nineteen lines, tercets plus the closing quatrain, and the two refrains should be recognizable as the same two lines each time they return even as they drift, so the reader can feel the form itself slowly losing grip. If you can, make the very last line land on the difference between the world being determined and the world being knowable, since that's the line the novel keeps circling.
