So I retired from a chemistry department a few years back and have been falling down the philosophy rabbit hole ever since, and right now I'm stuck on something that came up over dinner with my son-in-law, who works in public health. He was explaining how funding decisions get made in his world: treatments get evaluated by how many "quality-adjusted life years" they buy, where a year of life at full health counts as 1 and a year with some condition counts as less, and then these get simply added up across patients. So a cheap pill that gives a modest benefit to a huge number of people with mild conditions can outrank an expensive treatment that saves a small number of people from dying young, because the arithmetic total is bigger. He said all this like it was obvious, but it set off alarm bells for me, because I've just been reading about the "separateness of persons" objection to utilitarianism -- the idea that there's no single subject who experiences the sum of everyone's benefits, so adding my small gain to yours isn't like adding two gains inside one life. Nobody enjoys the total.

Here's my question. What exactly does this whole QALY-summing practice presuppose, philosophically? I want the implicit theory reconstructed: what does it assume about well-being (that it comes in cardinally measurable units? that your units and mine are interpersonally comparable? that it matters only via its total?), and which rival views of ethics or of welfare does the practice quietly rule out -- presumably anything that refuses aggregation across persons altogether, but what else? I'd also like you to be careful about the distinction between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely sits comfortably with. For instance, does summing QALYs actually commit you to utilitarianism, or could a prioritarian or a "fair innings" view run the same spreadsheet with different weights and keep most of the practice intact? And where is the practice genuinely underdetermined, so that several competing philosophies would license exactly the same arithmetic? Finally, if we took the separateness-of-persons worry fully seriously, what concretely would have to change in how these funding decisions get made -- not just "be more sensitive to distribution," but which parts of the machinery survive and which don't?
