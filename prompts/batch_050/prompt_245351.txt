ok so last week I fell down a wikipedia hole about "pragmatic encroachment" in epistemology -- the idea that whether you *know* something depends partly on the practical stakes. Same person, same evidence: you "know" the bank is open saturday when nothing rides on it, but once its really important you suddenly "don't know" and need to go check. There's also contextualism nearby, where the fact stays fixed but the standards for the word "knows" shift depending on who's talking. I find this whole area hilarious because it maps 1:1 onto prod incidents and blameless culture.

So: write me a satirical blameless postmortem (google-style incident review) from a company whose engineering org has fully, literally internalized pragmatic encroachment as official doctrine. The incident: an on-call engineer deploys a change on a sleepy tuesday, when everyone agrees she "knew" it was safe -- then it detonates under black friday traffic, at which point, stakes having risen, the org concludes she did not in fact know, and maybe never did. The postmortem should take this completely deadpan and straight-faced: a timeline where her knowledge status fluctuates with traffic graphs, a root-cause section that concludes the root cause was an insufficiently calibrated stakes threshold, action items like "re-derive what counts as adequate evidence relative to projected blast radius," one senior eng in the comments arguing from a contextualist position that "she knew" was true all along and the incident reviewers are just occupying a high-standards context, and an HR note clarifying she cant be blamed for failing to act on knowledge that (given the stakes at the time of inaction) she did not possess.

~600-700 words, standard postmortem format (summary, impact, timeline, root cause, action items, lessons learned). The humor has to come from the actual philosophy -- the low-stakes/high-stakes bank-case structure should be visibly reincarnated as the deploy -- so that someone who's read the stakes-sensitivity literature recognizes it as a fair parody rather than a random nerd joke. But still readable and funny to a regular engineer who's never heard of epistemology.
