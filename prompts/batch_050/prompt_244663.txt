"Karma Without the Cosmic Ledger"

Most western discussions of karma treat it as a claim about cosmic bookkeeping: good deeds get repaid with good fortune, bad deeds with bad fortune, across lifetimes. Critics then object that the world visibly doesn't work this way -- bad people thrive, good people suffer -- and karma defenders retreat to rebirth, pushing the repayment into unobservable lives. I want to try something different: read karma purely as a theory of moral desert, not as a theory of what actually happens to people.

On this reading, karma doesn't say the universe *does* reward the virtuous. It says the virtuous *deserve* reward and the vicious deserve suffering, in proportion to their intentions and actions. That's it. The mechanism -- rebirth, ripening, whatever -- is a separate empirical hypothesis about whether the deserved outcomes get delivered, and it can be false without the desert claim being false.

This move has a cost. A desert theory with no delivery mechanism is idle: it can't explain anything about the actual distribution of happiness and suffering, which was supposedly karma's main job. Compare: "everyone deserves to keep what they earn" is a norm, not an explanation of anyone's bank balance. So my reformed karma is philosophically safer but explanatorily empty.

But maybe emptiness is the point. The traditional function of karma-talk might be to mark that suffering *isn't* arbitrary -- that it tracks something about the sufferer -- and the desert reading preserves that while dropping the untestable metaphysics. Karma becomes the claim that the moral order and the causal order are answerable to each other in principle, even if no one cashes the check.

I'll argue that this is the only version of karma worth defending, even though its defenders lose the comfort of believing justice is actually enforced.
