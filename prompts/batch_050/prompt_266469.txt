I am a second-year undergraduate taking a course in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I have been asked to run a 75-minute workshop for 16 classmates. Design a classroom game that makes participants feel the force of Darwin’s and Turing’s strange inversions of reasoning: useful competence can be produced and exercised without any participant comprehending why it works.

Base the activity on the following scenario. The class is maintaining a fictional natural-history archive. Each specimen card carries several visible marks, and cards must be routed into one of four trays according to a hidden curatorial rule. The rule should depend on a non-obvious combination or parity of marks rather than on any real biological taxonomy. Participants maintain small populations of paper instruction genomes. Each genome is a sequence of very simple operations, such as inspecting a mark, comparing two positions, reversing a colour designation, skipping a step, or sending the card to a tray. During each generation, runners execute a few genomes blindly, judges score them on routing success, and surviving genomes are copied with tightly constrained mutations and, if useful, a limited form of recombination. In the final stage, give one successful genome to a runner who has not witnessed its evolution; that person should be able to operate it correctly while remaining unable to explain the classificatory principle it embodies.

Please provide:

- A complete materials list and an exact specification of the hidden curatorial rule, including enough example specimen cards to show that the game is runnable. The rule should be learnable by selection but not obvious from one or two cards, and random routing should not perform well.
- A minute-by-minute structure for 75 minutes, with roles for runners, judges and genome keepers; precise inheritance, mutation and recombination rules; and a scoring scheme involving points or tokens rather than money.
- A clear division between the Darwinian and Turingian phases: first, selection across generations should produce a successful procedure without a designer; secondly, a participant should execute that procedure through locally intelligible but globally meaningless steps.
- Safeguards against the activity becoming ordinary conscious problem-solving, such as rotating roles, restricting what runners may inspect or discuss, and using unseen test cards. These safeguards should not make the game feel arbitrary or unfair.
- A short facilitator introduction that establishes the archive fiction without naming Darwin, Turing, natural selection, computation or the strange inversion before the activity.
- A debrief plan mapping particular moments in the game onto variation, selection, inheritance, rule-following and competence without comprehension. Include questions that bring out the difference between the process that produced the procedure and the process of executing it.
- The mistakes or failures that will predictably occur when the game is first run, with practical ways of preventing or responding to them.
- An honest account of what the activity cannot capture: in particular, avoid presenting natural selection as mere trial and error, or computation as simply following instructions, and explain where the analogy becomes misleading.

Participants should leave having experienced the unsettling thought that a group can generate and operate an effective classification procedure even though no individual understands either why it evolved or why its successful steps work.
