I'm a novelist and I'm stuck on a scene I keep rewriting. Two old friends, call them A and B. Years ago A had an affair and was never found out -- he's never told a soul, and it's quietly eaten at him every since. Now B's affair has just blown up in public, and A goes over and tears into him for it. Midway through, B somehow finds out about A's past and throws it back at him: you of all people don't get to judge me.

Write the argument they'd actually have if both were smart and basically honest -- maybe 600-800 words of dialogue. The cartoon version ("you did it too!" / "so?") is useless to me. I want the real fight to be this: A concedes he did the same wrong, so the question is whether that alone strips him of the right to blame B, or whether what matters is what he's done with it since -- repenting, even though he never confessed or made amends to anyone. Have B press hard that A's private repentance is a kind of free-riding: A gets to feel morally clean only because he never paid the social cost B is paying right now, so his "growth" was purchased on the cheap. Have A press back that if standing to blame requires having publicly confessed and suffered, then blame turns into a status contest about who's paid their dues rather than a response to the wrong itself -- and the worst person to call out cruelty would be, absurdly, whoever got caught last.

Both of them should land genuine hits. No strawmen, and they need to respond to what the other actually said, not deliver little speeches at each other. Ideally the scene ends with them having cornered the real disagreement -- something like whether standing is about your current commitment to the norm you invoke, or about your moral status relative to the person you're blaming -- without resolving it. And please make it sound like people talking: interruptions, half-finished sentences, someone losing thier temper, not a seminar transcript.
