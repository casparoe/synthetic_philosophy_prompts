My business partner and I run a small consultancy together, and we have noticed that whenever we both study the same material — last quarter's figures, a shortlisted applicant's file, a draft proposal — we regularly reach opposite conclusions, each of us fully convinced the other is just as careful and just as clever. Reading around the epistemology of disagreement has given me the labels but not a way of working out which response our situation actually calls for, so I would like you to write me a decision tree. It should start from the node "someone I take to be my epistemic peer disagrees with me about p, and I have just learnt of it" and end, in eight to twelve branches, at named positions on how to respond. Phrase every branch node as a question I could answer about my situation without already knowing which view it leads to: at least two nodes should use concrete test cases (the shared restaurant bill we each total differently; the two forecast services that disagree despite reading the same data), and at least one node must turn on whether our evidence is fully shared or merely overlapping, since in our case each of us has usually read things the other has not. The leaves must include split-the-difference conciliationism, steadfastness, a total-evidence view, a justificationist position, and a view on which the disagreement itself functions as higher-order evidence that downgrades my trust in my own reasoning; please also include a leaf for anyone who concludes that the real dispute is over whether the other party still counts as a peer on this subject, and note how that move threatens to make every position self-sealing. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, mark it and say briefly whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect; and flag any pair of leaves your tree sorts together, with a sentence on what further question would be needed to prise them apart.
