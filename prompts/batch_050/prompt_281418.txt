Examination question

Consider the following two choice problems. Prizes are in pounds sterling and all probabilities are exact.

Problem 1. Choose between A1: £1,000,000 with certainty; and B1: £5,000,000 with probability 0.10, £1,000,000 with probability 0.89, and nothing with probability 0.01.

Problem 2. Choose between A2: £1,000,000 with probability 0.11 and nothing with probability 0.89; and B2: £5,000,000 with probability 0.10 and nothing with probability 0.90.

(a) State the independence axiom of expected utility theory.
(b) Show that the commonly observed preference pattern A1 ≻ B1 together with B2 ≻ A2 cannot be represented within expected utility theory, and identify the axiom the pattern violates.
(c) Assess one response available to a theorist who wishes to accommodate the pattern whilst retaining as much of the expected utility framework as possible.

Student's answer

(a) The independence axiom states that for any lotteries L, M and N, and any probability p strictly between 0 and 1, L ≽ M if and only if the compound lottery pL + (1−p)N ≽ pM + (1−p)N, where pL + (1−p)N denotes the lottery that yields L with probability p and N with probability 1−p. Intuitively, if each of two lotteries is mixed with a common third lottery in identical proportions, the preference between the mixtures must follow the preference between the originals; the common component is irrelevant to the comparison.

(b) Taken singly, the preference A1 ≻ B1 is already anomalous for expected utility theory, because B1 has the higher actuarial value (0.10 × £5m + 0.89 × £1m = £1.39m, as against £1m for A1). Strictly speaking, however, it is the pair of preferences that contradicts the theory. Each lottery can be rewritten as a compound lottery built on a common component. Let C be £1,000,000 for certain, and let D be the lottery yielding £5,000,000 with probability 10/11 and nothing with probability 1/11. Then A1 is the mixture 0.11C + 0.89[£1m]; B1 is 0.11D + 0.89[£1m]; A2 is 0.11C + 0.89[£0]; and B2 is 0.11D + 0.89[£0]. The first pair shares the common component [£1m] with weight 0.89; the second pair shares [£0] with the same weight. By the independence axiom, A1 ≻ B1 if and only if C ≻ D, and likewise A2 ≻ B2 if and only if C ≻ D. The theory therefore requires the agent's choices in the two problems to coincide, and the widely reported pattern A1 ≻ B1, B2 ≻ A2 violates the independence axiom. The psychological source of the pattern is the certainty effect: the guaranteed million in A1 exerts an attraction out of proportion to the single percentage point of ruin it removes, whereas in Problem 2, where both options will probably yield nothing, the agent reaches for the larger prize.

(c) One response is to relax the requirement that the value of a gamble be linear in probabilities, so that risk attitudes can do the explanatory work. On the risk-weighted expected utility theory developed by Lara Buchak, the agent ranks a gamble's outcomes from worst to best and evaluates the gamble by taking the worst outcome and adding, for each successive improvement, the gain it offers weighted by a risk function r applied to the probability of attaining at least that improvement. A convex risk function, such as r(p) = p², represents risk aversion: the agent discounts the probability of improvements relative to their objective probability, in effect inflating the significance of bad outcomes such as the 0.01 chance of nothing in B1. This lets the theory represent A1 ≻ B1 without any appeal to the curvature of the utility function, whilst leaving choices between genuinely risky prospects, such as B2 ≻ A2, comparatively undisturbed. Crucially, the theory retains the independence axiom itself: because the risk function is applied only after the outcomes have been ranked, mixtures with a common lottery continue to cancel as before, and all that is abandoned is the linearity of utility in probabilities. The accommodation is thus purchased at a modest axiomatic cost, and the expected utility framework survives substantially intact.
