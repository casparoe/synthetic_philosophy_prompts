Having spent my career in experimental physics and turned to philosophy only in retirement, I still tend to translate the literature into laboratory terms. At our reading group on Kripke's Wittgenstein, two members — Ingrid, a mathematician, and Paul, a philosopher — fell into a dispute I could not settle, and I should like it adjudicated.

The question was this: is there a fact of the matter about which arithmetical function a particular pocket calculator computes, independent of how any community of users would treat its outputs?

Paul argued there is not. Any finite record of behavior conforms to infinitely many functions, and the machine's dispositions are finite and fallible: a calculator with a worn key will "misadd," yet nothing in the device itself marks this as an error rather than conformity to some nonstandard function. Error, he said, is a status conferred by a practice, so correctness is communal through and through.

Ingrid replied that this conflates justification with constitution. The calculator's causal structure grounds genuine counterfactuals about untried inputs, and a worn key is physically distinguishable from correct operation; the regress of error-detecting devices terminates in brute physical regularities, which are not interpretations.

Paul answered that every such counterfactual holds only relative to an idealized description — no stuck keys, unlimited memory, no overheating — and the choice of idealization is ours, so normativity re-enters by the back door. Ingrid responded that one idealization is nonetheless the correct one: whichever best explains the device's design and casts its occasional failures as failures rather than as successes of a stranger kind.

Please say who is right about which specific point, identify what, if anything, both are missing, and give an overall verdict.
