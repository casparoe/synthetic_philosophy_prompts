I have been thinking about whether delusions are beliefs, and about the two-factor picture more generally — the idea that a delusion arises from some anomalous experience together with a failure to evaluate the hypothesis that experience suggests. Rather than argue from a definition, I would like to work out my view from my judgements on cases. Here they are, with my verdicts.

1. After a stroke affecting his right frontal lobe, Mr A insists that his wife of thirty years has been replaced by an impostor identical in appearance. He is distressed, searches her belongings for evidence of the substitution, and is unmoved both by argument and by her knowledge of intimate details of their life. Verdict: deluded, and the impostor claim is a belief.

2. Mr B has no neurological damage. He spends his evenings on internet forums and maintains that a secret cabal directs world governments. His evidence is threadbare, his commitment total, and he has resigned his job to pursue the matter. Verdict: false and profoundly irrational, but not a delusion.

3. Mrs C, a devout member of her church, reports that God speaks to her each morning and encourages her charity work. Her experience is vivid, her conviction unshakable by counterargument, and her congregation shares the framework. Verdict: not a delusion, and I am inclined to call it a belief.

4. Mr D, also with right-hemisphere damage, has a paralysed left arm but flatly denies that anything is wrong with it, producing elaborate excuses when asked to move it. Verdict: close enough to count as a delusion.

5. Mr E's son disappeared at sea a year ago; no body has been recovered. Mr E maintains that his son survived and will return. He is otherwise entirely rational, concedes the odds, yet cannot relinquish the conviction. Verdict: not a delusion — though I am genuinely unsure whether it is a belief.

6. Mrs F, another patient with the same condition as Mr A, insists her husband is an impostor. Yet she continues to cook his meals, sleep beside him, and shows no fear of the supposed substitute. Her conduct sits oddly with her words. Verdict: she is deluded — but I find myself doubting that she believes what she says.

7. Mr G, severely depressed, is convinced he is worthless and that his family would be better off without him. Nothing anyone says shifts this. Verdict: I keep changing my mind.

My questions are these. First, is there a single principle — about what a delusion is, and about what a belief is — that captures all of these verdicts, or nearly all? State it precisely, and indicate which case motivates each clause of it. Second, where my verdicts resist unification, say plainly which of them your preferred principle forces me to abandon: I suspect 1 and 6 are in tension, and that 2, 3 and 5 cannot all be saved together, but I want this demonstrated rather than asserted. Third, I would like the cost of each direction made explicit: what does an account on which delusions are beliefs do with Mrs F, and what does an account on which they are not beliefs do with Mr A, whose impostor claim looks for all the world like something he takes to be true? Finally, apply your preferred principle to two cases I have not considered: a woman with no brain injury who, since her bereavement, takes flickering lights and misplaced objects to be messages from her late husband; and a patient who reports that some of the thoughts in his head are being inserted into it by a distant transmitter, and seems unsure whether they are his. Please write your answer in British English.
