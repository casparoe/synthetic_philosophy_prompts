As a philosophy professor, I have been asked to deliver the opening address at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop attended by philosophers of biology, experimental researchers, and computational modelers. The workshop’s organizing theme is mechanism-washing: our term for the increasingly common practice of calling a causal network, statistical association, or machine-learning model a mechanism merely because it contains boxes, arrows, and variables with biological names. Write an eight-minute address organized into exactly five numbered movements of three to five sentences each, with no separate introduction or conclusion. The address should use a concrete but fictional example of a molecular pathway governing cellular recovery after oxygen deprivation to distinguish a genuine mechanism from both a mere causal graph and a completed parts list; explain, in terms accessible to non-philosophers, why entities, activities, organization, and productive continuity matter to mechanistic explanation; defend the explanatory value of incomplete mechanism sketches while giving the audience workable criteria for distinguishing a productive sketch from promissory hand-waving; address the worry that highly predictive multiscale or machine-learning models may make traditional mechanistic explanation obsolete without either dismissing that worry or treating prediction as sufficient for explanation; and end by charging the workshop with a specific, intellectually demanding question that the different disciplines could plausibly answer together.
