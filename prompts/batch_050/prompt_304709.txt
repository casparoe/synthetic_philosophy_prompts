I coordinate a monthly interdisciplinary discussion group at our university's business school -- mostly MBA students, a few behavioral economists in training, and a handful of philosophy undergrads who wandered in and stayed. Next session is on the endowment effect and loss aversion, and I've been asked to open with a twenty-minute framing talk before the discussion. The audience's problem is not unfamiliarity; it's overfamiliarity. Half of them have absorbed a pop-science version in which the mug experiments prove people are irrational, and the other half have absorbed a defense-of-the-consumer version in which preferences are sovereign and any apparent bias must be a rational response to hidden costs. I want the talk to land them in the genuinely contested middle: the question of when loss aversion is a bias in the agent's own terms and when it's a legitimate feature of their preferences that experimenters have mislabeled.

Plan the talk rather than giving it to me. Lay out the dependency structure: what must the audience already understand about reference points and revealed preference before the bias-versus-preference question is even well-posed, and what breaks if I introduce the normative debate before the experimental setup is clear? Choose one carrying example to build the whole talk around. The classic coffee-mug studies are out -- the business students have seen them in two prior courses -- so specify an alternative scenario (real or constructed) and explain what it must contain and must avoid containing in order to do the same work, including whether it should involve money at all. Then give me the misconception map in the order the misconceptions will surface: I'm expecting at least "loss aversion means people hate losing," "the effect shows ownership creates value," and "if there's an evolutionary story, the preference must be adaptive and therefore rational," but tell me which others to anticipate, where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact, and which one I should let stand until the very end because resolving it too early would kill the discussion.

Finally, specify the check for understanding: one question or mini-scenario I can pose at the close of the twenty minutes whose answer would reveal whether the group grasps that the same choice pattern can be read either as noise around stable preferences or as evidence that the preferences were never there in the required form -- and tell me what to deliberately leave out at this level (prospect theory's functional form? the demand-characteristics critiques of the experiments? the debate over whether loss aversion is even a robust effect?) and how to mark those omissions honestly without inviting a rabbit hole. No readings or citations, please; the talk has to stand on its own.
