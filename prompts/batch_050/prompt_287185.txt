I chair the grants committee of a private foundation that distributes roughly $40 million a year. Our charter directs us to fund whichever projects offer the greatest expected benefit to the world, and until now we have followed the obvious procedure: staff estimate each finalist's likely effects — lives improved, incomes raised, policies changed — and we rank the options accordingly.

At our last meeting, one member challenged the procedure itself. Her argument: everything we fund has consequences stretching centuries into the future. The people whose lives a health program saves will have descendants; the policy we help pass will shift elections, institutions, and technologies we cannot foresee; and the aggregate magnitude of these downstream effects plausibly dwarfs anything in our spreadsheets. Since we cannot estimate even the sign of these effects, let alone their size, no option has a knowable expected value, and ranking grants by expected benefit is an illusion. She proposes that we either score grants solely on their near-term, foreseeable effects or petition the board to drop the maximization mandate from the charter.

Another member replied that this proves too much. Our ignorance about remote consequences is the same for every option on the table: for every way the uncharted future could vindicate one grant, there is an equally probable way it could vindicate another. Symmetric ignorance cancels out of comparisons, so we remain rationally required to choose on the basis of the effects we can estimate. Besides, her colleague's argument, taken seriously, would show that no one ever has reason to prefer any action to any other — including the action of writing her objection — and no one actually believes that.

Draft the committee's decision memo. Recommend a decision procedure — continue ranking by best-estimate expected value, restrict scoring to foreseeable effects, or adopt some third option — with reasons both dissenting members could engage with. Address, at minimum: whether unknowable consequences genuinely cancel out across options that differ in kind (bednet distribution versus policy advocacy versus speculative research); whether a foreseeable-effects rule is principled or merely stipulates an arbitrary time horizon; what exactly the symmetry reply assumes about the shape of our ignorance, and whether that assumption holds when options have asymmetric upsides and downsides; and whether the fact that the cluelessness argument would paralyze ordinary decision-making refutes it or merely embarrasses the rest of us. Close with a bottom-line policy the committee can act on in the next funding cycle, including an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side.
