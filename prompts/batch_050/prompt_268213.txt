In debates about mind uploading, defenders of gradual replacement often reach for the following analogy: swapping a person's biological neurons for functionally equivalent silicon chips, one at a time, is like the ship that has every plank replaced while remaining continuously afloat -- the vessel is never interrupted, and what emerges at the end is still the same ship, not a replica. On this basis they conclude that a gradually uploaded mind preserves both personal identity and consciousness, since at no step is there a moment at which the person could be said to vanish. Critics reply that the analogy smuggles in exactly what is at issue: a plank's job (keeping water out, holding shape) is fully exhausted by its causal role, whereas it is precisely an open question whether a neuron's contribution to consciousness is exhausted by its causal role. Audit the analogy: give the explicit correspondence table (planks to neurons, ship to mind, being afloat to being conscious, and so on), identify where the mapping breaks, and state what the analogy quietly assumes about the metaphysics of persons and the relation between functional organization and experience. Say whether the plank-replacement picture is doing real argumentative work or merely decorating a prior commitment to functionalism, and, if you can, supply a better analogy for whatever conclusion the audit supports.
