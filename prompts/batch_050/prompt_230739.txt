I am setting up a graduate seminar on methodology in the science of consciousness, and before we evaluate any particular proposal I want the specification on the table. Write the desiderata: what any method for the empirical study of conscious experience must be able to deliver in order to count as an adequate method, with the case or consideration motivating each requirement.

Two constraints on the list itself, both of which I want treated as binding from the outset:

1. The method must be applicable, at least in principle, to subjects who produce no verbal reports at all — an octopus, a human infant, an unresponsive patient who may show covert signs of awareness. A method whose machinery is built around the interpretation of utterances cannot simply stipulate these subjects away or declare the question of their experience out of scope.

2. The method must remain usable in cases where report and reality come apart: subjects who confabulate reasons for choices they did not control, subjects who confidently describe experiences they did not have, subjects whose retrospective reports are demonstrably shaped by what happened after the experience rather than during it.

Beyond those constraints, the desiderata should cover at least the following territory, each with the case or finding that makes it a requirement rather than a preference: how the method must treat first-person reports (seriously enough to count as data, fallibly enough to survive the confabulation evidence); how it must handle the distinction between how things seem to the subject and what is actually occurring in the subject; how first-person data are to be connected to third-person measurements without either reducing one to the other or leaving the correlation unexplained; how the method avoids begging the question about which beings are conscious while still needing some interpretive framework to treat anything as a report in the first place (if adopting that framework already presupposes that the subject has a mind, I want that circularity addressed, not ignored); and what would count as the method delivering a verdict about a hard case rather than merely redescribing the data.

For each desideratum, mark whether it is genuinely non-negotiable for any empirical method in this area or whether it is a commitment of a particular camp. Then identify any subset of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied — I suspect at least one such subset exists, something in the vicinity of full first-person authority, incorrigibility-free use of reports as data, and metaphysical neutrality about what consciousness is. If the specification turns out to be inconsistent, that is a result, not a failure of the exercise, and I want it shown explicitly. Also flag which single requirement excludes the most existing approaches, since that tells me where the real disagreement lives.

Finally, and briefly — this should be the short part of the answer — score heterophenomenology, neurophenomenology, and a purely third-person functionalist approach against the finished list, stating for each which desideratum it fails worst and whether the failure looks repairable or structural.
