Before I take a side in the loss aversion fights, I want the spec sheet first. Everyone seems to agree the endowment effect is real and nobody agrees what it's evidence *for* -- a cognitive bias that welfare economics should correct, or a perfectly fine preference that happens to be reference-dependent. Write me the desiderata: what does any adequate account of loss aversion / the endowment effect actually have to do?

Here's what I mean by desiderata, with the cases that motivate them, and I want you to push back or add to these:

- It has to explain why the gap between willingness to pay and willingness to accept is big for mugs and chocolate bars but tiny or nonexistent for money itself or for tokens with known cash values. Any theory that predicts loss aversion for everything gets this wrong.
- It has to handle the exchange asymmetry: randomly hand half a room mugs and the other half pens, and almost nobody trades, even though by symmetry about half should want to swap. This is harder to explain with "people just love their mug" than it looks.
- It has to say something non-circular about why experienced market traders show much smaller gaps. "Experience eliminates the bias" is cheating if experience is defined by the effect going away.
- It has to distinguish genuine loss aversion from boring confounds: transaction costs, bargaining strategy, uncertainty about the good's value, wealth effects. What experiment would actually separate these?
- It has to take a stand on where the reference point comes from. Status quo holdings vs. expectations (if I expected to end up with the mug, does not getting it feel like a loss?) seem to make different predictions, and I want to know which cases discriminate.
- The big one: it has to say what would count as evidence that this is a *mistake* rather than a preference, without presupposing the answer. Some people argue basically no study has ever shown that, because the behavior is coherent given reference-dependent preferences. Is that right?
- And it has to face the welfare problem: if whether something counts as a loss depends on your expectations, what stable thing do nudges and cost-benefit analysis even maximize?

For each desideratum give the case or consideration behind it, and tell me which are genuine requirements vs. which are just one camp's prejudice dressed up as methodology. If some pair can't be jointly satisfied (I suspect "preferences are stable enough to ground welfare analysis" and "reference points move with expectations" are on a collision course) say so explicitly, that's the part I care most about. Then rank which single requirement knocks out the most existing accounts -- my guess is the WTP/WTA money puzzle, but convince me or correct me. Brief scoring of the main contenders (prospect-theory-style hardwired loss aversion, expectations-based reference dependence, the "it's not a bias at all" camp) at the end is fine, but keep it short.
