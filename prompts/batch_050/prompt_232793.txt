I co-organize a monthly metaethics reading group, and for the past year two of our members have been locked in the same argument. One is a thoroughgoing evolutionary debunker in the tradition of Street and Joyce: she holds that natural selection shaped our evaluative tendencies for fitness rather than for moral truth, that this genealogical fact puts pressure on the justification of any realist's moral beliefs, and that only a fairly spare, anti-realist constructivism survives the pressure. The other is a robust normative realist who thinks the debunking argument either proves too much (it would sweep away our epistemic and logical beliefs too, and thereby undercut the debunker's own premises) or is quietly answered by the observation that the very same facts that make an act morally good often also make valuing it adaptive. After one too many circular sessions, the two of them have agreed to try an adversarial collaboration, and I have volunteered to draft the joint statement for them. Please write that draft for me, in American English.

Structure it as a numbered section of claims both parties can sign, followed by a table of remaining disagreements, followed by the cruxes. For the agreed claims, I want formulations that are substantive rather than diplomatic mush -- candidates I'd like tested for joint acceptability include: that a belief's genealogy alone does not show the belief false (the genetic fallacy is a fallacy), but that genealogy can still defeat justification in some cases; that the debunking challenge is fundamentally epistemological rather than metaphysical; that if the correlation between fitness-promoting value judgments and moral truth is merely coincidental, that coincidence would cry out for explanation; and that some evaluative beliefs (for instance, that agony is bad) seem far less genealogically suspicious than others (for instance, intuitions about special obligations to kin or about purity), whatever one's camp. In the disagreements table, state each residual dispute in wording each side would accept for its own position -- I especially want clean statements of the dispute over the scope of the debunking argument, over whether the realist's coincidence-isn't-coincidence reply (the so-called third-factor or harmony strategy) explains anything or merely asserts what the debunker denies, and over whether the debunker's reliance on normative premises about justification is self-undermining. If any of these turns out, on inspection, to be partly verbal -- say, a hidden equivocation on what it means for evolution to explain a belief -- flag which part and why.

For the cruxes, give me the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party, and for each, a case or test both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I'd like the collaboration to use one specific device I proposed and both members liked: a calibration case. Describe an imaginary intelligent species on a planet whose fitness landscape made it adaptive to value something we would regard as morally abhorrent -- say, the routine killing of one's weaker offspring -- and stipulate that the species' members sincerely and reflectively judge this practice good, with exactly the phenomenology of moral conviction we have. For each crux this case bears on, write down what the debunker and the realist each predict or concede about it: does the realist accept that, had we been that species, we would be confident and wrong, and if so, what marks our actual case as different; does the debunker accept that some of our beliefs would have been held even in a fitness landscape indifferent to them, and if so, does that restore their standing? Also include one crux concerning the parity of moral and epistemic norms, with a case both sides accept as bearing on it. Where the two would make the same prediction about a case, say so explicitly -- that kind of convergence is exactly what I want the document to surface.
