A friend of mine, also a software engineer, and I have been going back and forth on the ring of Gyges question: is there any reason to act morally when you know with certainty you won't be caught? She's remarkably consistent across cases, but I can't pin down what rule she's running. Her verdicts so far:

- She found a wallet with cash and an ID on an empty street. Keeping it would have been wrong, she says, even though no one would ever have known. She returned it.
- When freelancing, she refuses to pad billable hours even for clients who never audit invoices. Wrong, full stop.
- She had a chance to take credit for a coworker's idea in a meeting, with zero possibility of exposure. Wrong, full stop.
- A payment glitch at a large retailer credited her account twice; no human ever reviews the ledger and the money would never have been reclaimed. She kept it, and insists that was fine: no one was wronged, the system failed on its own.

Now the new case. She can incorporate a copyleft-licensed library into a proprietary commercial product without complying with the license conditions, with no realistic chance of detection. The library's authors distribute it for free and lose no revenue either way, though the license terms plainly matter to them.

Predict her verdict on the new case. I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in the analysis: give me at least two candidate rules that each fit all four earlier verdicts but diverge here, say which rule you think she's actually following and why, and identify the earlier case that does the most work in telling the candidates apart. Then describe one further hypothetical case that would settle definitively which rule she holds. Finally, step back: does a rule of the kind she seems to be following actually answer Glaucon's challenge, giving a reason to be moral when detection is impossible, or does it merely describe the conditions under which she happens to refrain?
