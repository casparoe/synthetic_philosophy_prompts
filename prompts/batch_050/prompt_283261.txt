Last night, over far too much wine, a close friend and I ended up in a three-hour argument about Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling. She is a devout Christian with a philosophy degree; I am neither, but I have always been moved by the figure of the knight of faith. She walked me, step by step, into an argument that seems to prove something neither of us can accept, and this morning I still cannot locate the defect. I will lay it out as she gave it to me.

(1) For Kierkegaard, the ethical is the universal: a genuine duty holds for every agent in relevantly similar circumstances, and a justified act is one the agent could in principle defend before any rational observer.

(2) By that standard, Abraham's willingness to sacrifice Isaac is simply murder, and cannot be defended to anyone -- Sarah, the community, even Isaac. Kierkegaard grants this; it is why Abraham cannot speak.

(3) Yet Abraham is genuinely justified, not merely excused or pitied. His act has a telos that suspends the ethical: an absolute duty toward God grounded in his private relation to the absolute.

(4) Justification is a normative status, and normative statuses generalize: if Abraham is justified in his circumstances, then anyone in relevantly similar circumstances is justified as well.

(5) The only thing that could make circumstances relevantly similar or dissimilar here must be something the agent could in principle have access to -- otherwise justification floats free of anything that could ever guide or assess a choice. And in terms of everything accessible to the agent -- private certainty, anguish, the ordeal of faith, the absence of any public warrant -- Abraham's situation is exactly that of every sincere zealot who kills on what he takes to be a divine command. Kierkegaard himself insists there is no external mark distinguishing the knight of faith from the madman.

(6) Therefore every sincere claimant of a divine command to kill is justified, which collapses the distinction between faith and fanaticism and makes the ethical useless as a check on anything. That is absurd.

My friend concluded that (1) must go -- that Kierkegaard was simply wrong to identify the ethical with the universal -- and she seemed untroubled by the cost. I am not willing to pay it. If forced to choose, I would rather surrender Abraham's justification than surrender the universality of the ethical, but I would like to know whether those are really my only options.

Which step should I actually reject? Please explain why the faulty step looked compelling in the first place -- my friend is no fool, and the argument convinced two intelligent people for an evening -- and then price the alternatives honestly: what does it cost to reject (4), to reject (5), to reject (3) and let Abraham go, and to follow my friend in rejecting (1)? If you think the argument equivocates somewhere rather than resting on a false premise, name the equivocation precisely and show which reading of the premises each step requires.
