In Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790), Edmund Burke writes:

"A state without the means of some change is without the means of its conservation."

Write a dialogue between two equally competent constitutional scholars — Mira, a Burkean gradualist, and Theo, a rationalist reformer — who sit on an advisory commission considering a proposal to abolish trial by jury in criminal cases and replace it with panels of professional judges, on the grounds that juries are slow, inconsistent, and inexpert. Each of them claims the Burke sentence for their own side: Mira reads it as a warning that institutions must be reformed piecemeal rather than uprooted, while Theo reads it as a command to change what no longer works, since a state that cannot replace a failing institution cannot conserve anything at all. Have them contest what the "means of change" actually are in the case of the jury — what latent functions (legitimacy, civic education, a brake on prosecutorial power) the institution might be serving that its defenders cannot fully articulate, and whether conserving a thing by freezing it is conservation at all. Neither speaker may caricature the other: Mira must concede that reverence can become mere inertia, and Theo must concede that reformers reliably underestimate what they don't understand about inherited practices. Each speaker must answer the other's actual previous point rather than restate a prepared position. Present the dialogue as exactly five exchanges (ten speeches in total), each speech two to four sentences long, with speaker labels and no introductory or concluding narration.
