I am a handling editor at a mid-circulation cell and molecular biology journal, and I have a recurring problem I would like turned into something my reviewers can actually use. Perhaps a third of the manuscripts I send out claim, in the abstract or cover letter, to reveal or elucidate the mechanism of some phenomenon, and my reviewers routinely split on whether the claim is earned: one referee calls a paper a definitive mechanistic advance while another dismisses it as an expensive description. I recently came across the account of mechanistic explanation associated with philosophers Machamer, Darden, and Craver, and it struck me as the right diagnostic tool. On that view, a mechanism is entities and activities organized so that they produce (or constitute) the phenomenon, and genuinely explaining the phenomenon means showing how the organized components and their operations give rise to it -- with attendant distinctions between a mere mechanism sketch with black boxes still to be filled and a more complete schema, between showing how the phenomenon possibly could be produced and showing how it actually is, and between a finding located at the wrong level of organization and one pitched at the level where the phenomenon lives. My reviewers are excellent biologists but have no philosophy background, no patience for jargon, and about an hour to spend on this aspect of any manuscript.

Please convert this account into a decision procedure a reviewer could run on a submission: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points where the procedure terminates with the verdict that the paper does not deliver a mechanistic explanation (as opposed to delivering a partial one, or a demonstration of necessity without production, or a redescription of the phenomenon at finer grain); and the points where the procedure must hand the question back to expert judgment because it is not a matter of checklist logic. In building it, decide which of the philosophical distinctions have to survive the translation -- my guess is that the entities-versus-activities distinction and the sketch-versus-schema distinction do real diagnostic work, while some others can be dropped -- and be explicit about what you drop and why. The procedure must be able to handle, at minimum: knockout and rescue studies that establish that a component is necessary but say nothing about what it does; large-scale omics studies that deliver ranked correlates; papers that name entities but ascribe to them only vague verbs like mediates or regulates; papers that genuinely identify a component's activity but then push the black box down one level and stop; and papers whose phenomenon-level characterization is itself muddled. Verdicts need to be communicable to authors in two or three sentences of a referee report, and they will feed into reject-versus-major-revision decisions, so the procedure should distinguish not-mechanistic-yet from not-mechanistic-in-principle.

Then run the procedure on three representative submissions and show the working: (a) a CRISPR screen identifying six genes whose knockout abolishes a cellular stress response, with epistasis experiments ordering them into a pathway but no biochemistry; (b) a study showing that a specific enzyme phosphorylates a specific protein, that the phosphorylated form relocalizes to the structure where the response occurs, and that a phospho-dead mutant blocks the response while a phosphomimetic mutant partially triggers it; (c) a paper built around an elegant computational model that reproduces the phenomenon quantitatively but whose components have not been identified with anything physical. Please arrange things so that at least one of these applications returns a verdict my journal's community would find uncomfortable -- for instance, a paper of a kind that routinely gets published in high-profile venues coming out as merely a sketch -- and say candidly how a referee should phrase that verdict so it lands as an evaluation rather than an insult. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do: I assume it cannot tell me whether a mechanism is important, or adjudicate between two competing partial mechanisms, or catch fraud, but I would like the limits drawn precisely rather than assumed.
