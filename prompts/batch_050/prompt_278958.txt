So I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on has a subplot where a widow keeps chatting with a bot trained on her dead husband's emails and texts, and I keep having to decide whether the reader is supposed to find it sweet or creepy. That got me noticing that my own gut verdicts about this stuff are all over the place, and I'd like to know if there's actually a principle underneath them or if I'm just a mess. Here are the cases, with my reactions. Widow uses the bot knowing exactly what it is: totally fine, maybe even beautiful. Same setup, but the husband wrote in his will that he didn't want to be simulated after death: not fine at all, feels like a real violation. A museum builds an interactive bot of some 19th century physicist so kids can ask it questions: completely fine, nobody's being wronged. A company builds a bot of a recently dead celebrity that pitches products in her voice: gross, obviously not fine. The widow's bot starts inventing opinions the husband never expressed, confabulating answers to questions he never addressed: this feels worse to me than the original case even though I can't point to anyone new who's harmed. And then there's me: I'm literally putting words in a real dead person's mouth in my novel, basing a character closely on someone who actually lived, and I think that's just fine, that's what fiction is.

The pair I can't reconcile is the will case and the museum case. If the dead guy's explicit refusal matters, why does it stop mattering once you've been dead long enough? Nobody asked the physicist either. And the celebrity case vs. my novel bothers me too -- both put new words in a dead person's mouth without permission, so why is one a violation and the other just art? Is there one principle that gets all or most of these -- something about consent, or posthumous wishes, or fidelity to who the person actually was, or harm to the living people left behind -- and does it have to be some combination? If your best principle makes me give up one of my verdicts, tell me which one and why it's the expendable one. Also tell me what your principle says about a case I keep chewing on for the plot: the dead husband's two adult kids disagree, one finds the bot comforting and the other wants it deleted, and the widow won't pick a side.
