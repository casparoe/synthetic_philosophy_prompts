I am an organizational economist. My working vocabulary is contract theory: principal–agent models with moral hazard and private information, incomplete contracts in the Grossman–Hart–Moore style, delegation and the allocation of decision rights inside firms, mechanism design, and costly information acquisition (rational inattention, experimentation as R&D). I am fluent in all of this and essentially illiterate in the machine-learning literature.

I recently came across the following passage in Gwern Branwen's essay "Why Tool AIs Want to Be Agent AIs," where he argues against the proposal that advanced AI systems could be kept safe by restricting them to purely informational tasks ("Tool AI") rather than letting them act autonomously ("Agent AI"):

"Secondly, because Agent AIs will be better at inference & learning than Tool AIs, and this is inherently due to their greater agency: the same algorithms which learn how to perform actions can be used to select important datapoints to learn inference over, how long to learn, how to more efficiently execute inference, how to design themselves, how to optimize hyperparameters, how to make use of external resources such as long-term memories or external software or large databases or the Internet, and how best to acquire new data."

Here is what I would like. First, restate the argument of this passage — and the Tool/Agent distinction it presupposes — in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. My own first pass is that a Tool AI is simply capital: an asset with no discretion, whose outputs the principal reviews before any action is taken, so the proposal amounts to a refusal to delegate. An Agent AI is then, almost literally, an agent in the principal–agent sense: a party to an incomplete contract, holding decision rights and local information the principal cannot verify, which is exactly the standard case for why delegation creates value despite incentive misalignment. The items in Branwen's list look like information acquisition, optimal stopping in experimentation, and the purchase of complementary assets. Please do this translation properly rather than taking my sketch on faith, and flag every point where you have to bend a concept to make it fit.

Second, and this is the part I care about most, give me a residue report. Which elements of the passage translate cleanly into contract-theoretic terms, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all? Two items in particular strike me as suspect: "how to design themselves" and "how to optimize hyperparameters," since in every model I know the agent's type — preferences, cognitive architecture, cost functions — is fixed by assumption, and a manager cannot retrain his own brain the way this passage describes a system redesigning itself. Likewise, the passage's central mechanism is that the *same* learning procedure handles both acting and knowing; contract theory cleanly separates the production technology from the incentive structure, and I am unsure that kind of architecture-level economy of scope can even be stated in the language of delegation models.

Third, adjudicate the residue. Where the translation fails, is that a defect of my framework — evidence that contract theory's fixed-type assumption blinds it to something real, perhaps recursive self-improvement — or is it evidence that this part of Branwen's argument is doing no technical work and merely sounds like economics? Finally, tell me whether the apparent pun here is deep or shallow: the AI field's "agent" and economics' "agent" look like the same word for a reason, but is the principal–agent model actually capturing why autonomous AI systems are supposed to be riskier, or does the homonym conceal more than it reveals?
