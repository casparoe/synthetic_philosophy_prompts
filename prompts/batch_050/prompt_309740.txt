I've been going around in circles on what makes promises binding, so let me just dump my gut verdicts on some cases and you tell me what theory I apparently hold.

1. Promised my neighbor I'd water her plants while she was away. I forgot two days, she came home early, plants were fine, she never knew. I still think I did something wrong.
2. Guy mugs me and before running off makes me "promise" to wire him money. Zero binding, obviously.
3. When I was freelancing and desperate, I took a gig and promised a turnaround I knew was probably unrealistic, because if I'd been honest they'd have gone with someone else. I think that promise bound me anyway, even though it was squeezed out of me by circumstance almost as much as the mugger's was.
4. Promised my uncle on his deathbed I'd look after his awful dog. He's gone, dog's still awful. I think it binds me at basically full strength even though there's nobody left to let down.
5. I've taken the trash out every Tuesday for two years and my roommate has come to rely on it. Last week I skipped it, bins overflowed, he was mad. I don't think I did anything wrong -- annoying, sure, but I never promised.
6. My kid badgered me into saying "fine, I promise" about ice cream. Feels less binding than a promise I offered freely, but more than nothing. The worn-down "fine, I promise" isn't the mugger case.
7. Promised a friend I'd help him move Saturday. If I pass a car crash on the way and I'm the only one around, I stop and help and I don't even feel residual guilt about the move. But skipping the move for concert tickets I got last-minute: not fine, and I'd owe him something after.

Notice 2 and 3 pull apart (pressure voids the promise vs. doesn't), 1 and 5 pull apart in a different direction (wrong without any dissapointed expectations vs. disappointed expectations without wrong), and 4 seems to kill any account where binding is about not letting people down, since the dead can't be let down.

What I want: the principle, or small set of principles, that captures these seven, stated precisely enough that I can tell which clause is doing the work in each case. If no reasonably simple principle saves all seven, tell me which verdict I should drop and why that one's the weak link rather than the others. Then apply whatever you land on to a case I didn't list -- promises to myself (I "promised" myself I'd stop working past 7pm; is breaking that a wrong, a weakness, or a category error?) -- plus one other case of your choosing where your principle gives a verdict that surprised even you.

End with \answer{...} containing the one-sentence version of the principle you actually endorse, or, if you're overturning one of my verdicts, just the number of the case you'd flip.
