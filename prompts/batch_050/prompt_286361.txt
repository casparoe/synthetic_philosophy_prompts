I have taught causation in my graduate metaphysics seminar for some years, and I have gradually come to suspect that my own judgments about causation by omission do not form a coherent set. I would like to know whether any single principle captures them all, and if not, which judgment I should surrender. Here are the cases, with my verdicts:

1. I hire a gardener to water my orchids while I am abroad. He never appears; the orchids die. His failure to water them caused their death. Verdict: yes.
2. My neighbor, an amateur botanist who knew perfectly well that the orchids needed watering and watched them wilt day by day, did nothing. Her failure to water them is not among the causes of their death. Verdict: no.
3. A drought ruins the region's harvest. I am prepared to say that the absence of rain caused the crop failure, even though no one was under any obligation, or even in a position, to make it rain. Verdict: yes.
4. Last month I could have donated fifty dollars to a highly effective charity and thereby prevented a death from malaria; I did not donate. Despite the counterfactual dependence, my omission is not a cause of that person's death. Verdict: no.
5. An on-duty lifeguard watches a swimmer drown without moving from her chair. Her inaction caused the swimmer's death. Verdict: yes.
6. Sitting beside her is an off-duty lifeguard, an equally strong swimmer who recognizes the situation just as clearly and likewise does nothing. Whether his inaction caused the death, I genuinely cannot decide. This is the case I most want a principle to settle.
7. I notice that a stair in my house has come loose, fail to mention it to a dinner guest, and he trips. My failure to warn him caused his fall. Verdict: yes -- although I had hired no one, promised no one anything, and my guest had no particular claim on my vigilance.

The difficulties, as I see them, are these. Cases 2 and 7 appear structurally similar -- a bystander with knowledge and the capacity to intervene, bound by no explicit agreement -- yet my verdicts diverge. Cases 3 and 4 both involve absences on which an outcome counterfactually depends, yet I call one a cause and deny it of the other, and it is unclear what difference does the work, since no human norm governs the rain. Cases 5 and 6 tempt me to distinguish by role or duty, but I worry that this amounts to reading causal facts directly off moral ones, in which case "causation by omission" would not be a causal notion at all but a covert verdict about blame.

So: is there a precisely stateable principle -- perhaps something in the vicinity of "an omission causes an outcome when it departs from a norm or expectation, where the relevant norms may be statistical, conventional, or role-based" -- that delivers my verdicts in cases 1 through 5 and 7 without collapsing causal judgment into moral assessment? If you offer such a principle, please do three things. First, identify which case motivates each clause of the principle, so I can see where the load-bearing parts come from. Second, tell me what it predicts for case 6, and for two cases I have not listed: a plant that dies for lack of rain on a planet where no agents exist, and a security guard who, on instructions from his employer, fails to sound the alarm during a robbery the employer himself has arranged. Third, state honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, if any. And if you conclude that no principle survives all seven cases, tell me which single verdict is cheapest to abandon, and what the resulting view makes of the difference, if there is one, between saying that the drought killed the crops and saying that the gardener killed my orchids.
