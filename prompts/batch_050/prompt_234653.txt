Ok so I'm in a philosophy of mind module this term and we've just finished the panpsychism block — the idea that experience (or proto-experience, or whatever) goes all the way down to the fundamental level of physics. I got into an argument with a mate about what this implies morally. If electrons or quantum fields or spacetime regions have tiny slivers of experience, and some of those slivers could in principle be valenced (pleasant or unpleasant), then seemingly boring things — smelting iron, running a fusion reactor, even just heating up a cup of tea — might involve staggering amounts of suffering or bliss that nobody's counting. But every time I tried to build a thought experiment showing this, I either ended up smuggling little homunculi into the particles, or just stipulating the conclusion rather than deriving it.

So here's what I want: construct a thought experiment in which a panpsychist who's also a hedonic utilitarian gets committed to a moral verdict that a non-panpsychist hedonic utilitarian would find completely absurd — and where the absurdity comes specifically from the panpsychism, not from the utilitarianism (so it can't just be another repugnant-conclusion-style case, since utilitarians already have those independently). Constraints: (1) no miniature persons — the fundamental subjects only get extremely simple experiences, no thoughts or desires or narratives; (2) the scenario has to work regardless of whether the bearers of experience are particles, fields, or spacetime regions, so it can't hinge on one particular physics being right; (3) nothing science-fictional — no Maxwell's demons, no exotic energy scales, just ordinary matter being rearranged or processed at scales humans could actually manage; (4) the verdict has to fall out of the two views combined, not be written into the setup.

Then afterwards, walk me through it: what verdict the panpsychist utilitarian reaches and why, step by step; why the non-panpsychist utilitarian gets the boring normal answer; whether a cosmopsychist (the view where the universe as a whole is the one fundamental subject, and our experiences are aspects of it) escapes your verdict or just lands in a different weird one; and which structural feature of the case is doing the heavy lifting — the sheer aggregation across astronomical numbers of subjects, the combination problem, the assignment of valence to simple states, whatever it is — plus what happens to the verdict if you remove that feature. Finally, if a panpsychist wanted to dodge the verdict, what's the strongest way they'd attack the case, and does that defence cost them anything they'd definately rather keep?
