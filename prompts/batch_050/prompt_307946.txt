Exam question: Explain Bernard Williams's distinction between internal and external reasons. What is Williams's argument for doubting that external reason statements can be true? Critics sometimes object that if all reasons are internal, then an agent whose motivational set contains nothing relevant (e.g., a cruel person) cannot be said to have a reason not to act cruelly, which seems to undermine practices like blame. How does Williams respond to this kind of worry, and is the response satisfactory? Defend a verdict.

Student's answer: Williams distinguishes two readings of "A has a reason to φ." On the internal reading, the statement is true only if A has some motive that would be served or furthered by φ-ing; on the external reading, the statement can be true even if nothing in A's motivational makeup connects to φ-ing. Williams argues that only internal reason statements can be true. An agent's "subjective motivational set" — her desires, in Williams's term — fixes what reasons she has: if A has a reason to φ, there must be a sound deliberative route from her desires to a motivation to φ. Deliberation here is means-end reasoning: A works out what would achieve the ends her desires set. Williams's argument against external reasons is that reason statements must be capable of explaining action, and nothing can explain an agent's φ-ing unless she could come to be motivated to φ; but an external reason, by definition, is one she could not come to be motivated by, no matter how she deliberated. So external reason statements are false. As for the cruel-agent objection, Williams replies that blame works by reminding the agent of the internal reasons she already has, so the objection fails whenever the agent has any motivation that condemns cruelty. I find Williams's overall position convincing: the alternative leaves it mysterious how a reason could exist wholly insulated from anything the agent cares about.
