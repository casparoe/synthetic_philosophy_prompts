I am a professor of philosophy and have taught Kuhn's Structure of Scientific Revolutions in my philosophy of science seminar for the better part of a decade. For the last three years I have also served on my university's internal research-funding panel, and that service has exposed a gap in my own thinking I would like your help closing. Each funding cycle the panel receives two or three proposals that do not merely pursue a program within their field's reigning framework but explicitly challenge it: submissions whose authors claim, in effect, that the dominant paradigm in their discipline is exhausted, riddled with anomalies, or conceptually confused, and that they are constructing its successor. Our standard criteria -- track record, methodological soundness, fit with the existing literature -- systematically mishandle these submissions. They score near the bottom on 'fit' regardless of quality, and then my colleagues split predictably between dismissing them as crankery and romanticizing them as the next revolution. I have made both errors myself: I once argued successfully against funding a proposal that a neighboring university later supported to considerable acclaim, and I once championed a 'foundations-shaking' project that turned out to be a solitary obsession with no community, no worked exemplar, and no way of recognizing evidence against itself. I want to stop adjudicating these cases on instinct.

You are a philosopher of science with a specialist's command of Kuhn and practical familiarity with how funding decisions actually get made by non-specialists. Your task is to translate Kuhn's account of paradigms, anomalies, crisis, exemplars, and incommensurability into a review procedure my panel can genuinely run. The constraints are these: panelists are generalists who can devote at most twenty extra minutes to any flagged proposal; the decisions recur every cycle; the stakes are careers and internal money; and the panel's recommendation goes to a dean who must be able to follow the reasoning. There is a further, deeper constraint: Kuhn himself argued that paradigm choice cannot be reduced to a neutral algorithm, and a procedure that pretended otherwise would betray the very theory it operationalizes. So I want the procedure to be honest about which steps are genuine tests and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and I want it to say explicitly where it stops and hands the question back to a person. The procedure must also handle incommensurability practically: a reviewer who cannot parse a proposal needs a principled way to tell whether the problem is a translation failure between frameworks or genuine incoherence on the author's side.

Think carefully, and begin by briefly brainstorming which Kuhnian distinctions must survive translation into an operational procedure and which can be safely dropped for a non-specialist panel. Then structure your response in the following sections, in this order: 'Working notes' (the brainstorm just described, roughly 150 words); 'Design principles' (what the procedure must preserve from Kuhn and why, roughly 200 words); 'The procedure' (a numbered sequence of questions a reviewer asks in order, stating for each what counts as a sufficient answer, where the sequence terminates in a recommendation to decline, and where it escalates to specialist judgment, roughly 600 words); 'Worked applications' (roughly 450 words), applying the procedure to three hypothetical cases I supply: first, a cohesive group of cognitive scientists arguing that the information-processing framework in their field has accumulated intractable anomalies and proposing a thoroughly embodied, dynamical alternative with pilot results; second, a lone physicist of no institutional affiliation claiming the foundations of statistical mechanics are incoherent, in a manuscript of evident brilliance that cites no exemplars, engages no community, and specifies no result that would count against it; third, a proposal loudly advertised as paradigm-overturning that is in fact routine novelty within an emerging specialty. In the worked applications, ensure at least one case where the procedure delivers a verdict the panel will not like -- for instance, declining a submission I personally find dazzling, or recommending we fund a heterodox group our dean is known to distrust. Close with 'Where judgment cannot be eliminated' (roughly 150 words) and 'Failure modes' (the ways this procedure will predictably misfire, and what it structurally cannot do, roughly 150 words).
