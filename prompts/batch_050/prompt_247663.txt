I'm in a reading group working through Wittgenstein's On Certainty, and we got into a fight last week about the picture everyone takes away from it. At one point Wittgenstein says the propositions we can't doubt — that I have two hands, that the earth existed long before I was born — function like hinges: the door of questioning and doubting can only turn if the hinges stay put. The hinge epistemology people in my group (they keep name-dropping Moyal-Sharrock) lean hard on this image to argue against the skeptic: doubting everything is impossible because doubt itself needs something exempt from doubt to get leverage, so radical skepticism isn't a position we can genuinely occupy, it's just idle wheel-spinning.

But then later in the very same notes he switches metaphors and talks about a river: some of our convictions are like the hardened rock of the riverbed, but the bank consists of a mixture of hard rock and sand that gets washed away and re-deposited, and the same proposition can get washed from the bed into the flowing water, or hardened from the water into the bed. One person in the group said this quietly demolishes the hinge picture, since the whole point of hinges is that they're fixed hardware, and Wittgenstein is admitting the "hinges" can rotate over time -- a geocentrist's hinge becomes our flowing water. The Moyal-Sharrock fan said the river image changes nothing because at any given time there still has to be a bed.

So: audit the hinge analogy for me. Give me the explicit mapping -- what plays the role of the door, the turning, the frame the hinges are screwed into, the person opening it -- and then where the mapping strains. Doors have hinges installed by someone for a purpose, hinges can be oiled, replaced, or ripped out while the door stays the same size; do any of those features have counterparts in our system of beliefs, or are they just dead weight the metaphor drags in? Does it matter that real hinges are discrete pieces of hardware while the things Wittgenstein counts as hinge-like seem to shade off gradually into ordinary empirical claims? And the big one: does the river image actually contradict the hinge image, or are they doing different jobs (one synchronic, one diachronic)? If hinges can be swapped out across generations -- as the history of science suggests they have been -- does the anti-skeptical conclusion the analogy was built to carry still stand, or does it shrink into the much weaker claim that you must always be standing on something, never mind what? And if the hinge picture is misleading here, what would be a better analogy that keeps its anti-skeptical bite?
