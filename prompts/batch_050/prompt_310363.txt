I help run the statistics journal club at my hospital, and at the last session a colleague presented an argument that has been nagging at me, because its conclusion is absurd yet I cannot cleanly locate the failure. He began with a dataset our group knows well: a new drug versus standard care for a chronic condition, 2,000 patients. Among younger patients, the drug group recovered at 80% (80 of 100) against 70% for standard care (630 of 900); among older patients, 20% (180 of 900) against 10% (10 of 100). So the drug wins in both age strata. But pool the table and standard care wins overall, 64% to 26%, because the drug happened to be given mostly to older, sicker patients. Classic Simpson reversal, nothing mysterious. His argument ran as follows. (1) When an association reverses sign within every level of a third variable, the raw association was tracking that third variable rather than any genuine influence of the treatment. (2) Simpson's paradox shows that, compatibly with any observed table, such a third variable can always exist. (3) And once you find and adjust for it, nothing in the data rules out a further variable that reverses the comparison again, so adjustment can never be complete. (4) Hence an observed association, however strong and however conditioned, is never by itself evidence that a treatment causes recovery; it is evidence only relative to the assumption that no reverser exists, and that assumption is exactly what the data cannot check. (5) Random assignment changes nothing of substance: once the trial is finished, the assignment and the outcomes are just two more columns of observational data, and steps (2) through (4) apply to them exactly as before. (6) Therefore a large randomized trial is, as evidence for efficacy, on a par with a heap of anecdotes — both are associations awaiting a confounder. I refused (6), he called that sentiment rather than statistics, and the room split between people who attacked (1) and people who attacked (5), which made me suspect the argument hides a subtle slide rather than a plainly false premise. Tell me which step you reject, why it looked sound, and what rejecting it costs. If forced, I would rather surrender (1) than (5), but I want to know what I am paying either way.
