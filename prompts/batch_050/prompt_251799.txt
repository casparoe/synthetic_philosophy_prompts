As a retired experimental physicist lately reading philosophy, I have provisionally adopted a position I call the Plutarchan thesis: that sustained, attentive acquaintance with the lives of exemplary people is itself an efficacious instrument of moral improvement — not mere entertainment or civic instruction, but a mechanism by which character is actually formed. My warrant is Plutarch's own account in the opening of his Life of Aemilius Paulus: "the study of history and the familiarity with it which my writing produces, enables me, since I always cherish in my soul the records of the noblest and most estimable characters, to repel and put far from me whatever base, malicious, or ignoble suggestion my enforced associations may intrude upon me, calmly and dispassionately turning my thoughts away from them to the fairest of my examples."

What I want is a map of this position's exposure: what would count against it. Please take the passage seriously as a causal claim and address the following. First, which empirical findings would genuinely threaten it — for instance, situationist results suggesting character does little explanatory work, or evidence that admiration of exemplars produces moral licensing rather than moral effort — and which of these a committed Plutarchan could absorb by distinguishing his mechanism (habituation through habitual attention) from the naive emulation a critic might attack? Second, which possible cases would be decisive: avid readers of noble lives who remain vicious, or the moral improvement of people through practices involving no exemplars at all? Third, what does the thesis predict that its rivals — moral learning through reflection on principles, through practice, through community — do not, and is there any observation that would discriminate between them? Please rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which Plutarch's own text (note his emphasis on writing rather than reading, and on repelling intruded suggestions) would concede as genuine counter-evidence, and close with a candid verdict on whether the thesis, as qualified, still risks anything or has quietly become unfalsifiable — and if the latter, whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind.
