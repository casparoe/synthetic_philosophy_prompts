The question I set:

"Explain the Mīmāṃsā doctrine of svataḥ prāmāṇya (the intrinsic validity of cognitions). How does this doctrine support the Mīmāṃsā claim that the Veda is apauruṣeya, i.e. authorless? Your answer should also say something about how Mīmāṃsā accounts for error."

The student's answer, verbatim:

"For the Mīmāṃsākas, every cognition is intrinsically valid: validity is produced by the very same conditions that produce the cognition itself, so we don't need a second cognition or any external check to confirm the first one. The Naiyāyikas basically agree that validity is intrinsic, but they add that for testimony in particular we need to know the speaker is trustworthy (an āpta) before we can accept what is said. Invalidity, by contrast, is extrinsic for Mīmāṃsā: a cognition only counts as false when something from outside defeats it, like a later cognition that contradicts it.

This also explains their account of error. When I look at a piece of mother-of-pearl and judge 'silver', Prabhākara's analysis is that I've mixed up two separate cognitions -- my perception of the shiny thing in front of me and my memory of silver -- and placed the one into the other (this is anyathākhyāti, error as misplacement). Kumārila, on the other hand, denies there is any positive error at all: since validity is intrinsic, what we call error is just a failure to notice the difference between the two cognitions (akhyāti).

Because validity doesn't depend on a speaker, the Veda doesn't need an author to guarantee it. The Nyāya picture, where testimony is valid because God or a reliable sage composed it, is simply unnecessary: the words of the Veda are eternal, nobody remembers an author, and any alleged author would have had to learn the words from somebody else anyway. So authorlessness isn't a hole in the Veda's credentials -- the real point is that the very idea of the Veda needing a guarantor assumes the Nyāya view that validity comes from outside the cognition. And since validity is intrinsic, everything in the Veda, including its descriptions of past events and cosmology, is just as authoritative as its injunctions about duty."
