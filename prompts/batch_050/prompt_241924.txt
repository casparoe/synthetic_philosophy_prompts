I'm a PhD student who has been asked to run a single ninety-minute session on gauge freedom for second-year physics undergraduates. Their background is electromagnetism done with potentials, some vector calculus, and no Lagrangian mechanics or field theory. The lecturer whose course I'm assisting wants the session organised around one question: which parts of our description of the electromagnetic field belong to nature, and which are bookkeeping we could rewrite without consequence? His motive is that next term the students meet the Aharonov–Bohm effect, and he doesn't want them to experience it as magic — or, worse, as a contradiction, since they'll have spent two years being told the potential is 'just a mathematical convenience'. Don't explain gauge freedom to me; plan how I should explain it.

What I want from you is the architecture of the session. First, the dependency structure: what has to be in place before what, and why. For instance, I suspect the students need to feel the difference between a symmetry that does something (rotating a system) and a transformation that merely redescribes it before they can see why calling gauge invariance a 'symmetry of nature' is misleading — but tell me if I'm wrong about that ordering. Second, the carrying example: my instinct is to open with the electrostatics trick of shifting the potential by a constant, since they already trust it, and build to the Aharonov–Bohm effect as the climax where the bookkeeping starts to bite back. Say whether that's the right vehicle, and if not, propose a better one and explain what it must have and lack. Third, the misconception map: I expect at least three — that 'gauge-dependent' means 'wrong', that the Aharonov–Bohm effect proves the potential was real all along (or, in the brighter students, that it proves only loop integrals of it are), and that gauge invariance must yield a conserved charge the way their other symmetries did. Tell me which of these they'll actually form, in what order, and exactly at which point in the sequence I should head each one off — including where the global/local distinction has to land so that the charge misconception dies before it's born rather than being corrected after.

Finally, give me one question I can put to them at the end whose answer would show the idea has landed rather than been memorised, and tell me honestly what I should leave out at this level — fibre bundles, the hole-argument analogy, whatever you think — with a sentence on how to say 'there's more here' without opening doors I can't walk them through in the remaining time.
