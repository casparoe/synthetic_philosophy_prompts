I run the grants program at a small community foundation in a mid-sized American city. We distribute about two million dollars a year across roughly forty proposals, decided quarterly by a staff panel of generalists -- none of us are economists or philosophers, and our decisions have to be defensible in writing to a board that includes people with strong, conflicting intuitions about fairness. Last year the board formally adopted a distributive rule for our grantmaking, which they described to me as Rawlsian: rather than funding whatever produces the most total good in the city, we are to fund the portfolio that most improves the position of the least advantaged, and we should accept less aggregate benefit when that is what the rule requires. The board is satisfied that it has made a values choice; it has left me to figure out what the rule actually instructs us to do, proposal by proposal.

Please write the decision procedure my panel could actually run on each proposal, with the steps in the order we should take them. The translation problems I most need resolved: Rawls's principle is stated for the basic structure of a whole society, not for a funder choosing among programs, so tell me what the right translation is for our scale and what gets lost in it; tell me how we should operationalize "least advantaged" when Rawls defines it by an index of social primary goods and we have only program applications and census data, including what to do when two candidate groups look worst off along different dimensions (income, health, civic standing) and no index ranks them; tell me how to treat proposals that mostly benefit the already comfortable but claim to raise the floor indirectly, since Rawls permits inequalities that work to the advantage of the worst-off -- what evidence of the trickle-through mechanism would count as sufficient, and what is merely asserted; and tell me how strict the priority of the worst-off should be in practice, i.e., whether a small certain gain for them really beats a large certain gain for everyone else, and what to do when helping the worst-off is impossible in a given cycle but helping the second-worst-off is not. For each step, state what counts as a sufficient answer to proceed, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the question to human judgment because it is a value choice the theory leaves open -- mark those handoff points explicitly, and say which distinctions from the theory have to survive the simplification and which can be safely dropped.

Then run the finished procedure on three proposals we actually received: (a) a job-training and reentry program for adults leaving prison, expensive per participant with mediocre measured placement rates; (b) a beloved subsidized arts venue whose tickets are affordable to most residents and which argues that a vibrant cultural life is part of what the least advantaged are owed; and (c) a mobile dental clinic that would split its days between a homeless encampment and a low-income but stably housed neighborhood, with the option to fund only one of the two sites. Make sure at least one application yields an answer my board will dislike, and show how the procedure defends it. Close with a candid statement of what a procedure like this cannot do -- where a funder using it would still go wrong even while following every step correctly.
