I'm leading a reading group this term (mostly undergrads, a couple of grad students who keep showing up anyway) and we just finished Stevenson's Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde. For our last session on it I want to read the novella through Frege's puzzle about identity statements, but the more I prep the more I suspect the fit is either suspiciously perfect or secretly broken, and I'd like help sorting out which.

The puzzle as I understand it: "the morning star is the morning star" is trivial, but "the morning star is the evening star" was a real astronomical discovery, and since both names refer to the same object the informativeness can't live in the reference itself -- hence sense, modes of presentation, and the rest. Now, the whole plot of the novella seems built in exactly this shape. Utterson spends the book gathering evidence about two names, Jekyll and Hyde, and the climax -- Lanyon's letter, then Jekyll's own statement at the end -- is an identity revelation that lands the way Hesperus = Phosphorus must have landed. But here's where it gets weird: the two "modes of presentation" aren't just two descriptions of the same guy, they're alternating time-slices of one body, with different handwriting, different builds, different moral characters, and one mode doesn't even have access to what the other does with its evenings (or at least claims not to). And Jekyll's narration keeps sliding between "I" and "he" when he talks about Hyde, like he can't decide whether he's reporting an identity or an acquaintance.

So, the questions I'd bring to the group. First, does Frege's machinery actually explain why "Jekyll is Hyde" is informative, or does the case break it -- is this really two-names-one-referent, or something else entirely (one body with two persons, one person with two person-stages, a parthood claim dressed up as identity)? Second, if the identity is strict and numerical, what happens to responsibility: the trampled girl and the Carew murder are Hyde's acts, and if Jekyll just is Hyde the law's problem looks trivial, but the book plainly resists that verdict. Is the moral asymmetry simply outside Frege's remit, or does the novella think the metaphysics and the guilt come apart -- and is it right? Third, for anyone who wants the Kripke wrinkle: if both names rigidly designate the same individual, the identity should be necessary, yet the story makes it feel contingent and fragile, held together by a powder that might run out. Is that a confusion the fiction indulges, or a phenomenon the theories miss? Where does the book push back against the Fregean reading -- I don't want to force it -- and does a fictional case like this add anything to the identity debate that the standard astronomical examples don't? Also feel free to push back on my framing if you think I've got Frege (or the book) wrong.
