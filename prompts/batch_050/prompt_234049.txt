I'm a high school junior, and I've been turning over a question about the right way to respond when someone wrongs you: when should you handle it yourself, face to face; when should you go to someone in authority; and when, if ever, should you appeal to public opinion? I have strong gut reactions to particular cases, but I can't figure out what principle, if any, unifies them. Here are my verdicts.

1. A kid in my grade mocked a friend of mine in front of everyone at lunch, and my friend fired back with something sharper that had the whole table laughing at the first kid. I think that was completely fine — admirable, even — and it would have been pathetic of him to run to a teacher about an insult.

2. Someone kept taking things off my desk, pens at first, then a calculator. After the third time I told the teacher. I don't consider that snitching; dealing with theft is literally the teacher's job.

3. A girl on my soccer team found out a teammate had been telling people she only made varsity because her dad knows the coach. Instead of confronting the teammate, she posted screenshots in the team group chat and demanded a public apology. What the teammate did was worse than what she did, but the public call-out still felt wrong to me, like she owed it to everyone to handle it face to face first.

4. I read that a couple of centuries ago, gentlemen would fight duels over insults to their honor. I think that's barbaric, obviously — but I confess to a grudging respect for the fact that they settled things themselves rather than complaining to anyone.

5. A stranger slammed into me in the hallway and didn't apologize. I let it go, and I think demanding an apology would have been an overreaction on my part.

6. My uncle spent years at a company where a coworker kept presenting his ideas as her own. He never confronted her and never told their boss, and he still complains about it bitterly at family dinners. I think he mishandled the whole thing: he should have spoken to her directly, and if that failed, gone to the boss.

7. A kid I know was being shoved around and threatened by an older student every day after school. He told the principal. That was obviously right; nobody would say he should have "handled it himself."

The cases I keep circling are 2 and 3: in both, someone brought in third parties rather than confronting the wrongdoer, yet I judge one fine and the other not — and the rumor in 3 did more damage than my stolen calculator, so "seriousness of the harm" alone can't be the difference. And 4 bothers me too, because I'm not sure I can coherently condemn dueling while admiring the self-reliance it expressed, especially given my verdict on 1.

So here's my request. Is there a single principle, or a small set of them, that captures most of these verdicts? Please state it precisely and tell me which specific cases force each clause of it. Then tell me what it predicts about a case I haven't listed: someone anonymously posts a humiliating video of me that spreads beyond my school, so there's no one I can confront face to face — does your principle say I should report it, respond publicly, or stay quiet? And finally, be honest about which of my verdicts a clean principle would force me to give up, and whether you think I should give them up.
