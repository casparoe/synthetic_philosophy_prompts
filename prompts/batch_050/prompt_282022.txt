i'm trying to figure out what a theory of emotion is even supposed to accomplish before i pick sides between feeling theories, judgment theories, perceptual theories and the rest. so write me the spec: the desiderata any adequate account of what emotions are has to meet, each with the case or phenomenon that motivates it. here's the material i think the spec has to cover, but push back if some of these arent real requirements:

- recalcitrance: you can fear the house spider while judging it harmless and being embarassed by the fear. a theory has to explain how an emotion can conflict with the subject's own settled judgment without making the subject flat-out irrational, and without just saying the judgment was secretly absent
- fiction: real-seeming grief for anna karenina, sweaty palms at horror films, all directed at things you know dont exist
- the other direction of nonexistence: dread about next week's meeting, hope about the results. the object is a future state of affairs, so what exactly is the emotion "of" in the meantime?
- misattribution and hindsight: you can be snappy all afternoon and only at dinner realize it was the morning email, or conclude you were never angry at your colleague at all, you were just hungry. so first-person authority over your own emotions fails in both directions and a theory should say why
- objectless states: free-floating anxiety, sunday evening dread, diffuse irritability. are these emotions missing an object, or a different kind of thing (moods), and does a theory get to settle that by stipulation?
- fittingness: fear can be apt (the off-leash dog is dangerous) or inapt (the spider is harmless) quite apart from whether it's pleasant or morally fine. a theory has to make sense of emotions being assessable as getting the world right or wrong, not just as nice or nasty
- the body: lump in the throat, flush, gut drop. if the emotion just IS the perception of these, then someone paralyzed from the neck down or on beta blockers should feel less, and as far as i can tell the evidence there is genuinely murky -- a theory should say what it predicts and live with it
- animals and babies: dogs sure seem to have something like joy and fear. whatever emotions turn out to be, the account shouldn't rule animal emotion in or out by definitional fiat
- heterogeneity: grief runs for months, startle for a second, and some researchers argue there's no single brain system underlying even one emotion category. a theory owes a view on whether "emotion" names one natural kind at all

then: which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable and which are one camp's hobbyhorse (is the fittingness requirement a discovery or a judgment-theorist's stipulation? is the body thing a constraint or a nostalgia for james?), which pairs you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, and which single requirement eliminates the most contenders on its own. finally score the main families against the list -- james-style feeling theories, judgment/appraisal theories, perceptual theories like tappolet's or prinz's, and frijda-style action-readiness views -- one line each, and commit to an answer about which survives.
