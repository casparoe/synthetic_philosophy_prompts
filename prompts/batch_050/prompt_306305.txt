bit of an odd request, but bear with me. I teach a first-year metaphysics module and we're finishing the term on the identity of indiscernibles, and I want to close the last seminar by reading out a poem about Max Black's two spheres. For anyone who hasn't met it: Black asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres, a fixed distance apart, hanging in otherwise empty space. Every property of one is a property of the other — same size, same composition, same temperature, same everything — and even the relational stuff washes out, because there's nothing else in the universe for either sphere to stand in different relations to, and the whole set-up is symmetric. Yet, says Black, there are plainly two of them. So sharing every last property can't be what makes something identical to something, contra Leibniz.

Here's the constraint I want the poem to satisfy. It should have two main sections that are word-for-word identical — same words, same order, same line breaks, same punctuation — so the only difference between them is their position on the page. The point is that the reader then does the very thing the indiscernibility principle says is impossible: she tells two qualitatively identical things apart, just by pointing at "the first section" and "the second section". Numerically two, qualitatively one — which is exactly Black's claim about the spheres, enacted rather than stated.

Then I want a short coda, a handful of lines, that breaks the symmetry and gives the obvious reply: that the two sections were never really identical, since one comes before the other on the page — just as the spheres sit in different places, or differ in being *this* one rather than *that* one. The coda shouldn't settle whether that's a fair reply or a cheat that quietly assumes distinctness in order to prove it; thats the argument I want the seminar to have. So the poem itself should stay neutral, with the two identical halves doing the real work.

Roughly 10-14 lines per identical section, plus the coda, so somewhere around 25-35 lines total. Free verse is fine, no need to rhyme. Write it in British English please.
