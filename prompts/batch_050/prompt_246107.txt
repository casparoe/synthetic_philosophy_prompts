For my high school ethics class, we spent a unit on whether a company itself can be morally blameworthy, as opposed to the individual people who work for it. My teacher walked us through five cases and gave her verdict on each, and I kept track of them because I suspect she's applying a consistent rule, though I'm not sure which one. Her verdicts were:

1. A CEO learns that his company's best-selling product has a dangerous defect and personally orders the information concealed from regulators. She said the company is blameworthy, not merely the CEO.

2. A mid-level employee secretly defrauds customers for personal gain, violating the company's explicit written policies and the training he received, and the company reports him to the authorities as soon as it finds out. She said the company is not blameworthy; only the employee is.

3. A company designs a bonus system that heavily rewards managers for cutting costs, and although nobody is ever told to break safety rules, managers across the company predictably start cutting corners, and someone is eventually hurt. She said the company is blameworthy.

4. A delivery company follows every reasonable safety precaution in maintaining its vehicles, but a freak mechanical failure that no inspection could have detected causes an accident. She said the company is not blameworthy.

5. After a pollution scandal, the owners of a company dissolve it and immediately form a new company under a different name, with the same staff, the same factories, and the same business practices. She said the new company is blameworthy for what the old one did.

Here is the case she has assigned us for next week: a pharmaceutical company collects safety data on its drugs through two separate departments that never share information. Each department handled its own data in a way that was reasonable given what that department could see, and no individual employee intended harm, foresaw it, or even could have seen the full picture, because the company's filing system made it practically impossible for anyone to aggregate the two sets of data. Patients were harmed by a side effect that the combined data would have revealed.

Predict her verdict, and explain which principle you think she's been applying across the first five cases. I'd also like to know which other principles fit her five verdicts equally well but deliver the opposite verdict on the new case, and which of her earlier cases is the closest analog to this one. Finally, what additional case would best distinguish between the leading candidate principles? (I have a feeling a case where a committee reaches a decision that every individual member voted against might do some work here, but I'm not sure.)
