In the mid-1920s, after several workers died and many more were poisoned at the plants making tetraethyl lead, the US Surgeon General held a conference on whether leaded gasoline should be allowed on the market. Industry argued there was no evidence that the tiny amounts in exhaust fumes could harm the public, the conference declined to ban it and called for more research instead, and lead stayed in gasoline for another half century. Analyze this episode through the precautionary principle. Do the mapping carefully: what would the strong and weak versions of the principle each have prescribed in 1925 given the actual state of the evidence, who should have carried the burden of proof, and what would 'proof of safety' even have looked like back then? Then run it the other way: defenders of the principle point to leaded gasoline as exhibit A, but does the episode really vindicate it? You could argue the principle only tells you what to do once the harms are already visible (as they were in the plants) and goes quiet in the genuinely uncertain cases where it would matter most. And lastly: would a straight cost-benefit analysis with a reasonable value per life have reached a better decision in 1925, or would it have made the exact same mistake?
