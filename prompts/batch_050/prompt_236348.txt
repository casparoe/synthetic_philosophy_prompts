I work at a small publisher that produces science and nature books for kids aged 7-11, and I keep having the same fight with our authors. They write sentences like "Ducks lay eggs", "Bees make honey", "Sharks attack people", "Wolves live in packs", and our fact-checkers flag them because, strictly, they're false or misleading: male ducks don't lay eggs, almost no mosquitoes carry malaria, most sharks never attack anyone. The authors push back that this is just how English works and that nobody means ALL. My boss wants a consistent policy instead of this argument every single manuscript.

So: I want you to turn what the philosophy of language / semantics actually knows about generic sentences into a procedure our editorial staff can run on any generic sentence in a manuscript, one generic at a time, in a couple of minutes, and output one of: keep as-is, revise (with what revision), or cut. The theory I've half-absorbed says generics don't have a single exception-rate threshold -- "ducks lay eggs" is fine though barely half of ducks do it, while "books are paperbacks" is rejected though most books maybe are, and stuff about dangerous properties gets accepted on way weaker evidence ("mosquitoes carry malaria") than stuff about neutral ones. Whatever the right framework is, I want the parts of it that survive translation into a checklist, and I want you to be explicit about which theoretical distinctions you're dropping because they're unusable for us and which ones absolutely have to survive.

Concrete requirements:

- The procedure has to be runnable by someone with no linguistics background, as a short ordered list of tests or questions with a decision at each step, not an essay about generics.
- It needs to handle at least these cases we actually have in manuscripts right now, with a worked run-through of each: "ducks lay eggs"; "mosquitoes carry malaria"; "lions have manes"; "bats are blind"; "a triangle's angles add up to 180 degrees"; and "sharks eat seals" (true of some species, not others). At least one of these should come out as keep even though the fact-checkers flagged it, and at least one as revise or cut even though it 'sounds fine' -- if your procedure never overturns intuitions on both sides I don't trust it.
- Kids' books add a wrinkle: the sentence has to work for a reader with no background knowledge, so a generic that's technically fine but invites the wrong generalisation might need revising anyway. Say how the procedure handles that, or if it can't, say where it hands off to a human judgement call.
- Also tell me the cases where the procedure will misfire or can't decide -- I suspect sentences about people ("immigrants work hard", "girls are good at reading") break it, because whatever makes those okay or not okay isn't just about how many exceptions there are. If generics about social groups need a totally different rule, say so and give me that rule as a separate mini-procedure rather than pretending one checklist covers everything.

Answer in British English please.
