A colleague and I have been going in circles over an argument she built to attack the thesis that the world is becoming less violent. She thinks the standard way of defending that thesis collapses into something obscene, and I can't locate the mistake. Here is the argument. (1) How violent a society is, is a matter of how likely a typical member is to suffer violence — that is, rates per capita rather than absolute numbers. (2) So comparing eras for violence means comparing rates of violent death, not totals. (3) On that measure, a twentieth-century war killing tens of millions in a world of two-plus billion is a comparable or lesser outbreak of violence than a medieval war killing a tenth as many in a world of a few hundred million. (4) Any single atrocity's contribution to how violent its era was is likewise its share of the population then alive. (5) World population keeps growing. (6) Therefore, holding the number of victims fixed, each successive atrocity contributes less and less to how violent the world is: killing a million people in 2100 is, as violence, a smaller event than killing a million in 1800, and a smaller event still than massacring ten thousand in prehistory, and in the limit identical acts of mass killing shrink toward insignificance as humanity grows. (7) That is obscene, so the per-capita measure in (1) and (2) must be rejected — and with it goes the main evidence that violence has declined. I want to defend the declining-violence thesis, and I'd also rather keep (1) than accept (6), but I can't have everything. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: first, the step or premise you reject; second, why that step looked fine; third, what rejecting it costs; fourth, the next-cheapest alternative repair and its price; fifth, whether the declining-violence thesis survives your diagnosis, and in what sense of "less violent."
