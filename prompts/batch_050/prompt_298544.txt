I'm a first-year PhD student in philosophy (mostly history of ethics, with a side interest in Victorian science-and-religion stuff), and I'm drafting a short essay for a graduate seminar on Sidgwick. It tries to connect his epistemology of psychical research to the famous gloomy ending of the Methods of Ethics. A friend in the history dept told me it "sounds like a blog post," which stung, so I'd really like blunt comments — especially on accuracy, since I keep worrying I've built the whole thing on over-reading one sentence. Here's the draft as it stands:

---

The Marvel That Swallows Itself: Sidgwick on Evidence Beyond Science

Everyone knows the broad strokes: Sidgwick was the first president of the Society for Psychical Research, he spent years investigating mediums and thought-transference, and he never got results he considered decisive. What's less discussed is how he framed the enterprise. In his 1889 presidential address, "The Canons of Evidence in Psychical Research," he writes:

"I mean by 'the kind of evidence' evidence for marvels; evidence tending to prove the intrusion—if I may so call it—into the world of ordinary experience, material or mental, either of causes that find no place at all in science—i.e., in our systematised knowledge of the world of experience—or of unknown modes of operation, of known causes."

I want to argue that this definition creates a problem Sidgwick never faces squarely. Notice that a marvel, on his account, is not a kind of thing in the world; it is a relation between an event and our current stock of systematised knowledge. Telepathy is a marvel only because no accepted science accomodates it. But that makes psychical research structurally self-defeating: if the SPR ever succeeded — if thought-transference were established, measured, explained — it would by definition cease to be a marvel and become ordinary science. The society's total success condition is its own dissolution. Complete failure, meanwhile, is indistinguishable from a need for more evidence, so the inquiry can never rationally terminate either. Psychical research is committed, by its founding definition, to remaining permanantly "obscure and tentative" — Sidgwick's own words later in the same address.

My second claim is that this is no accident but a symptom. The Methods of Ethics ends in the famous dualism of practical reason: self-interest and universal benevolence are both rational, and they can only be reconciled by a postulate about the constitution of the universe (roughly, that virtue and happiness ultimately coincide) which ethics itself cannot establish. Commentators often treat the psychical research as a quirky hobby, but I think it was the only empirical route left open: if there were real evidence of, say, post-mortem survival, the postulate morality needs might become an empirical matter rather than a theological one. The "marvel" definition, which guarantees the inquiry can never conclude, then looks less like cautious methodology and more like a ratchet that protects a hope Sidgwick needed to keep alive but could not afford to test to destruction. The pessimism of the Methods and the open-endedness of the SPR are, I claim, the same epistemic predicament described from two sides.

(That's where the draft stops; I have a half-written conclusion arguing that Hume faces the same structure in the essay on miracles but bites the opposite bullet.)
