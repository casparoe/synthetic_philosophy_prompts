Two graduate students in my department have been arguing for weeks about whether the Capgras delusion -- the conviction that a spouse or close relative has been replaced by an impostor -- counts as a belief, and their last exchange ended in a standstill. I'd like you to adjudicate it. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Marta opened with the behavioral argument: beliefs are supposed to guide action, and Capgras patients mostly do not act on the impostor claim. A man who genuinely believed his wife had been replaced by an identical stranger would call the police, demand an investigation, search for the real wife. Instead he eats dinner with her. Clinicians have long noted this disconnect. Whatever is in his head, Marta said, it does not play the functional role of a belief, so it is not one -- call it an imaginatively adopted hypothesis or a fixed interpretation of an anomalous experience, but not belief.

(2) Leo replied that the behavioral test overgenerates. Plenty of things we unhesitatingly call beliefs fail to guide action: the religious believer who professes an afterlife but grieves and takes every medical precaution exactly as the unbeliever does; the person who sincerely asserts that the economy is rigged yet behaves like everyone else. And some Capgras patients do act -- they avoid the supposed impostor, barricade doors, occasionally become violent. If we withhold the label 'belief' from the quiet patient, consistency demands we withhold it from half of ordinary conviction.

(3) Marta then brought in the two-factor theory. The first factor -- an anomalous experience, plausibly a loss of the normal affective or familiarity response to the spouse's face -- explains why the impostor idea arises at all. The second factor is a failure to reject that hypothesis on grounds of implausibility, a deficit in belief evaluation. Her point: a belief is, in part, the output of a working evaluation system. When the impostor claim is maintained precisely because the evaluator is damaged, calling the output a belief is like calling the output of a broken calculator an answer. The second factor does not just explain the delusion; it disqualifies it.

(4) Leo's response: this builds rationality into the definition of belief, which begs the question against every account on which belief is just a stored representation deployed in inference, however irrationally acquired or maintained. Worse, it makes belief-hood hinge on a competence that comes in degrees. The conspiracy theorist maintains an absurd view through motivated but intact reasoning; the Capgras patient maintains one through damaged reasoning. If only the latter fails to believe, then 'belief' tracks degrees of rationality rather than any psychological natural kind -- and ordinary attribution is far more generous than that.

(5) Marta's final move was to suggest the question itself is malformed: whether the impostor claim is a belief depends on which functional role you care about -- predicting behavior, assigning moral and legal responsibility, explaining the patient's utterances -- and there need be no single answer across these. Leo said this was dissolving the question rather than answering it, and that a theory of delusion should say what kind of state it posits. Neither moved.
