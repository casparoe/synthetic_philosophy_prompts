I am a PhD student in fluid mechanics, and I have noticed a pattern in how secure certain theories are that I cannot find discussed anywhere. The equations I work with were written down when matter was conceived as a continuous medium; matter turned out to be made of atoms; atoms turned out to be quantum-mechanical; and through each of these upheavals the continuum equations did not budge, because at large length scales almost everything about the small-scale details washes out. From a statistical mechanics course I took, I gather the same thing happens elsewhere: a magnet near its transition and a liquid near its critical point are described by the same exponents despite sharing almost no microscopic structure. What strikes me is that in these cases the higher-level description seems to gain security when the lower-level picture is overthrown, rather than losing it. Colleagues in philosophy gesture at multiple realizability or emergence, but neither seems right: this is not merely that one pattern happens to be implemented by many substrates, but that whole classes of substrates are driven, for principled reasons, toward the same large-scale behavior. Does this phenomenon have an established name and literature, and if not, what should it be called? I am not after a bare label: I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is, how it differs from multiple realizability, supervenience, and ordinary robustness of a result under changes of assumptions, and where its edges are -- are there cases where a lower-level revolution does propagate upward and destroy the higher-level theory, and what distinguishes them? Please give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
