At a workshop on land-use policy, I listened to two researchers argue about a concrete proposal: converting a few hundred hectares of seasonally flooded forest into a solar farm. One of them works on wild animal welfare, the other on conservation biology. Strikingly, they agree on the empirical background: both accept that suffering is pervasive in the wild, that most wild animals die young from predation, starvation, disease, or exposure, and both are at least sympathetic to the claim that the average wild life in an ecosystem like this one contains more suffering than positive experience. The disagreement ran as follows, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) The welfare researcher argued that the conclusion follows directly from premises they share. If the habitat supports a large population of animals whose lives are, on average, net negative, then replacing it with a land use that supports far less wildlife reduces the total amount of suffering in the world. Consistency therefore requires that anyone motivated by animal welfare should welcome, or at least stop opposing, conversions of high-biomass habitat to low-biomass uses. Opposing the solar farm on animal-welfare grounds while accepting the shared premises is, he said, simply incoherent.

(2) The conservation biologist replied that the argument skips the transition. Clearing the forest does not make the current inhabitants cease to exist painlessly; it kills many outright and displaces the rest into already-occupied territories, where they die of starvation, exposure, or territorial conflict. He is comparing animals existing with animals not existing, while leaving out the middle column: animals dying badly in the process. Any honest ledger has to include those deaths, and they are among the worst wild deaths there are.

(3) The welfare researcher answered that this is a one-time, finite cost weighed against an ongoing stream of avoided suffering that recurs with every generation the habitat would have supported. Over a long enough time horizon, the avoided suffering dominates the transition cost no matter how bad the transition is. And if her objection were sound in general, it would paralyze every long-term intervention, since any change to anything hurts someone in the short run.

(4) The conservation biologist then raised two further problems. First, habitat removal rarely works as advertised: cleared land regrows scrub, edges proliferate, displaced animals crowd into what remains, and the community that re-establishes itself may be dominated by fast-breeding species with enormous broods and near-total juvenile mortality, so the long-run suffering could be as high or higher than before. Second, and more fundamentally, the inference from these lives are net negative to it would be better if they never existed carries commitments he will not accept: the same reasoning endorses any intervention that prevents future wild lives from beginning, up to and including deliberately sterilizing wild populations across entire ecosystems.

(5) The welfare researcher conceded the empirical uncertainty in her first point but said her second point proves too much in the other direction: she accepts the premise that these lives are net negative yet refuses every practical conclusion that follows from it, which suggests her real objection is not about welfare at all. She values wildness, species, or intact ecosystems for their own sake. That is a position she is entitled to hold and defend, but she should defend it openly rather than dressing it up as a dispute about animal suffering, where her own premises defeat her.

Neither of them convinced the other, and I genuinely cannot tell where the exchange went wrong, if it did. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it is clearly right, defensible but contestable, or clearly mistaken, with a brief justification for each rating. Then tell me what, if anything, both parties are missing -- in particular, whether there is a version of the welfare argument that survives the conservation biologist's objections, and whether there is a version of her position that does not depend on premises his final move has undercut. Please write your answer in American English.
