I am a consulting forensic psychologist retained by a public defender's office in a jurisdiction whose criminal-responsibility standard turns partly on whether the defendant, at the time of the offense, knew that what they were doing was wrong. About fifteen times a year I evaluate defendants who score high on clinical measures of psychopathic traits, and these cases generate the same problem every time. Most of them pass every verbal test I can administer: they define right and wrong conventionally, use moral vocabulary fluently, correctly predict what others will condemn, and can recite the relevant rules. Yet many behave as though these judgments have no grip on them, and a subset, in structured interviews, fail to treat moral and conventional rules differently -- they say it would be equally acceptable to steal or to wear the wrong clothes if the person in charge announced a rule change. Courts in my jurisdiction have no settled view of which of these findings answers the statutory question, and opposing experts routinely accuse each other of doing armchair philosophy instead of assessment.

I want a reusable written protocol I can apply across these evaluations: an ordered sequence of probes, with explicit criteria for what each result establishes, that translates the serious philosophical positions on what moral knowledge requires -- the cognitivist picture on which it is a matter of true belief and correct classification, the affective or sentimentalist picture on which it requires some motivating responsiveness to the reasons that make an act wrong, and capacity-versus-performance distinctions within each -- into things I can actually elicit in a four-hour interview plus a records review, with no graduate philosophy on the treatment team and a supervising psychiatrist who must countersign each report.

The protocol should include at least four discriminating probes, stated concretely enough that another clinician could administer them: for example, counterfactual questions about whether the defendant would have acted the same with detection certain or with an authority figure present; a test of whether the defendant treats moral rules as independent of any authority's say-so; a comparison of stated moral judgments against the defendant's behavior in low-stakes situations where restraint cost them nothing; and a check for whether apparently moral language is being used to predict others' reactions rather than to guide the defendant's own choices. For each probe, say what it can and cannot establish and which philosophical conception of moral knowledge it presupposes. Mark clearly the points where the protocol runs out and the report has to record a judgment call rather than a finding, and state plainly what the protocol cannot deliver -- in particular, whether it can ever support an affirmative finding rather than merely ruling things in or out.

Then run the protocol on three vignettes and report what it outputs for each: a contract killer with flawless moral fluency who describes each killing as "wrong, obviously" and did it anyway; a young man with severe callous-unemotional traits who killed in an impulsive bar fight and shows distress only about his own imprisonment; and a defendant with psychopathic traits who desisted from one planned offense, apparently because the intended victim reminded him of his sister. Include at least one case where the protocol's output is the one my office will not want to file, and say how the report should handle that honestly.

Conclude with a candid assessment of the protocol's own vulnerability: which empirical result, if it were established, would force a redesign; which philosophical argument would most plausibly show that the protocol is measuring the wrong thing; and where, on cross-examination, its reasoning is most likely to come apart.
