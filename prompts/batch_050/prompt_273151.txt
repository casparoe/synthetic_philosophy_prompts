A friend and I keep circling the same question: if my true belief gets me exactly where knowledge would have gotten me, what did knowledge add? He says nothing — being right is being right. I suspect he is wrong, but every case I come up with either gives the knower a better outcome (which concedes his point) or smuggles in a difference that isn't knowledge itself — more confidence, better evidence, a more careful method.

So I want a case built to order. Construct a thought experiment with two characters who hold the same true belief about the same matter, reach it by the same method on the same evidence with the same confidence, act identically, and receive identical outcomes — yet where it still seems that the one whose belief amounts to knowledge is better off in some way that matters. Constraints: a realistic, present-day setting involving ordinary people; no standard philosophy examples (no fake barns, no stopped clocks); and the sole difference between the characters must be whatever makes one a knower and the other merely right — presumably some kind of luck in the wider environment — and you must state explicitly where you located that difference. The purpose is to test my hunch that knowledge's extra value is a kind of stability: the knower would still have been right had nearby circumstances differed, while the mere believer was simply fortunate. A case that just asserts the intuition rather than generating it counts as cheating.

Structure your answer in five sections, in this order: The scenario (one paragraph); Spec check, walking through each constraint (~100 words); What the case shows, including whose position it supports and which feature does the work (~150 words); The leveling objection, giving my friend's reply that identical outcomes mean nothing of value can differ, plus my best response (~150 words); Pressure tests, covering the smallest change that destroys the intuition and whether the verdict survives if the case is repeated over a lifetime of similar situations (~150 words).
