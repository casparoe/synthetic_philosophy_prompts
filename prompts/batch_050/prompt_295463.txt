I have no academic background, but I have been trying to think through on my own whether famines should be classed as natural disasters or as political crimes, and I have noticed that both sides of that argument lean heavily on analogies rather than on the particulars of any actual famine. One side says a famine is like a drought or an earthquake: a misfortune that descends on a society, against which the only sensible responses are foresight, food reserves, and charity after the fact. The other side says a famine is like a siege: starving people are being kept from food that exists, by identifiable actors, and the right responses are to name those actors, stop them, and punish them. I have seen the first analogy used to argue that assigning blame for famine is usually confused moralism, and the second used to argue that famines should be prosecuted as crimes even where nobody intended the deaths.

I would like an audit of how far each analogy actually holds. Specifically:

1. Give me the correspondence table for each. In the siege analogy, what plays the role of the besieging army, the walls, the smuggling routes that keep a city alive, and the relief column? In the weather analogy, what plays the role of the storm, the forecast, and the levees that were never built?

2. Identify what each analogy quietly imports that a real famine may lack. The siege analogy seems to import an intending villain and a clean line between those inside the walls and those outside; the weather analogy seems to import unavoidability and the passivity of everyone involved, victims included. Test each import against three cases: the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s (during which food continued to be exported from Ireland), and the Great Leap Forward famine in China around 1959 to 1961. For each case, say which analogy fits better and what each analogy hides about that case.

3. Consider the possibility, which I have seen attributed to Amartya Sen, that people can starve while food sits in nearby shops because they can no longer afford it — death by price rather than by siege-line or by failed harvest. Does either analogy have a slot for that mechanism, and if not, what does the omission do to the conclusions each analogy was built to support?

4. Finally, say whether either analogy can still carry the conclusion it was used for — blamelessness on one side, prosecution on the other — once the disanalogies are laid out. If neither can, propose a better comparison. I have wondered whether famine is more like a bridge collapse after years of ignored inspection reports: culpable, but with no besieger. Audit that candidate with the same rigor you applied to the other two.
