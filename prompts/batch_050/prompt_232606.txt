Construct a thought experiment that cleanly separates three positions routinely run together in discussions of contradiction: classical logic, paraconsistent logic without dialetheism, and dialetheism proper.

Specifications for the scenario. First, it must be realistic — set it in a courtroom, a regulatory agency, a university appeals committee, a tax authority, or a piece of software enforcing a rulebook — not in a logic seminar, and with no contrived devices (no omniscient beings, no sentences that announce their own falsity out of thin air). Second, the contradiction at the center of the case must arise from the ordinary machinery of the scenario — conflicting clauses in a statute enacted by the same legislature, a will with two inconsistent provisions, a rule that turns out to govern its own application, inconsistent findings a body is procedurally required to endorse — rather than being stipulated. Third, the three positions must issue three distinguishable verdicts about what a competent reasoner in the scenario should accept, not merely three descriptions of the same behavior; in particular, the non-dialetheist paraconsistentist and the dialetheist must part ways on what is true, not only on what may be asserted, recorded, or tolerated pending revision. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph.

After presenting the scenario, do the following. (a) State what each of the three positions says the reasoner should accept and why, making explicit how explosion (ex falso sequitur quodlibet) enters the classical logician's reasoning and precisely which inference the paraconsistentist blocks to avoid it. (b) Identify the single structural feature of the case that makes the dialetheist's verdict diverge from the non-dialetheist paraconsistentist's, and say whether removing that feature collapses the case into a routine inconsistency-management problem on which those two parties agree. (c) Explain how a defender of whichever position fares worst would attack the case — for instance, by arguing that the contradiction is merely apparent (a scope ambiguity or a shift of context between the conflicting clauses), or that it is deontic rather than alethic, so that nothing in the world is both ways at once but only the rulebook is defective.

Conclude by addressing the common slide from we need a paraconsistent logic to reason sensibly amid inconsistency to therefore some contradictions are true: does your scenario vindicate that inference, expose it as a non sequitur, or show that it depends on a premise the scenario itself leaves open? Say which, and why.
