So I've been reading around in philosophy of biology and the functions debate seems kind of stuck. The etiological people (selected effects, going back to Wright in the 70s) say a trait's function is whatever it did historically that explains why it got selected -- so the heart's function is pumping blood because that's what hearts did that got them kept around, even if some particular heart never pumps anything. The causal-role people (Cummins and followers) say function is just about what a part actually contributes to the capacities of the system it's in, right now, no history needed. As far as I can tell everyone knows all the standard cases -- vestigial organs, accidental benefits, the first ever token of a trait, swamps and mutants -- and both sides just keep re-describing the same cases in their own vocabulary and calling the other side's verdicts counterintuitive.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't one disagreement at all: the etiological camp is really answering "why is this trait here?" and the causal-role camp is answering "how does this system work?", and the word "function" got asked to do both jobs and now the camps are talking past each other. But maybe I'm being too quick and there IS a substantive disagreement about what the concept of function is for in biology -- like what explanatory or normative work (malfunction, dysfunction, disease) genuinely requires history, and what doesn't.

So: diagnose the impasse rather than pick a winner. Is it verbal, substantive, two-questions-under-one-label, or a disagreement about which cases count as data vs which starting intuitions are non-negotiable? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress -- a distinction between two function-concepts, a principled argument that biology needs one unified notion, some payoff in how we classify disease or traits, or an argument that no resolution should be expected?
