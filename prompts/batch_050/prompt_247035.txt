Exam question (20 points):

In the philosophy of legal proof, "naked statistical evidence" is evidence that bears on a defendant's liability only through his membership in a class. Consider two standard examples. (1) The gatecrasher: 1,000 people attend a rodeo, but the promoter has sold only 499 tickets and has no record of who paid. One attendee, selected at random, is sued for the price of admission; the only evidence against him is the attendance figure and the ticket count, which together make it 0.501 probable that he did not pay. (2) The blue bus: the plaintiff was negligently forced off the road by a bus she could not identify; the Blue Bus Company operates 80 percent of the buses on that stretch of road, and that figure is the only evidence linking the company to the accident. In both cases the probability of liability exceeds the civil standard of a preponderance of the evidence. (a) Explain why courts and commentators balk at liability in such cases even though that standard is met, and why the reluctance cannot be dissolved simply by demanding a higher probability. (b) Set out one diagnosis of what is supposed to be wrong with naked statistical evidence, and assess whether it succeeds.

Student's answer:

Both cases generate what appears to be the same puzzle. In the gatecrasher, the probability that this defendant failed to pay is 0.501, which satisfies the preponderance standard, since that standard requires only that the plaintiff's claim be more likely true than not. In the blue bus case, the probability that a company bus caused the accident is 0.8. Yet most people judge that neither defendant should be held liable on this evidence alone.

The reluctance cannot be explained by saying the probabilities are too low. If 900 of the 1,000 attendees had gatecrashed, so that the defendant's probability of liability were 0.9, we would still balk at a judgment against an attendee selected at random, and the same holds at 0.99. The objection is therefore not to the strength of the evidence but to its kind. The standard contrast is with eyewitness testimony: had a single witness testified that she saw this defendant climb the fence, a court could find liability on that testimony even if the witness's measured reliability were no better than the attendance figures. What distinguishes the two cases is that the testimony concerns this defendant in particular, whereas the statistic concerns a class to which he merely happens to belong.

The most influential diagnosis of the puzzle holds that statistical evidence is simply less reliable than individualized evidence, and that courts rightly discount it on that ground. Attendance counts and market-share figures are estimates, whereas a witness speaks to what she herself observed. This diagnosis seems to me essentially correct, and it yields a workable rule: statistical evidence should ground liability only when its reliability exceeds that of the eyewitness testimony courts routinely accept. Since the attendance figures in the gatecrasher case are stipulated to be accurate, the rule implies that liability is appropriate there after all, and that the contrary intuition is just judicial discomfort with statistics — a form of base-rate neglect that better training in probability would eliminate.

If one remains unpersuaded, the intuition that liability demands more than 0.501 has a natural explanation. A judgment of liability is a serious imposition, and a probability just over one-half is nothing like the near-certainty we demand before imposing it; our practice rightly reserves adverse judgment until the probability is overwhelming. The blue bus case is admittedly harder for this explanation, since 0.8 begins to approach such a threshold, but even there the evidence leaves a one-in-five chance that the company is innocent, and a court should not impose liability on odds like those.
