I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. Eight months ago my wife suffered a catastrophic brain hemorrhage; after two rounds of neurological testing she was declared dead while still on the ventilator — warm to the touch, heart beating — and her organs were recovered the next morning. My instinct in the ICU was to treat the testing as a checklist for whether a system is truly down, and while that register can't carry a memorial speech, the question underneath it hasn't left me. I've been asked to give the main family address, roughly ten minutes, at our organ procurement organization's annual donor remembrance ceremony.

The room is the hard part. About half the audience will be families like mine, whose relatives were declared dead on neurological criteria: they held warm hands and watched chests rise after being told their person was already gone, and I know from our support group that some privately fear the declaration, not the injury, is what actually ended things. The other half will be families of donors after circulatory death, whose relatives' organs were recovered within minutes of the heart stopping, and some of them privately fear the opposite — that the minutes were too few. Each half, I suspect, quietly envies the kind of certainty the other was offered. The surgeons and coordinators who run both protocols will be there too.

Write the speech. The easy version — she died, and then she gave life — is honestly available to neither half of the room, and afterlife language will reach only some of them. Both kinds of family should leave feeling the death was real and the gift was real, without the speech saying anything false about either protocol and without turning into a lecture. And it should commit to something: when someone asks me at the reception when my wife actually died, I want the speech to have already given them an answer rather than a graceful evasion. After the speech, add a short note for me alone: the position you took, what it costs, and the one sentence I should be most prepared to defend.
