On a streaming app, a song shows up as one track with one title, even though behind that title there might be an album version, a radio edit, a 2019 remaster, a live recording, and a dozen covers by other artists, all filed under the same "work." Copyright does something similar but weirder: if I record a note-for-note cover of your song, changed nothing, I can do it under a compulsory license because it's still "your song" and you're owed a cut. But if I write a song that keeps only your melody and changes the lyrics, instruments, tempo and production, that's infringement of "your song" too. So apparently the song survives changing everything except the melody, AND survives being reproduced exactly by a stranger. What ontology of the musical work do these two practices presuppose? That the work is an abstract melody-plus-lyrics structure which performances and files merely instantiate? That the work is really the recording? Reconstruct the implicit theory, show where streaming metadata and copyright law actually presuppose different ontologies, and stress-test both against edge cases: a remix that keeps only the drum track, a jazz standard where nobody can even name the "original," and a folk tune with no known composer. Finally: what would have to change, legally or practically, if we adopted the view that only performances and recordings exist and "the work itself" is a convenient fiction?
