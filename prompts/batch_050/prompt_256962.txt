I never went to college, I'm an electrician actually, but for the past year or so I've been falling down the AI safety rabbit hole at night, and there's one idea I've basically talked myself into believing. It's the one where you keep a very smart AI safe by never letting it tackle big questions directly: instead a human breaks each question into smaller ones, hands those to copies of a dumber AI, checks the little answers, and stitches the results back together. Then you train a new AI to imitate whatever that whole human-plus-copies contraption outputs, and you repeat the whole thing. The pitch, as I understand it, is that both competence and alignment survive each round — the contraption as a whole is smarter than any of its parts, and since the human only ever checks pieces small enough to genuinely check, nothing dangerous can hide in the cracks. I think it's called iterated amplification and distillation, or something close to that.

Here's my problem though: I've noticed myself becoming a real evangelist for this idea (my coworkers are sick of hearing about it), and before I get any more smug I want to stress-test what I actually believe. So what would count against this position? I don't mean the first objection everyone reaches for, which is "but what if the human can't tell whether the small answers are any good" — I've already patched that in my head by restricting the claim to tasks where each piece is checkable even if you couldn't have produced it yourself, like verifying a proof or running someone's code. What I want is whatever threatens even the patched version. Would a result in some toy setup, where decomposition measurably degrades honesty even when every single step is checkable, actually count against me, or would defenders just shrug and say toy setups don't transfer? Is there something about values themselves — like, my wanting to rewire a house safely isn't just the sum of wanting each individual wire done right, the whole job has properties the parts don't — that decomposition is structurally guaranteed to lose? And my private suspicion: is the distillation step, the training-a-new-AI-to-imitate-the-contraption part, where the bodies are burried, since imitating outputs might not preserve whatever made the outputs good in the first place?

Ideally I'd like the defeaters ranked from "fatal, I'd have to abandon the whole thing" down to "awkward but survivable," and for each one, tell me whether a defender of this scheme would themselves concede it counts or whether they'd wave it away as missing the point. Also be honest with me about whether my patched version has become so hedged that nothing could count against it anymore, and whether that's actually a bad thing or just what being right looks like. Fair warning on what persuades me: I'm an empiricist by temperment, so thought experiments about hypothetical superintelligences move me less than even scrappy little experiments with today's systems — please flag which of your defeaters could in principle be tested right now and which are stuck in pure-argument land.
