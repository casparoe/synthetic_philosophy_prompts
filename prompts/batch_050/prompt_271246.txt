Last night I had a long, unresolved argument with a friend about the epistemic regress problem, and I want an independent audit of the three analogies we kept trading, because I suspect the pictures were doing more work than our actual arguments.

My friend defended foundationalism with the edifice metaphor: a body of justified beliefs is like a building, which must rest on foundations that are not themselves propped up by the storeys above, so that removing or doubting the foundations brings the whole structure down. She used this to argue that justification must terminate in basic beliefs that are justified without deriving support from any further beliefs.

I countered with the ship metaphor associated with Otto Neurath: our beliefs form a vessel we must repair plank by plank while staying afloat on it, so no plank is in principle unrevisable, and justification consists in the mutual support of the whole rather than in resting on a privileged base. I used this to argue that coherentism does not lose the regress so much as dissolve it.

She replied that both coherentism and infinitism amount to stacking turtles: an endless or circular structure of justification is like the apocryphal cosmology in which the earth rests on a turtle, which rests on another turtle, and so on all the way down — a picture she regards as self-evidently absurd, and hence as a reductio of any view that refuses to ground the structure.

Please audit all three analogies in turn. For each one: give an explicit table of correspondences (what plays the role of the ground, the load-bearing walls, the planks, the sea, the turtles, and so on); identify the features of the source domain that are quietly imported into the epistemic target without argument — for instance, whether the building metaphor presupposes that epistemic support must be asymmetric and one-directional, whether the ship metaphor assumes a stable external medium and the practical impossibility of wholesale replacement, and whether the turtle picture illicitly transfers the physical absurdity of unsupported weight to the normative relation of one belief justifying another; and separate the disanalogies that genuinely bear on the conclusions each of us drew from the ones that do not. Where an analogy misleads, say what a less misleading one would look like, if any exists. Finally, tell me whether any of the three analogies can actually arbitrate between foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism, or whether they merely restate the positions in pictorial form — and if the latter, what considerations the dispute should properly turn on.
