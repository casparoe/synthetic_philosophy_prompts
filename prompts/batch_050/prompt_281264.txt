For my seminar on idealized-preference theories (in ethics and in AI alignment), I need a case built to order. Construct a thought experiment in which two accounts that both claim to tell us what a person really wants come apart: (a) a full-information account, on which the person's idealized preference is what they would want if they possessed all relevant non-evaluative information and vividly appreciated it, and (b) an extrapolated-volition account, on which what matters is what a more knowledgeable, more coherent, more self-aware version of the person, grown together with others under favorable conditions, would converge on wanting. The two must deliver different verdicts about what to do for one specific person, here and now. Constraints: no science fiction (the extrapolation may only be sketched in ordinary psychological terms); the person must not be grossly irrational, since the aim is to separate the theories rather than to show that information corrects mistakes; and the divergence must not be stipulated via an extrapolation that obviously goes haywire -- it should fall out of a structural difference in what the two accounts idealize. After the scenario, give each theory's verdict and reasoning, identify which feature of the case drives the wedge, and assess whether the divergence survives if the full-information advisor is also allowed to simulate the person's deliberative trajectory -- and if it does not, whether the two accounts were ever genuinely distinct. Close with the strongest reply available to whichever theory comes out worse. Finally, state how confident you are that the case really separates the two theories, and single out the one point in your construction you are least sure of.
