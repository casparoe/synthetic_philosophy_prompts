I run a little discussion group that meets at my local library every other week — none of us are academics, just people who like arguing about weird stuff. Usually 8 to 12 folks show up, sessions run about 75 minutes. A while back a friend told me about this idea from a philosopher called Black (I think that's the name?): imagine a universe containing nothing but two perfectly identical metal spheres, same size, same stuff, floating some distance apart, and literally nothing else. The question is whether "there are two spheres" even makes sense, or whether it's really just one sphere twice somehow, since nothing could ever tell them apart. It kept me up at night, honestly.

I want to build an activity around this instead of just talking it through, because when we just talk, the same two people dominate and everyone else checks out. So: design me a game we can actually play with cheap stuff I can get from the supply closet or a dollar store — coins, cups, playing cards, stickers, whatever. Some things I'm hoping for:

- The players should get pushed into the problem, not told about it. At the start everyone should feel totally confident that two identical things are obviously two things (like, duh), and the game itself should erode that piece by piece before I ever mention any sphere.
- I want real stakes, even if it's just a chocolate bar or points on the whiteboard — there should be a moment where someone has to actually bet on being able to tell two objects apart, or on which one is "the original," and then has to sit with what it means if nobody could ever win that bet.
- Write the rules clearly enough that I can basically read them off an index card, give me a rough timeline for the 75 minutes, and include a harder variant in case the group cracks the main game too fast.
- Then a debrief plan: what questions to ask, and how to connect specific moments in the game (like the first time someone looses track of which token was which) to the actual philosophical problem, since I need help building that bridge without turning it into a lecture. Nobody should hear the philosopher's name until afterwards.
- Tell me what's likely to go wrong. I suspect someone will try to cheat by secretly scratching a coin or memorizing tiny imperfections — is that a disaster or is that actually the interesting part? I genuinely don't know.
- And be honest about the one thing the game can't reproduce: the spheres are supposed to be the ONLY things in their universe, while my players will obviously be standing outside the game looking down at it with a whole room of reference points around them. If that asymmetry undermines the whole thing, say so — or tell me how to deal with it in the debrief.

What I want people to walk away seeing: that their gut certainty that "two is just two" quietly depends on stuff like location, history, and having watched them the whole time — and once you take all that away, they're stuck either insisting on something they can't back up or swallowing something that feels wrong. If the game can make that feeling happen in the room rather than me just saying it, I'll consider it a win.
