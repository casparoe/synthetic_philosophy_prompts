Next month I'm giving the toast at the retirement dinner of my old philosophy professor, who spent his entire career defending the view — I believe the technical term is dialetheism — that some contradictions are genuinely true, and that the right response is not to hunt them down and eliminate them but to work in a logic where a contradiction doesn't license inferring absolutely everything. Roughly half the room will be logicians who have spent decades arguing with him about exactly this; the other half will be his family, including his sister, a retired judge who finds the whole idea faintly scandalous and once told me at a Christmas party that in her courtroom a contradiction was the end of a case, not the beginning of a career. So I need the speech to land for people who have published on paraconsistent logic and for people who think it's an elaborate practical joke, ideally in the same five minutes.

The toast has also acquired some unexpected personal weight for me lately. I left academia years ago and now work on legacy regulatory software, where the rulebooks we encode are genuinely, incurably inconsistent — different statutes, written decades apart, flatly contradict each other on edge cases — and the standard engineering instinct is to purge every inconsistency before the system can be trusted with anything. It finally clicked for me this year that this was his point all along: you can reason carefully and usefully inside an inconsistent system without being committed to concluding everything. I'd like the toast to weave this in somehow, so the family understands what he was actually fighting about for forty years and the logicians hear it as a genuine concession from a former skeptic rather than a summary.

Please write the speech. It should contain one good joke built on a contradiction, but it needs to navigate a trap that this particular audience makes unavoidable: in ordinary classical logic, once you grant a contradiction, anything whatsoever follows — so any sentence of the form "X and not-X" in a tribute risks, by classical lights, "proving" that his career was a failure, or that no one should drink the wine, or whatever else. I want the joke to walk into that trap knowingly and then defuse it the way he would. Two further constraints: don't just run the liar paradox straight ("everything I say tonight is a lie") — he has been hearing that at conference dinners since before I was born — and the closing line, the actual raising of the glasses, has to be something both a classical logician and a dialetheist can drink to in full sincerity, which rules out anything like "may your retirement be free of contradictions," since for him that would be closer to a curse than a blessing.
