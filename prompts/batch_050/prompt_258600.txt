I am a novelist, not a philosopher, but my current project has dragged me into your territory and I want to know whether I have wandered into a defensible position or a comfortable one. The novel alternates chapters between a human translator and three animal narrators: a captive octopus, a carrion crow, and a worker honeybee. Researching them has left me convinced of something I now want stress-tested rather than praised: that phenomenal consciousness is very widely distributed across the animal kingdom, extending at least to cephalopods and insects, and that the burden of proof should rest on anyone who would deny it to an animal with an integrated nervous system rather than on those who attribute it. I have also developed my own rough evidential instrument, which I call the writeability test: an animal is probably conscious to the extent that a well-informed novelist can compose a passage from its point of view that neither collapses into human thought wearing a costume nor degrades into a bare chronicle of stimulus and response. My rationale is that consciousness is precisely whatever makes first-personal description of a creature's situation non-vacuous, so that disciplined, biologically informed imagination tracks it the way a key tracks a lock.

Treat this combination as the position and tell me what would count against it. Which empirical discoveries would force a retreat — for instance, findings that the behaviors motivating these attributions can be fully produced by mechanisms that, in us, operate without experience, or systematic dissociations between behavioral flexibility and any plausible neural substrate of experience — and which would be merely awkward? What would have to be true about the structural prerequisites of consciousness for honeybees to drop out while octopuses remain? Is there a coherent reading on which the writeability test measures nothing but my own imaginative facility and my readers' susceptibility to it, and what observation or argument would establish that? Please sort the candidate defeaters by decisiveness, note what defenders of widespread consciousness would themselves concede as counting against the view (not only what its critics claim), and assess whether the position as stated risks anything at all: if "consciousness may be present without behavioral signature" is always available as a defense, has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability, and is that fatal for something that is partly a methodological stance rather than an empirical hypothesis? For what it is worth, the counterevidence I would personally find hardest to wave away is divergence between my test and the science — a creature where disciplined imagination succeeds but the biology speaks against experience, or the reverse.

Take on the role of a philosopher of mind who specializes in comparative cognition and the science of consciousness, writing for an intelligent non-specialist who will actually make use of the answer. Think carefully before composing: begin with a brief inventory of the defeaters you judge worth discussing, then develop the assessment, and be willing to conclude that the position is well insulated — saying plainly whether you take that to be a virtue or a defect. Do not flatter the writeability test on account of its author's profession; if it is an elaborate way of mistaking my own talent for evidence, I would rather learn that now than in a review.
