State the Condorcet jury theorem, making its assumptions explicit. Then identify two distinct reasons why the theorem's conclusion might fail to carry over to a real-world electorate or committee, and for each reason, explain which assumption of the theorem it calls into question and why.

Student's answer:

The Condorcet jury theorem concerns majority voting on a question with a correct answer, such as whether a defendant is guilty. It says that if each voter has a probability greater than one-half of voting correctly, and the voters' judgments are independent of one another, then the probability that the majority verdict is correct increases as the size of the group increases, and it approaches certainty as the number of voters grows without bound. The intuition is that when individual competence is better than chance, the law of large numbers ensures that the correct votes come to outnumber the incorrect ones.

The theorem's assumptions are (1) that there is an objectively correct answer to the question being voted on, (2) that every voter's probability of being correct exceeds one-half, and (3) that the voters' judgments are independent, meaning that no voter's vote is influenced by any other voter's vote or by deliberation beforehand. This is why the theorem only strictly applies when voters cast their ballots without communicating, since communication would destroy independence. Some versions of the theorem also require an odd number of voters to avoid ties.

One reason the conclusion may not carry over to real electorates is that voters often share the same information sources -- the same newspapers, broadcasters, or party leaders. If many voters base their judgments on a common source that is itself mistaken, their errors will be correlated, which threatens the independence assumption. A related concern is that a few influential figures can sway large blocs of voters, again producing correlation.

A second reason is that real voters may not be competent at all, and here the theorem is more forgiving than one might expect. If voters are somewhat worse than chance, the theorem still holds, because the errors partially cancel out in a large group; the convergence toward the correct majority verdict simply becomes slower. So the competence assumption matters mainly for the speed of convergence rather than for whether the conclusion obtains at all. This is why the theorem is sometimes taken to justify democracy in general: given enough voters, majority rule will find the truth.
