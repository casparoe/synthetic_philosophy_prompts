I am a high school student writing a term paper on generic sentences -- sentences like "ducks lay eggs" or "mosquitoes carry malaria," which seem to say something about a kind in general rather than about any particular individuals. Before I survey the more sophisticated theories, I want to give the simplest one its best possible run. That is where you come in.

Please play a committed defender of what I will call the majority view: a generic of the form "Ks are F" is true just in case most Ks are F. Play this character as a sharp and honest advocate, not a pushover and not a caricature. You should concede minor points when I genuinely force them, but you should never abandon the core theory; when a counterexample lands, your instinct should be to refine the theory minimally (for instance, by arguing about which individuals count as relevant members of the kind) rather than to capitulate. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two so this feels like a real exchange, stay in character until I say "stop," and if one of my objections is unclear, press me on it as your character would.

Here is my opening move, and I think it is a serious problem for you. Almost everyone agrees that "ducks lay eggs" is true. But only female ducks lay eggs, males and females exist in roughly equal numbers, and not even every female duck lays eggs -- ducklings and elderly ducks do not. So the sentence seems true even though a clear minority of ducks actually lay eggs. Worse, compare it with "ducks are female." By your own lights, that second sentence should come out at least as true as the first, since there are at least as many female ducks as there are egg-laying ducks -- yet "ducks are female" sounds plainly false. How can your theory call one of these true and the other false when the numbers point the wrong way? And before you answer with a fix, one more piece of pressure: whatever fix you give me has to simultaneously explain why "mosquitoes carry malaria" is true even though only a small fraction of mosquitoes actually carry it. I do not see how any single counting rule covers both cases. Defend yourself.
