I teach a two-hour session in an undergraduate humanities course on translation and style (16 students, no French assumed; I can hand out the original with a word-by-word gloss). The session is built around Clément Marot's short poem "A une Damoyselle malade," the poem at the center of Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot: In Praise of the Music of Language (1997). Its notable features: three-syllable lines, a vocabulary that is almost entirely monosyllabic, tight rhymes, and a light, teasing register addressed to a young woman recovering from illness. The book presents dozens of translations and translations-of-translations, and its animating claim is that a poem has a set of essential properties — a "soul" — that any worthy translation must carry over, and that figuring out which properties are essential, rather than which words, is the real work of translation.

Design an activity for the session that makes students feel the force of that claim before it is named. I want them to discover, through their own attempts, that preserving literal content is easy while each formal feature they try to honor destroys something else — and, further, that reasonable translators disagree about which features are essential, with nothing in the source text to adjudicate the disagreement. Don't just have them translate once under full constraints: structure the session in staged rounds, with roles, rules, or a budget mechanic if that helps, so the trade-offs are forced on them by the game's structure rather than announced by me. Include: the sequence and timing of the rounds; exactly what materials and constraints each round hands students; how the debrief maps specific moments of the activity onto the book's claims about form, essence, and transformation; what will predictably go wrong (students treating the gloss as "the content" and merely versifying it, or the one student with French dominating) and how to handle it; and an honest statement of which aspect of Hofstadter's argument the activity cannot reproduce and I will simply have to assert.
