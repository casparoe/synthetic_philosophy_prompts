I help run a monthly discussion group for early-career civil servants and political science grad students, usually 16 to 20 people, and we have 80 minutes for a session on direct democracy. Everyone is familiar with the obvious referendum criticisms—voters may be uninformed, campaigns can be misleading, and turnout can be skewed—so I want an activity that reveals a subtler problem: a procedure can be perfectly majoritarian and transparent while citizens still have surprisingly little control over the final outcome. The focus should be the combination of agenda-setting, option bundling, abstention, and the privileged status of the status quo. Imagine that a referendum presents a yes-or-no choice, but an organizer decided which policies were bundled together, whether several popular but incompatible alternatives were allowed onto the ballot, whether abstention effectively supports one side, and whether changing the default requires a simple majority or an additional turnout threshold. The participants should discover that winning under the stated rules is not the same as having designed the choice they were given.

Act as an experienced facilitator of political philosophy workshops and think carefully before giving the final design. First brainstorm a few possible mechanics privately, then select the strongest one. The finished activity should include the fictional policy decision, roles for an agenda-setter and perhaps competing campaigners, concrete voting and information rules, several rounds in which only one procedural variable changes, payoff points or another small incentive that does not involve money, a minute-by-minute timeline, and instructions for keeping participants from realizing the target lesson too early. Include a debrief connecting moments in the game to agenda control, the Condorcet problem of cycling majorities if relevant, vote splitting, quorum effects, and status-quo bias. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, how to adjust for a group of 14 or 24, and which important feature of real referendums—such as constitutional constraints, media framing, or unequal organizing resources—the game cannot realistically reproduce.
