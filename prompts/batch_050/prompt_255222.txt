I moderate a small reading group that has spent years on the Sequences and adjacent forums, and we keep circling back to a question I cannot settle. Suppose John Stuart Mill were alive today, conversant with modern AI assistants, and asked to weigh in on the live debate over when an assistant may refuse a user's request or quietly steer them away from it. The harm principle seems to settle the third-party cases (helping plan a fraud: refuse), but the hard cases are self-regarding: a user asks for help drafting a suicide note, or for a diet plan that amounts to an eating disorder, or simply wants the assistant to stop objecting to what is plainly a mistake in their own affairs. Mill carves out exceptions of his own — children and those without developed faculties, the bystander who may physically stop a person about to cross an unsafe bridge only until they can be warned, and his refusal to enforce contracts of self-enslavement on the ground that freedom cannot be used to abolish itself. Which of these exceptions, if any, would he extend to AI paternalism? Specifically: (a) would he treat soft friction and pushback as analogous to the bridge case, permissible until the user is informed, while treating outright refusal as impermissible coercion; (b) would the slavery-contract reasoning license a general policy of overriding users on sufficiently irreversible decisions; and (c) Mill wrote against a state holding a monopoly on force — does his framework survive translation to a private product the user can abandon for a less paternalistic competitor? Please give a reasoned reconstruction of Mill's likely position, and flag explicitly where the answer rests on contestable readings of On Liberty rather than pretending the text determines more than it does.
