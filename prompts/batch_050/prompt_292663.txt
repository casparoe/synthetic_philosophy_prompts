My film club is doing a dystopia month and I got assigned to lead the discussion on District 9, and I want to try reading it through ubuntu and relational theories of personhood, the whole "a person is a person through other persons" idea. On the surface the fit seems almost too clean: the prawns get herded into a slum, called by a slur, force-evicted, experimented on, and basically denied personhood precisely by being cut out of the web of human relationships and mutual recognition. And Wikus's arc looks like the inverse -- he starts as this petty bureaucrat fully embedded in the human community (wife, job, in-laws, everyone toasting him at the office party) and the more his body transforms, the more that community withdraws recognition from him, until the only people who treat him as a someone are Christopher Johnson and the other prawns. So is the film dramatizing the ubuntu claim that personhood is constituted by relations rather than located in biology? His body literally changes species, but the film seems to treat "who counts" as tracking social bonds, not DNA.

But here's where I want the reading to get pushed rather than just illustrated, because I think the film complicates ubuntu in at least two places. First, Christopher Johnson seems to have full moral standing and agency even though he's almost completely excluded from human society -- his relations are mostly just his son and his dead friend -- so does the film secretly smuggle in a more intrinsic-dignity picture, where exclusion is a wrong done to someone whose personhood doesn't depend on being recognized at all? If personhood really is made by community, it feels like the oppressor gets to define who's a person, which is exactly the problem District 9 is about. Second, the ending: Wikus, fully transformed, makes those little metal flowers for Tania, and she keeps them. Is that the film saying relational ties can survive and even reconstitute personhood across a total change of form, or is it saying his personhood never depended on anyone's recognition in the first place? I'd love a reading that takes the actual philosophy seriously (including the objection that ubuntu-style views make dignity hostage to social acceptance), points at specific scenes the club should watch closely -- the eviction raids, the weapons testing, the birthday party video, the final mech fight where Wikus goes back for Christopher -- and is honest about where the film resists the ubuntu reading instead of forcing it. Bonus points for what a monster movie can show about personhood that a philosophy paper can't.
