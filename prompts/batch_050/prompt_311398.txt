I am preparing the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of biology. The students have taken one prior course in philosophy of science and have spent three weeks on the species problem, covering the major species concepts and the debate between species monism and pluralism. I need one essay question that students would answer in 75 minutes, together with a model answer of roughly 600 to 700 words and a marking scheme. This question is worth 20 marks, the entire exam.

The question should be built around a fictional case, fully specified on the exam paper itself so that no outside knowledge is required, along the following lines (you may adjust the details provided the structure survives): a genus of flowering plants occupies a chain of five mountain valleys. The case data given to students should establish that adjacent populations cross-pollinate freely and yield fertile offspring, that gene flow between non-adjacent valleys drops off sharply with distance, and that the two populations at the ends of the chain produce no viable offspring even under controlled greenhouse crosses. The data should further include a sixth population that sets seed without fertilization and is morphologically almost indistinguishable from one of the valley populations, and a seventh population that is fully interfertile with its neighbors in the greenhouse but flowers six weeks earlier, so that it is reproductively isolated from them in the wild. Students should also be told that the asexual population and the early-flowering population each exploit a soil type the other populations cannot tolerate.

The question itself should have three parts: (a) state how many species the genus contains under each of three named species concepts (the biological species concept understood in terms of actual or potential interbreeding, a phylogenetic concept understood in terms of independently evolving lineages, and an ecological concept understood in terms of niche occupancy), identifying precisely where the verdicts diverge and which feature of the data drives each divergence; (b) argue for a verdict on what the divergence shows — whether the right response is species pluralism, the eliminativist conclusion that there is no single kind the term picks out, or the monist claim that one concept is correct and its rivals defective; (c) state what, if anything, would count as a further fact of the matter about how many species there really are, and what follows for practices such as conservation listing that presuppose stable species counts if no such fact exists.

The question must be impossible to answer well by reciting definitions. Part (a) should be routine for a prepared student, but parts (b) and (c) should force students to distinguish the claim that species boundaries are vague from the stronger claim that there is no concept-independent fact of the matter, and to connect the metaphysical verdict to practical consequences.

The model answer should take a definite position rather than survey the options even-handedly, while acknowledging the strongest consideration against the position it defends. The marking scheme should distribute the 20 marks across the three parts; name five or six specific moves an A-range answer must make (for instance, noticing that the asexual population renders the biological species concept inapplicable rather than merely ambiguous, and treating the ring-like chain as a challenge to transitivity of the interbreeding relation rather than as a mere borderline case); state how many marks each move carries; and attach to each move a one-line note describing how weaker answers characteristically fail at it, so that the scheme separates students who understand the problem from students who have memorized the labels.

Please write everything in polished American English.
