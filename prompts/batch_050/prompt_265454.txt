I work as a software engineer at a mid-sized company, and my director of engineering has a track record of kill-or-continue decisions that I've been quietly collecting. A decision is coming up and I'd like to predict how she'll call it — and, frankly, to argue my own position in terms she'll find compelling. Here are her past calls, as I understand the facts:

1. Metrics pipeline. We spent six months building an internal metrics pipeline. Halfway through, a vendor released a product that did the same thing better and cheaper, and migrating our (partial, unlaunched) work onto it would have taken about two weeks. She killed our version. Verdict: abandon.

2. Deployment system. The team spent a year building a custom deployment system. An excellent open-source alternative now exists, but our system is wired into a dozen internal tools and two engineers have deep expertise in it. She kept ours. Verdict: continue.

3. Unwanted feature. A three-month prototype produced convincing evidence that users didn't want the feature. Only about two weeks of work remained to ship it. She killed it anyway. Verdict: abandon.

4. Database migration. A migration was roughly 70% complete when a higher-priority project threatened to shelve it indefinitely. She had us finish it first, on the grounds that pausing and resuming later would cost far more than finishing now, since half the team's context would be gone. Verdict: continue.

The new case: an internal documentation tool, built by a team of four over nine months, never announced outside the engineering org, and used by almost no one. Usage data says it isn't pulling its weight; the two engineers who built most of it have since left the company; finishing the remaining roadmap would take two engineers about three months, and a decent commercial alternative costs less than that. The only argument anyone has offered for continuing is, in a colleague's words, that it would be a shame to waste nine months of work. There are no commitments to walk back, nobody outside the team knows the tool exists, and what the team learned building it isn't obviously transferable to anything else we do.

What will she say? More than the verdict itself, I want the rule. Which decision rules are consistent with all four past calls, which of those rules diverge on this new case, and what does each predict? I know the phrase "sunk cost fallacy," but her calls in cases 2 and 4 at least sound like honoring past investment, even though she always justifies them in forward-looking terms; I can't tell whether that's a real pattern in her reasoning or a coincidence of these particular examples. If her rule does treat past investment as a genuine reason in some cases, is she making a mistake, or is the orthodox view about sunk costs too simple? And if the four cases genuinely underdetermine her rule, describe the additional past or hypothetical case that would settle it.
