I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on collective responsibility, and I'm stuck on a consistency problem in my own intuitions about corporate blame. I'll give you my verdicts on a set of cases, plus the tentative principle I keep reaching for, and I'd like you to find the principle (if one exists) that systematizes the verdicts -- or tell me honestly which verdict has to go.

Case 1. A pharmaceutical CEO, against the advice of her own scientists, personally orders that a side-effect finding be left out of a regulatory filing. Patients are harmed. Verdict: the CEO is blameworthy; blaming *the company* feels redundant or mistaken -- the company here seems like just a tool of one blameworthy person.

Case 2. A shipping company restructures so that safety inspections, maintenance budgets, and staffing decisions sit in three different divisions, each with incentives to cut costs and none with the authority or information to see the aggregate risk. No individual did anything that, given their role, was unreasonable. A preventable accident follows. Verdict: the company is blameworthy even though no individual is -- or at least, no individual's blameworthiness comes close to matching the magnitude of the failure.

Case 3. Ten years after the accident in Case 2, every manager and employee involved has left. A court now fines the company, which is staffed by entirely new people, and the fine depresses the wages of current workers who had nothing to do with it. Verdict: blaming the company in the sense of *holding it answerable* still seems right, but punishing it now seems wrong, because the punishment lands only on innocents.

Case 4. A chemical firm follows standard industry practice circa 1985 -- practices everyone in the industry used and no regulator had questioned. The practice turns out to have caused slow environmental harm. Verdict: some blame, but clearly mitigated; I resist saying the firm is exactly as blameworthy as a firm that secretly cut corners in the same era, even if the harm is identical.

Case 5. A lone employee at a bank commits fraud in violation of explicit, well-designed, genuinely enforced policies, hiding it from supervisors. Verdict: the bank is not blameworthy, or barely -- and I notice this verdict is in tension with Case 2, because in both cases the failure was in some sense produced by the organizational structure, and in both cases individuals acted as their roles made rational. I can't currently articulate why structure is exculpatory in one case and inculpatory in the other.

Case 6. A founder-run five-person company and a multinational do functionally the same wrongful act through functionally the same internal process. Verdict: I'm more comfortable saying the small company is blameworthy -- the line between firm and persons is thinner -- and I don't think this is just a heuristic. But I also hold (per Case 1) that when a company really is one person's instrument, corporate blame is redundant. These two feel like they're pulling in opposite directions.

My tentative principle: a company is blameworthy for an outcome when the outcome traces to features of the company's *constitutive* structure -- its decision procedures, incentive systems, and information flows -- rather than to the discretionary choices of identifiable individuals, and blame lapses when the structure itself has been replaced. This handles Cases 2 and 5 the way I want but seems to get Case 1 wrong (it predicts the company is blameless, whereas I'd rather say blame is *redundant*, not absent), it's silent on whether Case 3's punishment intuition is coherent, and I'm not sure what it implies for Case 4's mitigation.

Here is what I want from you. First, state precisely the principle, or small set of candidate principles, that best captures my verdicts, and identify which clause of it each case motivates. Second, tell me which of my verdicts no reasonably simple principle can accommodate -- I suspect at least one of the Case 1 / Case 5 / Case 6 cluster has to give, and I'd rather be told which than have a baroque principle built around all of them. Third, give the principle's verdicts on two cases I haven't considered: (a) a company whose harmful practice was installed by managers long gone but knowingly retained by current management who benefit from it, and (b) a company that reaps the reputational *credit* for an accident of good luck it did nothing to produce -- I'm curious whether my framework is symmetric between blame and praise, since I notice I never tested it on praise. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in the analysis overall, and single out the specific step -- a case reading, a clause of the principle, a prediction -- where you are least sure you're right.
