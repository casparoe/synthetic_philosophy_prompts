You are a philosopher of science who also keeps up with the engineering literature on fault detection and machine monitoring. Think carefully before answering; sketch the candidate questions first and then prioritize.

Here is the situation I want your help with. I run a small research group working on what I call mechanistic anomaly detection: given a system whose parts and operations we understand reasonably well, deciding whether some observed deviation in its output signals that the mechanism itself has changed, or merely that the same mechanism is operating under different inputs, degraded components, or unusual background conditions. This comes up everywhere — a cell biologist asking whether a drug-resistant tumor is running the same pathway with a broken part or has rewired to a different pathway; an engineer asking whether a bearing's vibration signature indicates wear in an intact machine or a structural change in the machine; arguably an interpretability researcher asking whether a fine-tuned model still implements the same circuit.

What strikes me is that the field has no agreed answer to the identity question underneath all of this: what makes it the case that two tokens — across time, across individuals, or across levels of description — are instances of the same mechanism, such that deviations within one are noise and deviations across them are anomalies? Without that, anomaly thresholds look arbitrary.

My question: what are the main open conceptual questions here, and which two or three should my group prioritize over the next three years? Candidates I can see include: the identity criteria for mechanisms (organization vs. components vs. causal-graph structure vs. function); how to distinguish parameter drift from mechanism change when degradation is continuous rather than discrete; whether sameness of mechanism is level-relative in a way that makes cross-level anomaly claims incoherent; and how much of the philosophical work on mechanism individuation (e.g., debates over constitution and etiological vs. constitutive relevance) actually constrains engineering practice rather than merely redescribing it. But don't just work from my list — add what I've missed.

For each question you prioritize, tell me: why it is upstream of the others (or not), how tractable it is for a mixed philosophy-and-engineering group rather than pure experimentalists, and what would change your ranking — i.e., what result or development over the next few years would move a question up or down the list. The deliverable I want is a defended agenda with explicit criteria, not a survey of the territory.
