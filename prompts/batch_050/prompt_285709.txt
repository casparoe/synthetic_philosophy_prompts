My two friends got into it after watching some youtube video about the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, and I keep flip-flopping on who's right, so I want a referee.

Dan's opening move: the whole point of many-worlds is that every outcome happens, so probability makes no sense there. If a measurement goes one way in one branch and the other way in another, there's no single future to be uncertain about, so saying an outcome is "90% likely" is literally meaningless -- 90% likely to happen where? Everything happens.

Mara agreed it's a real problem but thinks it's fixable. Her move: after the split there are multiple copies of you, and you don't know which one you are. That self-locating uncertainty is enough to ground probabilities, and the "amount" of you in each branch tracks the amplitude-squared thing, so the quantum rule falls out. Dan replied that the number of copies is just the number of branches, and a simple experiment has one branch per outcome, so counting copies gives 50/50 even when quantum mechanics says 90/10 -- her fix quietly assumes the very weights it's supposed to explain.

Mara's next move: drop the metaphysics, look at behavior. A rational person in a branching world should make bets and choices as if the Born probabilities were true -- take the bet that pays off big in the 90% branches, refuse the mirror-image bet -- and that pattern of rational preference is all probability ever needed to be anyway. Dan said that only shows how you should act, not what's true; he compared it to Pascal's wager, where it's arguably rational to act as if God exists without that making God exist.

Mara's last move: his own side has the same problem, since probability is weird even in a single world (a fair coin lands heads -- in what sense was the 50% ever "true"?). Dan said in a single world there's at least a fact about which outcome will happen, so uncertainty is ordinary ignorance, whereas in many-worlds there's nothing to be ignorant about at all.

Please adjudicate this move by move -- for each exchange, say who's right and briefly why, then say who comes out ahead overall. And when you answer, please clearly separate what's basically settled among people who actually work on this from what's still genuinely contested -- I don't want one contested side presented to me as the consensus.
