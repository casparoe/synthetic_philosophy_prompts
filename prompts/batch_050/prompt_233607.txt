I'm in a decision theory reading group, and for the third year running we've circled back to the Ellsberg urns without anyone budging. The facts aren't in dispute: most people strictly prefer betting on a draw from the urn with a known 50-50 composition over the urn with an unknown composition, and they do so in a pattern that no single probability assignment to the unknown urn can rationalize. One camp in the group -- call them the permissivists -- holds that preferring known odds to unknown odds is a perfectly rational response to a genuine difference in one's epistemic situation, and that subjective expected utility theory simply fails to capture everything rational choice should be sensitive to. The other camp -- the orthodox -- holds that the pattern violates the sure-thing principle and related consistency constraints, and that once you've accepted the Savage framework, ambiguity aversion is just a bias to be debiased, like the gambler's fallacy. What strikes me is that both sides fully agree on the experimental data and on the formalism; the disagreement seems to be about something upstream, and I'd like to know what.

Here's my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two camps are really disagreeing about what norms of rationality are allowed to govern. The orthodox think rational norms apply only to an agent's first-order degrees of belief, full stop; the permissivists think such norms can also apply to the quality of the evidential basis underlying those degrees -- to how much confidence, in the sense of resilience or robustness, a credence deserves. If that's right, then the debate might be partly verbal (each side using 'rational' with a different scope) and partly substantive (a real question about whether second-order evidential considerations can bear on first-order choice without double-counting). I even sketched a possible empirical lever for my friends: a variant where subjects themselves prepare the unknown urn using a procedure whose reliability they can estimate from track record. If ambiguity aversion scales with distrust of one's own evidence rather than with unknown odds as such, that seems to favor one reading of the phenomenon over the other. My friends were unmoved, each camp claiming the experiment would vindicate them.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a substantive normative dispute about what rationality demands, a verbal dispute about the scope of the word 'rational', or two parties answering different questions under one heading -- one about coherence of preferences, one about the epistemic credentials of the beliefs behind them? Please engage with what each side actually says rather than surveying the literature, assess my hypothesis including whether my little experiment would settle anything or is itself ambiguous between the two readings, and tell me what, if anything, would count as genuine progress here: a new experiment, a representation theorem, a conceptual distinction, or an argument that the question itself needs reframing. If you think the impasse is permanent, say why.
