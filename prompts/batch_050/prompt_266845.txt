I'm a management consultant who's been writing a book (half manifesto, half memoir) about dignity at work, and the theoretical backbone of the whole thing is Hegel's master-slave dialectic from the Phenomenology of Spirit, which I first read in a grad seminar years ago and have been leaning on ever since. Before I send the proposal to agents I want to stress test the core commitments, because my partner read the draft and said something feels off but couldn't tell me what. Here's the package I've been working with for awhile:

(a) Recognition is only worth anything if it's freely given. That's the whole lesson of why the master gets no lasting satisfaction from the slave, whose recognition is extracted under duress.
(b) Because of (a), real recognition can actually be engineered: a well-designed company, with mandatory peer-feedback cycles, structured gratitude rituals, and proper promotion ceremonies, can reliably produce genuine mutual recognition between employees and management.
(c) The slave's formative labor is the key to everything: by shaping objects, the worker achieves a self-certainty the master never gets, and this self-certainty is complete in itself — it doesn't need anyone else to ratify it. A worker who throws herself into her craft can be fully self-made.
(d) Full self-consciousness requires mutual recognition between two genuinely free and equal parties; one-sided recognition is inherently deficient, which is why hierarchy can never be a resting point.
(e) The life-and-death struggle is literal in Hegel, obviously, but in my book I treat it as wholly metaphorical — office politics, status games on Slack, the fear of getting fired — and I claim the dialectic transfers to the modern workplace without any loss of analytical power, no translation work needed.
(f) The master comes to depend on the slave for everything, while the slave still depends on the master for recognition, and that's a stalemate the dialectic just leaves unresolved.

What I want from you is an audit, not a summary of Hegel. Tell me which of these commitments pull against each other, how bad each collision is (rank them by severity, don't just tell me it's all fine or all doomed), and for each one what I'd have to give up or soften to fix it. Also tell me which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection — my partner kept insisting (f) is a flat contradiction and I'm fairly sure it isn't, but I'm too close to the material to trust myself. If there's one single revision that buys the most consistency for the least sacrifice, name it. Please answer in American English.
