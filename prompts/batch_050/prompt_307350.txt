I am writing a term paper for a seminar on Korean Neo-Confucianism, and my group has run into a puzzle about the Four-Seven debate — the extended sixteenth-century exchange between Yi Hwang (Toegye) and Ki Dae-seung (Gobong) over whether the four sprouts described by Mencius and the seven emotions listed in the ritual classics are issued by principle (li) and psychophysical stuff (ki) respectively, or whether the four are simply an especially pure strain within the seven, with ki issuing and li riding along in every case. Our professor likes to settle such questions with a classification game: she describes a feeling and asks whether it is four-like or seven-like. Her verdicts so far have been: a startle of fear when a glass smashes behind one — seven; the alarm and commiseration that arise unbidden at the sight of a small child crawling toward the rim of a well — four; the settled reverence a student develops through months of quiet-sitting — four; irritation at a colleague who takes credit for one's work — seven; shame at catching oneself having exaggerated a friend's virtues in a letter of recommendation — four; the ache of homesickness that comes over one on an autumn evening — seven. She insists this pattern is perfectly coherent and indeed obvious, though one member of my group suspects she is smuggling the very distinction at issue into her way of describing the cases.

Now she has set the class a new case, and I would like your prediction before we discuss it: a young scholar's fervent, almost impatient longing to become a genuinely good person — a desire that keeps him awake at night, that curdles into frustration whenever he fails, and that he half suspects is tangled up with vanity. Will she call it four-like or seven-like, and why? More importantly, tell me what rule she seems to be applying, because my group has produced at least three candidate rules, each consistent with all six precedents: that four-like feelings are those responsive to moral reasons as such, while seven-like feelings track one's own weal and woe; that four-like feelings are those that would survive, refined but continuous, in a fully cultivated sage, while seven-like feelings are what cultivation harmonizes away; and that four-like feelings are directed at universalizable features of a situation (a child in danger, a lie told) while seven-like feelings are directed at the self's particular fortunes. These rules disagree about the longing for sagehood, and they seem to line up differently with Toegye's, Gobong's, and later Yulgok's positions in the actual debate. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the verdict, say so honestly, and name the single additional case — some feeling, real or contrived — that would most efficiently tell the three rules apart. Finally, say which historical position each candidate rule amounts to, and whether you think the professor's pattern of verdicts is defensible by the lights of the debate itself or whether my skeptical classmate is right.
