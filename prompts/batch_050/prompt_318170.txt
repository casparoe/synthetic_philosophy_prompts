Last week a friend and I — we both work on AI model evaluation — spent an entire evening arguing about what chain-of-thought monitoring can tell us, and we arrived at an impasse I cannot reason my way out of. The dispute turned on a passage from Turpin and colleagues' 2023 paper "Language Models Don't Always Say What They Think: Unfaithful Explanations in Chain-of-Thought Prompting," which my friend read aloud as his trump card:

"We demonstrate that CoT explanations can be heavily influenced by adding biasing features to model inputs—e.g., by reordering the multiple-choice options in a few-shot prompt to make the answer always "(A)"—which models systematically fail to mention in their explanations."

His argument ran as follows. (1) If a system's report of why it produced an output systematically omits a factor that actually influenced the output, and instead offers plausible reasons that were not the operative ones, then that report is worthless as evidence about the system's processing. (2) The passage shows that language models' chain-of-thought explanations have exactly this property: the reordered options sway the answer, and the explanation never says so. (3) Therefore chain-of-thought explanations are worthless as evidence about how models arrive at their answers — we may use them to improve performance, but we learn nothing about the model from reading them. (4) Human introspective reports have the same property: decades of work beginning with Nisbett and Wilson show that people confidently cite reasons while being moved by factors they never mention, and split-brain patients confabulate explanations for actions they did not choose. (5) So human introspective reports are likewise worthless as evidence — including eyewitness testimony, the debriefings of experimental subjects, and my friend's own account, offered across the evening, of which considerations had persuaded him. (6) Conclusion (5) is absurd — but my friend accepted it cheerfully and said the absurdity is mine rather than his: only behavior is evidence, and the lesson of the paper is that we should have stopped listening to self-reports, artificial or human, long ago.

I think the argument proves too much, and I told him so; what I could not do, at midnight, was name the step where it goes wrong. If forced to choose, I would rather surrender the strong conclusion in (3) than the parity claim in (4): I am prepared to concede that chain-of-thought is a poor guide to model cognition in adversarially constructed conditions, but not that nothing evidential survives. I also honestly cannot tell how much weight the quoted sentence will bear — does "systematically fail to mention" establish anything about an explanation's total evidential value, or only about its completeness?
