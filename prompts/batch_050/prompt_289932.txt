Just had a long arguement down the pub (fittingly enough) with my mate Sam about whether Plato was basically anti-drink. Sam's line was that the Athenian stranger in the Laws only tolerates wine because banning it outright is unenforceable, and that deep down Plato reckons drunkenness is nothing but slavishness. I think that's wrong, and to prove it I've been listing all the positive reasons Plato gives, mainly in Books I and II of the Laws, for actually valuing wine and regulated drunkenness rather than merely putting up with them. Here's what I've got:

1. The training argument: you can't become courageous without being exposed to fear, so by the same token you can't become self-controlled without being exposed to pleasure and shamelessness. Wine is the cheap, low-stakes way to let people practise keeping their heads whilst their shame is stripped away.
2. The testing argument: character is ruinously expensive to test in real life (you don't find out what someone's like by handing them your army or your household), whereas getting a man drunk is a quick and harmless way to see what his soul is actually made of.
3. The rejuvenation argument: Dionysus gave wine as a medicine against the crabbed stiffness of old age, so that the old become supple and youthful enough again to sing and be re-educated, hence the Dionysiac chorus of elders.
4. The symposion argument: a drinking party run by a sober, wise master of the feast is itself an instrument of education and civic friendship, so drunkenness properly governed is a school of self-command.
5. The anti-prohibition argument: societies that ban pleasures wholesale never train anyone to resist them and just breed hypocrisy, so regulated indulgence beats blanket prohibition.

Sam definately reckons those are all the reasons there are, and that none of them say drink is good, only useful. Two questions then. First: is there another distinct positive reason Plato gives, in the Laws or anywhere else in the corpus, for valuing wine or drunkenness that isn't on my list? It has to be genuinely different, not a rewording, special case or mash-up of the five above, and not just "wine is pleasant and pleasure is sometimes fine". Say roughly where in the text it shows up. Second, and this matters more for settling the pub dispute: how does your candidate bear on Sam's comeback? Does Plato anywhere treat wine or drunkenness as worth having for its own sake, rather than as a tool for education, testing and social glue, or is Sam right that the valuation is always conditional on what it does for the city and the soul? Be honest if you think he's right, I'd rather lose the argument than win it on a dodgey point.
