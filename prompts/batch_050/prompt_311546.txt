my city is arguing about removing a statue of a 19th-century industrialist from the main square because he made much of his fortune through violently exploited colonial labor. At the public meeting, one councillor said removing it would be like tearing a chapter out of the city’s history textbook: future residents literally would not know what happened. Someone else replied that a statue is less like a textbook chapter and more like a gold star or trophy displayed in the entrance hall — it doesn’t just record the person, it publicly endorses them. The proposed compromise is to move the statue to a museum, leave a detailed marker and 3-D scan at the original site, and rename the nearby street while listing its former name underneath for ten years.

Audit the two analogies. Give the correspondence table for each: what is the textbook, page, author, reader, eraser, trophy, judges, audience, and so on. Which mappings fit and which quietly smuggle in assumptions about public space, collective memory, honor, or who gets to speak for the city? Does moving rather than destroying the statue matter? And once the disanalogies are on the table, does the erasing history arguement actually support keeping the statue where it is, or only some weaker duty of preservation?
