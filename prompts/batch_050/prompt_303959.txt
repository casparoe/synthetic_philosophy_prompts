A friend and I have been circling the same argument about the shutdown problem for most of a year, and last weekend, after several hours of it, we finally admitted that we keep talking past each other and should try an adversarial collaboration instead. Her position is that shutdownability is achievable in principle within the expected-utility framework: you design the agent's objective so that it neither seeks nor resists being shut down, using some combination of indifference constructions, setups where the agent's uncertainty about what is actually good for its operators gives it positive reason to defer to them, and penalties on actions that influence the shutdown mechanism or the people near it. Where these constructions have known failure modes, she thinks they are engineering difficulties to be patched case by case. My position is that the failures are systematic rather than incidental: every patch in this family seems to generate a mirror-image problem (an agent made indifferent between shutdown and continued operation becomes indifferent to whether its indifference is preserved, including by its successors; an agent given the right kind of uncertainty acquires incentives to manage the evidence its operators see), and I suspect the whole approach of writing shutdownability into the objective is the wrong level of intervention, with any viable solution having to come from an agent design that isn't a straightforward maximizer at all.

Write the joint statement we should produce from this collaboration. It should open with a numbered section of claims we can both sign — I expect these to include, at minimum, that a sufficiently capable naive maximizer has instrumental reasons to resist or disable its shutdown channel, that shutdownability therefore has to be designed for rather than assumed, that the relevant operators are fallible and can in principle be manipulated, and that neither of us is claiming current systems already exhibit the failure modes we're disputing. Then give a disagreements section phrased with real care, where each disagreement is stated in neutral wording that each of us would accept as a fair description of her own view, followed by the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of us. I suspect one crux is whether genuine uncertainty about the operator's preferences rescues the deference argument or merely relocates the manipulation incentive; another is whether any construction in this family survives an agent capable of reasoning about its own training and modification process; a third is whether shutdownability is binary or comes in degrees, and what partial success would even look like. For each crux, specify a concrete case, construction, or line of argument that we both agree bears on it, and write down in advance what each of us predicts we would find. Format the whole thing as the agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table with a prediction column per side. Don't include citations or a bibliography — we'll fill in references to the actual literature ourselves — and where you need to gesture at existing constructions, describe them rather than naming them.
