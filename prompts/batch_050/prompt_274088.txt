I have been working my way through Frank Knight's Risk, Uncertainty and Profit (1921) with a reading group on the foundations of decision theory, and one claim in the chapter "Structures and Methods for Meeting Uncertainty" has been nagging at me. After drawing his famous distinction between measurable risk and unmeasurable uncertainty, Knight writes:

"When an individual instance only is at issue, there is no difference for conduct between a measurable risk and an unmeasurable uncertainty. The individual, as already observed, throws his estimate of the value of an opinion into the probability form of 'a successes in b trials' (a/b being a proper fraction) and 'feels' toward it as toward any other probability situation."

Taken at face value, this says that for one-shot decisions, conduct collapses uncertainty into risk: people form a probability-like estimate and then treat it exactly as they would treat a known probability. But this looks flatly contradicted by the experimental literature on ambiguity aversion that grew out of Daniel Ellsberg's 1961 paper -- a body of work that accumulated mostly after Knight's death in 1972 and which he apparently never addressed. In the standard setup, subjects choose between betting on an urn known to contain fifty red and fifty black balls and an urn containing a hundred balls in unknown proportions. By Knight's lights, a subject's best estimate for the unknown urn is fifty-fifty by symmetry, and since the bet is a single instance, the quoted passage implies she should "feel toward" that estimate exactly as toward the known urn and be indifferent. Instead, subjects reliably pay a premium to bet on the known urn, and many prefer red-on-known yet black-on-known in combinations that no single probability assignment over the unknown urn can rationalize.

Suppose Knight had lived to see several decades of these replications and was pressed to respond. What would his position most plausibly have been? Would he concede that the quoted claim is simply empirically false -- that people do not, even for single instances, treat estimates like probabilities -- and revise the risk/uncertainty distinction accordingly? Or would he more likely object that urn experiments fail to instantiate genuine uncertainty in his sense at all: the unknown urn's composition is a stable fact that is in principle knowable, the trials can be grouped and repeated, and the situation lacks the uniqueness that, for Knight, is precisely what prevents a distribution from being formed -- in which case ambiguity aversion would be a laboratory curiosity about his risk category rather than his uncertainty category? If he took the second line, would the defense actually hold up, given that he himself concedes people "feel" toward estimates as toward probabilities, or would it reduce his famous distinction to a claim about which situations exist rather than about how agents behave? Please ground your answer in Knight's actual arguments elsewhere in the book rather than in generic summaries of his position, and lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- your single best prediction of his response -- followed by the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
