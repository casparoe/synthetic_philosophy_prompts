I'm TA-ing a philosophy of mind seminar this fall and the undergrads keep doing the thing where they pick a solution to the problem of other minds and then just argue past each other -- one week the analogy-from-my-own-case person and the Wittgensteinian criteria person talked for forty minutes without once disagreeing about what a solution was even supposed to accomplish. So for the session where we wrap up the unit, I want to hand them a spec sheet first: what does a solution to the problem of other minds actually have to do to count as adequate? Write me that list of desiderata, with the case or consideration motivating each one.

A few constraints on what I'm looking for. First, I want the spec relativized to a purpose, because I've noticed "good enough for what?" is doing a lot of hidden work: my preferred purpose is an account strong enough to underwrite ordinary moral practice (we extend consideration to the locked-in patient, we don't to the toaster) but not so strong it has to refute the solipsist on the solipsist's own terms -- and I'd like you to flag how the requirements list would change if the purpose were full anti-skeptical refutation instead. Second, the list has to include the cases a solution must get right (dream characters, split-brain patients, octopuses or something similarly distant from us, current AI chatbots, and the boring case of the person across the seminar table) and the distinctions it can't collapse -- e.g., between knowing THAT someone is minded and knowing WHAT it's like to be them, and between having a justified belief and having a usable test. Third, and this is the part I care most about: tell me which pairs or subsets of the desiderata you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable, because I have a hunch that the demand to justify other-minds beliefs without leaning on my own case as evidence collides with the demand to cover creatures radically unlike me, and if the list itself is inconsistent that's a result I want them to see. Fourth, mark which requirements are genuine desiderata versus which are just the prejudices of one camp dressed up as common sense.

After the list, briefly score the main contenders against it -- argument from analogy, the criteria/Wittgensteinian approach, inference to the best explanation, theory-theory and simulation theory, and the direct-perception or phenomenological view -- but keep the scoring to a few lines each, since knowing the contenders well is their job, not the handout's. If one single requirement knocks out the most theories, name it. A table for the desiderata is fine. Don't pad it with a bibliography, they have the syllabus for that.
