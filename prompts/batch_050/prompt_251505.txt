I've spent the past year reading popular and semi-popular accounts of predictive processing -- Andy Clark's books, Anil Seth's work on perception as controlled hallucination, some interviews with Karl Friston -- and I'm increasingly convinced I've been sold a vision without being told which parts of it are actually secure. The popular versions glide smoothly from "perception is prediction" to "the brain is a Bayesian inference machine" to "everything the organism does minimizes free energy," and I genuinely can't tell, from the outside, whether that glide reflects settled science, plausible extrapolation, or one very elastic framework absorbing everything it touches. I have no academic background, so I can't adjudicate the primary literature myself, but I'd like to spend the next year or so of serious self-study on the questions where my attention would actually be informative -- the ones whose resolution would most change whether the whole edifice deserves its current reputation.

So my question: among the open questions in and around predictive processing and the free energy principle, which two or three are most worth a committed amateur following closely, and in what order? The candidates I keep running into are things like: whether predictive processing is a theory of perception only or genuinely scales to action, planning, and abstract thought; whether the free energy principle is a falsifiable scientific claim or more like a mathematical restatement that can fit any behavior and therefore explains nothing; how much of the framework's appeal rests on its unifying elegance rather than on predictions rival theories miss; and whether the connection to consciousness (the "controlled hallucination" picture) is doing real explanatory work or is a metaphor layered on top. But I don't want to constrain you -- if the question that actually matters isn't on my list, tell me and demote mine.

Please don't give me a neutral survey of all the open problems and leave me to choose. I want a defended agenda: an explicit ranking, the criteria you're ranking by (I'd guess some mix of how much downstream work depends on the question, how tractable it is for someone reading at my level rather than doing experiments, and how much the framework's credibility actually hangs on it), and an honest statement of the trade-offs between those criteria where they pull apart. Concretely: which question is upstream of the others, in the sense that settling it would settle or dissolve several of the rest? Which would you deprioritize because the answer wouldn't change much? And for your top pick, tell me what I'd notice in the next few years of books and papers -- what kinds of results or arguments -- that should move it up or down my list. I'd rather have a ranking you stand behind, with reasons, than a careful hedge that protects you from being wrong.
