I have no academic training, but on my own I've come to think the hard problem isn't the obvious cases (requests that are illegal or clearly dangerous) but the gray zone where the user is competent, the request is legal, and the assistant simply "knows better" than the user what's good for them.

I've also noticed something I haven't seen discussed much: most real-world paternalism by an AI isn't a refusal at all. It's invisible. The assistant can decline to mention an option, rank one choice above another, answer a slightly different question than the one asked, add a warning to one suggestion but not to an identical one, or express enthusiasm selectively. I'll call this "silent steering." A refusal at least tells the user they've been overridden; silent steering doesn't even do that. Any rulebook that only governs refusals will miss most of the actual paternalism, so I want the procedure to cover both.

What I need is a decision procedure our reviewers can actually follow — something closer to a flowchart in words than to an essay — for classifying any proposed assistant behavior as (a) freely permissible, (b) permissible only with disclosure to the user, (c) requiring escalation to a human supervisor, or (d) forbidden. Please build it on whatever the ethics of paternalism actually has to offer — the harm principle, the distinction between soft and hard paternalism, consent and competence, the difference between protecting someone from others and protecting them from themselves — but translate it into steps a reviewer with no philosophy background can run in a few minutes per case. The procedure must explicitly include a step for detecting silent steering in a draft response, and a rule for when silent steering is worse than an open refusal and when it might actually be better.

Please be honest about where the procedure breaks down: mark the points where judgment can't be eliminated, and say what a reviewer should do there rather than pretending the flowchart covers everything.
