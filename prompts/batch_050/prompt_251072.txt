I'm collecting the mechanisms by which philosophical thought experiments can mislead us -- ways an intuition pump can generate a confident verdict that doesn't actually support the conclusion it's deployed for. So far my list has: (1) loaded description, where the case is narrated in vocabulary that already presupposes the target theory, so the intuition responds to the framing rather than the facts; (2) irrelevant affective detail, where features with no argumentative role (vividness, sympathy for one party, disgust) drive the verdict; (3) underspecification, where we silently fill gaps with normal-world defaults, so the intuition is partly about our filler rather than the stipulated case; (4) question substitution, where the verdict answers an easier nearby question than the one the argument needs answered; and (5) unacknowledged impossibility, where the case could not obtain as described, and our intuition is corrupted by responding to an incoherent scenario. Is there a distinct sixth mechanism I'm missing? It should be genuinely different from these five -- not a special case of framing, filler, or incoherence, and not merely a combination of them. Please describe the mechanism precisely, give a plausible example of a well-known thought experiment it might afflict, and say how it relates to the five listed (including whether you think it reduces to one of them after all). At the end, state how confident you are that this is a real, independent mechanism rather than a relabeling, and identify the single point in your answer you are least sure of.
