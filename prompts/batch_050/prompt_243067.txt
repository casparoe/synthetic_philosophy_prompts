Our film society is doing a season on Cold War cinema, and I've volunteered to introduce the screening of Dr. Strangelove. Rather than give the usual talk about Kubrick's satire or the historical backdrop, I want to frame the film through an idea I've been reading about in game theory and the philosophy of rational agency: the commitment race. The rough picture is this. Where two or more agents face a conflict, it can be rational for each to try to bind itself first -- to make some future course of action irrevocable, so that the other side must choose under the shadow of a commitment it can no longer bargain with. But this generates a race: whoever commits first, credibly, and observably gains an enormous advantage, while a commitment that is unknown, reversible, or incredible is worse than useless. And there's a deeper paradox in the whole business: a commitment device works precisely by removing the committer's own future discretion, which means surrendering exactly the capacity for flexible, case-by-case judgement that we normally think of as the mark of a rational agent.

I'd like a reading of the film that takes this framework seriously and tests it against the plot in detail. The Soviet doomsday machine looks like the purest possible commitment device -- automatic, irreversible, deliberately placed beyond human recall -- and yet it fails catastrophically, and the film even has a character diagnose the failure: a deterrent that hasn't been announced deters nothing. Meanwhile General Ripper's unauthorised attack is arguably a different kind of first move in a commitment race, an attempt to force his own government's hand by presenting it with a fait accompli. Does the film show that these two strategies fail for the same underlying reason, or for different ones? I'm also interested in the asymmetry the film dramatises: commitment theory typically assumes the other party is rational enough to respond predictably to your commitments, but Ripper is not rational, and the doomsday machine cannot distinguish a genuine attack from an accident. Does the film suggest that the very conditions that make commitment devices attractive -- distrust, high stakes, time pressure -- are also the conditions under which their presuppositions collapse?

Finally, I'd like the reading to be honest about where the film resists the framework rather than being forced into it, and to end with a view about what, if anything, Dr. Strangelove contributes to the philosophical discussion that abstract game-theoretic argument doesn't already supply. My suspicion is that the comedy itself is doing work here -- that the absurdity is how Kubrick registers something about the mismatch between idealised rational agency and actual institutions staffed by actual people -- but I'd be glad to be argued out of that. This is for an introductory talk of about twenty minutes, so please aim for a reading that a general audience could follow while still being precise enough about the theory to satisfy the few philosophers in the room.
