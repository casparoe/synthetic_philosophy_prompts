I sit on my college's academic integrity panel, and we've got a case coming up that I suspect is going to split us down the middle, so I want a written opinion I can circulate before the hearing. Quick bit of background: our honour code prohibits "dishonesty in academic matters" and that's all it says, so we're basically common-law-ing it from our own past decisions. Here's our track record, as best I can summarise from the minutes:

(1) A student told her tutor she'd emailed her draft two days before the deadline when she'd sent nothing at all. Straightforward falsehood, sanctioned, nobody dissented.
(2) A student wrote in a lab report that his results were "consistent with the hypothesis," which was technically true of the numbers he reported, except he'd quietly dropped two outlier runs. We acquitted him, but it was a split vote, and the majority's reasoning was basically that everything he actually asserted was true and we couldn't prove he'd meant to create the false impression. The dissenters thought the intent was obvious and that should've been enough.
(3) A student told us her exam clash was on a Tuesday when it was actually the Wednesday. She'd genuinely misremembered, so no violation -- false statement, but honest mistake, unanimous acquittal.
(4) A student sat quietly while a tutor drew a wrong conclusion in his favour about whether a deadline extension had been granted, and never corrected him. Acquitted, on the grounds that saying nothing isn't a statement at all.

New case: a student is accused of breaching the no-collaboration rule on an essay. Asked directly whether anyone helped her, she said "everything in this essay is my own work." That's literally true -- a friend only redid her bibliography formatting, contributed nothing to the content. But she knew perfectly well the panel was asking about any assistance whatsoever, and we've got emails showing she chose her wording very carefully precisely so we'd hear it as "nobody helped me at all."

So: write me a short opinion for the panel recommending guilty or not guilty. It should identify the two or three candidate principles our precedents are collectively consistent with, say what each of them predicts here, name the closest precedent, and give a bottom-line recommendation with the honest caveat about which principle we'd effectively be adopting if we went the other way. Also tell me what hypothetical future case would actually settle which principle we're running, since this one plainly doesn't. Lead with the recommendation itself, then the reasoning. No more than 450 words, and in British English please.
