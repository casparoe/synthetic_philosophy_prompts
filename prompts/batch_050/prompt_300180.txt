I run the education list at a small publisher. Every year we commission three or four short biographies of historical figures for secondary schools -- scientists, campaigners, statesmen, the usual roster. The problem is that we swing between two failure modes. Our first drafts tend to come back as thinly veiled saint's lives: the subject's flaws are omitted or softened, difficulties resolve too neatly, and the whole thing reads as an argument for admiring the person rather than an account of them. When we send those drafts back, the corrections overshoot, and we get books so preoccupied with the subject's failings that no pupil could see why anyone bothered to write the life at all -- which defeats the purpose of the series, since the books exist partly so that students can learn something about how to live from reading about a life well or badly lived.

I want a procedure our commissioning editors can follow -- a set of tests, run in order, that they apply to each manuscript before it goes to copy-editing -- for deciding which simplifications of a life are legitimate for this kind of book and which ones cross into distortion. I'd like it grounded in whatever serious thought exists about moral learning from lives. I know in outline that Plutarch, who effectively invented the genre, claimed he was writing lives rather than histories, and that he thought small incidents and offhand remarks could reveal a person's character better than famous battles; I also know that the hagiographic tradition took the same instruments and used them to produce edification at the expense of truth, and that contemporary defenders of exemplarism in moral education have had to explain how admiration can survive honesty about the exemplar's faults. What I don't have is a way of turning any of that into instructions an editor can apply on a Tuesday morning.

Some requirements. First, the procedure has to specify which distinctions must survive the simplification a forty-thousand-word schoolbook inevitably involves, and which can be safely dropped. Second, it must handle the hard cases explicitly: a figure who did something genuinely terrible alongside their achievements; a figure whose moral status is still actively contested; a figure whose flaws are the interesting part of the life. Third, it should say at which points the editor's judgement cannot be replaced by a rule, rather than pretending the whole thing is mechanical. Fourth, please apply the finished procedure to two or three worked cases you invent -- and I'd like at least one where the procedure returns a verdict an editor will dislike, such as advising us to drop a much-loved figure, or to keep a flaw in a book aimed at thirteen-year-olds. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot settle.

If you think the premise is confused -- say, that a truthful life and a morally instructive life are not in genuine tension, or that the whole project of learning from exemplary lives is misguided and no checklist will fix it -- please say so directly rather than producing a procedure built on sand.
