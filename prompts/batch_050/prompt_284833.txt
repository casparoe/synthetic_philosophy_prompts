Next month I'm recording an episode of a small philosophy-of-science podcast I co-host, and I'd like help preparing. Some context on me: I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I read a fair amount on my own, and I recently fell down a rabbit hole on theories of biological function — the debate between causal-role accounts in the style of Cummins, on which a part's function is just whatever it currently contributes to some capacity of the system containing it, and etiological or selected-effects accounts in the tradition of Wright and Millikan, on which a trait's function is whatever past selection explains its being there. Our guest is a philosopher of biology who has spent her career defending the etiological view, and she's very good at deflecting hard hypothetical cases with some version of "your intuition pump is underdescribed — functions are historical kinds, so of course a snapshot with no history is indeterminate." I want a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that makes this deflection progressively harder to lean on, each question statable in two or three sentences, and each paired with the reply she's most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to that reply.

Please make sure the sequence covers the points where the literature genuinely presses: whether the very first token of a beneficial mutation already has the function it will later be selected for, and if not, when exactly the function "switches on"; a Davidson-style swampman case — an atom-for-atom duplicate of a person assembled by sheer accident — and whether its heart has the function of pumping blood, and if not, precisely what is missing; traits that persist through drift or linkage rather than selection, and whether the view is committed to denying them functions even while they do the same causal work as selected traits; and how to individuate the relevant selection history, meaning how far back it reaches and what distinguishes a genuinely new function from the continuation of an old one in exaptation-type cases. Where her predictable answer is to call the case underdescribed, the follow-up should ask her to state which additional description would settle the matter, so the deflection itself becomes the topic.

I'd also like two or three questions that bring the debate into my own territory, since I suspect our listeners will find it concrete: a function in a codebase written years ago to generate a report nobody reads anymore, which turns out to be load-bearing because a monitoring service now fails if it stops running — on her view, is it malfunctioning at its old job or properly doing a new one, and can an artifact even acquire a new function without anyone intending it? Vestigial code that is never executed but must be retained for compliance audits — does it have a function, and whose history counts? And a machine-learning module produced by an evolutionary search algorithm rather than by a designer's intention — does it have selected-effects functions in her sense, and if so, does the account end up saying something univocal about "function" across biology and engineering or does it fracture? Close the sequence with a question about what case, finding, or argument would actually count against the etiological view for her, with a follow-up ready in case she answers that no conceivable case could — namely, whether that would make the theory definitionally true rather than empirically or conceptually vulnerable. Please write everything in American English, and keep the questions pointed enough that they can't be answered by restating the view.
