I am a professor of philosophy, and each year I close my seminar on Keynes's 1921 Treatise on Probability with a creative assignment. This year I would like you to produce the assignment for me: write a short story of roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words that dramatizes two ideas from the Treatise which almost never survive the journey into popular discussion of "uncertainty."

The first idea is that not all probabilities are numerical or even comparable. We speak casually of the probability that it will rain tomorrow and the probability that a novel form of catastrophe will strike within the century, as though both could sit on a single scale, but Keynes held that many judgments of probability cannot be expressed as numbers and some cannot even be ranked against one another. The second idea is what Keynes called the weight of argument: the degree of confidence we are entitled to place in a probability judgment, depending on how much evidence underlies it. Two people may each assign a probability of one-half to some event, yet one does so on the basis of a century of observations and the other on the basis of complete ignorance, and it would be absurd to treat their positions as equivalent. Standard decision theory has no natural place for this distinction.

The story I have in mind concerns a market in which everything is priced. Imagine an exchange, perhaps set a few decades from now, where contracts are bought and sold on any contingent event whatsoever, and where the pricing of such contracts has become a prestigious profession. The plot should turn on a client who approaches the exchange with a request to insure against an event of a wholly unprecedented kind, one for which no data, no analogy, and no actuarial tradition exists. A senior underwriter must produce a number, because producing numbers is what the profession does, and the story should explore what it costs her, intellectually and morally, to do so. Consider giving her a junior colleague who cannot see the problem at all, and who insists that a probability is a probability, or an algorithmic system that generates a quotation instantly and without distress. The drama should arise from the tension between the number the institution demands and the evidential situation the underwriter actually occupies, and from her growing suspicion that the entire market rests on an unexamined convention that the future will resemble the past closely enough to be priced.

The philosophical content must be carried by the narrative itself, by plot, dialogue, and characterization, not by expository speeches or an authorial lecture. Do not name Keynes or the Treatise in the story; a reader who knows the material should recognize it, and a reader who does not should still feel the difficulty. The prose should be continuous throughout, as in a published short story, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italicized text, and no tables.
