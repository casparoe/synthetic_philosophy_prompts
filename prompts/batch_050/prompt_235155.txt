Next month I'm presenting a paper at a two-day metaphysics workshop on dispositions, and I need to be ready for the question period. The paper defends what I call the unification thesis: the standard distinction between finks and masks is not a distinction between two kinds of interference but between two temporal profiles of a single mechanism, and a suitably reformed conditional analysis — roughly the Lewis-style version, with an explicit clause requiring the object to retain its intrinsic basis for a sufficient interval after stimulation — handles both uniformly. Masks, on my view, are just finks that operate on the manifestation rather than by removing the disposition itself. My running example contrasts a live wire whose conductivity is removed by a safety device the instant current flows with a fragile glass wrapped in packing that absorbs the impact: I argue the second is the first with the intervention point moved downstream, and that once this is stated plainly, the famous counterexamples stop being two separate problems and become one problem with one fix.

The audience is about fifteen people. The two I most need to survive are a senior metaphysician who has published sympathetically on causal structuralism and dispositional essentialism, and a mid-career Lewisian who works on Humean supervenience and knows the conditional-analysis literature cold. There is also a philosopher of biology who thinks disposition-talk earns its keep only if it tracks experimentally individuated causal roles, plus several sharp graduate students. Nobody in the room shares my optimism that the conditional analysis can be saved.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the seven or eight objections I will actually get from this audience — not generic questions about quidditism, but the specific pressure these people will put on the unification thesis — ordered by how much damage each would do. The structuralist's objections will come from the possibility of ungrounded powers; the Lewisian's from the fine print of the reformed analysis (intrinsic bases, temporal intervals, whether my 'sufficient interval' clause does anything more than rename the problem); the philosopher of biology will push cases where the masking mechanism is the ordinary causal environment rather than a contrived device. Phrase each objection the way that person would actually phrase it, then give me what I should concede, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a note on where the reply is weakest. Flag any place where the unification thesis buys its uniformity at the price of losing the intuition that masks, unlike finks, leave the disposition in place — I suspect that is my soft spot. Finally, tell me the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and the single sentence I should have ready if someone does. Write the whole thing in American English.
