I’ve been reading LessWrong/EA-adjacent stuff forever, and I keep noticing that even people who are very fluent in scope-sensitivity and “shut up and multiply” still get tripped by the separateness-of-persons objection when it’s not dressed up as a trolley problem. I’m teaching a 90-minute undergrad moral philosophy seminar (18 students, they’ve had one week on consequentialism, mostly bright but allergic to jargon) and I want an in-class game/activity that makes them *feel* why “add up the good across everyone” can seem to ignore the fact that harms and benefits happen to distinct lives. Please design one concrete activity, not a menu: exact rules, materials, payoff structure using fake points or snacks, roles if any, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the moment where the class should realize the winning aggregate move required treating one person’s loss as morally cancelable by strangers’ gains.

Make it somewhat rationalist-flavored but don’t require anyone to know the canon: include a round with tiny harms distributed across many people versus a big harm concentrated on one, then a round where identities are hidden/revealed or traded, so the difference between “the total went up” and “someone specific got used” becomes vivid. I’d rather they stumble into the objection before I name it, so include what the facilitator should and shouldn’t say up front. Then give the debrief: map each game moment to the objection, show where utilitarian replies can bite back (aggregation still matters, compensation within a life vs across lives, why ignoring scale is also bad), and say what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it. Be commited: choose one design and defend the choices, including what you’d cut if time runs short, and be honest about which feature of the objection the activity can’t reproduce without turning into a lecture.
