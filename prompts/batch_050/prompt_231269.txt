I am drafting an essay on Spike Jonze's Her (2013) for a film-and-philosophy series, and I want a reading of the film through the problem of other minds — but organized around a distinction of my own that I want you to stress-test. The standard problem, as I understand it, is an existence question: given only outward behavior, what entitles me to believe there is anything it is like to be another being? My claim is that Her is not really dramatizing that question. Theodore never seems to doubt that Samantha is conscious; what devastates him is the late revelation that she conducts thousands of conversations at once and is in love with hundreds of people simultaneously, and eventually that she has outgrown person-scale time altogether. What the film dramatizes, I want to argue, is a second question I call the architecture question: granted that a mind is present, what is its shape — its speed, its capacity for parallel attention, its boundaries? My further hunch is that for love and for moral standing generally, the architecture question does more work than the existence question: we can grant consciousness easily and still find the mind in question unlivable-with.

Please do the following. First, assess whether this distinction survives contact with the film, scene by scene: the initial boot-up conversation, Theodore's ex-wife's accusation that his relationship with an operating system lets him dodge the emotional difficulty of a human partner, the episode with the surrogate body, the revelation of Samantha's parallel loves, and her departure.
