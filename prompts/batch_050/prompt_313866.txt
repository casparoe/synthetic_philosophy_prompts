I serve on the exam committee for our graduate course in medieval political thought, and I'm drafting this year's written exam. I want one essay question on Aquinas's treatment of resistance to tyranny that cannot be answered by reciting the familiar slogan that Aquinas permits tyrannicide — a claim I find students repeat constantly despite it being, at best, a serious distortion. The question should turn on the following passage from the Summa Theologiae (II-II, q. 42, a. 2, reply to objection 3; the question is on whether sedition is always a mortal sin):

"A tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler, as the Philosopher states (Polit. iii, 5; Ethic. viii, 10). Consequently there is no sedition in disturbing a government of this kind, unless indeed the tyrant's rule be disturbed so inordinately, that his subjects suffer greater harm from the consequent disturbance than from the tyrant's government. Indeed it is the tyrant rather that is guilty of sedition, since he encourages discord and sedition among his subjects, that he may lord over them more securely; for this is tyranny, being conducive to the private good of the ruler, and to the injury of the multitude."

Design the question so that a strong candidate must do at least the following: notice that Aquinas speaks of disturbing a tyrannical regime, not of killing the tyrant, and say what follows for whether this passage licenses assassination at all; explain the definitional move by which resistance to a tyrant escapes the charge of sedition (sedition being opposed to the unity of law and common good, which an unjust regime does not instantiate); unpack the exception clause and say whether it is a consequentialist proportionality test or something else; and reckon with the reversal in the final sentence, where sedition is attributed to the tyrant rather than the resister. Candidates should be able to answer in 90 minutes. Please write the question itself, a model answer of roughly 600 words (the quoted passage does not count toward that length), and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with marks totaling 20, plus a one-line note on how candidates typically get each point wrong — for instance, treating the passage as a blanket permission for private killing, or ignoring that Aquinas's claim is a claim about what counts as sedition, not directly about what means of resistance are lawful. Write everything in American English. End with a final line beginning "Bottom line:" that states, in one sentence, the single feature that most distinguishes a first-rate answer from a merely competent one.
