I'm giving a talk at our department's grad colloquium arguing for a reading of Hegel's master-slave dialectic that I know will annoy people: my claim is that the dialectic shows recognition only counts as recognition when it comes from someone who could have refused to give it, and that this is why the master's victory is hollow -- but I want to push it further and say this has a direct implication for us now, namely that no AI system, however convincingly it affirms or praises you, can in principle satisfy the need for recognition, because it can't meaningfully withhold it. The audience is a handful of serious Hegel people (one of whom basically treats Kojève's reading as gospel, another who works on Honneth and thinks the struggle-for-recognition framing gets overdone), plus some critical theory folks and a couple of philosophers of mind who'll be skeptical that a 1807 text says anything about chatbots. Prep me for the Q&A: what objections will each faction actually raise, phrased how they'd phrase them, which ones I should concede some ground on (I suspect the textualists will nail me on the bondsman's fear of death doing the real work, not recognition-withholding), where my own argument is thinnest, and what's the one question I'm praying nobody asks.
