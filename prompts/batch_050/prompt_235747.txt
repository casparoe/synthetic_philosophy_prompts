Okay so I write novels for a living, which means I haven't had a "job" in the normal sense for fifteen years, and I keep watching my friends in publishing and tech go through these four-day week pilots with this weird mix of hope and suspicion, and it's got me thinking about Keynes.

Specifically his 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren." Early in it he writes:

My purpose in this essay, however, is not to examine the present or the near future, but to disembarrass myself of short views and take wings into the future. What can we reasonably expect the level of our economic life to be a hundred years hence? What are the economic possibilities for our grandchildren?

So here's the thing that gets me. A hundred years from 1930 is 2030. We are basically AT the destination he was taking wings toward. And his famous guess was that by now the economic problem would be more or less solved and we'd all be working something like fifteen hours a week, maybe spreading what little work remained thin across everyone so nobody felt useless. What actually happened is closer to the opposite: we got the growth he predicted (or more of it), and hours barely moved, and then, in just the last decade, we got the Iceland public-sector trials (2015-2019, roughly 2,500 workers, hours cut to 35-36 with no pay cut, productivity holding or improving) and the big UK pilot in 2022 (sixty-odd companies, most of whom kept the four-day week after it ended). These are, as far as I can tell, the first real-world tests of his actual prediction, run almost exactly at his deadline.

Do the analysis for me, in both directions. First: what would Keynes's own framework predict about those trials, and did it come true? He thought productivity gains would eventually be taken as leisure rather than stuff; the trials forced exactly that trade artificially, at constant pay. Does that make them a vindication of him, or a cheat, since his mechanism was supposed to be people freely choosing leisure once their needs were met, not employers restructuring the week? Second, the harder direction: he has that distinction between absolute needs and relative needs, where the relative ones (feeling better off than the next guy) might be insatiable, and he worries there's an old Adam in most of us that needs work to feel human. A hundred years of hours NOT falling looks like evidence for the insatiable-relative-needs reading. But then the trial participants reportedly didn't fall apart with their extra day -- they slept, did laundry, saw their kids, and mostly refused to go back. So which Keynes won? Was he right about the destination and wrong about the road, or do the trials show his whole psychological worry (the dread of leisure, the people who can't enjoy themselves) was a posh Bloomsbury prejudice that ordinary workers never shared? And if the relative-needs point is the real explanation for why we're not at fifteen hours, does the four-day week movement even address it, or is it just tinkering at the edges of the thing that actually keeps us at our desks?

I'm not an economist so keep the jargon light, but don't sand it down either. And I'd love a straight answer at the end: is 2030 Keynes going to look prophetic, or is this his "teh market can stay irrational" moment in reverse?
