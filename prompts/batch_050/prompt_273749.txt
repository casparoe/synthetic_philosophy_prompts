I'm a second-year PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and I've gotten myself into an argument with my cohort that I'd like an outside referee on. It started when someone in our reading group gave the standard Galileo spiel -- heroic empiricist, telescope, backward Church, truth wins eventually -- and I pushed back that this is textbook Whig history in Butterfield's sense: the past narrated as progress toward us, with everyone sorted into people who anticipated the present and people who blocked it. Fine, everyone nodded. But then I claimed something stronger and we got stuck: I said the usual anti-Whig corrective to the Galileo story is *itself* a kind of Whig history, just with the polarity flipped. Think about how the corrective goes: Galileo had no proof of heliocentrism (no stellar parallax, and his tidal argument is just wrong), Bellarmine's demand for demonstration was epistemically reasonable, the Church funded astronomy, Tycho's model was a live option. Sure. But notice *why* those points land with us now: it's because we care about underdetermination, theory-ladenness, and fallibilism, so we reach back and retroactively enlist Bellarmine as a fellow fallibilist and Galileo as a dogmatist. That's still sorting the past by its resemblance to the present, just grading different people as the winners.

So here's what I want. First, take the standard heroic telling of the Galileo affair and do a disciplined inventory of its Whig moves -- the compressions, the omissions, the anachronistic standards it applies -- being careful not to overcorrect (some things the heroic telling says are just true, and I don't want a reflexive both-sidesism). Second, evaluate my reverse-Whig claim: is the anti-Whig corrective genuinely guilty of the same sin, or is there an asymmetry -- e.g., maybe judging past actors by their own epistemic situation is methodologically different from judging them by ours even when the *interest* in their situation is motivated by present concerns, so intent and verdict come apart somehow? Third, the question my advisor hit me with when I ran this by him: is any selection of historical material non-Whig, given that you have to select from somewhere and 'somewhere' is always the present? If some degree of present-orientation is unavoidable, what distinguishes legitimate present-oriented history from the Whig sin -- is it about whether the narrative is teleological, whether it moralizes, whether it suppresses the losing side's reasons, or what? Seperately, if you think my reverse-Whig point is old hat and I'm rediscovering something obvious, tell me that too.
