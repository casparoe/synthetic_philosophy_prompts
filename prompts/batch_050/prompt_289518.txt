So last week my girlfriend (she's from Malaysia, been here four years) got into it with my uncle at a family dinner. He said something about how immigration is fine as long as people "accept Western values," and she just asked him, totally politely, which values those are. And he kind of floundered. Freedom, democracy, equality... and she pointed out that Malaysia has elections, her dad is way more into free speech than half our family, and also like, ancient Greece had slavery and women couldn't vote until recently, so were those not "the West"? It got awkward, everyone changed the subject, and I've been chewing on it since because honestly I couldn't have answered her either.

Write me a dialogue between two smart, good-faith people arguing this out. One of them thinks "Western values" names a real, coherent thing -- maybe not a precise thing, but real the way "Scandinavian design" or "the Mediterranean diet" is real, a cluster with blurry edges but actual content and actual history. The other thinks the phrase is basically a rhetorical club: it just means "whatever my side currently believes," it's applied totally inconsistently, and the historical story behind it is a retrofit (Aristotle gets drafted as a founding father of liberal democracy when it suits people, the Church gets credited with human dignity and blamed for theocracy in the same breath).

Some requirements so it doesn't turn into the usual sludge:

- Neither of them is an idiot or a jerk. The defender of the category should NOT be my uncle -- no dog-whistle stuff, give them the strongest version. The critic shouldn't be a smug relativist either; they can still think liberal democracy is good, just that calling it "Western" does no work.
- At some point they should both engage with this test one of them proposes, call it the transplant test: if a value can be fully adopted by a society nobody calls Western, without anyone saying that society has thereby "become Western," does that show the value was never really Western, or just that it spread? Have them actually fight about what that test proves, because I genuinely can't tell.
- The defender has to deal with the history problem honestly: slavery, colonialism, and denying women the vote are as much a part of the actual Western record as rights and parliaments are. The critic has to deal with the fact that we DO seem to successfully identify a thing when people say it -- nobody is confused about which set of countries is meant, roughly.
- Have them each concede at least one real point. And let the conversation shift their positions a little by the end -- not into agreement necessarily, but they should end up somewhere more precise than where they started, because that's what actually happened at my dinner table afterwards at 1am and it was way more interesting than the dinner itself.
