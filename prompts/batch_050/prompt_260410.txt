I've recently joined the board of a small land trust, and what I thought would be straightforward conservation work has become philosophically fraught. Several of us got into an argument over whether acquiring and preserving a wetland actually improves or worsens total wild animal welfare, given that the site supports enormous numbers of small, short-lived animals, most of whom die as juveniles. One board member argued that preserving habitat just manufactures more lives full of starvation and predation; another countered that you can't reason about populations this way at all. I realized I don't even know what I'd want from a framework that could settle this -- so before I read any more advocacy in either direction, I want the specification first.

Write the desiderata that any adequate method for estimating the net welfare consequences of habitat preservation or destruction would have to meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it: for instance, it presumably has to handle populations dominated by r-selected species where the overwhelming majority of offspring die before maturity, rather than quietly extrapolating from the welfare of surviving adults; it has to say something principled about seasonal and stochastic population bottlenecks, where average abundance conceals enormous variation in how animals actually die; it has to reckon with trophic structure, since removing a habitat patch doesn't subtract welfare uniformly across the food web; and it can't simply assume that the counterfactual to preservation is empty land, since habitat is usually converted to something else that also supports animals. I'd like to know which of these are genuine requirements versus artifacts of one camp's preferred conclusion -- for example, whether the demand that the method yield a single welfare number is itself a substantive commitment rather than a neutral convenience.

Then do two further things. First, flag any pairs of desiderata you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable -- my guess is that handling juvenile mortality honestly and producing decision-relevant estimates from data that field ecology can actually supply will turn out to be in tension, but I want your view on where the real collisions are. Second, briefly score the main existing approaches against the list: hedonic-balance estimates based on life-history traits, QALY-style adaptations from human health metrics, and approaches that refuse to aggregate and instead report welfare distributions. Keep the scoring subordinate to the criteria themselves; what I'll bring back to the board is the requirements list, not a verdict.
