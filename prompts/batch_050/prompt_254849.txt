A colleague recently pressed the following argument on me. Its conclusion strikes me as a reductio rather than a program, yet each premise seems defensible on its own, and I have been unable to say where the reasoning fails. The background project is the proposal, sometimes called the hedonistic imperative, that biotechnology should be used to abolish the biological substrates of suffering.

(1) Suffering is bad in itself, and its badness does not vary with whose suffering it is: the suffering of a human, a wild animal, a future person, or an artificial mind counts equally, intensity and duration held fixed.
(2) How good or bad an outcome is, all things considered, is exhausted by the quality of the experiences it contains; nothing that makes no difference to anyone's experience can make one outcome better or worse than another.
(3) Within a few generations, genetic engineering, neural intervention, and ecological management will make it technically feasible to eliminate involuntary suffering in all sentient life and to sustain levels of well-being far above anything natural selection has produced.
(4) If one feasible outcome is better than another, and achieving it requires nothing of value to be sacrificed, then we ought to achieve it.
(5) The engineered outcome — a biosphere, and eventually a civilization, in which suffering has been abolished and well-being is pushed to its sustainable maximum — sacrifices nothing of value, since every loss it involves, from the disappearance of predation and wilderness to the erosion of the struggles out of which art and character grow, is either itself a deterioration in someone's experience or matters only insofar as it affects experience.
(6) Therefore we ought to pursue this outcome: the phased re-engineering of sentient life, human and animal, culminating in the conversion of as much matter as possible into states of maximal well-being.

What I cannot accept is the endpoint. I do not believe that a civilization which declined to convert itself and the accessible universe into maximal-pleasure states would thereby be doing something monstrous, and I balk at the idea that this is the central moral imperative of our time. At the same time, I am not willing to give up (1), and I find (3) plausible; more importantly, I want to retain the argument's intermediate implication that wild-animal suffering is a genuine and enormous problem on which we may someday be obliged to intervene. If forced to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (3) than accept (6), but I want to know what I am paying for that preference.

Please tell me which premise or step I should reject, why it looked acceptable in the first place, and what rejecting it costs. Address specifically whether the trouble lies in (2), in (4), or in (5); whether (6) actually follows from the premises as stated or quietly requires an additional commitment about aggregation that the argument never defends; and what becomes of the abolitionist project — the elimination of suffering as such — if the maximization project is severed from it. If you believe more than one step is doing illicit work, rank the candidate fault lines and say which is cheapest to repair and which is fatal.
