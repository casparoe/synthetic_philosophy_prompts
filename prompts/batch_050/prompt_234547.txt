I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm writing a long piece on whether the formal machinery of rational choice theory can accommodate the texture of ordinary life -- habits, temptations, gradual change. While reporting, a philosopher I interviewed walked me through a well-known thought experiment, usually credited to Warren Quinn, and then used it to build an argument whose conclusion strikes me as absurd, though I cannot locate the error. I'd like a diagnosis before I describe the case in print.

The case: imagine a device fitted permanently to a person's arm, with settings 0 through 1000. The device can only be turned up, never down. At each setting it delivers a mild electric stimulus. Moving up one setting earns the person $10,000, deposited immediately and irrevocably. The stimulus increases with each setting, but the difference between any two adjacent settings is too small to be felt; yet at setting 0 the person feels nothing at all, while at setting 1000 he is in constant agony. Stipulate that, by the person's own lights, no amount of money compensates for life at 1000: he would gladly surrender everything he earned, and much more, to be back at 0.

Here is the argument my interlocutor built from the case:

(1) A choice is rational when, by the chooser's own lights, its benefit outweighs its cost.
(2) At every setting n, moving to n+1 brings $10,000 at the cost of a pain increase too small to be felt; since a cost one cannot feel is, for practical purposes, no cost at all, moving up is rational at every step.
(3) A sequence composed entirely of rational choices is itself rational; rationality, unlike strength or solvency, does not leak away through iteration.
(4) Therefore the sequence of moves from 0 to 1000 is rational.
(5) Yet the endpoint of that sequence is, by the agent's own lights, foreseeably worse than his starting point, and the whole sequence was avoidable at every stage.
(6) No sequence of choices can be rational if it foreseeably and avoidably leaves the chooser worse off, by his own lights, than simply standing still.
(7) Since (4) and the conjunction of (5) with (6) cannot both hold, at least one of (1), (2), (3), or (6) must be rejected.
(8) If (2) goes, then trading an imperceptible cost for a substantial benefit can be irrational -- and since any everyday trade-off (a slightly longer commute for a higher salary, marginally richer food for marginally more money) can be embedded in a long enough chain of just such steps, ordinary economic rationality collapses with it.
(9) If (3) goes, then the rationality of a policy is never secured by the rationality of its individual steps, which would undermine the justification of every long-term project -- saving, dieting, decarbonization -- that commends itself to us one decision at a time.
(10) If (6) goes, then rationality is compatible with calmly and foreseeably ruining oneself, which drains the notion of the very point that made it worth theorizing about.
(11) Each disjunct is intolerable; hence the case shows that our ordinary concept of rational choice is incoherent, and the decision theorists I have been interviewing are formalizing a fiction.

My interlocutor added, for good measure, that the case also proves that a rational agent's preferences must be intransitive -- preferring each higher setting to the one before while preferring 0 to 1000 -- and that such agents can be systematically exploited through repeated trades, so that rationality turns out to be a liability by design.

I would very much like to keep the rationality of ordinary trade-offs, and I would like to keep something in the vicinity of (6). But I cannot defend a conclusion I find absurd. Which step of this argument should I reject, and why does the rejected step look so plausible on first inspection? What does rejecting it cost -- and does the auxiliary claim about intransitivity and exploitability survive the diagnosis, or does it fall with the main argument?
