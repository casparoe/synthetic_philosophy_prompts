hey so i'm a second-year PhD student in epistemology and my dissertation chapter is on the dogmatism paradox -- roughly, the argument that if you know p, then you're in a position to know that any future evidence against p is misleading, and so you're apparently rational to just disregard that evidence before it even arrives. i'm writing a workshop paper and i need a thought experiment built to some fairly tight specs, and i figured i'd just describe exactly what i need rather than fight with my own drafts.

The job of the case: i want the three main escape routes from the paradox to give *different practical verdicts* about what the agent should do, at different times. Specifically:

1. the deny-closure route: the agent knows p, but it doesn't follow that they know the incoming evidence is misleading
2. the knowledge-loss route: the agent knows p now, but once the misleading evidence actually shows up, their knowledge is destroyed -- so ignoring it *after* it arrives is irrational even if dismissing it in advance was fine
3. the bite-the-bullet route: the agent can rationally disregard the counterevidence, full stop, before and after

So: build me a realistic scenario from ordinary professional life (a pharmacist, a building inspector, a school admissions officer, an accountant -- your pick, just not a detective and not a scientist running an experiment, those are overused). Hard constraints: no fake barns, no lotteries, no evil demons, no perfect predictors, no brain injuries. The scenario must have:

- an agent who plausibly knows p at the outset via a totally mundane method
- misleading evidence against p that hasn't arrived yet but is *predictably on its way* -- this is the crucial bit, the agent has to know in advance that misleading counterevidence is coming, because without that the closure route and the knowledge-loss route tend to collapse into the same advice and the case goes mushy
- a concrete decision the agent faces twice: once before the evidence arrives and once after, so the three routes issue in visibly different actions rather than different inner monologues

Then do the demonstration: state p, state the method, state what the misleading evidence is and why it's misleading despite being genuinely evidence-against-p, and walk through what each of the three routes says at each decision point and why. After that, tell me which feature of the case is doing the real work in pulling the three apart, and what happens if i strip that feature out -- does the case collapse back into the boring version where everyone agrees what to do?

The paper's thesis is that the paradox is really about the temporal structure of evidence rather than about anything weird in the concept of knowledge, so the case should make *that* vivid. If your construction ends up leaning on some quirk of the justification source instead, tell me and we'll call it a fail.

Finally, give me the strongest attack a defender of the bite-the-bullet response would make against the case -- the thing they'd say in Q&A to argue it doesn't actually separate the positions -- so i can preempt it.
