Bit of an odd one for me, so bear with. I write crime novels for a living, which normally involves googling things like how long rigor mortis takes, but I've somehow landed a seat on a citizens' panel that's meant to advise our parliament on defence policy. We're a mid-sized country, not in any military alliance, and the live question is whether to keep our system of near-universal conscription — everyone (well, currently just the men, though that's part of the argument) does about a year of service and then stays in the reserves with refresher training — or replace it with a smaller, fully professional army like most of the countries around us have done. The panel has heard from generals, economists, a pacifist priest, and one extremely long-winded historian, and now each of us has to put our own recommendation in writing before the final vote. Mine is due Friday and I keep flip-flopping, which as a novelist I find embarassing because I'd normally have the plot sorted by now.

The professional-army side of the panel makes points I struggle to wave away: modern warfare runs on technical skill you can't build in a year's stint, conscription is a massive infringement of personal liberty that falls almost entirely on young men while everyone else gets the protection for free, and the reserve system is a paper tiger since half the reservists couldn't find their kit, let alone fight. The keep-conscription side argues back that an army drawn from every layer of society is a safeguard in itself — a professional force can drift into becoming its own caste, loyal to itself rather than to the public — that a credible territorial defence is exactly what lets a small country stay out of other people's wars, and that the shared stint is one of the last institutions where the plumber's son and the banker's daughter actually meet. I notice both sides talk past each other a lot: one counts costs and liberty-violations, the other talks about the kind of society the army reflects and produces.

Could you draft my position memo? Roughly a page, written so the other panellists (non-specialists, but not stupid) can act on it. It should land on a clear recommendation — keep conscription, go professional, or some genuine hybrid rather than a mushy compromise — with the reasons laid out, and it has to take the strongest argument of the losing side seriously rather than setting up a strawman; the chair has already warned us she'll send back anything one-sided. If it helps, I'm most torn on the fairness point: I honestly can't decide whether spreading the burden across everyone is more fair or just more equal in its unfairness. Please write it in British English.
