ok so my roommate (physics major, too much philosophy podcast) hit me with this over dinner and it's been bugging me for days. his argument:

(1) Evidence confirms a theory only when the theory, given the evidence, does better than its rivals at accounting for that evidence -- that's just what confirmation means.
(2) For any theory T and any body of evidence E, you can always construct a rival T' that fits E exactly as well: bolt on an epicycle, add an undetectable entity that arranges for the data, or just take "everything T says, plus the universe behaves differently starting next Tuesday."
(3) Since T' exists and fits E equally well, E cannot favor T over T'.
(4) But T was arbitrary, so no evidence ever favors any theory over every rival. All theories consistent with the data are equally well confirmed, always.
(5) It's irrational to believe a theory when rivals are equally confirmed -- that would be picking favorites for no reason.
(6) So it's never rational to believe any scientific theory, including the ones we use to build bridges.

He says this is just underdetermination taken seriously and that scientists only *think* they have evidence because they refuse to consider the weird rivals. The conclusion is obviously insane, and every premise looks like something a reasonable person could sign up for, so something has to give. Where does it break? I want to know exactly which step fails and why it *looked* fine, because each one has a good sales pitch. I suspect the trouble is somewhere between (2) and (3) -- like maybe "fits the evidence equally well" is doing two different jobs -- but I also worry that (5) sneaks in something about belief that the first four steps never earned. If it turns out the argument is actually valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to drop and what I lose by dropping it. I'd rather give up (5) than (1) if I have to choose, but price out the options honestly, including what a realist about science has to say about the "next Tuesday" rivals.
