So I'm in this philosophy reading group and we've been working through Locke's memory criterion of personal identity and the usual battery of objections, especially Reid's brave officer case -- the young officer remembers being the flogged boy, the old general remembers being the young officer, but the general no longer remembers the flogging, so Locke's criterion seems to say the general is the boy and also isn't, since identity is transitive and memory apparently isn't. We spent a whole session on the standard patch, where you swap direct memory for an overlapping chain of memory connections, and half the group thinks the patch obviously fixes Reid and half thinks it just relocates the problem. I want a thought experiment that would actually settle something at our next meeting rather than another case we just talk past each other about.

Here's the assignment: construct a case in which Locke's simple memory criterion and the overlapping-chain view deliver *different* verdicts about whether a particular elderly person is the same person as a particular child, and where the memory criterion's verdict is the counterintuitive one (in Reid's original case it's weirdly unclear which answer is the absurd one, and I want a case that puts real pressure on Locke in one direction). Constraints: entirely realistic -- no brain transplants, no teletransportation, no mad scientists, no fission; and please don't rest the whole thing on a single clean amnesia event like a dramatic head injury, since that feels like cheating, make the forgetting the ordinary gradual kind that actually happens to people. Keep the scenario itself to one short paragraph.

Then do the follow-through: state each theory's verdict and walk through why it gives it, identify which specific feature of your case is doing the work of prying the two theories apart, and say what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed. Crucially, tell me whether your case reinstates Reid's transitivity problem for the overlapping-chain view (just one step further down the chain) or genuinely dodges it, and why. Finally, steelman the diehard defender of the simple memory criterion: what would they say to argue that *their* verdict is actually the intuitively right one in your case, rather than the chain-theorist's? If the case can't survive that pushback, say so honestly -- I'd rather bring a case that fails in an instructive way than one that only works because nobody stress-tested it.
