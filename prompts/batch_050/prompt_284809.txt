Last month, while negotiating a long sequence of small trades with a colleague, I realized I preferred bundle A to B, B to C, and C back to A — and had paid a small fee at each swap, ending up strictly worse off than where I started. A friend told me I'd been money-pumped, and that what rationality actually demands of someone in my position is a genuinely contested question. I'm now preparing a handout for a workshop and want a decision tree that sorts a reader from the starting point "I have just discovered that some of my preferences are cyclic" to a named position on what, if anything, rationality requires in response.

Build the tree so that:

- Each branch node is a concrete question the reader can answer without knowing any theory — for example, whether they owe anything to their future self, whether adopting a plan and later abandoning it is itself a defect, or whether the irrationality of the cycle lies in the overall pattern or only ever in individual choices.
- The leaves must include at least: revising one's preferences to restore transitivity; resolute choice (rationally binding oneself to a plan and sticking to it even when a later option looks better); sophisticated choice (reasoning backward about one's future self as if it were another player, and only adopting plans one will actually complete); the view that rationality has only synchronic requirements, so being pumped is not irrational provided each choice is locally sensible; and the view that cyclic preferences can themselves be permissible, with the real mistake being agreeing to keep trading.
- Use at least two concrete test cases as branch diagnostics beyond the standard three-option cycle — for example, a sequence of imperceptible increments in which each step is individually harmless but the sequence is ruinous.
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, and note any combination of answers that appears unoccupied, saying briefly whether that position seems incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored.

Structure your answer in these sections, in this order: "The tree" (the bulk, roughly 400–600 words, with each node phrased as a first-person question), "Leaf guide" (one or two sentences per leaf: the position's name and its most awkward commitment), "Diagnostic cases" (a short description of each case used and why it discriminates), and "Gaps and inseparables" (about 150 words on unoccupied branches and leaves your tree cannot distinguish).
