Our department's policy group — applied economists and analysts fluent in social welfare functions, cost-benefit analysis, and distributional weighting, but with no training in normative ethics — has asked me to take one ninety-minute session of its internal seminar. The topic is the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarian aggregation: the criticism, canonically associated with Rawls's A Theory of Justice, that adding welfare across people ignores the morally significant fact that gains and losses fall to distinct individuals. I do not want the lecture written for me; I want a plan for how to teach it, ninety minutes, whiteboard only. The plan should cover the following.

First, order and dependencies. What must be in place before the objection can even be stated, and in what sequence. My working hypothesis is that the prudential analogy has to come first — the familiar point that tradeoffs within a single life, present pain for future health, are justified because costs and benefits fall to the same subject — since the objection is precisely that this justification breaks at the boundary between persons. Say whether that ordering is right, what it depends on, and where plain interpersonal aggregation, welfarism, and maximization each have to be introduced relative to it.

Second, the carrying example. Specify the one case the session should be built around by giving the criteria it must meet, not a list of options. It must isolate the aggregation move from three confounders that stock examples always drag in: inequality as such (the objection is not egalitarianism), rights violations (it is not a side-constraint complaint), and the numbers problem (it is not about whether to save the many or the few). Should the case hold total welfare fixed across two distributions, or vary it in a controlled way? Diagnose the failure modes of the obvious candidates, in particular the case in which one person is badly harmed to secure small benefits for many others, which audiences reliably hear as being about torture or majority tyranny rather than aggregation.

Third, the misconception map, calibrated to this audience. I expect at least five: that the objection is merely a preference for a different social welfare functional — concave, weighted, maximin — rather than a complaint about aggregation itself; that it is really about the impossibility of interpersonal welfare comparisons; that it is the demandingness or integrity objection under another name; that prioritarianism straightforwardly answers it; and that the ubiquity and unavoidability of cross-person tradeoffs in actual policy dissolves it. For each: where in the sequence it will surface, how to defuse it there, and which is hardest to defuse without the session being captured. Relatedly, say when to permit the audience to formalize (my guess: only after the objection is stated, to show that reshaping the functional does not touch it) and when to refuse.

Fourth, the check for understanding. Two or three things participants should be able to do by the end: state exactly which structural feature of classical utilitarianism the objection targets and which features it leaves untouched; distinguish it from its two nearest neighbors on demand; and reconstruct the strongest utilitarian reply — denying the disanalogy, or denying that separateness is as morally deep as it appears — well enough to say what would count for or against it.

Fifth, what to leave out, and say so: population ethics, whether Rawls's own view escapes the objection or merely relocates it, arguments from the reduction of personal identity, the non-identity problem. For each, give a one-sentence bridge for the participant who asks.

Throughout, flag where the standard textbook presentation of the objection oversimplifies, and separate what is settled among people who work on this — that the objection targets cross-person aggregation and its prudential justification, and that its canonical statement is Rawls's — from what is genuinely contested: whether it generalizes against all consequentialist aggregation or only sum-ranking, whether prioritarianism escapes it, whether deontological and contractualist views face a structurally parallel problem, and what it implies, if anything, for the legitimacy of cost-benefit practice. Do not present a contested resolution as the consensus.
