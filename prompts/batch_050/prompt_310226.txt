I'm writing the final exam for my second-year course on early modern political thought and I want one essay question on the justice-and-force fragments in Pascal's Pensées. Here's my problem: every year half the class writes "Pascal thinks might makes right, same as Thrasymachus" and calls it a day, which flattens him completely. I want a question that can't be answered that way.

The question should push students on the bits of Pascal's view that don't fit that reading:

- the asymmetry he insists on -- justice without force is powerless, but force without justice is tyrannical, and since we can't make what is just strong, we've made what is strong just (so the direction of his pessimism is the opposite of the Thrasymachus reading)
- his point that custom does the real legitimating work: laws seem just mostly because they've been around and people have grown up obeying them, which is also why he thinks would-be reformers who want to tear it all down and rebuild from reason are making a mistake
- his observation about magistrates in red robes and ermine, doctors with their titles, etc. -- that force needs costumes and ceremony because people are governed by imagination as much as by reason
- and the wierd consequence for his own position: if he's right, can a ruler ever be justified in just *announcing* that the laws rest on force?

Feel free to build a small hypothetical into the question to make it concrete, e.g. a state that abolishes all ceremony and openly grounds its laws in enforcement capacity alone -- would Pascal predict it's more stable or less? Then give me:

1. the exam question itself, phrased as it would appear on the paper, answerable in about 60 minutes
2. a model answer of around 500-600 words
3. a marking scheme with five must-hit points, marks for each (total 20), and for each point one line on the typical way students botch it

Level is second-year undergrads who've done one lecture plus a seminar on the Pensées, so don't assume they've read the secondary lit.
