I am a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and I have agreed to lead a ninety-minute session of our department's metaphysics reading group on the question of what is real in a gauge theory. The participants are fluent in analytic metaphysics but have essentially no physics: they remember what a phase is from waves at school, and none of them has ever worked with Maxwell's equations. The aim is not to teach them gauge theory but to get them to see the philosophical problem in its sharpest form: that our best physical theories describe the world with more mathematical structure than the world seems to contain, that the gap between the mathematics and the reality is what physicists call gauge freedom, and that deciding which parts of the formalism represent something real turns out to be forced on us by experiment rather than being a matter of interpretive taste.

Please plan the session rather than deliver it. Specifically:

Order and dependencies. In what sequence should the pieces be introduced so that the problem feels forced rather than stipulated? My current plan is to begin with a low-tech case where a mathematical freedom clearly answers to nothing physical (shifting the zero of potential energy in Newtonian mechanics), then introduce electromagnetism through its potentials and the freedom they bring, and only then produce the case where the surplus apparently acquires empirical content. Tell me whether that ordering is right, what each step must establish before the next one can land, and at what point the general notion of surplus structure should be named explicitly rather than left implicit.

The carrying example. I intend to build the whole session on the Aharonov–Bohm effect: a shift in an interference pattern produced by a magnetic field confined to a region the particles never enter, vanishing everywhere along the paths they actually take. Say what this example must have in my presentation to carry the weight — it needs to make "just eliminate the surplus" look costly, and it needs to make "the potential is simply real" look costly too — what it must lack to stay usable for this audience, and whether there is a better carrier I am overlooking.

The misconception map. List the misreadings this audience will predictably form, in the order they will form them. I anticipate at least: that gauge symmetry is a symmetry of nature on a par with rotation; that whatever changes under a gauge transformation is thereby unreal (when the real structure may live in gauge-invariant relations rather than pointwise quantities); that the Aharonov–Bohm effect straightforwardly proves the vector potential real; that gauge freedom is the same thing as freedom of coordinates in general relativity; and that fixing a gauge amounts to discovering the theory's true variables. For each, say where in the sequence it will arise, why it will be tempting to this particular audience, and how the session's ordering should head it off or deliberately exploit it.

The check. Give me one question to put to the room at the end whose answers would show that the idea has landed — that they can say which quantities are gauge-dependent, why the theory nonetheless forces us to take the surrounding structure seriously, and what the live interpretive options are — rather than that they have merely learned to say "gauge" with a serious expression.

What to refuse. Tell me what I should openly decline to explain at this level, and say so during the session. I am assuming the constrained Hamiltonian formalism, non-abelian gauge theory, and the hole argument are all out, and that when someone asks about the Higgs mechanism "breaking" gauge symmetry I should defer rather than improvise. Correct me if any of these is more load-bearing than I think.
