I teach an upper-division philosophy of science seminar and every year I do one class where we watch Minority Report and talk about whether the pre-crime program is even coherent as a concept. My students always get stuck in the same place: they think the movie is about determinism vs. free will, and I want to push them toward what I think is the more interesting reading, which is that it's about what kind of thing a probability can be. So here's what I'd like you to do. Read the film through the debate between frequentist, subjective Bayesian, and propensity interpretations of probability. The core tension as I see it: pre-crime assigns probabilities to single, unrepeatable events -- this particular man murdering this particular person on this particular morning -- and frequentism famously struggles to say what a single-case probability even is. Does the film smuggle in an illicit frequentism by treating previsions as if they were readouts from a long run that doesn't exist? Or is the pre-cog system better understood as assigning propensities -- real dispositions in the world, lodged in Anderton's situation -- that can be high without the outcome being fixed?

Then do the harder part, which is where I think the movie actually earns its keep philosophically. There's the business of the minority report itself: one of the three precogs sometimes disagrees, and the system conceals this because unanimity is what makes the probabilities feel objective. Read that against the subjectivist picture, where probabilities are credences and disagreement between well-informed agents is exactly what you'd expect -- but note that the film presents the dissent as a hidden fact about the future, not as one more opinion. And the climax, where Anderton sees his own predicted murder and then chooses otherwise: the film seems to want this both ways, treating the prevision as accurate (it correctly showed what he was heading toward) and as avoidable. Agatha keeps showing him his future while insisting he can still choose differently. Which interpretation of probability best makes sense of that scene without collapsing into either fatalism (the probability was really 1, so the choice is illusory) or a cheat (the probability was never real to begin with)? Be honest about where the film resists the reading -- I don't want it flattened into an illustration for a lecture slide, and I suspect Spielberg is looser about this than the philosophers are. End by telling me what, if anything, the movie dramatizes about single-case probability that the standard debate between the three schools tends to miss, because that's the part I want my students to write their papers on. Oh and no bibliography please, I just want the argument.
