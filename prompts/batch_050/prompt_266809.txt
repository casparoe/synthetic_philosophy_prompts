I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and part of my job is line-editing other writers' drafts. Our section has a recurring problem I want to solve systematically: our reporting is descriptive (studies, measurements, mechanisms), but our sentences keep sliding into the prescriptive — "this finding means parents should…", "policymakers must now…", "there is no excuse for continuing to…" — and when readers or subjects complain, the writer's defense is always that the conclusion follows from the facts. Sometimes it nearly does; often it plainly doesn't; and I have no principled way to tell the difference at the speed my job requires. I make this call on perhaps a dozen sentences a week, under deadline, and my rulings need to be defensible to writers who know more about their topic than I do.

What I want is a reusable procedure, grounded in the philosophical literature on the is-ought gap and the naturalistic fallacy, that an editor without philosophy training can run on a single sentence in under five minutes and produce one of three rulings: the inference is licensed as written, it is licensed only if a missing premise is made explicit, or it must be rewritten. I am aware the underlying philosophy is contested — whether any "ought" can be derived from an "is," what the naturalistic fallacy actually was as opposed to what it is popularly taken to be, whether thick evaluative concepts and institutional facts complicate the picture — and I want the procedure to encode the settled and usable core of that material rather than take sides in the live disputes.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

**Summary** (roughly 100 words): the rule the procedure operationalizes, stated plainly enough to quote in an editorial meeting.

**The procedure** (roughly 600–800 words): the steps in order, each phrased as an operation on the sentence under review — identify the conclusion's normative term, inventory the stated premises, test for a suppressed evaluative premise, and so on. For each step, say what output ends the review early. Distinguish explicitly between cases where the normative content hides in the premise (a word like "harm," "healthy," "normal," or "risk" doing double duty) and cases where it hides in the inference itself, because my writers conflate these constantly. State which philosophical distinctions you are deliberately dropping for usability and why the loss is acceptable.

**Worked applications** (roughly 500–700 words): run the full procedure on three sentences of the kind I actually see. First: "The trial showed the supplement improved recall in older adults, so doctors should consider recommending it." Second: "Sea level in this region will rise half a meter by 2070; the city has no choice but to relocate the port." Third: a sentence of your own construction in which the procedure forces a ruling a sympathetic science editor will dislike — a sentence that reads as responsible, careful science communication but that the procedure flags as requiring an explicit evaluative premise. For each, show the procedure's steps applied, not just the verdict, and give the rewritten sentence where the ruling calls for one.

**Where judgment cannot be eliminated** (roughly 200–300 words): the points in the procedure where two competent editors will disagree no matter how carefully the steps are specified, and what an editor should do at those points — escalate, flag for the writer, or pick a default and document it.

**What this procedure cannot do** (roughly 150–250 words): an honest statement of the failure modes — the kinds of normative smuggling it will miss, the legitimate inferences it will over-flag, and the editorial questions it simply does not address.

Keep the philosophy accurate but subordinate to usability; do not cite texts or name arguments beyond what a careful non-specialist would recognize, and do not give me a reading list.
