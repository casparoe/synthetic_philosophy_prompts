I recently picked up a well-reviewed popular book on the philosophy of psychiatry (I'd rather not name it, since I may be misremembering details), and its chapter on Ian Hacking's idea of "looping effects" has been sitting uneasily with me for weeks. The book's summary runs roughly as follows. According to Hacking, natural science studies things like quarks and blood pressure, which are entirely indifferent to how we classify them, whereas the human sciences study kinds of people, and people are aware of how they are classified and react to it. A person diagnosed with, say, bipolar disorder reads up on the condition, recognizes herself in the case studies, adjusts her behavior accordingly, and perhaps resists or resents the label; meanwhile clinicians observe the new ways patients present and revise the diagnostic criteria in response. This back-and-forth between the classification and the classified is the "looping effect," and Hacking's term for such categories is "interactive kinds." So far, the book says, so intuitive. But then it draws its big conclusion: because psychiatric categories are caught up in these feedback loops, they are not real kinds the way diseases of the body are, and conditions like multiple personality disorder or depression are best understood as socially constructed performances that exist only because the label gave people a script to follow -- which means psychiatry, unlike medicine, cannot claim to discover anything, only to invent.

Something about the last step feels too fast to me, but I can't locate the problem precisely. I gather Hacking developed this idea partly through historical work on how multiple personality diagnoses surged and faded, so the book's use of that example seems at least motivated, and the basic picture of people reacting to their classifications does sound like something he'd say. What I'm unsure about is the leap from "the classification changes the people classified" to "the condition is therefore unreal, a mere performance, invented rather than discovered." Couldn't a category be responsive to the people in it and still pick out something genuinely wrong with them? I also wonder whether the summary is right that looping requires the person to be consciously aware of the label and deliberately performing it -- it seems like there might be subtler channels -- and whether Hacking would accept the flat contrast the book draws between psychiatry, which allegedly only invents, and bodily medicine, which allegedly only discovers, given that things like obesity or blood pressure thresholds arguably involve feedback too.

Could you go through the book's summary and tell me which parts a philosopher familiar with Hacking's actual position would accept, which parts are distortions or overreach, and whether the concluding picture of psychiatric diagnosis follows from the accurate parts? If the book has turned a modest observation about feedback into a sweeping claim about unreality, I'd like to know exactly where the slide happens. And if it turns out the strong conclusion is closer to what Hacking himself argued than I suspect, I'd rather know that too.
