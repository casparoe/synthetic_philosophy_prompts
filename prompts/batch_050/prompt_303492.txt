I'm a backend engineer with about a decade of experience building distributed systems, and no formal training in philosophy. Over the past year I've been reading about split-brain research on my own -- the classic commissurotomy studies, the standard philosophical interpretations, and the recent debate about whether partial unity survives callosal section. Next month I've been invited to give a forty-minute talk to an interdisciplinary consciousness reading group at the local university, followed by thirty minutes of questions. The audience will be roughly: two philosophers of mind (one sympathetic to the view that phenomenal unity is all-or-nothing and that split-brain cases must involve either two subjects or a single consciousness that switches between hemispheres; one broadly illusionist about introspective reports), a clinical neuropsychologist who has actually tested callosotomy patients, and a handful of grad students from both departments.

My talk defends a thesis I arrived at by noticing that my own field uses the phrase "split-brain" for network partitions in distributed systems. Roughly: I argue that the unity of consciousness is best understood not as a binary property a system either has or lacks, but as an integration property that comes in degrees, analogous to consistency guarantees in a distributed system. On my picture, the intact brain maintains something like strong global consistency across its components; after callosotomy, you get two locally consistent processes that cannot synchronize globally, but which still exchange information through subcortical channels and environmental side channels -- I compare this to gossip protocols and eventual consistency. I then argue that this framework dissolves the forced choice between the "two subjects" verdict and the "one switching subject" verdict: the question "how many subjects are there?" is, I claim, like asking whether a partitioned cluster is "really" one system or two -- a question with no fact of the matter independent of which consistency property you care about. I conclude that consciousness science should stop asking the counting question and start asking which integration properties matter for which explanatory purposes (moral status, reportability, unified agency).

Help me prepare for the question period. Give me the objections I'm most likely to receive, ordered roughly by how likely they are to come up and how damaging they'd be if I handle them badly. Phrase each objection the way that specific audience member would actually phrase it -- the unity theorist will press on whether degrees-of-unity is even coherent given the phenomenology; the illusionist will ask why I trust the patients' reports at all; the neuropsychologist will likely know experimental details I've glossed over, like cross-cueing and the right hemisphere's rudimentary language. For each objection, tell me what I should concede, what I should hold, and give me a reply I could deliver in under two minutes. I also want you to stress-test the distributed-systems analogy itself: where does it mislead rather than illuminate, and am I smuggling in the assumption that integration is what makes a subject? Finally, tell me the single objection you're most confident would sink the talk if I have no answer to it, and where in my own argument I should spend my preparation time rather than polishing slides.
