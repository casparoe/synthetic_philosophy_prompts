At a party last weekend two friends of mine got into it about the Nuremberg and Tokyo trials, and it's still bugging me because I genuinely can't tell who won. Setting: we're all non-experts, we'd just watched a documentary, and both of them were making points that sounded good until the other one answered.

Sofia's opening shot was that both tribunals were victors' justice dressed up in legal robes. "Crimes against humanity" and "conspiracy to wage aggressive war" simply did not exist as crimes when the acts happened -- the London Charter was written in 1945 by the winners and then applied backwards to the losers, which violates the most basic rule of legality. You can't punish someone for breaking a law that didn't exist yet, full stop.

Daniel answered by pulling up the actual Nuremberg judgment on his phone, because apparently the tribunal had heard exactly this objection. He read us this bit from the section on the law of the Charter:

"In the first place, it is to be observed that the maxim nullum crimen sine lege is not a limitation of sovereignty, but is in general a principle of justice. To assert that it is unjust to punish those who in defiance of treaties and assurances have attacked neighbouring states without warning is obviously untrue, for in such circumstances the attacker must know that he is doing wrong, and so far from it being unjust to punish him, it would be unjust if his wrong were allowed to go unpunished."

His point was that the retroactivity objection was anticipated and answered on the merits: aggressive war was already outlawed by the Kellogg-Briand Pact, which Germany and Japan had both signed, and murder and the laws of war (Hague conventions and so on) long predated any of this. So there was no genuine legal novelty, just a new forum.

Sofia said that passage is slick but doesn't actually do the work. There's a huge gap between a treaty that "renounces war as an instrument of national policy" and individual criminal liability carrying a death sentence -- nobody in 1939, including the signatories, understood Kellogg-Briand to mean heads of state could be hanged for starting a war, and notice that the judgment's own argument quietly slides from "the attacker must know he is doing wrong" (a moral claim) to "therefore punishing him is legal" (a legal one). And even if you grant the law existed, it was only ever enforced downward: the Soviets sat in judgment on wars of aggression after invading Poland and Finland, Soviet prosecutors straight up tried to pin Katyn on the Germans at Nuremberg, nobody answered for Dresden or Hiroshima, and at Tokyo the Emperor was deliberately spared because the occupation was easier with him in place. Law that only binds the defeated isn't law, it's sentencing with extra steps.

Daniel came back with: selective enforcement doesn't void the law itself -- a speeder caught on camera can't get off by pointing at all the speeders who weren't -- and the alternative everyone forgets is that Churchill wanted to skip trials entirely and just shoot the top Nazis. Instead the defendants got counsel, a massive documentary record, and three of them were actually acquitted at Nuremberg, which is a weird outcome for a show trial. He also said the Dönitz case cuts against Sofia: when the US admitted its own submarines did the same thing, the tribunal effectively declined to punish the submarine-warfare charge, which is the opposite of ignoring tu quoque.

Sofia's last word was that three acquittals out of twenty-two and one mitigated charge don't change the structural point: the judges were appointed only by the four victorious powers, no neutral states were involved, and at Tokyo Justice Pal's dissent argued the whole enterprise was ex post facto -- so the best you can say is that the trials were a morally defensible political act, and people should stop insisting they were legal justice.

They both turned to me and I had nothing.
