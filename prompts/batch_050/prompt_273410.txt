I keep running into the slogan "you are the story you tell yourself." I first saw it as the organizing idea of a self-help book, and since then I've heard it repeated on podcasts and in conversations, always as if its meaning were obvious. In the book it was made to do several different jobs: it explained why people stay stuck in bad situations (they keep retelling themselves the same story), it justified keeping a journal and deliberately rewriting one's account of past failures, and at one point it was offered as a claim about what a person literally is. I have no training in philosophy or psychology, but those strike me as very different claims wearing the same words.

Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a single reading, the distinct precise theses this slogan might be expressing? For instance, I can see at least the outlines of: a descriptive claim that people do in fact organize their memories and self-understanding into narratives; a claim that what makes someone the same person over time just is their self-narrative; a claim that changing the story changes who you are, not merely how you feel; and a therapeutic claim that deliberately editing one's self-narrative improves one's life. There are probably others I've missed. For each candidate reading, I'd like to know: Is it nearly trivial once stated precisely, or is it substantive? If substantive, is it defensible or false? And which of the slogan's typical uses -- especially the practical advice -- does it actually support? I'm particularly curious whether the advice to "rewrite your story" requires the strong claim that the self is nothing beyond the narrative, or whether it follows from something much weaker.

Finally, please be careful to distinguish what is settled among people who study this (whether in psychology or philosophy) from what is genuinely contested. If one of the readings is a minority view or a live dispute, say so plainly rather than presenting it as the accepted picture.
