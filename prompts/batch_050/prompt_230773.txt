I'm putting together a catalogue for a reading group on explanation by symmetry in physics. The organizing question is when a symmetry genuinely explains a phenomenon rather than merely redescribing it in invariant terms. So far the catalogue has five entries:

1. Noether's first theorem: every continuous global symmetry of the action yields a conserved quantity, so time-translation invariance explains energy conservation, spatial-translation invariance explains momentum conservation, and so on. The conservation law ceases to be a brute regularity and becomes a consequence of the structure of the laws.

2. Curie's principle: an asymmetry in an effect must already be present in its causes. This explains why perfectly symmetric setups cannot produce asymmetric outcomes -- a needle balanced exactly on its tip has no preferred direction of fall, and Buridan-style equilibria cannot resolve themselves until something breaks the symmetry.

3. Gauge symmetry as a contrast case: the gauge invariance of electromagnetism is usually read as descriptive redundancy in our formalism rather than as a symmetry of nature itself, so it is taken to explain nothing. This entry marks where explanation by symmetry runs out.

4. Spontaneous symmetry breaking: the laws remain symmetric while the ground state is not, which explains how asymmetric phenomena -- a ferromagnet's magnetization picking a direction, the Higgs mechanism -- emerge from symmetric dynamics.

5. The symmetry-to-reality inference: invariance under a transformation is taken as evidence that whatever changes under it is not physically real. Galilean invariance is used to argue that absolute velocity is unreal, and one reading of Einstein's hole argument draws a similar conclusion about spacetime points.

I want a sixth entry that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or combination of anything on it. In particular, I take the list to already cover every explanation that proceeds by deriving a conservation law, a selection rule, or a classification scheme from an invariance, so "symmetry X explains conserved quantity or taxonomy Y" does not count as new. Ideally the new entry should exhibit a different explanatory structure -- symmetry explaining something without deriving a consequence of that kind -- or else put pressure on the idea that symmetries explain at all. State the entry precisely, say how it relates to the five above, and assess honestly whether it survives the standard objection that symmetries are features of our descriptions of the world rather than features of the world itself.
