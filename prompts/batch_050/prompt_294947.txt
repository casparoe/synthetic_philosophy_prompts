Last night a friend and I argued until two in the morning about Gödel's rotating universes and what, if anything, they show about the reality of time. We parted without resolution, each convinced the other had missed the decisive point. I would like to see the dispute conducted more rigorously than either of us managed, so I am asking you to stage it properly.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers, Mara and Tomas, continuing our argument. Both must be equally competent and charitable: neither is a foil for the other, and each must engage with what the other actually says rather than rehearsing a prepared position.

Mara defends Gödel's own conclusion. In 1949, Gödel produced solutions to Einstein's field equations describing a rotating universe in which every observer could, in principle, journey into their own past along closed timelike curves. Mara argues that if a physically possible world governed by the same laws as ours contains such curves, then time cannot be an objective, mind-independent feature of reality — and since those solutions obey the very laws that govern us, this conclusion infects our universe as well. Time, she holds, is ideal: something in how the world appears to creatures like us, not something in the world itself.

Tomas resists on two grounds. First, our actual universe expands and shows no sign of the required rotation, so Gödel's model is at most a curiosity about worlds we do not inhabit, and nothing follows about time here. Second, even inside a Gödel universe, events stand in perfectly definite relations — some are in others' pasts or futures, causes still precede effects locally, and no observer ever experiences a contradiction. The most Gödel's construction shows, Tomas insists, is that a single global linear ordering of all events is not guaranteed by physics, which is something special relativity had already taught us. Losing the global ordering is not losing time.

Partway through, Mara introduces the scenario that ended our own argument, and the dialogue must give it real weight: suppose future engineers build a colossal machine that spins a region of spacetime faster and faster until closed timelike curves form within it. On Mara's challenge, Tomas must say whether time becomes unreal in that region the moment the machine succeeds. If he says yes, he owes an account of how a machine can manufacture unreality. If he says no, he must explain what was lost when the machine switched on — and whether that thing, whatever it is, was ever essential to time's reality in the first place. Tomas must answer this directly rather than dismiss the machine as impossible, since its possibility is precisely what is under dispute.

The dialogue should run roughly 900 to 1300 words and should end not with agreement but at the point where the deepest remaining disagreement has been isolated and stated as sharply as possible.
