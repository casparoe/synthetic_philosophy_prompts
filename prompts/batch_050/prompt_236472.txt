As a longtime reader of Overcoming Bias and LessWrong, I keep noticing how much of the effective altruist worldview seems prefigured in Leibniz. I have in mind especially his Meditation on the Common Concept of Justice, written around 1702, where he defines justice as the charity of the wise -- caritas sapientis, universal benevolence disciplined and directed by knowledge -- and argues against Pufendorf and the voluntarists that right cannot rest on anyone's command or power, not even God's, because the norms of justice belong to the eternal truths that bind any intellect as such. So benevolence is not optional sentiment but duty, and it is only genuinely just when guided by understanding of what actually helps. Strip the theology off and that reads like the founding intuition of EA.

Here is what I want: suppose Leibniz were briefed on the modern effective altruist movement -- GiveWell-style cost-effectiveness estimates, earning to give, cause prioritization across global health, animal welfare, and existential risk, and longtermism's concern for far-future persons. What would he make of it, and would he sign up? Please do not answer with a generic old-philosophers-were-different-from-us survey. Reason from his actual commitments, and resolve the friction points rather than just listing them. The four tensions I see below are my candidate load-bearing issues; tell me whether I have identified the right ones and which way each breaks.

First, wisdom versus metrics. His conception of justice makes knowledge constitutive of right action, and he spent his life dreaming of a universal characteristic that would let disputants settle questions by sitting down to calculate. That sounds like an endorsement of QALY-style aggregation. But the good at the bottom of his system is perfection -- harmony, variety in unity, above all the flourishing of minds -- not pleasure minus pain. Does the gap between perfection and welfare sabotage any translation into cost-effectiveness terms, or does it merely change what gets measured?

Second, optimism and theodicy. He holds that God has already actualized the best of all possible worlds. Does that leave room for human deliberation to be genuinely world-improving in the EA sense, or does it collapse the whole project into quietism? Does his own reply -- that the world's being best overall is compatible with particular evils being genuinely evil and locally remediable -- do the work an effective altruist needs?

Third, impartiality and aggregation. His universal jurisprudence pushes toward equal concern for all rational beings, and his community of minds under God extends moral consideration remarkably far. But would he accept the person-neutral frame in which a stranger's child counts exactly as much as one's own, and in which not-yet-existing persons count as much as present ones? Where do his views on our duties to posterity and to the commonwealth of minds land him on longtermism and on the astronomical-waste argument?

Fourth, motivation. EA is routinely attacked for combining extreme demandingness with impersonality. Leibniz grounds moral motivation in love, which he analyzes roughly as taking pleasure in another's happiness -- a relational, particularist psychology. Can that psychology actually support impartial global scope, or does it predict that his charity of the wise will always shrink back to one's household, city, and church?

End with a verdict: on balance, is Leibniz an effective altruist avant la lettre, a sympathetic critic who would take the cause-prioritization machinery but reject the axiology, or an outright opponent? And name the single feature of his system that decides it.
