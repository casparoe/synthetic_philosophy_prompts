I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar that has spent the last few weeks on panpsychism, and a classmate circulated an argument whose final conclusion strikes me as absurdly strong, even though each individual step looks defensible. I'll reconstruct it as faithfully as I can. Take panpsychism to be the view that fundamental physical constituents have experiential or proto-experiential properties, and that consciousness like ours is constituted out of these micro-level experiences.

(1) If panpsychism is true, then my consciousness exists only in virtue of the consciousness of micro-level parts of me, so a complete explanation of my experience would have to show how it results from the combination of micro-experiences.
(2) There are only two ways a property of a whole can result from properties of its parts: by aggregation, the way the mass of an object results from the masses of its parts, or by organization, the way being a clock results from gears arranged in the right structure.
(3) Aggregation of experiences cannot yield a new, unified experience: an experience of red had by one small subject together with an experience of green had by another is just two experiences, not a single subject experiencing red-and-green. Summing subjects never produces one subject.
(4) Organization cannot yield a unified subject either: any arrangement of parts is fully describable in third-personal, structural terms, and no purely structural description entails that there is something it is like to be the arranged system. My classmate notes that this is the standard hard-problem consideration that panpsychists themselves press against physicalists.
(5) So panpsychism cannot explain our consciousness; the combination problem is fatal to it, not a mere research program.
(6) But the reasoning generalizes. Any theory on which my consciousness is constituted by properties of my brain's parts faces the same two options, aggregation or organization, and they fail for the same reasons. Consciousness therefore cannot be explained by the properties of anything's parts.
(7) So consciousness admits of no reductive explanation at all — not panpsychist, not physicalist, not any account that builds minds up from simpler components, which covers every candidate theory currently on the market.

I find (7) incredible, but I can't locate the misstep. Ideally I'd like to keep (3) through (5) — I do think the combination problem is a serious, possibly fatal objection to panpsychism — while blocking the universal conclusion. Which step should I reject, why did it look fine, and what exactly does it cost to keep the anti-panpsychist verdict without being saddled with (7)? Please go step by step rather than giving me a general verdict, and if more than one premise is doing suspicious work, say so and compare the costs of rejecting each.
