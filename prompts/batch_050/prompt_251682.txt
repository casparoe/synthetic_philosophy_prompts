I am a programming-language researcher. I am fluent in the vocabulary of type systems, denotational and operational semantics, effect systems, and compositionality, but I have essentially no training in metaethics. I have come across the expressivist view in moral philosophy — roughly, the claim that an utterance like "stealing is wrong" does not describe some feature of the world but expresses an attitude of the speaker, on analogy with "boo to stealing" — together with the standard objection known as the Frege–Geach problem, which concerns what happens to moral sentences when they are embedded in conditionals, negations, and logically valid arguments. The example usually given involves the inference from "if tormenting the cat is wrong, then getting your little brother to torment the cat is wrong" and "tormenting the cat is wrong" to the conclusion that getting the little brother to do it is wrong.

Please restate expressivism and the Frege–Geach problem in my vocabulary, as rigorously as the material allows: moral sentences as expressions of a non-boolean type, or as commands and effectful computations rather than pure values; assertion and denial as operations with characteristic effects on a state; the Frege–Geach worry as the observation that such expressions cannot inhabit the argument positions the logical connectives are typed to accept. Then give me a residue report with three parts:

1. What translates cleanly. Does the descriptive/moral distinction map onto the value/effect or expression/command distinction? Does the embedding worry become exactly the point that one cannot negate a command, or place one in the antecedent of a conditional, without a type error? Is there a semantic notion in my field — say, a context-change potential rather than a truth condition — that captures what the expressivist takes moral meaning to be?

2. What translates only under distortion. The cat inference certainly looks valid, and expressivists typically reply that it preserves something other than truth — say, consistency among one's attitudes. Is there anything a language designer would recognize as the analog of validity for inferences among effect-typed expressions, and how much violence does one do to the philosophical notion by identifying it with preservation of some invariant over a nonstandard state? Where does the translation have to smuggle truth back in to make modus ponens work?

3. What has no counterpart at all. Consider, for instance, the sense in which two speakers who utter "tormenting the cat is wrong" and "it is not wrong" genuinely disagree with each other, rather than merely issuing clashing directives toward the world, and the fact that moral sentences embed under attitude verbs like "believes that" and "wonders whether." Is there anything in the philosophical dispute that a type system or operational semantics simply cannot represent?

Finally, I want your judgment on what the residue shows. My instinct as a PL person is that the Frege–Geach problem is just a type error dressed up as a metaphysical puzzle, and that once you stop forcing moral sentences into the boolean fragment the problem dissolves. Is that instinct a genuine insight the philosophers have been groping toward, or is it a case of my framework being unable to see what they are worried about? Where exactly does the answer to that question turn on features of language — force, commitment, interpersonal disagreement — that my vocabulary treats as implementation detail?
