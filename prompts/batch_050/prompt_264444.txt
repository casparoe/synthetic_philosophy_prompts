Consider the standing that advance directives have in medical law and clinical practice. A person who is fully competent writes a living will refusing, say, artificial nutrition and hydration should she ever reach advanced dementia, or appoints a proxy with instructions to that effect. Years later, with the directive in force, the patient appears reasonably content in her diminished condition — she eats when food is offered, resists when it is withheld, and gives every sign of wanting to continue living. In most jurisdictions the earlier document controls: the current, observable wishes of the patient carry little or no weight against it. Yet outside medicine the law is far less deferential to earlier selves. Contracts can be renegotiated, wills rewritten, consent withdrawn at any moment before the relevant act, and a competent person's present refusal will defeat a signed authorization every time. The advance directive is thus a striking exception: a standing instruction that the later self cannot effectively revoke, enforced precisely against the person who is now on the scene.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? It seems to require some view about the unity of the person over time — that the author of the directive and the demented patient are the same subject in the sense that matters, or at least that the earlier self retains authority over the later one. But which view, exactly? Does the practice presuppose a narrative or biographical conception of the person, on which what happens in the final chapter is answerable to the whole life? Does it presuppose that critical or considered interests outrank experiential ones? Or could it be defended on entirely practical grounds — for instance, that we would otherwise be unable to reassure competent people that their wishes will be honored — without any theory of identity at all? And is the practice internally coherent given the law's refusal, elsewhere, to let earlier selves bind later ones? Finally, say what the practice would have to look like if we instead took the apparent preferences of the currently incompetent patient as authoritative, and whether that revision would be an improvement or a mistake.
