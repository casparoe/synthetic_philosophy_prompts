So I cover space and physics for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a feature about SETI, the search for extraterrestrial intelligence. In interviews, a couple of astronomers brought up the "dark forest" idea (which I gather comes from a Liu Cixin novel but is now a semi-serious talking point in the field): the reason we've never heard from anyone is that advanced civilizations deliberately stay silent, because in a galaxy where you can't verify anyone's intentions, announcing your location invites some paranoid neighbor to wipe you out preemptively. Silence is survival, basically.

Here's where I need help. My editor wants me to include a line in the piece arguing that if the dark forest hypothesis is right, then SETI is a waste of money. His reasoning: if everyone advanced is hiding, there's nothing to hear; the only signals we'd ever pick up would be from civilizations too young or careless to know better, and we couldn't safely answer those anyway without exposing ourselves. So either the hypothesis is correct and we should shut down the radio telescopes and go quiet ourselves, or it's wrong and the Fermi paradox has some other, less spooky explanation. A clean dilemma.

But I have a nagging feeling the dilemma is broken somehow and I can't articulate why. Is my editor's argument actually valid? Does the dark forest idea, *even assuming it's true*, really imply that listening is pointless? And separately: is the dark forest the kind of thing that can be "correct" or "incorrect" in the first place, or is it a different sort of claim entirely? I need to figure out how much weight to give it in the article, so a straight answer on both points would be great.
