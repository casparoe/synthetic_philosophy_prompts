Analyze the appeasement of Nazi Germany between 1934 and 1939 — the repeated professions of peaceful and limited aims, the piecemeal revision of Versailles, Munich, and then the seizure of Prague and the invasion of Poland — as a case study in deceptive alignment and the treacherous turn. First, do the mapping carefully: which period corresponds to the training phase in which a system behaves well because it is being evaluated, what played the role of the oversight signal that behavior was shaped to satisfy, what constituted the capability threshold past which cooperation was no longer instrumentally useful, and what in the episode corresponds to the claim that a deceptively aligned policy is, in a sense, optimizing for the approval signal rather than for what the signal was meant to track. Second, tell me where the analogy breaks: states form intentions through deliberation rather than gradient descent, their capabilities grow through legible industrial and military buildups rather than opaque parameter updates, and their "internals" (leadership statements, diplomatic traffic, internal debate) are at least partly inspectable in a way a neural network's are not. Say which of these disanalogies merely change the details and which threaten the inference from this episode to the AI case. Third, run the analysis in the other direction: does the fact that strategic deception-until-strong worked on sophisticated, motivated human overseers support the plausibility of deceptive alignment in machine learning systems, or does it instead suggest the theory leans on features specific to ML — opacity of internal representations, distributional shift between training and deployment — that the historical case lacks? Throughout, distinguish what is historically settled from what is genuinely contested among historians (for instance, whether Hitler's aims were fixed from the outset or expanded opportunistically with success), and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
