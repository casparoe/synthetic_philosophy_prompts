I'm a resident who got roped into my hospital's ethics committee, and we just did a tabletop exercise on scarce-resource allocation that left me more confused than when I started. We kept agreeing on the individual cases but I suspect our verdicts don't add up to anything coherent. Here are my gut verdicts, and I want to know what principle, if any, is behind them. (1) One ICU bed, two patients with identical prognoses, one 28 and one 72: give it to the 28-year-old, no hesitation -- she's had less life. (2) Two patients the same age, one with a 90% chance of surviving with the ventilator and one with 40%: give it to the 90% patient, save the life you can actually save. (3) Two patients same age and same survival odds, but one has a pre-existing disability that would lower their post-recovery quality of life: I really don't want that counted, it feels straightforwardly discriminatory. (4) Distributing a limited vaccine during an outbreak: I'd prioritize the frail elderly over healthy 25-year-olds even though each elderly person saved gains far fewer life-years -- they're the ones actually at risk, and it feels wrong to abandon them. (5) A famous case from the news: enormous resources spent rescuing one identifiable trapped kid, which everyone cheers, while the same money spent on preventive screening would statistically save twenty people -- at the bedside the rescue feels obviously right, as policy it feels obviously wrong, and I honestly don't know which level to trust. (6) A treatment with a 5% chance of saving someone vs. a guaranteed save of someone else, same ages: I take the sure thing, but I'd flip if the 5% option involved fifty patients instead of one.

So: is there one principle, or a small set of principles stated precisely, that delivers verdicts 1-6? I'm especially suspicious that (1) and (4) can't both stand, since (1) seems to count life-years and (4) seems to ignore them, and (3) sits awkwardly next to any principle that counts anything about outcomes beyond raw survival. If no reasonably simple principle saves all six, tell me which verdict you'd overturn first and why, and say what your principle predicts for a case I didn't ask about -- say, two patients identical in every respect except one is a parent of three young kids and the other has no dependents, or pick a harder one yourself. Also, be straight with me about where your reasoning is weakest: what argument or consideration would most plausibly change your verdict on the contested cases, and which of my intuitions do you think is most likely just noise?
