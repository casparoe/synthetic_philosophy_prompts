I help run the film-and-theology night at my church (it's a small group, mostly grad students and a couple of retirees, and we argue a lot), and next month we're doing Scorsese's Silence, the one where the two Jesuit priests go to Japan looking for Ferreira and Rodrigues ends up apostatizing by stepping on the fumie. I want to prepare a discussion sheet, and I keep circling around the divine hiddenness problem. The standard philosophical worry is roughly: if a perfectly loving God existed, he'd make sure anyone open to a relationship with him could at least tell he's there, so widespread reasonable nonbelief is evidence against God. But Silence seems to dramatize a weirder version of this, because Rodrigues isn't a nonbeliever -- he's a believer screaming into the void, enduring and watching others endure horrific suffering while God says nothing, for years. His faith doesn't save him from the silence; if anything it makes the silence worse.

So here's what I'd like: read the film through the lens of divine hiddenness, and take both sides seriously. On one reading, the film is basically an extended illustration of the problem -- the silence is never explained, Ferreira's defection and Rodrigues' apostasy are what hiddenness does to actual people, and the final shot with the crucifix in the coffin is a private consolation at best. On another reading (the one my co-organizer pushes), the film thinks the silence is itself the answer -- that God speaks through or as the silence, most obviously in the fumie scene where Rodrigues finally hears a voice, and that the whole thing is a vindication of a kenotic, suffering-with-us God rather than a hidden one. Which reading does the film actually support? Where does each reading strain against specific scenes -- I'm thinking of the Japanese Christians who die without any voice answering them, Ferreira's "the silence" speech, and the ending. And does the film's version of hiddenness (suffering believers rather than nonbelievers) put pressure on the philosophy, i.e. is Schellenberg-style reasoning even aimed at the right target?

Also, since I have to defend whatever I put on the sheet: tell me what would change your verdict. Like, which scene or detail, read differently, would flip you to the other reading, and where do you think your interpretation is most likely to be wrong or overreaching? I'd rather go in knowing the weak points than get ambushed by my co-organizer.
