I am the commissioning editor for a science fiction imprint, and roughly a third of the manuscripts on my desk involve time travel. My readers complain, with increasing sophistication, about incoherent plots, and my junior editors currently have no shared standard: one rejects anything with a causal loop, another waves through blatant contradictions if the prose is good. None of us has a physics or philosophy background, and we spend perhaps two hours per manuscript at the assessment stage. I would like you to turn what philosophers and physicists have said about the grandfather paradox and time travel consistency into a working procedure my team can actually run on a submission. It should include: a short taxonomy of the time-travel logics a story might be operating under (a single fixed past, a changeable past, branching timelines, and any hybrids you think worth distinguishing); a method for establishing which logic a given manuscript assumes, including what to do when the text never states its rules; a sequence of tests to apply, in order, to the story's causal chains — tracing who causes what, checking whether any event is both brought about and prevented, and asking whether information or objects in the plot have an origin anywhere; and clear verdict categories distinguishing a coherent plot, a coherent plot that exacts a price the reader must be willing to pay, a fixable inconsistency, and a fatal one. The procedure should distinguish the grandfather-paradox problem (a traveller undoing the conditions of their own journey) from the quite different problem of an information or object loop with no origin, since I gather these are often conflated and call for different editorial responses; it should also have a step that catches stories which switch logics mid-plot to escape a corner they have written themselves into. Mark explicitly the points where the checklist runs out and an editor's judgement has to take over — for instance, deciding whether an apparent inconsistency is a deliberate artistic device or an accident. Then run the procedure on these three synopses: (1) a woman travels back to prevent the house fire that killed her sister, succeeds, and returns to a present in which her sister lived and she therefore never had reason to travel — this is the submission I most want to buy, so I expect the procedure to tell me something I will not enjoy; (2) an ageing engineer hands a young stranger the schematic for the machine that made his career, and it emerges that the stranger is his younger self, to whom he will one day hand the same schematic, which was never designed by anyone; (3) a story that spends two hundred pages showing every attempt to alter the past instead causing it, then lets the protagonist genuinely change history in the final chapter with no explanation offered. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do — I suspect it cannot tell a good manuscript from a competent but lifeless one, and I would rather have that stated than discover it.
