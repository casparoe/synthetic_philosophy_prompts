I am a novelist at work on a comic novel about a man who cannot make himself finish anything, and I need a set piece for the middle of the book: a formal legal document that my narrator, in a fit of self-help grandiosity at 2 a.m., draws up as a binding treaty among his successive selves. I would like you to write that document for me, in his voice, so that I can adapt it.

The document should be styled as a treaty, custody agreement, or social contract among the narrator's temporally extended selves — the signatories being, say, the Self of Tonight, the Self of Tomorrow Morning, and the indefinite parliament of Future Selves — written in deadpan legalese with numbered clauses, definitions, and severability provisions, running about 600–700 words. The comedy must come from getting the underlying psychology and philosophy right rather than from inventing absurdity. In particular, the document should embody, without ever naming, the actual structure of the akrasia problem: preference reversal as a deadline draws near (the clause that seemed wise at 2 a.m. is tyrannical at 9 a.m.); the brute asymmetry that only the present self can act, so every later self holds the whole agreement hostage; the resort to commitment devices by a self that distrusts its successors; the temptation to renegotiate, defect, or exploit loopholes, and the anxious preference for bright-line rules over flexible ones because flexible ones will be gamed; and the question of who, if anyone, has authority to bind the others. A reader who knows the literature on weakness of will and intertemporal choice should recognize that the author understands what is being parodied — ideally that the treaty re-enacts, clause by clause, the real difficulties of intrapersonal bargaining. The irony should stay straight-faced but legible: no winking asides, and my narrator must genuinely believe this document will save him. The target is a pattern of thought, not any real person.

After the document, please add a short note of roughly 150 words, addressed to me as the novelist, separating what is reasonably settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers and behavioral scientists. Where the treaty leans on a contested picture — for instance, treating hyperbolic or non-exponential discounting as the explanation of akrasia, or treating the talk of multiple selves as more than a useful metaphor — flag that and indicate what the main alternatives or objections are, so that I know which jokes rest on solid ground and which ones take sides in a live dispute. Please do not present any contested reading as the consensus.
