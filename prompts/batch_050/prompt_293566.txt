I host a recorded seminar series for advanced undergraduates in philosophy of language. Next week's guest is a philosopher who defends a dispositionalist response to Kripke's rule-following paradox: roughly, that what a speaker means by an expression like "+" is constituted by her dispositions to answer problems involving it, with suitable idealization doing the work wherever her actual dispositions fall short. She knows the standard objections cold and gives polished, compressed answers, so I need questions that push past the textbook version of the debate rather than let her recite it.

Draft ten interview questions, organized under the following five section headings, in this order, with a one-sentence follow-up to every question:

1. The Setup (one question): Force her to state, in plain terms, exactly which fact about a speaker the dispositionalist takes meaning to consist in, without retreating to talk of use or practice.

2. Error and Correctness (three questions): Her most likely move is to say that a speaker's mistakes are deviations from an idealized version of her dispositions. Build a sequence that makes the cost of that move explicit: if the idealization is fixed by what the speaker herself would count as correct, the account looks circular, and if it is fixed some other way, she owes a non-semantic fact that selects the idealizing conditions. Press for that fact.

3. Finitude (two questions): No actual disposition covers arbitrarily large addends, so ask why the idealized extension of a finite disposition should select addition rather than some quus-like function that agrees with addition on every input the speaker could ever encounter.

4. Normativity (two questions): Dispositions describe what a speaker will do, not what she ought to do. Ask what, on her view, makes an answer wrong rather than merely unexpected, and whether wrong can mean anything more than out of step with the surrounding community.

5. The Skeptical Solution (two questions): Ask why she refuses to follow Kripke into a picture on which meaning-talk is governed by conditions of justified assertion rather than by facts that make it true, and close with a question about what argument or observation could move her off dispositionalism.

Each follow-up should be written against the answer she is most likely to give: anticipate the reply, then tighten the screw, rather than restating the question. No generic questions about her career, influences, or the state of the field. Format: use the five headings exactly as named; each question should run one to two sentences; each follow-up exactly one sentence; nothing between sections except the questions themselves; roughly 400 to 500 words in total.
