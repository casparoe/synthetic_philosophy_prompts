Ok so I'm doing a reading series with some friends where we pair a play with a philosophy text, and next up is Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead alongside this bit from Hume's Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section VII (Of the Idea of Necessary Connexion):

"When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other."

I want to read the whole play off that passage, especially the opening where the coin keeps coming up heads and Guildenstern runs through his increasingly desperate list of explanations for it. On a first pass the scene seems almost too Humean: all Guildenstern ever has is one flip following another, and every "law" or "principle" he reaches for is just something his mind projects onto the run of results. But here's the thing that makes the play interesting and not just an illustration -- the audience knows something Guildenstern doesn't, which is that in his world there really IS a hidden necessary connection binding events together, namely the script. He's a character in Hamlet; his coin flips, his summons, his death are all literally written. So is the play a world where the skeptical reading of Hume is true from the inside but the "New Hume" reading (that there are real powers governing events, just permanently inaccessible to us) is true from the outside? And does Stoppard's point depend on the fact that what the characters can't access isn't a deep physical power but something as contingent and almost arbitrary as an author?

Give me a reading that takes the Hume seriously (regularity vs necessary connection, what the New Hume debate is even about) and doesn't just use the play as decoration: where does the coin scene get Hume right, where does Guildenstern's reasoning go beyond or against anything Hume would license, where does the reading strain (I'm suspicious the deaths and the Player's fatalism don't fit as neatly as the coins do), and what, if anything, does the play add to the causation debate that a straight philosophical argument can't? Lead with your overall take in the first sentence -- whether the play ultimately vindicates, complicates, or quietly refutes the Hume passage -- then work through it, no preamble and don't restate the question back at me.
