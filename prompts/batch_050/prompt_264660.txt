So I got voluntold to give a ~25 minute slot at our engineering all-hands in a few weeks. Audience is like 40 software engineers, mostly backend and infra, smart people but zero philosophy background and most of them only know AI safety from twitter threads and that one time our model kept apologizing in prod. Topic I picked: the difference between intent alignment and value alignment, i.e. the narrow version of "the alignment problem" (getting a system to actually be trying to do what its operator wants) vs the big fuzzy version (getting it to want the right things, whatever those are).

I don't want the talk itself written for me. I want a plan for how to teach it. Specifically:

- The order the pieces have to come in. My guess is I need to first get them to feel the difference between a system that fails because it's incompetent and a system that fails because it's not even trying to do the thing you wanted, before any of the rest lands. But maybe something else has to come first, like the gap between what you literally asked for and what you meant. Tell me what depends on what.
- One example to carry the whole talk. I was thinking of using the junior dev who implements the ticket exactly as written vs the one who implements what the PM actually wanted vs the one who ships what's genuinely good for the company -- three rungs, mapping roughly to literal obedience, intent alignment, value alignment. Does that example actually work, and what does it need (or need to avoid) to not collapse? Like, the middle rung is doing a lot of work.
- The misconceptions, in the order they'll form in the audience's heads, and where in my sequence I should kill each one. Ones I'm expecting: "alignment just means following instructions literally" (but then where does the genie/King Midas failure mode fit -- is a system that does exactly what you said, not what you meant, intent-aligned or not? I genuinely am not sure how to present this), "if it passes our evals it's intent-aligned," "value alignment is just intent alignment with a better spec," and probably "this is all just saying we need better requirements docs." There's also the deceptive alignment thing -- a system that behaves aligned during evals -- and I'm not sure whether to bring it up as its own misconception or weave it into the evals one.
- One question I can pose at the end where the answer tells me they got the distinction and aren't just nodding along.
- What I should openly punt on. I'm assuming "ok but whose values / which values" is a tarpit I should explicitly refuse to enter at this level, but tell me if there's a clean one-liner that closes it off without sounding like a dodge.

Keep in mind these are engineers, so they'll instantly reach for analogies to compilers, type systems, and testing -- feel free to tell me which of those analogies are load-bearing and which are traps. My talk definately can't assume anyone's read anything, including me honestly.
