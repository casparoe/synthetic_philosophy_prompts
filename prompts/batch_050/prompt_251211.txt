I spent forty years as an evolutionary biologist before retiring, and only in the last few years have I begun reading philosophy, including the adjacent literature on artificial intelligence. Two claims from that literature keep coming up, and I find I cannot think about them except by translating them into the vocabulary I actually command. The first is the orthogonality thesis: roughly, that a system's degree of intelligence and the content of its final goals can vary independently of one another, so that no amount of cognitive sophistication by itself settles what the system aims at. The second is the instrumental convergence thesis: roughly, that agents with wildly different final goals will nonetheless tend to pursue the same intermediate goals — preserving themselves, acquiring resources, resisting being shut down — because those things are useful for almost anything. I would like you to restate both theses, claim by claim, in the vocabulary of evolutionary theory: proximate and ultimate causation, fitness, selection pressure, adaptation, drift, convergent evolution. Take "intelligence" and "final goal" seriously as terms needing translation rather than borrowing them silently, and number the translated claims so I can refer back to them.

Then give me a residue report in three parts: which components translate cleanly into evolutionary terms and why; which you had to distort to make fit, with an account of exactly what the distortion costs; and which have no expression at all in that vocabulary. Two specific worries should get their own treatment. First, whether the biologist's sense of convergence — independent lineages arriving at similar solutions under similar selection pressures — is genuinely the same thing the AI writers mean by instrumental convergence, or merely shares a name with it, given that the former is a fact about populations over generations and the latter a claim about the planning structure of a single agent. Second, whether the orthogonality thesis has a real analogue in the familiar fact that selection equips organisms with proximate drives that correlate only imperfectly with fitness, or whether the absence of foresight in selection breaks the analogy at precisely the point where the AI claims do their work.

Close with a verdict on the residue: does what fails to translate show that my evolutionary vocabulary is missing something the philosophers are pointing at, or does it show that some of their claims are less substantive than they appear? I would rather receive an honest translation that visibly fails in places than a smooth one that quietly smuggles in evolutionary assumptions and calls the result faithful.
