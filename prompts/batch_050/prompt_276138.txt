Here's roughly where I've landed on free will after teaching it for years, and I want to know if the package hangs together before I put it in the syllabus:

(1) I'm a compatibilist of the reasons-responsiveness sort: determinism is no threat to moral responsibility, what matters is that the agent acts from a mechanism of their own that is suitably responsive to reasons.

(2) Frankfurt cases convince me that the ability to do otherwise is not necessary for responsibility.

(3) But manipulation cases still move me: an agent whose values were directly implanted by a neuroscientist, and who then acts on them, is not responsible for that action, full stop.

(4) I also think responsibility verdicts shouldn't turn on purely historical facts -- if two agents are psychologically identical right now, inside and out, and one was manipulated while the other came by the same psychology through an ordinary upbringing, they should get the same verdict. Anything else feels like punishing someone for their biography.

(5) I think the luck objection is fatal to libertarianism -- adding indeterminism at the moment of choice gets you randomness, not freedom. That said, I suspect some randomness in which options occur to you during deliberation might actually enhance freedom rather than undermine it.

Which of these are in genuine tension, which only look like it, and how bad is each one? Rank them by severity and tell me the cheapest fix for each -- I don't already know which one I'd drop, so don't assume. And one test case for whatever survives: a court orders a compulsive offender fitted with a neural implant that renders him reasons-responsive for the first time in his life. Is he responsible for what he does the day after activation, or only once he's had time to "live into" the new mechanism, and does your repaired view have the resources to say?
