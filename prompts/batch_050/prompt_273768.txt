I never studied philosophy, but I've kept a journal for about fifteen years in which I record moral and political judgments I feel strongly about, and going back through it I've noticed something that bothers me. My views have clearly changed, but not in response to new facts so much as through a slow back-and-forth: I'd catch a general principle I endorsed implying something I found unacceptable, and then either I'd water down the principle or I'd talk myself into accepting the implication. Years later, I can't tell whether the endpoint I arrived at is better justified than where I started, or whether it just reflects which of the two I happened to adjust first. I've since learned that philosophers have a name for something like this back-and-forth process -- bringing principles and case judgments into mutual adjustment until they cohere -- and that it's often treated as the best available method for justifying moral beliefs. What I haven't seen addressed, at least in what I've read, is the question my journal raises: if the process is path-dependent, so that the order in which you make your adjustments determines where you end up, then reaching a stable, coherent endpoint seems to show only that you stopped, not that you were justified.

I'm applying to a small grant program that funds one-year projects by independent researchers without institutional affiliation, and I want to propose turning this worry into a proper study. My rough plan has two strands: a conceptual strand asking whether path-dependence is a defect in the method itself or merely an accident of how fallible people happen to carry it out, and a modest practical strand in which I would have a small number of volunteers work through the same set of moral dilemmas in deliberately different orders over several sessions, keeping structured records of what they revise and when, to see whether their endpoints diverge in the way my journal suggests. Write the proposal for me, about two pages. It needs: a precise statement of the question, in a form that could actually have an answer; an explanation of why the standard defenses of the method (that coherence is the best we can hope for in ethics) and the standard objections (that it merely systematizes the prejudices you started with) both leave my question untouched; a month-by-month account of what I would actually do, realistic for someone doing this part-time with no lab and no students; an honest treatment of the obvious weakness -- that a dozen volunteers recruited online proves little, and why the conceptual strand matters even if the practical strand is suggestive at best; and a statement of what would count as the project succeeding and what would count as it failing. I'll add any citations myself, so refer to positions and arguments by description rather than by author and title.
