I've been running a reading group on population ethics, and last week we got stuck in a familiar place: everyone rejected the repugnant conclusion in the abstract, and then everyone accepted premises that seemed to walk them straight back into it. It made me want to pin down my own intuitions properly rather than gesture at them. So here are my verdicts on a set of comparisons, stated as concretely as I can manage, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, lies behind them. In each case, assume the lives described are all that exists or will exist in the relevant outcome, that welfare is well-defined and comparable, and that no one is wronged in any deontological sense — these are pure axiology questions.

Case one: outcome A contains one person living a very long, rich, deeply happy life; outcome B contains ten billion people, each living a life that is just barely worth living — enough small pleasures to make existence preferable to non-existence, but nothing more. I judge A better than B, and fairly confidently. Case two: we start from a world of a billion flourishing people, and can add a further billion people whose lives are good but clearly less good than the first billion's. I judge the addition makes things no worse, and probably better. Case three: given a fixed resource, we can either substantially raise the welfare of the existing billion or instead create a new billion at moderate welfare; I judge raising the existing people's welfare better. Case four: a choice between one person living an excellent life and one hundred people each living a life only slightly less excellent than that one — I judge the hundred better, without much hesitation, even though this looks uncomfortably like the aggregation I refused in case one. Case five: a choice between a small population, all of whose lives are overall not worth living, and an enormous population, all of whose lives are barely worth living — I judge the enormous population better, and I refuse the option of treating the small miserable population as preferable.

What I'd like from you: a candidate principle — or a small family of candidates — stated precisely enough that I can apply it myself to new cases, with each clause motivated by the cases above. Please be explicit about which of my five verdicts your preferred principle captures and which, if any, it forces me to give up; I suspect at least one has to go, and I'd rather have that said plainly than have a principle gerrymandered to save everything at the cost of ad hoc complexity. Then tell me what your principle predicts for two cases I haven't listed: first, a comparison where the larger population is worse-off on average but contains enormous inequality among its members; second, a sequence of steps, each individually an improvement by my own verdicts, that ends somewhere I clearly reject — does your principle diagnose where the chain goes wrong, or does it bite the bullet? Finally, please indicate how confident you are in your overall recommendation, and single out the one judgment in your analysis — whether about my cases or your principle — that you are least sure of.
