I'm helping run a political theory discussion group, and our meetings on judicial review keep collapsing into the same exchange: one person says unelected judges overriding elected legislatures is obviously undemocratic, and another says obviously rights would mean nothing otherwise. We both suspect the real question is prior to that: what would a theory of legitimate judicial review have to accomplish before we started arguing about who is right? Assume a reasonably stable democracy with an entrenched constitution, regular elections, a constitutional court, and an amendment process that is difficult but not practically impossible. The theory needs to cover not just laws restricting speech or manipulating elections, but also messier cases: a legislature funding benefits in a way the constitution's vague equality clause may or may not require, an emergency law that is popular but hard to reverse, and a court remedy whose implementation would require judges to supervise a budget for years.

Write the desiderata for an adequate theory, with the consideration or case motivating each one. Include what it must say about where judicial authority comes from, which constitutional provisions are judicially enforceable, how much deference elected institutions deserve, what counts as protecting democracy rather than substituting the court's politics, how institutional competence and error costs affect the answer, and what remedies are permissible when the court lacks a clean way to fix the violation. Distinguish requirements that seem genuinely non-negotiable from assumptions associated with particular camps, and say whether any familiar-looking set of desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. I'd especially like to know which single demand excludes the most plausible theories. Please don't just compare originalism, living constitutionalism, departmentalism, etc.; you can mention them to illustrate, but the specification itself is what I need. I want something our group can use before everyone retreats to their favourite doctrine.
