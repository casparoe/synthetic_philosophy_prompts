My roommate read some Taurek last semester and has been unbearable ever since, and last night he walked me into a corner I can't get out of. His argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

1. Each person matters for their own sake, so you can never justify a choice by pooling people into a total -- saving someone isn't made more important by how many others happen to get saved along with them.
2. So if I can save either one stranger or five strangers from the very same death, the four extra lives give me no reason at all to pick the group; the only fair procedure is to give everyone an equal chance, e.g. flip a coin.
3. But notice the principle in 1 says nothing about what's at stake. The respect owed to each person doesn't shrink just because the harm facing them is smaller -- if it did, I'd be pooling things again, just weighing harms instead of people.
4. So by the same reasoning, if I can either save one person from death or save five people from a nasty hangover, I should flip a coin there too.
5. That's obviously deranged. But he insists the only way out is to admit that what happens to the many gives me a reason -- which is just aggregation with extra steps, and lands you right back at carving up one patient to save five.

Something has clearly slipped, but I honestly can't tell whether it's step 2, 3, or 4, and every fix I try seems to smuggle the numbers back in through the side door. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost me if I want to hold onto the idea that persons aren't fungible?
