a friend of mine who defends traditional armchair philosophy has a stock reply to the experimental philosophy results -- the studies finding that people from different cultural backgrounds give systematically different verdicts on classic thought experiments, like Gettier-style cases about knowledge. he says: variation in intuition is no more a problem for relying on intuitions in philosophy than cultural variation in susceptibility to visual illusions (the Müller-Lyer arrows and so on) is a problem for relying on perception in science. perception also varies across people and cultures, and nobody concluded that observation is bankrupt. so the challenge to the armchair fails.

i want to know how far this actually holds. lay out the mapping first -- what plays the role of the perceptual system, the stimulus, the veridical case, the illusion -- and then the disanalogies. one i can see myself: with the illusion we have an independent check on the right answer (get out a ruler), whereas with intuitions the whole fight is about what a check would even be. another: perception mostly converges on one shared world, and it's not clear intuitions about knowledge do. but maybe he can reply that a ruler is just more perception, so the "independent check" point is overblown? and would the better comparison be testimony rather than perception, and does that change who wins?

bottom line i want: does the analogy work as a defense of armchair method, and if it fails, does it fail in a way that also undercuts the original experimental challenge (i.e. are both sides overclaiming)? keep it under 600 words.
