Here's the exam question I set:

"State the knowability principle and the Church-Fitch derivation. What exactly does the derivation establish? Does it refute the claim that all truths are knowable in principle? Present and evaluate one strategy for blocking the derivation."

And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"The knowability principle (KP) says that for every truth p, it is possible that p is known: p → ◇Kp. Fitch's paradox shows this collapses into the much stronger claim that every truth is actually known. The derivation: suppose p is an unknown truth, i.e. p ∧ ¬Kp. By KP it's possible to know this conjunction. But knowledge distributes over conjunction, so K(p ∧ ¬Kp) implies Kp ∧ K¬Kp, and by factivity K¬Kp implies ¬Kp, giving Kp ∧ ¬Kp, a contradiction. So no conjunction of the form p ∧ ¬Kp is knowable, and by reductio, if KP holds then p → Kp for all p.

One might object that the derivation smuggles in the KK principle (that knowing p entails knowing that you know p), since we move from knowing the conjunction to knowing each conjunct. But KK is controversial, so this is a weak point in the argument.

The more natural escape is to deny that knowledge distributes over conjunction. Real agents don't automatically know every conjunct of things they know -- you can know that it's raining and cold without separately registering each fact. So the distribution principle is psychologically unrealistic and the anti-verificationist conclusion doesn't follow.

I conclude that the paradox is interesting but doesn't actually refute verificationism, since the verificationist can just retreat to the claim that every truth is known in some possible world, which the derivation leaves untouched."
