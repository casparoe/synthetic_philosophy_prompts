I'm a PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on promissory obligation, and I've realized I've been reading theories of promising without a settled standard for judging them -- every author I read seems to assume a different set of cases that a theory "obviously" has to get right, and then conveniently their own theory gets those cases right. Before I go further, I want the specification first, independently of any particular theory. So: write the desiderata that an adequate theory of why promises bind must meet. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it. I want the full list: the phenomena it has to explain (why a promise creates an obligation rather than merely a reason, why the obligation is owed to the promisee specifically and not just a general duty, why release and forgiveness make normative sense, why promises seem to bind even when keeping them would make things slightly worse overall), the hard cases it must deliver a verdict on rather than wave at (promises to do something immoral, promises extracted by pressure or deception, promises to people who have since died or forgotten, promises the promisee never cared about, promises made in communities with no shared social practice of promising), and the distinctions it cannot be allowed to collapse (being wronged versus being harmed, obligation versus reason, the power to create obligations versus merely triggering pre-existing ones).

A few constraints on how I'd like this done. First, for each desideratum, tell me whether it strikes you as a genuine constraint or a prejudice smuggled in by one tradition -- for instance, I suspect that "the theory must explain why duress voids a promise" is load-bearing, whereas "the theory must explain bindingness without appeal to any social convention" may just be an anti-Humean reflex, but I want that argued rather than asserted. Second, flag any pairs of desiderata that look jointly unsatisfiable -- my working worry is that wanting promises to bind regardless of local practice sits badly with wanting the theory to explain why the obligation has the precise shape our practice gives it (transferable rights, release, the optionality of accepting a promise), and I'd like to know whether that tension is real or an artifact of my framing. Third, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding contenders -- which one knocks out the largest number of the standard positions in one move.

Finally, and keep this brief relative to the list itself: score the main contenders against the desiderata in a few sentences each -- the expectation-based account associated with Scanlon, the social-practice account in the tradition of Hume and Rawls, and the view that promising is an exercise of a sui generis normative power. I'm less interested in who wins than in which requirements each theory treats as negotiable, since that will tell me what my own chapter actually has to defend.
