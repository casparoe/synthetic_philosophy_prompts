I work at a mid-sized natural history and science museum, and my job is reviewing text before it goes on walls -- exhibit labels, gallery intros, timeline panels, the whole thing. We redo a couple of galleries a year plus rotating temp exhibits, so I probably look at 200+ panels annually, and I keep running into the same problem: our writers (smart people, mostly science backgrounds) keep producing narratives where the past is basically a waiting room for the present. Everything marches toward what we now know is true, the figures we celebrate are the ones who "got it right," the ones who didn't are obstacles or footnotes, and the implicit question of every story is how did we get here rather than what was it like to be there.

I've read a bit about this -- Butterfield's old critique of whig history, the stuff about presentism, teleology, judging dead ends as failures instead of as live options -- so I have the vocabulary, but I don't have anything my writers can actually *use*. "Don't be whiggish" is not actionable feedback at 4pm before a print deadline.

So: turn the historiographical critique into a working review procedure I can run on a panel text in about fifteen minutes. I want a sequence of tests or questions I apply to a draft, in order, each one concrete enough that I can point to a sentence and say it fails test three. Things like: how do you tell whether a figure is being treated as a precursor of us rather than as someone with their own project? When is mentioning what later became of an idea legitimate context vs. smuggling the ending into the beginning? Is there a difference between a timeline (which is unavoidably arranged toward the present by its format) and whig narration, and if so where's the line? Include what counts as a *fix* for each failure -- not just "rewrite this" but the actual moves available (re-centering on the actor's own categories, giving the losing side its reasons, etc.), since my writers will ask.

Two things I specifically want addressed:

1. Where the procedure should hand off to judgment. Some of this is genuinely contested among historians -- e.g., whether a gallery about, say, the development of germ theory *owes* Semmelweis's contemporaries a sympathetic hearing, or whether anachronistic moral judgment is sometimes the right call (we do an exhibit touching on eugenics and I am not interested in a procedure that tells me to be neutral about it). Mark the spots where the checklist runs out and a person has to decide, and say what kind of decision it is.

2. Run the procedure on two short cases I'll invent right now so you have something to chew on: (a) a panel on alchemy that currently frames the whole thing as "fumbling toward chemistry" and treats Newton's alchemical work as an embarrassing hobby; (b) a timeline panel on extinction that goes Cuvier -> Boltwood/radiometric dating -> Alvarez, with everything in between skipped, which is exactly the kind of story I suspect is fine-but-whiggish and I want to see the procedure either convict it or acquit it with reasons.

Constraints: my reviewers aren't historians, the fix suggestions have to fit in ~80 words of label text (museum real estate is brutal), and our director has to sign off on anything that reads as editorializing about living political disputes, so flag where a recommended fix might trip that.

One more thing: if you think the premise here is muddled -- like if "whig history" as I've described it conflates several different sins that need different procedures, or if you think the real problem with my example panels is something else entirely -- say that straight up rather than politely building me the checklist I asked for. I'd rather get told the request is confused than get a confident procedure built on sand.
