So my friend has this argument that virtual harms basically aren't real harms, and at first I nodded along (longtime LW reader, very sympathetic to the whole "don't confuse the representation with the thing" genre), but then I pushed it one step further and it seems to eat money and borders too, which can't be right. Here's the argument as cleanly as I can reconstruct it:

(1) A virtual thing -- a sword in an MMO, a plot of metaverse land, your pile of in-game gold -- is nothing over and above data plus whatever meaning we choose to project onto it. Delete the projection and there's just bits.

(2) If X is nothing over and above Y, then anything X can do to you, Y can do to you. There are no extra causal powers hiding in the "over and above" part.

(3) Bits on a game server can't damage your body, your physical property, or your legal standing. The only channel by which they can affect you at all runs through your own mind: you have to find out what happened and then feel bad about it.

(4) A bad feeling that only arises because you chose to take a representation seriously isn't a real harm -- otherwise the novelist who kills off your favorite character has injured you, and losing at chess is a wound.

(5) So virtual theft, griefing, scamming someone out of their items, etc. are not real harms. Unpleasant, sure, but they belong in the category of fiction and games, not the category of injury.

Now the part that makes me think it proves too much:

(6) Swap the example. Money is nothing over and above entries in bank databases plus meanings we all project; delete the projection and it's just bits. Same for national borders (imaginary lines), promises (vibrations in the air), marriages (words plus paperwork).

(7) The bits in the bank's server can't damage your body either. If the bank zeroes your account while you sleep, you suffer only once you find out, through your own mind, because you chose to take the representation seriously.

(8) So by (2)-(4), draining someone's account, annexing the territory across an imaginary line, or breaking a promise aren't real harms either -- just upsets in people who decided to care about representations.

(9) That's obviously false. So something in (1)-(5) has to give.

My friend bites the bullet and says fine, the social stuff counts as real only because enough people keep projecting hard enough, and virtual worlds haven't crossed that threshold yet -- which to me sounds less like a principled line and more like counting heads. But I can't definately say which numbered step he should give up instead. If I had to pick, I'd rather keep the intuition that losing your life savings is categorially worse than losing your Diablo loot than keep any particular premise -- I just want to know what that preference commits me to.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically -- if you reject (4), do you have to say the novelist harmed me? If you reject (2), are you stuck positing some weird extra causal juice that virtual objects have and mere data doesn't? And if you reject (6)-(8) by denying the analogy, what's the actual disanalogy, given that game economies have real enforcement, real markets, and real people's livelihoods at this point?
