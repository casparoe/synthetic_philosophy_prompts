For an upper-level undergraduate seminar in moral philosophy (about 14 students, one 75-minute session), design a structured activity that makes participants feel the force of Hume's argument in the Treatise of Human Nature, Book III, Part II, Section V ("Of the obligation of promises"). The pivot of the session should be this passage:

The act of the mind, exprest by a promise, is not a resolution to perform any thing: For that alone never imposes any obligation. Nor is it a desire of such a performance: For we may bind ourselves without such a desire, or even with an aversion, declar'd and avow'd. Neither is it the willing of that action, which we promise to perform: For a promise always regards some future time, and the will has an influence only on present actions.

I want an activity in which students must attempt, under staged "pre-conventional" conditions, to bind one another to future conduct using nothing but acts of mind -- resolution, desire, willing -- and discover for themselves why each fails, before any shared form of words, practice, or expectation exists. Then let the group try to engineer whatever it was that made binding possible, and observe what they actually had to build. Include: roles or rules and the constraints that simulate the absence of convention; a timeline; small real stakes if you can devise them without money; and a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the passage (the failed candidates, the "willing of the obligation" it leads to, and the convention Hume says must replace them). Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- especially students smuggling in the very conventions the scenario is meant to exclude -- and how to handle it. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: students already live inside the practice of promising and cannot unknow it, so say what the facilitator must supply instead. Finally, when the debrief turns to whether Hume's convention account succeeds against rivals (for example, accounts on which promises bind by knowingly creating expectations), have the debrief notes separate what is settled -- that Hume argued this, and what his argument is -- from what is genuinely contested among philosophers, without presenting any contested verdict as the consensus.
