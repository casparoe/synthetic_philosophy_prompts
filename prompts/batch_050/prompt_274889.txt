So I've been chewing on Chesterton's fence lately — the idea that you shouldn't tear down a fence (or a rule, or an institution) until you understand why someone put it up in the first place. Here's what's bugging me. We never *fully* understand why any institution exists. The original reasons are often lost to history, the functions are partly hidden, the effects ramify in ways nobody's mapped. So taken seriously, doesn't the principle rule out reforming anything, ever? It seems like a conservatism built on Chesterton's fence commits you to keeping literally everything, which is a position basically nobody holds — not even Burke, who as far as I can tell distinguished reform from revolution and was fine with the former. So which is it: is the fence principle incoherent, or does it just prove way too much? And if it's one of those, why do people keep wheeling it out in arguments as though it settles things, when at best it seems to be a gesture at caution that applies to literally any change whatsoever? I'd rather you didn't hedge here — take an actual position on whether the principle, charitably understood, has real bite or whether it's just a conversation-stopper dressed up as wisdom, and defend the call. If I'm misreading what the principle even claims, say so plainly rather than working around it.
