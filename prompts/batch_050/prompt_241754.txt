I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and since retiring I have been working my way through the philosophical literature on decision theory, where I keep running into a claim that strikes me as odd: the idea, associated with Isaac Levi and others, that an agent in the middle of deliberating cannot coherently assign probabilities to her own forthcoming choices, because to predict what she will do would already be to have settled what she will do. Deliberation, on this view, crowds out prediction. I have been chewing on this while rewatching Denis Villeneuve's Arrival, which I take to be the most interesting fictional treatment of exactly this problem, and I would like a careful philosophical reading of the film through that lens.

Here is what I want the reading to engage. Louise Banks, having learned the heptapod language, comes to experience her life non-sequentially: she knows, in advance, that she will have a daughter who will die young, and she proceeds anyway. The standard puzzle about the film is whether she could have done otherwise. But my question is more specific and, I think, more interesting: what is the epistemic status of her foreknowledge relative to her agency? When Louise "chooses" to have the child, is she deliberating at all, or has she crossed into something like the third-person stance -- treating her own future conduct the way I would treat a prediction about a particle's trajectory, as a fixed fact to be accommodated rather than an open question to be decided? The Levi-style view says deliberation requires regarding one's options as genuinely open, which seems to mean Louise cannot be deliberating about what she already foreknows. Yet the film plainly presents her as choosing, and presents the choice as morally weighty -- Ian's eventual devastation suggests he, at least, thinks there was something to blame her for, and blame presupposes a deliberating agent. So the film seems to dramatize a position the philosophy says is incoherent: certain knowledge of one's own future act, coexisting with genuine choice of it.

I would like the essay to do several things: first, lay out the "deliberation crowds out prediction" thesis accurately, including the strongest arguments for it (and the objections -- I gather some decision theorists think agents can and must have credences over their own acts, and I'd want that side represented). Second, read the film against this debate scene by scene where relevant: does Louise's case actually fit the theoretical puzzle, or does the film smuggle in something that dissolves it (for instance, does she foresee her choices as decisions she endorses, or merely as events that befall her)? Third, take a stand on what the film ultimately shows: is it a coherent picture of an agent who both knows and chooses, a dramatization of compatibilism done right, or a beautiful confusion that the decision theory exposes? I am particularly interested in whether the film contributes something to this debate that the journal literature does not -- whether experiencing Louise's perspective from the inside, so to speak, gives us an intuition pump that pure argument cannot. I would rather have a reading that concludes the film fails, if that is where the argument leads, than one that flatters it. Where the film resists the philosophical framing, or the framing distorts the film, I want that said plainly.
