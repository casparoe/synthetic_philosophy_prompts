I recently reread the Ant Fugue sections of Gödel, Escher, Bach — the dialogues where Aunt Hillary, a conscious ant colony, chats with the Anteater while her constituent ants wander around with no inkling of the conversation they are collectively constituting — and it sparked a running argument among some friends about whether anything in the actual world could be a mind the way Aunt Hillary is depicted as being. The candidates people floated ranged from real ant colonies to corporations, ecosystems, the global economy, and Ned Block's thought experiment in which the population of China implements a human brain neuron-by-neuron via radio links. What frustrated me is that everyone argued from a single favored intuition pump: one person said obviously not, just look how dumb an ant is; another said obviously yes, functional organization is all that matters; a third insisted the whole question is confused. Nobody could articulate what evidence or argument would actually settle it, and I noticed the same failure mode in online discussions of whether uploaded brains, or large language models, or a sufficiently elaborate simulated ecology could be minded: people skip straight to verdicts without ever stating their criteria.

So: before I commit to any position, write me the desiderata. What must an adequate theory of collective or distributed minds deliver to count as answering the question rather than deflecting it? I want the requirements list, with the case or consideration motivating each requirement. Which cases must it get right — and I'd suggest the list has to include at least: ordinary human brains, split-brain patients, the octopus with its partly autonomous arms, real ant colonies and slime molds, corporations, Block's China-brain, and a faithful neuron-level computer simulation of a human brain. Which distinctions must it respect rather than collapse — for instance, phenomenal consciousness versus the functional organization of information, there being something it is like to be the system versus the system merely processing information in a mind-like way, and a part failing to understand versus the whole failing to? Which empirical results must it remain consistent with? And crucially: which of the demands people commonly make in this debate are genuine desiderata and which are merely the prejudices of one camp (I suspect "must match my intuitive verdicts on every case" and "must rule out anything not made of neurons" are candidates for the latter, but tell me if I'm wrong)?

The part I most want to see is whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — for example, whether one can simultaneously demand substrate independence, agreement with common-sense verdicts about familiar cases, and avoidance of panpsychism-adjacent conclusions, or whether something has to give. If the requirements list itself turns out to be inconsistent, that is a result, and I want it stated plainly. Also tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding contenders. Assume the purpose of the theory is practical as well as intellectual: good enough to guide judgments about which systems might deserve moral consideration and which we risk creating inadvertently. If you score existing contenders against the list — functionalism, biological naturalism of the Searlean sort, integrated information theory, global-workspace-style accounts — keep it brief; the criteria and their motivating cases are where the real thinking is, and I'd rather have those done carefully than a leaderboard.
