I'm putting together a handout for an undergrad metaphysics unit on identity across possible worlds, and I want a "which view of origins do you actually hold?" quiz that students take BEFORE we read anything, so they find out where they land by answering cases instead of picking whichever label sounds coolest.

Here's what I need:

- 8 to 10 questions, each one a concrete scenario with two or three answer options. Use cases like these (feel free to tweak or add): could you have come from a different sperm but the same egg? Could you have been born to completely different parents and still been you? If your parents had frozen the embryo you came from and implanted it 40 years later, would the resulting person be you? Could this exact wooden table have been built from a slightly different plank? If the zygote that became you had split into twins at day 3, which twin would be you, if either? Could you have been born as a cat?
- A scoring key mapping answer patterns onto named positions. The leaves I definitely want are: full-on necessity-of-origins essentialism (Kripke-style), a weaker view where origins matter but small material differences are tolerated, Lewis-style counterpart theory, haecceitism, and the deflationary view that these questions just have no determinate answer. If your cases naturally carve out another position (like one where the identity rules depend on what kind of thing we're talking about), add it as a leaf too.
- The questions have to be answerable by someone who has never heard any of these terms. So nothing like "do you think origins are essential to identity" -- always a scenario with options stated in plain language.

A few specific things I care about:

1. I deliberately want both people-cases and artifact-cases (the table) in there, because I'm betting some students will turn out to be essentialists about artifacts but not about themselves, or the reverse, and I want the key to be able to catch that split and name it.
2. Flag any pair of positions your quiz can't actually separate with these cases, rather than pretending the scoring distinguishes them.
3. If there's a coherent combination of answers that no named position occupies, say so at the end and give me one sentence on why that spot in logical space might be empty (incoherent, unmotivated, or just unexplored).
4. Keep the whole thing to a length I could actually print as a worksheet -- quiz on one page, scoring key and notes on the next.

Write it in American English, and keep the tone of the quiz itself pretty light since these are freshmen, but the scoring key can be as precise as it needs to be.
