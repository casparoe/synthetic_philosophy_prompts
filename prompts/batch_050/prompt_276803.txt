So I keep coming back to Kavka's toxin puzzle and I want to actually understand it rather than just nod along. Quick recap so we're on the same page: an eccentric billionaire offers you a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely *intend* to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin makes you miserably ill for a day or so but does no lasting damage. The payout depends only on your midnight intention -- whether you actually drink is irrelevant, and you'll know that. What I want is an explanation of the conceptual issue, gut-level rather than a formal proof, structured roughly like this:

1. The mechanism. Why exactly is forming the intention supposed to be impossible (or irrational, or however you want to frame it) for a fully rational agent? The rough idea is that at midnight you already know that tomorrow you'll have no reason to actually drink, since the money will already be decided. But I'd like the cleanest statement of *why that blocks the intention now*. Is the problem motivational (you can't bring yourself to), evidential (you can't believe you'll drink, and intending requires believing you will), or normative (you have no reason to intend it, full stop)? Do these come apart?

2. The stances. As far as I can tell there are at least three broad positions people take: (a) the Kavka-ish line that a rational agent simply cannot form the intention and the money is unreachable, which supposedly shows that reasons for action don't automatically generate reasons for intention; (b) the line that the intention *is* rational because the whole plan -- intend tonight, drink tomorrow -- is the best plan available ex ante, even though its final step is locally irrational when you get there; (c) the line that you can get the money through self-manipulation (hypnosis, a pill that makes you resolute, hiring someone to monitor you) but that this misses the point, because the puzzle is about what intentions are, not about clever ways to win prizes. For each, explain what it says about the relationship between reasons-for-intending and reasons-for-acting, and what it has to say about whether a rational agent in this situation ends up poorer than a slightly irrational one.

3. Two variants I made up -- run the analysis on these, since I think they're stress-tests:
   - Variant A ("the slow toxin"): instead of one drink, the toxin must be sipped continuously from 8am to 8pm tomorrow, and you may stop at any point; the total harm is the sum of the sips. Does the puzzle dissolve, stay the same, or get worse? I'm especially curious whether stance (b) fares differently, because now "the plan" has hundreds of separable steps, each of which is locally skippable.
   - Variant B ("the auditor"): no midnight intention check. Instead, tomorrow at noon -- before drinking -- you sit a perfect lie-detector interview, and you get paid iff at that moment you still sincerely intend to drink at 6pm. You know all this tonight. Does moving the check closer to the act change which stance looks best? What if the check is at 5:59pm, one minute before you'd drink?

4. Verdict. What is the puzzle actually *about* -- the nature of intention, the gap between rationality and reasons, time-inconsistency, something else? And if you think some chunk of the disagreement between the stances is verbal rather than substantive, say which chunk and why.

No citations or reading lists needed, I just want the reasoning done carefully.
