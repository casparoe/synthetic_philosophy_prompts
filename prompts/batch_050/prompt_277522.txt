A friend of mine has Huntington's disease in her family — her grandmother had it, her mother has started showing symptoms, so she knows she's at 50% risk herself (the disease is autosomal dominant, one bad copy of the gene is enough, and onset is usually somewhere in middle age, with no cure and no real treatment). Ever since the gene was pinned down in the 90s there's been a reliable predictive test, and it's cheap or even free in a lot of places. She refuses to take it. And apparently she's pretty typical: only a small minority of people who know they're at risk (I've seen numbers like 5-20%) actually go get tested, even though plenty of them describe the not-knowing as agonizing.

This surprised me because I recently learned about Good's theorem (I.J. Good, back in the 60s) in a decision theory course I'm taking: roughly, it says that for an ideal Bayesian agent — someone who updates by conditionalization and always maximizes expected utility — free information can never have negative expected value, so long as it might bear on some decision you face. The proof intuition I was taught is that you can always just ignore the info, so deciding after learning can't be worse on average than committing to a decision before learning. So by the theorem, either my friend is leaving expected utility on the table, or she's irrational, or the theorem was never talking about situations like hers.

I want you to run her case (and the broader pattern it belongs to) against the theorem carefully, in both directions. First, state the theorem and its assumptions precisely enough that we can see exactly what would have to fail for declining the test to be rational — the freeness of the info, the Bayesian updating, the requirement that the information can actually feed into at least one choice downstream, whatever else is load-bearing. Then do the mapping: for an untreatable disease, what decisions does a test result genuinely change (having kids, financial planning, career, how you spend your thirties?) and is "I want to preserve hope" a decision the theorem even recognizes, or does it smuggle in utility over beliefs themselves, wich the theorem brackets by construction? There are also institutional wrinkles: in the US, for instance, the main genetic-discrimination law covers health insurance and employment but not life or long-term-care insurance, so having a positive result on record can literally cost you money — does that just mean the "free information" premise fails and the case is boringly outside the theorem's scope, or is there something deeper going on? Finally, go the other direction: what does the low uptake show about the theorem? Does it refute it, does it vindicate its assumptions as doing real work, or does it reveal that Good's result was never meant to describe creatures whose hope and dread are themselves part of the outcome? End with an actual verdict on whether my friend is being irrational — and be honest about where the theory just goes silent rather than forcing it to speak.
