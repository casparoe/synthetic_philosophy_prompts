I'm applying for a small departmental grant to fund a summer of independent research (3 months, ending in a paper of about 9000 words) and I need a proper proposal, roughly one page. Here's my half-baked idea, please turn it into something fundable.

Background, in case it's not all familliar: there's a cluster of ideas around "strange loops" and "tangled hierarchies" — think of Escher's lithograph Drawing Hands, where two hands are each drawing the other into existence, so the hierarchy of drawer and drawn collapses into a circle. One idea often discussed alongside this is the "inviolate level": when a hierarchy gets tangled, there's supposedly always some level that stays outside the tangle, unaffected, looking down on the loop. In the Drawing Hands case the inviolate level is Escher himself, the actual artist who drew both hands. The thought is sometimes extended to minds: consciousness is a strange loop, but maybe there's some inviolate level of the brain that the self-model can't reach or change.

My half-formed thought is that people slide between two very different claims here, and the slide does all the work. Claim one is epistemic: any self-model must leave things out, because a map as detailed as the territory is useless and arguably impossible, so the "inviolate level" is just whatever got abstracted away. Claim two is causal: the real puzzle is when the model can *affect* the thing modeled — when the drawing hand actually moves the pencil of the other hand — and the inviolate level is whatever is outside the causal loop. If claim one is doing the work, inviolate levels are cheap and everywhere (every model has one trivially). If claim two is, then the interesting question is which systems actually contain causal self-loops, and inviolate levels might be rarer than people assume.

The question I want the project to answer: which of these two notions of inviolate level is the one the strange-loop literature actually needs, and does the answer settle whether a present-day AI system that has a textual self-model — say an LLM fine-tuned on an accurate description of its own architecture, or one that reads its own logs and adjusts its behavior accordingly — contains a genuine strange loop in the causal sense, or only an epistemic sketch of one?

In the proposal, please include: the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; why the obvious quick answers ("it's just the epistemic notion, move on" or "any self-reference is already causal") don't settle it; what I'd actually do month by month (this is conceptual work, not experiments, so be specific about what that involves when there are no citations to gather — I'll add references myself later); what the two or three plausible conclusions are and which considerations would decide between them; and what would count as the project failing, e.g. finding out the two notions collapse into each other after all. Also a short honest bit on why a philosopher and not a computer scientist should be the one doing this.
