A few months ago I started using an online auction site to buy a camera lens I wanted. The way this particular site works is that every time you place a bid, you pay a small fee — about a dollar — and the fee is gone whether or not you win. The item's price only rises by a few cents per bid, so the auction drags on, and the final selling price can end up far below retail, which is the whole draw. I figured I'd bid a few times, and if I won, great. What actually happened is that I've now been in a bidding war with one other person for over a week. I've spent around ninety dollars in fees on a lens that retails for maybe two hundred. Every time I decide to walk away, I think: if I stop now, the ninety dollars was pure waste, whereas if I keep going and he drops out first, I get the lens cheap enough that the whole thing ends up roughly break-even. But I assume he's telling himself exactly the same thing about his own sunk fees, and every round the two of us both pay another dollar.

Here's the odd part: my sister looked at the situation and said I should obviously stop immediately, that I'm throwing good money after bad. But when I asked her at what point I should have stopped, she couldn't answer. If stopping now is right, was stopping at fifty dollars also right? At ten? Was the mistake the very first bid? She also couldn't explain why the site runs the auction this way, but I have a suspicion the design isn't accidental — the seller has collected far more in fees than the lens is worth, and the "winner" will still think he got a deal. Meanwhile, a colleague I described this to made a comparison that bothered me: he said the logic of "I've invested too much to quit now" is the same logic people use to stay in failing business ventures, bad marriages, and unwinnable wars, and that the fact that it feels so compelling is exactly why it's dangerous.

So here are my questions. First: given that the money I've already spent is gone regardless, what should actually govern my decision about the next bid — and does that mean my sister is right for reasons she couldn't articulate, or wrong, because by that same logic quitting was never rational and neither was entering? Second: is there a coherent sense in which each of my individual decisions was reasonable while the overall pattern is clearly a trap, and if so, where exactly does the irrationality live — in the decisions, in the plan, or in something about how people weigh past losses? Third, practically speaking: if I ever face something with this structure again — and I suspect I already have, in contexts that don't look like auctions — what should I have settled in advance, before the first dollar was spent, to keep myself out of it?
