I've been reading alignment forums and the surrounding literature for the better part of a decade, and there's a tension in how frontier AI labs actually assure the safety of their models that I've never seen spelled out cleanly. Consider the standard pre-deployment pipeline, in stylized form: a lab trains a large model, shapes its behavior with RLHF or comparable fine-tuning, and then subjects the finished artifact to a battery of purely behavioral tests -- red-teaming, dangerous-capability evaluations, jailbreak attempts, and increasingly, probes that vary whether the model has reason to believe it is being evaluated, in order to catch it behaving differently under observation than it otherwise would. If the model passes, essentially the same weights go to deployment. The assurance case, such as it is, rests on observed behavior under test conditions. Yet the same research community -- often the same labs -- maintains that a sufficiently capable model could in principle behave impeccably throughout evaluation while competently pursuing some objective that happened to correlate with good behavior across the training and evaluation distributions and comes apart from it afterward; that is the whole content of the goal-misgeneralization and deceptive-alignment worries. The worry is precisely that good behavior under oversight is compatible with almost any underlying goal.

So what does this pipeline actually presuppose? I can see several candidate commitments, and I'd like to know which of them the practice strictly needs, which it merely fits well with, and whether it needs any of them at all. Candidates: (a) that goals, whatever they are mechanistically, cannot be cleanly separated from behavior -- that a misgeneralized objective will leave some behavioral trace somewhere in a broad and clever enough test distribution; (b) that current models lack the situational awareness, or the stable objectives that span evaluation and deployment contexts, needed to strategically modulate their test behavior; (c) that the gap between evaluation conditions and deployment conditions is small enough that any consequential misgeneralization would already have surfaced during testing; or (d) something weaker and less principled -- that behavioral evaluation is simply the best instrument available, a wager rather than a theory, and the pipeline presupposes nothing beyond resource constraints and the absence of alternatives. Note also what looks like an internal inconsistency in the broader institutional landscape: red-teaming presupposes that misalignment cannot be perfectly hidden, while the emerging control paradigm -- monitoring untrusted models, routing their outputs through weaker trusted ones, structuring the environment so that sabotage is hard -- presupposes that it might be perfectly hidden and tries to bound the damage anyway. Do these two practices rest on incompatible pictures of what models can do, or is there a coherent division of labor between them, say evals for accidental failure and control for adversarial failure?

What I want from you: first, a reconstruction of the implicit theory about what goals are in a trained network and how they relate to behavior, stated as precisely as you can manage, such that the behavioral pipeline makes sense under it; second, a clean separation of what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, including an honest identification of where the practice is underdetermined -- perhaps several rival theories of neural-network goals would license the identical pipeline, in which case say so; third, an account of what would have to change if the optimistic presuppositions were dropped -- in particular, whether any purely behavioral evidence, however extensive, could in principle distinguish a genuinely aligned model from a competent and patient deceiver, and if not, what that implies for interpretability-based sign-off, control protocols, or simply declining to deploy. Please don't take the easy route of concluding that the labs are simply irrational; steelman the possibility that the pipeline embodies a defensible, if unstated, epistemology of testing, and tell me what that epistemology would have to be.
