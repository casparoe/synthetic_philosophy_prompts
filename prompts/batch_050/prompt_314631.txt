So I'm a philosophy grad student and in three weeks I'm giving a department colloquium talk defending a reading of Berkeley's master argument, and I need to survive the Q&A. The passage my whole talk turns on is this bit from section 23 of the Principles (A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge):

"When we do our utmost to conceive the existence of external bodies, we are all the while only contemplating our own ideas. But the mind taking no notice of itself, is deluded to think it can and doth conceive bodies existing unthought of or without the mind; though at the same time they are apprehended by or exist in it self."

My claim, roughly: the standard dismissal (the one people associate with Russell, that Berkeley just confuses the act of conceiving with the thing conceived) misses the point. I argue the passage is really posing a dilemma for anyone who wants to use conceivability as evidence for the possibility of mind-independent objects: either the object as you conceive it is itself an idea, in which case your thought experiment proves nothing about mind-independence, or you haven't actually conceived the thing at all, in which case there's no evidence on the table. So the argument isn't a cheap self-reference trick, it's a challenge about what conceivability-based reasoning can ever establish. I also lean on the "mind taking no notice of itself" bit to say Berkeley's diagnosis is basically about a cognitive blind spot, which makes the argument feel less antique.

The audience is a mixed philosophy department: two early modern specialists who care a lot about historical accuracy and will notice if I over-psychologize Berkeley, a metaphysician who works on modality and thinks conceivability is a lousy guide to possibility anyway, a philosopher of mind who's a committed physicalist, and some analytic generalists who mostly know Berkeley from undergrad. No specialists in the recent secondary literature, but the early modern people will have Opinions.

Prepare me for the objections. Specifically: what each of those audience types will actually ask, phrased the way they'd phrase it (the metaphysician will talk about epistemic vs. metaphysical possibility, the early modernists will push on whether this reading is even in the text); which objections have real force against my dilemma reading and which are just the Russell line in new clothes; what I should concede up front to buy goodwill; and what my one strongest reply is. Also tell me the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks, and what I do if the "isn't this still just equivocation" objection comes back after I've given my best answer, because it will.

Please answer in American English, and keep it to roughly 600-700 words, not counting the Berkeley passage above.
