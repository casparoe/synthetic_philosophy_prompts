I serve on the clinical ethics board of a rehabilitation hospital. One of our long-term patients, a man with advanced ALS, communicates through an implanted speech neuroprosthesis whose decoder is assisted by a predictive language model: it reconstructs sentences from his neural activity and, when the signal is ambiguous, completes them in the style of his past speech. He has now used the system to state that he wishes to revoke his advance directive and discontinue ventilatory support. The problem is that his care team has logged several recent exchanges in which he disavowed the device's output, saying it finished thoughts he had not intended, and his family argues that any high-stakes statement passing through an autocomplete layer cannot be attributed to him rather than to the model. Our existing policy on informed consent predates such devices and says only that a patient's statements must be 'reliably his own.' Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before our vote: a concrete recommendation on whether to treat the revocation as valid, with a time frame and any conditions, that takes seriously the strongest version of the family's worry—that fluent output can mask absent or impaired authorship—without simply demanding an impossible standard of unmediated expression that no BCI user could ever meet. Format the opinion as exactly five numbered sections (recommendation; facts assumed; core reasoning; the strongest objection and why it does not carry; safeguards and conditions), each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
