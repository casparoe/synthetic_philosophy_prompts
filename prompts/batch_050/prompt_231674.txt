I've been reading chapter V of Locke's Second Treatise of Government (the property chapter) for a political theory reading group and I think I've found a flat-out contradiction, but everyone else in the group reckons I'm misreading it. Two passages, about a page apart. First he says:

"for this labour being the unquestionable property of the labourer, no man but he can have a right to what that is once joined to, at least where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others."

Then almost immediately he writes:

"Thus the grass my horse has bit; the turfs my servant has cut; and the ore I have digged in any place, where I have a right to them in common with others, become my property, without the assignation or consent of any body. The labour that was mine, removing them out of that common state they were in, hath fixed my property in them."

So hang on. In the first passage labour is the unquestionable property of the labourer and nobody else can have a right to what it's joined to. In the second, the servant does the actual labour of cutting the turfs, and the turfs become the master's property, apparently because the labour counts as his. These can't both be true as stated, surely? Either the labourer's labour is his own, in which case the servant should own the turfs, or labour can just be reattributed to whoever's paying, in which case the first passage is empty.

And it seems to me this unravels the whole chapter. If what matters is who the labour "belongs" to rather than who physically did it, then property doesn't come from mixing your labour with the world at all -- it comes from whoever holds the purse strings, i.e. from prior arrangements and contracts. But Locke's whole point is supposed to be that property arises in the state of nature before any contract or consent. So the turfs passage looks like it quietly gives up the labour theory and replaces it with a contract theory, which defeats the entire arguement.

Am I right that this is a genuine contradiction Locke can't wriggle out of, or is there a reading where both passages stand? And if the servant's labour really does count as the master's, what's stopping me "appropriating" half of unowned England just by hiring enough servants -- does the bit about leaving "enough, and as good" for others even constrain that?
