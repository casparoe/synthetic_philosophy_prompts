In discussions of forecasting and rational judgment, the "inside view" refers to reasoning from the particular details of the case at hand—its mechanisms, intentions, and distinguishing features—while the "outside view" refers to setting those details aside and assigning one's case to a reference class, adopting the base rate of outcomes in that class as one's own forecast. Practitioners of both are typically told to blend them, weighting each according to its reliability. Consider, however, a stronger position, which we may call the Displacement Thesis: when a well-established base rate conflicts with a confident inside-view judgment, rationality requires not a compromise between the two but the complete displacement of the inside-view judgment by the base rate—at least until the person holding the inside-view judgment can demonstrate, from a track record of past predictions, that judgments of that kind have historically outperformed the base rate in question.

The standard objection to the Displacement Thesis is that it is self-undermining. The decision to defer to a base rate is itself a judgment, made in a particular case, on the basis of reasoning about mechanisms and features—precisely the sort of thinking the thesis tells us to discard. If the thesis is correct, the objector argues, we have no non-circular warrant for adopting it; and if we may use inside-view reasoning to adopt it, we have already conceded that such reasoning can be authoritative after all.

Please argue that the Displacement Thesis survives this objection and is correct as stated. In the course of your argument, address at least the following: (1) whether there is an asymmetry between first-order judgments and the second-order policy of deferring to base rates that blocks the charge of self-undermining, and whether that asymmetry does the needed work or merely relocates the problem; (2) why weighted averaging, the standard compromise, is less defensible than outright displacement—consider, for instance, that an agent who averages must still estimate the weight to give their own judgment, which is itself an inside-view assessment; and (3) what the thesis implies for cases where the relevant base rate is genuinely contested or the reference class is ambiguous, and why those cases do not defeat the thesis in its core domain. You may illustrate with a concrete forecasting scenario of your own construction, but the argument should be general rather than case-bound. Write in American English.
