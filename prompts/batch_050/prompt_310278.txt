I teach a first-year seminar on the Meditations and after class last week two of my stronger students, Nadia and Tomasz, got into an argument about the cogito while packing up their stuff. It was good enough that I stayed out of it and just listened. Neither would budge, and they asked me to referee at the start of next class. I have my own views but I'd like a second pair of eyes before I weigh in, partly because I'm not sure my read is fair to both of them. Here's the exchange as best I can recal it:

(1) Nadia opened by saying the cogito is basically a trick of grammar. All Descartes is really entitled to is that thinking is going on; "I think" smuggles in the subject it was supposed to establish. Compare "it's raining" -- we don't infer a hidden rainer lurking behind the rain.

(2) Tomasz replied that doubting isn't weather. The whole setup is "I may be deceived by the demon," and a deception has to happen to someone. Subjectless doubting isn't a possibility the demon leaves open, because if there's no one there there's nothing to deceive and the whole exercise collapses before it starts.

(3) Nadia said fine, maybe you get a thinker, but only for the instant of the thinking. The cogito can't tell you the thing thinking now is the same thing that was thinking a second ago, so at best you get a momentary subject, not a self. And Descartes quietly helps himself to the strong version -- I am a thinking *thing*, a substance -- when only the weak one was earned.

(4) Tomasz said he's happy to concede the persistence point but she's judging the cogito against a job description it never applied for. It's a foothold, not a theory of personal identity; the continuity gets added later, off the back of the God argument. And he pushed back on her opening move too: the content of the doubt is first-personal -- the thought is *I may be deceived* -- and you can't restate that subjectlessly without losing the very thought that's supposed to be indubitable. Unlike "the present king of France is bald," which you can perfectly well think without any king existing, thinking "I exist" makes it true. It's self-verifying in a way the grammar point doesn't touch.

(5) Nadia's last move: self-verifying cuts less ice than he thinks. What makes the thought occur while it's thought is one thing; what guarantees a continuing bearer of "I" is another, and he already gave her that in his concession. So even on his own terms the cogito proves at most that a deception-token exists at a time, and Descartes's later use of it still rests on the unearned upgrade.

Then they both looked at me and I mumbled something about next week.

What I'd like from you: go through the five moves one at a time. For each, say whether it clearly lands, clearly fails, or lands-with-qualifications, and why. Then give an overall verdict: who is right about what the cogito actually proves, if either of them is? And tell me what they're *both* missing. One thing I noticed in the moment is that they both just assumed the cogito is an argument with premises and a conclusion -- I seem to remember Descartes himself insisting somewhere in the Replies that it isn't a syllogism at all, that you grasp your own existence directly. If that's right, does it change the scorecard for any of the five moves, or does it just relocate the fight?
