A mate of mine who sits on our local planning committee has one move, and it's the fence analogy. His version: if you come across a fence built across a road or round a field and you can't see what it's for, you're not entitled to tear it down -- first you work out why someone went to the trouble of building it, and only if that reason is genuinely dead can you take it apart. (It's the one that usually gets attributed to Chesterton, though he's never read him.) He wheels it out for everything: an old licensing rule for market traders, a bylaw about where pubs can put tables, even the committee's own standing orders. And the conclusion is always the same -- keep the thing until the reformer has done the homework, and the burden of proof sits entirely with whoever wants the change.

I've got a creeping feeling the analogy is doing more work than he admits. He reaches for it even in cases where we *do* know why the rule exists and the reason still seems terrible, and "leave it up while we investigate" isn't actually costless, because the rule keeps doing whatever it does in the meantime. So I want a proper audit. What maps onto what: the fence, the builder, the field, the traveller who didn't build it, the tearing down, the going-away-to-think. Then the disanalogies -- but sorted into the ones that actually matter for how *he* uses it versus the ones that are just pedantic. The ones I suspect matter: rules aren't inert like fences, they shape behaviour, so sometimes the reason for the rule only still exists *because* the rule exists; rules carry ongoing costs and harms while a fence mostly just stands there; and sometimes the builder's reason is perfectly well documented, or the fence was put up to keep particular people out of the field and that was the whole point. But tell me which of those genuinely bite, and whether there are better ones I've missed.

Then the verdict: does his general conclusion -- burden always on the reformer, keep everything until proven removable -- survive, or does only a much weaker version make it through? And for the cases where the fence picture breaks down, is there a better analogy for what scrapping a rule is actually like?

Format: give me the correspondence as a table with columns -- fence-story element | policy-case counterpart | does the mapping hold (yes / partly / no) | why it matters for his argument. Then a short prose discussion of the disanalogies, then the verdict as a JSON object with fields: conclusion_survives (one of "full", "weakened", "no"), weakest_link (the single disanalogy doing the most damage), salvageable_core (one sentence on what the analogy still genuinely shows), better_analogy_for_failure_cases (a short phrase). And write the whole thing in British English, please.
