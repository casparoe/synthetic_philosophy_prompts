I understand that conventions like tipping, standing in line, and waiting at crosswalks are essentially arbitrary—any number of alternative arrangements would have coordinated the same activities, and no one ever actually agreed to the arrangement we happen to have. Since moral obligations either arise from consent or from the content of the act itself (helping, harming, promising), and these conventions have neither, it seems to follow that violating them is morally neutral, even if rude. A tourist who never tips in a country where tipping is expected, or who walks straight to the front of a queue, may be breaking a local rule but isn't doing anything wrong, since the rule never acquired moral force through anyone's agreement. Given this, when people say visitors "ought" to follow local conventions, are they just confusing etiquette with morality, or is there something I've overlooked? If there is a genuine obligation here, explain precisely where it comes from and why the absence of consent doesn't defeat it.
