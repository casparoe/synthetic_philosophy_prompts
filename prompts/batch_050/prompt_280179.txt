You are a professional philosopher with particular expertise in social ontology and the metaphysics of kinds, preparing a graduate seminar session. I am a philosophy professor, and a strong graduate student has presented me with the argument below, which purports to show that the familiar thesis that gender and race are socially constructed kinds cannot be stated in a way that is both coherent and substantive. The argument feels wrong to me, but I have not been able to put my finger on the exact point of failure, and I want a rigorous diagnosis before the seminar meets. Please think carefully and do not rush to the first plausible objection.

Here is the argument, as the student gave it:

(1) Money is the paradigm of a socially constructed kind: slips of paper and entries in ledgers are money because, and only because, a community collectively accepts them as a medium of exchange. Remove the acceptance and the paper and ink remain, but the money does not. Nothing was money before such practices existed.

(2) So for a kind to be socially constructed is for its members to be members of it in virtue of our attitudes and practices: construction is a dependence of the kind's membership on our minds.

(3) But which groupings we single out at all, and where we draw the boundaries of any kind, is always fixed by our interests, purposes, and classificatory practices. Consider the line between mountain and hill; the distinction between a weed and a mere plant; the vote by which astronomers settled whether Pluto counts as a planet. Even in the sciences, the kinds we actually employ are the kinds our practices have carved out.

(4) Hence for any kind we can actually talk about, things count as its members, and are grouped together as its members, only in virtue of our practices -- precisely the dependence that (2) identified as social construction.

(5) So every kind we can discuss is socially constructed in exactly the sense in which money is.

(6) The constructionist thesis about gender and race was supposed to be substantive and revisionary: a claim true of those kinds but false of tigers and electrons. If (5) is correct, the thesis is true of everything and revises nothing. And if the constructionist restricts the thesis to avoid triviality, the restriction requires a principled line between the constructed and the non-constructed -- but (3) shows there is no such line, only degrees of one and the same dependence on us.

(7) Therefore the thesis that gender, or race, is a socially constructed kind is either trivially true of all kinds whatsoever, or incapable of being stated in a way that marks a genuine contrast with natural kinds.

I would like to preserve both (1) and the intended contrast in (6); I am confident the slippage occurs somewhere between (2) and (5).

Your task, in four parts. First, a brief brainstorming phase: set out at least three distinct candidate diagnoses of the argument, naming the specific premise or inferential step each one targets, before you commit to any of them. Second, select the diagnosis you judge strongest, state precisely which step it rejects and under which disambiguation the argument fails, and explain why that step looked compelling -- a good diagnosis must account for the argument's plausibility, not merely locate its error. Third, address the intermediate cases directly: explain why weeds and the Pluto case do not by themselves collapse the distinction between money and tigers, and assess whether a constructionist who concedes that constructedness comes in degrees has given up the substantive thesis or merely restated it. Fourth, price the alternatives: for each of the main lines of resistance available to the constructionist (including ones you reject), say what it costs and which commitment it forces her to surrender.

Do not supply citations or a bibliography; I will add references myself. Conclude with a one-paragraph verdict suitable for me to deliver to the student at the start of seminar.
