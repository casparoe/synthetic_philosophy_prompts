So I cover physics and philosophy of science for a magazine, and I'm writing a piece on whether time "flows." I keep running into this argument, usually traced back to Rietdijk and Putnam, that special relativity basically forces eternalism: if which events count as "now" depends on your reference frame, and moving observers disagree about simultaneity, then events I take to be future are already settled-present relative to someone else, so the whole block universe is supposedly unavoidable. I asked a physicist friend about it over beers and he said it's airtight; a philosopher friend said it's a notorious overreach. They can't both be right and honestly I can't tell who's bluffing.

Here's what I'd like. Give me your credence, as an actual number, that the following proposition is true: special relativity, on its own, without any further metaphysical premises, entails that the future is as real and determinate as the present (i.e., eternalism or the block universe). Note I said "on its own" -- that's doing a lot of work, so treat it seriously.

But I don't just want the number, I want the derivation. Decompose your credence into the sub-questions it depends on -- for example: the probability that the relativity of simultaneity is a fact about reality rather than about coordinate conventions or our epistemic access; the probability that simultaneity-relative "reality" claims can be made coherent at all (the Rietdijk-Putnam argument needs some relation like "is real for" to be transitive, and that step is contested); the probability that quantum indeterminacy, if fundamental, blocks the argument even if relativity goes through; and whatever other terms you think genuinely belong. Give a rough probability and one line of justification for each, say how you combine them (and flag if the terms aren't independent, since I suspect they aren't), and land on the final number.

Then the part I actually need for the article: tell me which term in your decomposition your final credence is most sensitive to, and what specific argument or result -- from physics or metaphysics -- would move it most. If my physicist friend is right and the number is very high, tell me what the best anti-eternalist would have to establish to bring it down. If the philosophers are right and it's low, tell me what the strongest pro-block-universe evidence would be. I'm trying to figure out where the real disagreement lives, because I suspect both of my friends are skipping the step where their position is weakest.
