Okay so here's the situation. I work at a mid-sized hospital, and in two weeks we're holding what I can only describe as a retirement party for a metric. Fifteen years ago we introduced the "92-minute rule" -- the target that 95% of patients coming through our emergency department would be seen, treated, and either admitted or discharged within 92 minutes. It was a big deal, we built a whole dashboard around it, bonuses were tied to it, the works. Leadership has finally decided to scrap it, and the outgoing CEO wants a speech at the send-off event. I've been volun-told to write and deliver it.

Here's why it's delicate. The first five years, the metric genuinely worked -- waits dropped, people got seen faster, it probably saved lives. But the last several years have been a masterclass in Goodhart's law: patients getting "admitted" to a hallway bed at minute 88 so the clock stopped, ambulances asked to wait in the parking lot, staff gaming timestamps, entire teams reorganized around hitting the number rather than treating people. Everyone in the room knows this.

The audience is the hard part. It's maybe 60 people: half are nurses and doctors who *hated* this metric and consider it a symbol of everything wrong with management-by-spreadsheet. The other half is the analytics and quality-improvement team who built the dashboard -- including Priya, the woman who designed the 92-minute rule back in the day, who will be sitting front row. Her view, which she's said publicly, is that the metric never failed: it kept measuring exactly what it always measured, and *we* failed by forgetting the number was only ever a proxy for the thing we actually cared about. She thinks scrapping metrics wholesale is the real lesson people will wrongly take from this.

So the speech has to thread a needle: honor what the metric accomplished, be honest about the gaming without humiliating Priya or the analysts, let the clinicians feel *seen* without turning it into a roast of the QI team, and land on something true about why this kind of failure isn't really anybody's fault -- but also isn't nobody's fault, if that makes sense. The philosophical idea should do real work in the speech, not just be a name-drop; I want the structure of the speech to basically *be* an argument about the difference between a target going bad and the people around a target going bad.

Pretend you're a speechwriter who also happens to know a lot about philosophy of science and Goodhart's law / specification gaming type stuff. Think carefully about the angle first -- maybe brainstorm 2-3 possible framings and pick the strongest one before writing. Then write the full speech, around 4-5 minutes when read aloud. No fake statistics please, and don't invent a famous quote to stick at the top -- I want it to sound like a real person talking, not a LinkedIn post.
