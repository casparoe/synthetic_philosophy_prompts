In my philosophy of mind course this term, the phrase "the ghost in the machine" -- Ryle's derisive label for the Cartesian picture -- keeps doing argumentative work that it doesn't seem entitled to do. Our lecturer introduced it as a slogan summarizing Ryle's critique of substance dualism, but in seminars and in one classmate's essay I've since seen it deployed against much weaker targets: the idea that there are inner mental states at all, the intelligibility of first-person authority, even property dualism and panpsychism, as though repeating the phrase settled those questions too. When I pressed my classmate on what exactly the slogan claims, he couldn't say more than that dualism is obviously wrong.

Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses the slogan might be expressing? For example, I can imagine it meaning anything from the trivial (the mind is not a small person literally housed inside the skull), to the substantive (mental vocabulary never had the job of referring to states of a non-physical substance, and treating it as if it did is a category mistake), to the very strong (talk of inner episodes, sensations, and privileged self-knowledge is confused tout court), and presumably several readings in between concerning what it is for mental concepts to be applied and understood. For each reading, I'd like a verdict: is it trivially true, substantive and defensible, or overstated -- and under that last heading, I'd especially like to know whether rejecting a Cartesian immaterial subject actually commits one to rejecting inner mental life or first-person authority, as the slogan's more aggressive users seem to assume.

Finally, given where I actually met the phrase, could you say which of these readings, if any, would support the use my classmate made of it -- dismissing any position on which the mental is not straightforwardly reducible to the physical? If none of the defensible readings get him that conclusion, I'd like to understand where the gap lies, since the phrase's rhetorical force in our seminars seems to derive entirely from conflating the different things it might mean.
