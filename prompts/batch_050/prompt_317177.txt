I'm in a philosophy of language course this semester and I got into a dumb argument with my roommate about generics, those plain little sentences like "ducks lay eggs" that somehow stay true even when tons of ducks don't do the thing. My working theory was simple: a generic is true when the property is statistically normal for the kind, roughly when most ordinary members have it. But when I tried to check that against my gut verdicts on cases, it fell apart almost immediately, and now I want to know whether any principle can capture my intuitions or whether I have to give some of them up.

Here are my verdicts. "Ducks lay eggs" is clearly true, even though male ducks never lay eggs and alot of females are too young or too old, so well under half of all ducks ever lay one. "Ducks are male" is clearly false, even though about half of ducks are male, which already annoys me, because why does a property held by under half pass while one held by about half fails? "Mosquitoes carry malaria" strikes me as true, even though at any given moment only a tiny fraction of mosquitoes actually carry it. But "horses are black" is false, even though black horses are surely more common than malaria-carrying mosquitoes. Last summer I drove through a stretch of countryside where nearly every barn was red, and it still felt wrong to say "barns are red." "Birds fly" is obviously true even though penguins, ostriches, and baby birds don't. And "sharks attack swimmers" — this is the one I go back and forth on, because almost no shark ever attacks anyone, yet the sentence feels basically fine, or at least way more acceptable than "horses are black" despite the much worse statistics.

So what principle, if any, gets all of these, or close to all? State it precisely, test it against each case, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop if I want something simpler — I suspect "sharks attack swimmers" or the mosquito one has to go, but I'd rather be told than guess. Then tell me what your repaired principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: in the town where I grew up, about half of teenagers have tried a cigarette at least once but only a small share smoke regularly — is "teenagers smoke" true there, and why? Also say whether you think these sentences are literally true or just acceptable loose talk, since my roommate insists the whole thing is pragmatics and no real semantics is needed. Please answer in continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — just write it like an email — and in American English.
