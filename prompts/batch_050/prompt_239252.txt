Ok so I keep running into versions of the Pascal's mugging problem in ordinary life and I want to figure out what rule I'm actually using, because my gut answers don't seem consistent. Here's where I land on cases:

- Guy on the street says give me your wallet or I'll snap my fingers and destroy the universe, and he's wearing a lab coat so he looks faintly credible: not paying, obviously.
- A stranger emails me saying they've built an AI that will simulate and torture a trillion copies of me unless I send them $40: I delete the email and feel zero guilt.
- A friend argues I should donate to asteroid-deflection research because even a one-in-a-million chance of extinction this century makes the expected value huge: I'm genuinely tempted, seems like a real consideration.
- Someone argues I should take religion seriously purely on wager grounds, infinite stakes and all: I find this totally unpersuasive even though I can't articulate why it's different from the asteroid thing.
- My university offers a deal where for a small fee they insure my diploma against the tiny chance of a "credential apocalypse" where all degrees become worthless: instinct says scam, but the expected value math they showed me actually checks out on their numbers.
- I decline to spend even one second worrying that I'm a brain in a vat, but I do spend real money on travel insurance with absurdly specific clauses.
- A startup pitches that there's a 0.001% chance their app stops a catastrophic bioterror attack, and asks me to work for them at half my salary: no. But if the same numbers came from the WHO asking for a donation of the same amount, I'd seriously consider it.

So cases 1, 2, 4, 5, 7 feel like clear no's and 3 and 6 feel like yes's, but I can't find the line. It's not just "ignore small probabilities" because of the asteroid. It's not "ignore people who stand to gain from your belief" because the WHO donation also benefits people who asked. My tentative rule was something like: dismiss any threat or reward that's generated by the other person's claim rather than existing independently of it -- the mugger's threat only exists because he says so, the asteroid exists whether or not my friend mentions it. But case 5 breaks that, since the credential apocalypse is also just their claim, and I still feel differently about 5 than about 2, worse somehow.

What principle actually captures this set? Or do two principles do it jointly? And if the only clean principle forces me to flip one of these verdicts, tell me which one should go and why -- I'd rather have an honest account than save all my intuitions. Also curious what it implies for a case I haven't resolved: donating to a group that claims a small chance of preventing human extinction from something most experts consider science fiction.
