I am preparing notes on Aristotle's De Interpretatione for a graduate reading group, working from E. M. Edghill's Oxford translation. In chapter 4 (16b26–17a7), Aristotle writes:

"A sentence is a significant portion of speech, some parts of which have an independent meaning, that is to say, as an utterance, though not as the expression of any positive judgement. Let me explain. The word 'human' has meaning, but does not constitute a proposition, either positive or negative. It is only when other words are added that the whole will form an affirmation or denial. But if we separate one syllable of the word 'human' from the other, it has no meaning; similarly in the word 'mouse', the part 'ouse' has no meaning in itself, but is merely a sound. In composite words, indeed, the parts contribute to the meaning of the whole; yet, as has been pointed out, they have not an independent meaning.

Every sentence has meaning, not as being the natural means by which a physical faculty is realized, but, as we have said, by convention. Yet every sentence is not a proposition; only such are propositions as have in them either truth or falsity. Thus a prayer is a sentence, but is neither true nor false.

Let us therefore dismiss all other types of sentence but the proposition, for this last concerns our present inquiry, whereas the investigation of the others belongs rather to the study of rhetoric or of poetry."

Here is where I am stuck. In chapter 9, Aristotle considers the case of a naval engagement that may or may not take place tomorrow, and argues that if the corresponding affirmation or denial were already true or already false, then nothing could be otherwise than it will be and deliberation would be pointless. On a familiar reading of that chapter, his conclusion is that such future-tense claims about contingent events are, as of now, neither true nor false. But if that is right, then by the criterion stated in the passage above -- that only sentences which "have in them either truth or falsity" count as propositions -- a sentence asserting that there will be a sea battle tomorrow would not be a proposition at all, any more than a prayer is. That seems absurd, since chapter 9's whole argument treats it as exactly the kind of sentence whose truth status is at issue, and since a prayer and a prediction about a battle are plainly not defective in the same way.

I would like help with three things:

1. Reconstruct the reasoning of the quoted passage step by step: why does Aristotle move from the claim that sentences are significant by convention to the restriction of propositions to truth-bearers, and what work is the prayer example doing?

2. Explain the main options for resolving the tension I have described -- for instance, reading the criterion as requiring aptness for truth or falsity rather than actual possession of one or the other; treating chapter 4's definition as provisional or loose; or denying that chapter 9 rejects bivalence in the first place (on the alternative reading, Aristotle's target is only the inference from the necessity of the disjunction to the necessity of each disjunct). Which of these options fits the quoted passage best, and does the tension look sharper or milder depending on which reading of the sea-battle chapter one adopts?

3. Tell me whether anything in the quoted passage itself, or in the fact that Aristotle explicitly assigns prayers to rhetoric and poetics rather than to his present inquiry, gives us independent evidence for how he would classify a future-contingent assertion he took to lack a truth value.

Finally, please be careful to separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this text. I know that the interpretation of chapter 9 is one of the most disputed questions in Aristotelian scholarship, and I would rather be told that a crux is unresolved -- and why -- than be given one reading presented as the consensus. Where the answer turns on the translation or on the Greek terms lying behind words like "proposition," please flag that explicitly rather than letting the English do silent work.
