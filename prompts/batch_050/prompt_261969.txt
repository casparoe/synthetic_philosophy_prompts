For my ethics seminar i'm writing an essay on Nolan's The Dark Knight read through the paradox of deontology — the puzzle that if rights violations are so terrible, you seemingly ought to violate one right to prevent five worse violations, yet constraints forbid exactly that. The ferry scene basically stages it: each boat can blow up the other to save itself, so blowing one up is the way to minimize deaths, and the film's "answer" is just that nobody pushes the button, which is faith in ordinary people rather than an actual solution to the puzzle. Meanwhile Batman's one rule is don't kill, but he happily builds a city-wide surveillance machine that Lucius treats as an abuse no one person should have, and at the end Harvey Dent dies when Batman tackles him to save Gordon's kid — so is the film's constraint even coherent? Give me a reading that takes the philosophy seriously (agent-relative vs outcome reasons, the minimizing-violations objection, whether the film conflates personal rule-worship with genuine constraints) but doesn't flatten the movie into a thought experiment. Does the film actually have a view on why constraints bind, or does it just find them dramatically useful? And where does it honestly resist the deontological reading?
