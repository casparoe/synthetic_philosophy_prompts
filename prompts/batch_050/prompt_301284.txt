I'm preparing the discussion materials for a graduate seminar on philosophy of technology, and this term I'm pairing Langdon Winner's essay Do Artifacts Have Politics? with a historical case the essay itself never touches: Baron Haussmann's rebuilding of Paris under Napoleon III, roughly 1853 to 1870. The standard account, which you can take as given, is that Haussmann drove immense straight boulevards through the old medieval fabric of the city, and that among the motives conventionally attributed to the project was counter-insurgency: broad avenues would be hard to barricade, would let cavalry and artillery move quickly into restive working-class districts, and would break up the dense neighborhoods where revolts had incubated in 1789, 1830, and 1848. Equally conventional is the observation that the motives were never only that: the boulevards also served sanitation, traffic circulation, water and sewer infrastructure, slum clearance and real-estate speculation, imperial spectacle, and the employment of large numbers of workers. And there is the famous coda: when the Paris Commune rose in 1871, insurgents built barricades across the new boulevards anyway, and the army's recapture of the city owed at least as much to numbers and ruthlessness as to street geometry.

For readers who haven't memorized the essay, Winner's core claims are these: first, that technical arrangements can embody forms of authority and exclusion in their very design (his example is the allegedly low overpasses on Long Island parkways, which kept buses — and hence the poor and Black people who relied on buses — off Jones Beach); and second, more strongly, that some technologies are inherently political in that adopting them requires, or at least strongly tends toward, particular kinds of social organization (his example being nuclear power and its apparent demand for centralized, secrecy-heavy, quasi-authoritarian management). I want an analysis of the Haussmann case through both of these claims, in both directions. Which of Winner's two theses, if either, actually describes the boulevards — are they an instance of politics inscribed in an artifact, or does the multiplicity of motives mean the political reading is just one story among several that the stones underdetermine? Does the political content of an artifact depend on the designer's intent (Haussmann and Napoleon III were fairly candid about strategic considerations, unlike the murkier Moses case), or can a boulevard be counter-insurgent in effect regardless of why it was built?

Then run the case back against the theory. The boulevards conspicuously failed at the politics allegedly built into them: barricades went up in 1871 regardless, and the avenues proved at least as hospitable to mass demonstration, festive occupation, commerce, café life, and eventually the métro as to cavalry charges. What should Winner's framework say about an artifact whose inscribed politics misfire, or which users wholesale repurpose — is that a counterexample to the claim that artifacts have politics, a refinement of it (politics in artifacts are real but defeasible, more like affordances than scripts), or a sign that the theory silently assumes the designer's vision survives contact with actual use? Finally, locate the case on the determinism spectrum: does Haussmann's Paris support a strong reading on which built form compels social outcomes, a weak reading on which it merely shifts costs and probabilities, or does it suggest that the politics of artifacts live less in the artifacts than in the enforceable social arrangements — policing, property law, military organization — that the artifacts presuppose? I'd like the answer to be disciplined about the mapping throughout: say exactly which feature of the case answers to which element of Winner's theory, and say plainly where the theory simply has nothing to say rather than stretching it to cover the case.
