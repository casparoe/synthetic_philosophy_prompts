After four decades as a working biologist I have retired and, somewhat to my own surprise, joined a local philosophy reading group. The group has lately been circling questions about meaning, communication, and trust, and I have been asked to lead a forty-minute session on costly signaling theory — in particular the handicap principle, the result that a signal can be reliable precisely because it is expensive, and because it is more expensive for a low-quality signaler to fake than for a high-quality signaler to produce honestly. The group has essentially no biology beyond school level, and I expect them to hear words like "signal," "honest," and "costly" as carrying far more intentionality than the theory licenses.

Do not explain the theory to me — I know it. Plan how I should explain it. In particular:

First, the order of ideas. Which concepts must be securely in place before the handicap principle itself can land (conflicts of interest between signaler and receiver, the instability of cheap claims, the idea that a cost structure can sort hidden types), and why does each depend on what precedes it?

Second, the single example that should carry the session. I am torn between the peacock's train and stotting in gazelles. Tell me which to build on and why, specifying what the carrying example must have (a clear conflict of interest, a visible and plausible cost asymmetry) and what it must lack (associations with human courtship or language, contested empirical status that would derail a lay audience).

Third, the misconception map. I anticipate at least these: the group-selection misreading that signaling evolved for the good of the species; the reading of the principle as saying that wastefulness is somehow virtuous; the conflation of absolute cost with differential cost; the assumption that a signal requires a sender with intentions; and the overgeneralization that the principle claims every reliable signal must be a handicap. For each, state exactly where in the sequence I should head it off, and how.

Fourth, one closing question I can pose that will reveal whether they have grasped the stability argument rather than merely retained a slogan.

Finally, what I should openly decline to cover at this level — the formal models, the equilibrium analysis, the empirical disputes — and how to say so without seeming evasive. One more thing: the philosophers will inevitably press me on whether "honesty" here is literal or metaphorical. Position the plan so that this question arrives as a natural capstone rather than an interruption in minute five.
