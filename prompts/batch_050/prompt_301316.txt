The debate between presentism and eternalism appears to have reached a standstill. Presentists maintain that only present objects and events exist: dinosaurs and future cities belong to reality only in the derivative sense that reality was or will be a certain way. They argue that this best preserves change, temporal passage, and the apparent asymmetry between a fixed past and an open future. Eternalists maintain that past, present, and future objects and events all exist equally, with pastness and futurity functioning more like spatial relations than differences in ontological status. They argue that this best explains cross-temporal reference, relations among non-simultaneous events, and the treatment of time in modern physics. Yet both sides can accommodate ordinary tensed speech, agree on the observed sequence of events, and offer reconstructions of the other side’s central claims.

My working hypothesis is that the debate fuses at least three distinct disagreements: whether existence is absolute or indexical; whether presentness is a metaphysical feature of reality or merely a perspective from within it; and whether an adequate theory must explain the felt difference between past, present, and future or only systematize our temporal inferences. Please evaluate that hypothesis. What kind of disagreement is really at issue here—an ontological dispute about what exists, a semantic dispute about unrestricted quantification, a methodological dispute about what metaphysical theories owe to experience, or several questions being argued under one heading? Identify which standard pressure points, such as truthmaking for past-tense claims, cross-temporal relations, temporal passage, and appeals to physics, represent genuine constraints and which merely shift the burden back and forth. Finally, say what would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a formal result, new empirical evidence, a reconstruction of one side’s commitments, or an argument that the central question should be replaced. If no development could settle the dispute, explain why its persistence reflects its structure rather than a temporary lack of ingenuity.
