Here's the exam question: "We often say things like 'the gardener's failure to water the plant caused its death.' But the Queen of England also failed to water the plant. Explain the problem this creates for treating omissions as causes, present at least one way philosophers have tried to solve it, and assess whether the solution works."

And here's my partner's answer:

When we say the gardener's failure to water the plant caused its death, we're treating an absence as a cause, and that creates the problem of proliferation: millions of people didn't water the plant, including the Queen of England, so if omissions count as causes whenever the effect counterfactually depends on them, then every non-waterer becomes a cause of the plant's death, which is absurd. The standard test for causation is the but-for test: but for the gardener's not watering, the plant would have lived. But the same is true of the Queen. The most common solution is to say that only omissions that deviate from a norm or expectation count as causes. The gardener had a duty to water the plant, so his omission stands out against the background of what was supposed to happen; the Queen had no such duty, so her omission is a non-cause. This solution comes from David Lewis, who argued that absences cannot be causes because an absence is not an event and therefore cannot stand in causal relations. The norm-based solution works well for ordinary causal talk, and since the but-for test counts omissions as causes, omissions must count as causes in every context, including moral and legal ones. So the gardener's omission caused the death, the Queen's did not, and the problem is solved.
