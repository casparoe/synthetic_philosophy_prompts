For my upper-level seminar on the ethics of virtual action, I want to pressure-test an analogy my students reach for constantly: that a video-game character is like a character in a novel and the player like the novelist, so that nothing done to a non-player character can be morally better or worse than what an author puts on a page. They deploy it to dissolve the puzzle of why virtual murder strikes nearly everyone as permissible while certain other virtual acts strike nearly everyone as repugnant: if it is all just fiction, the differential reaction is mere squeamishness with nothing underneath it. Please audit the analogy properly. Begin with the correspondence table — what plays the role of the author, the page, the character-as-description, the reader — then locate the breaks: the player's agency versus the novelist's, the persistence of NPCs with memory across sessions, the difference between representing suffering and instantiating a process that reproduces its functional profile, and the difference between what an act expresses about its performer and what it does to its object. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic. Then commit: does the fiction analogy carry the permissive conclusion, does it fail only at the margins, or is it the wrong tool altogether? If the last, supply a better one (theatrical improvisation, doll-play, a flight simulator, or another candidate of your choosing) and show that it handles the cases the novel analogy cannot — including procedurally generated NPCs whom no human ever authored, and a game in which the engine, not the player, determines what happens to them.
