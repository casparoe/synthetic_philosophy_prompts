A colleague presented this argument at our decision theory reading group, and it bothers me because the conclusion is absurd, yet I can't locate the error. The setup is the standard dollar auction: a $1 bill is auctioned, the highest bidder wins it, but the two highest bidders both pay their bids.

(1) Consider any moment at which you are the second-highest bidder, having bid $X while the leader stands at $X + d, d ≤ $1. Folding means a certain loss of $X; bidding $X + d + ε gives you some chance of winning the dollar and capping your loss. Given suitable beliefs about how much longer the other bidder will persist, the bid has higher expected value than folding. So no individual bid in the auction is irrational in itself.

(2) Rationality of a choice is assessed at the time of choice, given the agent's information at that time. A course of action composed entirely of choices each rational at their time is therefore rational as a whole; you cannot condemn a sequence without condemning some step in it.

(3) Hence an escalation to $5, $20, or $200 for a one-dollar bill can be a sequence of entirely rational choices.

(4) But it is obvious ex ante that escalation is where such an auction leads once two stubborn bidders are involved, and sitting out entirely is strictly better than any escalation.

(5) Therefore the only rational policy is never to bid at all. And the point generalizes: any iterated commitment -- a degree program, a startup, a lawsuit, a war of attrition -- can develop the auction's structure once sunk costs accumulate, so rational agents should never begin anything they might later be reluctant to abandon.

Step (5) proves too much; it would paralyze ordinary life. Which step do you actually reject? Note that I'd like to keep (1) if possible, since denying it seems to require claiming that agents can have a duty to hold beliefs about the rival's future behavior that the evidence doesn't support.
