At a family dinner last month, an uncle defended the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki with a single sentence: the bomb saved a million lives. I've since heard the same line from a coworker, in a podcast, and in the comments under a newspaper article, always delivered as a conversation-ender. I want to know what that sentence could actually mean, because it seems to me that several very different claims are traveling together under it.

Here is what I'd like. First, unpack the slogan into its distinct component theses. I can already see candidates: a causal claim that the bombings, rather than the Soviet entry into the war, blockade, or the prospect of modified surrender terms, produced Japan's surrender; a counterfactual claim that the war would otherwise have continued to a full invasion of the home islands; a quantitative claim about the casualties that invasion would have inflicted (and note that the slogan never says whose lives -- American, Allied, or Japanese -- count toward the million); a comparative claim that bombing deaths fell short of the counterfactual deaths; and a moral claim that this arithmetic settles the permissibility of targeting two cities. Lay these out as a menu, distinguishing claims the slogan literally asserts from claims its users typically presuppose.

Second, give a verdict on each component: which are near-platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are contentious among serious historians, and which are simply false or unfalsifiable (I suspect the precision of the figure itself belongs in this last category, but correct me if I'm wrong). In particular, tell me whether the strongest defensible reading -- whatever it turns out to be -- is actually the reading my uncle's argument needed, given that he was responding to the claim that the bombings were wrong.

Please write in American English, and structure the answer in four sections, in this order: "The Component Claims" (the menu, roughly 350 words), "Verdicts" (a claim-by-claim assessment, roughly 400 words), "What the Argument Needs" (which reading the slogan must take to do the work it was doing at my dinner table, roughly 250 words), and "Bottom Line" (your overall assessment of the slogan as a move in debate, roughly 150 words).
