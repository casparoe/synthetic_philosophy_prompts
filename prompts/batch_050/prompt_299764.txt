I am a retired field ecologist, and over the past two years I have been working my way into philosophy by way of a reading group. A recent session, combined with an argument at my camera club, left me with a question I cannot settle on my own. For decades I used motion-triggered trail cameras to document wildlife I never encountered in person, and I always said, without thinking much about it, that I had seen the animals in question. When a fellow club member recently claimed the opposite, insisting that I had seen only pictures, someone else tried to end the dispute with the old line that the camera never lies. That settled nothing, and the conversation splintered further when another member raised AI-generated images, asking whether a synthetic photograph of a bird that never existed could lie even though no camera and no bird were involved.

What I would like is a careful unpacking of the slogan itself. It seems to me that the camera never lies could be expressing several quite different claims, and I want them separated. It might mean the modest empirical point that photographs are generally reliable records of what was before the lens. It might mean something more specific about mechanism: that a photograph is counterfactually dependent on the scene in a way a painting or a verbal description is not, because the scene itself, rather than an agent's beliefs about the scene, produced the image. It might mean the stronger thesis I gather some philosophers hold, that photographs are transparent, so that in looking at my trail camera image I literally saw the lynx, much as I might see it through binoculars or a periscope. It might instead be a claim about evidence and what a court or a scientist may justifiably infer from an image. Or it might be the deflationary point that lying requires an intention to deceive, which a camera cannot have, so that the slogan is true but trivial and consistent with photographs misleading us constantly.

Please lay these readings out as a list, and for each one say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false, giving the reasons briefly. Then, and this is the part I most need, tell me which of these readings would actually support the uses the slogan was put to in my club: first, settling whether I saw the lynx or merely a depiction of it; second, bearing on whether a convincingly realistic but entirely synthetic image can lie or deceive. I suspect the slogan survives in casual use precisely because its different readings trade places as needed, and I would like that suspicion either confirmed or corrected.
