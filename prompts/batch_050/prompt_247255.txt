I work in ML, mostly ranking and recommendation systems, so the vocabulary I actually think in is scores, logits, calibration, normalizing across models, ensembling. I keep bouncing off the welfare-econ / philosophy debate about interpersonal utility comparisons -- the ordinalist challenge (you can know what someone prefers but never how much, so summing utilities across people is meaningless), and the main replies: Harsanyi-style aggregation, moves where you fix a zero and a unit per person (like the 'zero-one rule' where each person's worst and best feasible outcomes anchor their scale), and Sen's ladder of partial comparability, from purely ordinal no-comparisons up through ordinal level comparisons, cardinal unit comparisons, and full cardinal comparability.

Restate the whole debate in my vocabulary, as precisely as you can: each person's utility as the uncalibrated score output of a model nobody trained, level comparability as getting the offsets/zeros aligned across models, unit comparability as agreeing on a scaling, full comparability as the scores living on one shared scale, ordinalism as the claim that scores are only defined up to monotone transformation so any cross-model calibration is arbitrary. Then give me the part I actually care about, the residue report: wich parts of the debate map cleanly onto calibrating heterogeneous models, which parts you had to fudge to make them fit, and which parts have no counterpart at all in how we handle scores. Some things I suspect don't survive translation: (a) in ML we built the models, so we can retrain them against a shared loss -- nobody built the persons; (b) whether the 'scores' track anything like felt intensity, or only revealed choices, and whether that distinction even has an analogue when a model's outputs are just numbers; (c) when we calibrate, the calibration target is ours to pick, and I genuinely can't tell whether the welfare economists think they're discovering the right target or choosing one.

Then the verdict: my standing intuition is that skepticism about interpersonal comparisons is just the truism that logits aren't probabilities, and that a big chunk of the literature is a normalization problem dressed up as metaphysics.
