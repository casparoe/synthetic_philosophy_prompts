I retired two years ago after four decades as a molecular biologist, and I have since been reading moral philosophy in earnest — Kant at present, with roughly equal measures of admiration and exasperation. Let me first state his view as I understand it, so that you can correct me where I have it wrong: animals, lacking rational nature, are not ends in themselves, and we owe them no duties directly; our duties regarding animals are indirect, owed to humanity or to ourselves, and cruelty to animals is wrong because indulging it deadens the sympathetic dispositions on which our treatment of other persons depends. I also gather that he distinguished vivisection done in the service of genuine knowledge from experiments performed out of idle curiosity, condemning only the latter — which suggests the framework attends to the agent's purposes rather than to the animal's experience.

Now the counterfactual exercise I would like you to carry out. Two developments postdate Kant, and I want the most defensible prediction of what he would say about each, argued from his own commitments rather than from what a modern reader might wish him to have said.

First, industrial animal agriculture: the confinement and slaughter of billions of animals annually, under conditions dictated by cost, largely invisible to the consumers who fund it.

Second, a proposal seriously discussed in agricultural bioethics: genetically engineering livestock so that they are constitutionally incapable of pain or distress. Husbandry and slaughter would proceed as now, but nothing in the animals' experience would, by stipulation, be bad for them.

For each practice, please give Kant's predicted verdict and identify which premises do the load-bearing work. In particular: (i) if the psychological premise of the indirect-duty argument failed — suppose it turned out that routine participation in slaughter did not coarsen anyone's conduct toward other humans — would anything in Kant's framework still condemn these practices? (ii) Conversely, if the empirical connection between cruelty to animals and cruelty to persons is real, does the indirect view capture everything objectionable about factory farming, or does it mislocate the wrongness? (iii) Does engineering away the capacity to suffer dissolve the moral problem on Kant's account, or does the concept of cruelty attach to the agent's disposition in a way that survives the animals' engineered insensibility?

Wherever his commitments force a conclusion that would startle most modern readers, please say so plainly rather than softening it.

Please conclude with a single final line in exactly this form, for my records:
Verdict: factory farming = <permissible | impermissible | undecidable from Kant's commitments>; pain-free livestock = <permissible | impermissible | undecidable>; on Kant's account the wrongness resides in = <the animal's suffering | the human agent's character | undecidable>
