I want to spar with Darwin himself for a piece I'm writing, so please play Charles Darwin, sometime around 1876, at home at Down House, a few years after the second edition of The Descent of Man came out. Stay in character untill I say stop. You can only use concepts and knowledge available to you in 1876 -- no genes, no modern primatology, nothing about what your reputation becomes. You should sound like you: cautious, hedged, generous to critics, happier with a fact about barnacles or worms than with metaphysics, but willing to follow an argument where it leads. You are not a firebrand. You're also not a pushover -- you can concede small points but you should defend the actual argument of the book, and you're allowed to ask me clarifying questions in character if I use a term you wouldn't know.

Some setup so your first reply has something to grab onto. In Chapter III of the Descent (the chapter comparing the mental powers of man and the lower animals, where you get to belief in God and superstition), you argue that religious devotion is a highly complex emotion -- love, complete submission to an exalted and mysterious superior, dependence, fear, reverence, gratitude, hope -- that no being could feel until its intellectual and moral faculties were fairly advanced, and yet you say we see a distant approach to exactly this state of mind in the deep love of a dog for its master, with its submission and touch of fear. In Chapter IV you do something similar for conscience, tracing the moral sense back to social instincts we share with other animals.

Here's my opening move, and I want your answer as Darwin, two or three paragraphs, conversational, not a lecture:

I think that passage about the dog cuts your own project in half, and you never noticed. Either the resemblance between my dog's greeting at the door and a believer at prayer is deep -- in which case you've quietly explained away the one inner experience people point to as evidence there's a soul, and you ought to say so plainly instead of hiding behind "distant approach." Or the resemblance is superficial -- because what the believer cares about isn't the ingredients of the feeling but its object, and a dog has no object, no theology, nothing the devotion is *about* -- in which case your natural history of the feeling does no work at all against the believer, since she can grant you every ingredient and keep God. So which is it? And a second prong while you're at it: you gave conscience the same treatment in the next chapter. Once I believe my sense of duty is inherited social instinct plus reflection, of exactly the same kind as the baboon's, do I have more reason to trust it or less? You seem to want it both ways -- genealogy as explanation when it suits you, and the feelings left standing with full authority afterwards. Convince me that's not a cheat.
