I am drafting a paper on the ethics of deception and need a thought experiment constructed to order; it will also serve as the centrepiece of a graduate seminar session, so it must be watertight enough to survive hostile scrutiny.

The background is this. Much contemporary discussion of lying and misleading proceeds on the assumption that lying is defeasibly worse than misleading: where lying is saying what one believes to be false, and misleading is avoiding the false saying while intentionally conversationally implicating the falsehood, the lie is taken to be the graver offence, typically because it violates the norms of assertion in some further way, or abuses the hearer's willingness to take testimony on trust. I want a case that pressures this default from the opposite direction.

Construct a pair of scenarios, a lying version and a misleading version, in which the lie is morally better than the misleading. The specifications:

1. A realistic setting — a hospital, a workplace, a family — with no science fiction and no stipulated conventions that simply attach extra penalties to lying, such as an oath; that would settle the question by fiat rather than generate the verdict from the distinction itself.
2. The two versions must involve the same speaker, the same hearer, the same false belief intentionally induced, the same stakes, and the same speaker knowledge. The sole structural difference is whether the falsehood is said or merely conversationally implicated.
3. Any divergence in downstream consequences between the versions is permitted only if it flows from the structural feature that distinguishes them, not from bolted-on contingencies — a more gullible hearer in one version, for instance — which a defender of the standard view could use to dismiss the pair as improperly controlled.
4. One paragraph per version.

Then demonstrate that the case works: (a) state precisely which analysis of the lying/misleading distinction you are relying on and show that each version falls on the correct side of it; (b) identify the feature that reverses the standard verdict — one promising route is that a belief the hearer is led to generate for themselves is more resilient, and recruits the hearer's own agency into the deception, but argue for whatever feature you think actually does the work; (c) give the strongest objection available to a defender of the standard view, who will presumably reply that the extra badness belongs to a second wrong present only in the misleading version — manipulation, or the exploitation of the hearer's reasoning — rather than to misleading as such, and say whether the case can be refined to answer this or whether the reply in fact collapses the putative distinction between the feature and misleading itself; (d) state what happens to the pair if the workhorse feature is removed.
