I'm a second-year PhD student in history (my dissertation is on municipal record-keeping in a colonial port city, roughly 1880-1930) and I've been drafting an essay for a workshop next month. The thing is, the essay isn't really about my dissertation material at all, which might be the problem. Below are my notes / the current argument sketch. I'd love comments before I turn this into a full draft, especially on whether the central analogy actually holds or whether I'm just pattern-matching two things that both involve "records."

The essay's claim, roughly: historians have long known that archives aren't neutral containers of the past. What survives depends on who had the power to create documents, what institutions bothered to preserve, which documents were considered worth cataloguing, and what later scholars decided was significant enough to write about. The silences aren't accidents; they're produced at every stage. Famously, people like Trouillot and Hartman have worked through this for Atlantic slavery, and Hartman's response was to sometimes write *against* the archive, using disciplined speculation to reconstruct lives the records only register as cargo listings or punishment logs.

My move is to argue that large text corpora used to train AI language models are archives in exactly this sense, and that the same four moments of silencing apply: (1) who gets to produce text that exists at all (literacy, internet access, which languages are typed vs. spoken), (2) what gets collected into the corpus (scraping decisions, paywalled vs. free text), (3) what gets filtered out or downweighted (deduplication, "quality" filters that encode somebody's judgment), and (4) what the resulting system treats as sayable vs. weird (retrieval at inference time, more or less). Then the provocative bit: if that's right, is there an analogue of critical fabulation for these systems? Can a model write against its own corpus, or is it constitutionally unable to voice what its archive silenced, so that any "recovery" just re-drapes the dominant record in plausible-sounding fabric? My tentative answer is that the model case is *worse* than the human historian case, because the historian at least knows the archive has edges, whereas the model's corpus is invisible to itself. But I'm not confident in that, and a friend in my cohort thinks I have it backwards.

What I'd find most useful: comments on whether the four-stage mapping is accurate to how these corpora are actually built, whether the critical-fabulation question is well-posed or confused, and where the essay is least interesting/most obvious.
