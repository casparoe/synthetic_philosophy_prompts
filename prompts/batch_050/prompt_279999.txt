At a departmental gathering last week I listened — deliberately saying nothing myself — to an argument between two colleagues, Daniel and Ingrid, about a decision facing their city's transit agency: whether to build a proposed light-rail line. Polling shows that most residents along the corridor say they would ride it. The exchange ran roughly as follows.

Daniel opened by dismissing the surveys. Stated enthusiasm is cheap, he said, while behavior is honest: the express bus along the same corridor runs half-empty, and few drivers pay the toll for the faster lane. Choices reveal preferences, and what these choices reveal is that residents value transit far less than they claim, so the line would fail an honest cost-benefit test.

Ingrid replied that one can only reveal a preference among options that actually exist. The bus is slow and infrequent, and the rail line does not yet exist. Observing that people decline what is currently on the menu says nothing about how they would choose from a better one; at most, his data reveal preferences over present options, not over the proposed one.

Daniel conceded that much but called it a counsel of despair. Planners must extrapolate from related choices: what people pay to live near existing stations, how they trade commuting time against housing costs. Those are real, high-stakes choices, and they let us price what we cannot observe directly.

Ingrid objected that this assumes revealed preferences are stable attributes of persons that persist across contexts, which they appear not to be. Participation in retirement savings plans jumps when enrollment is the default rather than an option one must actively select, so the same employees reveal opposite savings preferences depending on how a form is designed. If framing determines choice, she argued, then either the theory is unfalsifiable — any choice reveals whatever preference rationalizes it — or it reveals nothing about the person at all.

Daniel answered that she mistakes the ambition of the program. Revealed preference is not a theory of the soul but a discipline about evidence: choices, not reports, are the data of economics. Even if preferences are partly constructed by context, a planner still needs behavioral predictions — ridership, willingness to pay — and observed choices in comparable situations remain the least corruptible signal available.

Ingrid pressed harder: then he should concede that choice data yield predictions of behavior, not measures of welfare, and cost-benefit analysis needs welfare. Consider a smoker who pays for a program that fines her whenever she buys cigarettes, and later buys them anyway and pays the fine. Two choices reveal opposite preferences. Which one tells Daniel what is good for her? If he answers by picking the choice that reflects well on her welfare, he has applied a standard of welfare that did not itself come from choice.

Daniel responded that no such smuggling is required. Model the smoker as ranking choice sets: she prefers the menu from which cigarettes are absent, and acts on that preference when she can. A single, consistent preference over menus explains both decisions; she is sophisticated about her own weakness, not inconsistent.

Ingrid closed by saying that a preference over menus which can be tailored after the fact to accommodate any pattern is a labeling scheme, not an explanation; a theory flexible enough to fit everything forbids nothing, and revealed preference has been rendered safe only by being rendered empty.

Please adjudicate the exchange turn by turn, from Daniel's opening inference through Ingrid's closing charge of emptiness: for each move, say whether it is right, wrong, or partly right, and justify the verdict briefly. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing, and give an overall judgment — with an indication of your confidence — as to who is more nearly right about the underlying question of what, if anything, choices reveal.
