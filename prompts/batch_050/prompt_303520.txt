I chair the ethics committee of a mid-sized teaching hospital, and our transplant program has asked to adopt a procurement protocol that I need the committee to rule on next month. Under our current practice, a patient whose family has consented to donation and whose care is being withdrawn is taken off life support; once the heart stops, the team waits five minutes of continuous circulatory arrest, declares death, and begins organ recovery. The new proposal would, immediately after that declaration, surgically clamp the arteries supplying the head and then restart circulation to the rest of the body with a perfusion machine, keeping the abdominal organs oxygenated in situ so they survive transplant better. The perfusion team assures us the clamps isolate the brain completely. One of our neurologists is not reassured: she argues that collateral vessels are known to exist, that nobody has proven zero blood reaches the brain in every patient, and that if even partial cerebral perfusion were restored, the declaration of death five minutes earlier would have been a declaration that turned out to be false rather than a declaration that held. She also presses a conceptual point that has split the committee: our jurisdiction's statute requires irreversible cessation of circulatory function, and she holds that once we ourselves plan to restart circulation in the torso, the cessation was never irreversible -- at most it was permanent in the weaker sense that no one intended to let the heart start beating again on its own. A second committee member dissents from the other direction: he thinks the five-minute declaration is defensible on the permanence reading, but believes honesty requires us to tell donor families explicitly that blood flow will be restored to the body after death is declared, and he doubts the program will consent to that disclosure. A third has suggested, only half in jest, that we should stop insisting the donor is dead at all and instead defend procurement as something a dying, consenting patient may authorize -- a suggestion the transplant surgeon calls a public-relations catastrophe. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting: a clear recommendation to approve the protocol, reject it, or approve it with conditions (specifying any conditions concretely enough that the surgical team could implement them); a reasoned position on whether irreversible or merely permanent cessation is doing the work in the declaration, and on how much certainty about cerebral isolation is enough; an honest statement of the strongest argument for the losing side; and a paragraph on what, if anything, families must be told.
