Alright, some background first. I'm a retired biochemist — spent thirty-some years doing protein crystallography — and since retiring I've fallen headfirst into philosophy, mostly the question of whether LLMs genuinely understand anything or whether they're just, as the snappy phrase goes, stochastic parrots. I've read the usual greatest hits: Searle's Chinese Room, the symbol grounding arguments, the Turing-style position that behavior is all there is to check. My honest problem is that I flip sides depending on which paper I read most recently, and I don't love that about myself.

Here's the practical hook. I'm on the advisory panel for our local science center's new AI exhibit, and every few weeks some vendor or grad student demos something for us — a chatbot, a robot tour guide, an LLM that troubleshoots lab protocols — and inevitably somebody turns to me and asks, "but does it actually understand what it's saying?" I'd like to stop winging it. What I want is a reusable procedure: a checklist or an ordered set of tests that takes the serious positions in the debate (behaviorism about understanding, the Chinese Room, grounding/embodiment views) and turns them into steps I can actually run, ending in something like "attribution of understanding: warranted / not warranted / genuinely indeterminate, and here's why."

Constraints on the thing: it has to be runnable in about an hour of me poking at a system; it needs instructions for what to do when a test can't be performed at all (say, a text-only system where any embodiment check is moot); and it has to mark honestly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure — I've sat through enough grant review panels to know that smell. It also needs to handle at least these hard cases: a chatbot that aces every conversational test but turns out to be recycling canned scripts when you go off-script; a system that solves genuinely novel folding problems but falls apart when you ask it why its solutions work; and — bear with me — my old grad student Dave, because half the tests that would disqualify the machines would disqualify Dave on a Monday morning, and I suspect that's exactly where the interesting part is hiding.

Then apply the procedure to at least two of those cases, worked through step by step, and include one application where it spits out a verdict I probably won't like (full disclosure: my gut says the chatbots don't understand squat). Finish by saying what the procedure flat-out cannot settle no matter how well it runs.

Please answer in American English. And since I asked for rigor: state how confident you are in the main claims you're making, and single out the one point in your answer you're least sure of.
