A few months ago I fell down a rabbit hole after a dinner-table argument with some friends about equality. None of us has any academic training in philosophy, but we ended up arguing past midnight over a puzzle I later learned has a name: the leveling-down objection. As I understand it, if you think inequality is bad in itself — not merely bad because it tends to arrive together with poverty, domination, or humiliation — then you seem committed to saying there is something good about making the better-off worse off, even when doing so helps absolutely no one, simply because the result is more equal. My friends treated this as a knockdown refutation of the whole idea that equality matters intrinsically. I keep suspecting it isn't, or at least that the people who hold the intrinsic view know exactly what they are committed to and accept it with open eyes. I would like to see that commitment taken as seriously as possible, from the inside, and pushed to where it actually goes.

Please act as a satirist who also has a professional philosopher's command of the debate between egalitarians and prioritarians. Before writing, think carefully — sketch it out if it helps — about which moves a consistent intrinsic egalitarian genuinely has to make: where they must bite the bullet, and where they can push back honestly rather than being trapped. Then write a straight-faced public letter or op-ed of roughly 650–750 words from the fictional founder of a civic organization — call it the Society for Substantive Equality — announcing the Society's policy platform. The platform should consist entirely of subtractive measures: policies that close gaps purely by lowering the better-off, with no redistribution anywhere. A luxury tax collected in the ceremonial destruction of surplus goods rather than their transfer; scheduled utility outages calibrated to income brackets so that consumption converges downward; a national program of standardized modest housing into which anyone above a threshold is relocated; perhaps an equality dividend paid out in cancelled services. Each proposal should follow the target's logic one step further, and the founder should describe each in the warm, reasonable tone of a think-tank press release.

Three constraints matter most. First, stay deadpan throughout — no winking, no mustache-twirling villainy; the satire should land because the founder is right about what his view entails and the reader recoils from the entailment, not because he is stupid or sinister. Second, he must calmly concede, in the piece itself, that his policies make no one better off, and defend that concession with the strongest honest version of the intrinsic view: equality as a relation between people rather than a feature of anyone's life, so that improving the relation needn't improve any life, with a comparison to retributive punishment, where we also accept making someone worse off with no one made better off because a relation of fittingness is restored. Third, without ever naming it, let the reader glimpse the prioritarian alternative — that giving extra weight to the worse-off only ever matters when someone actually benefits — so they can feel precisely why that view escapes the objection, and why the founder's does not. He must never secretly become a prioritarian halfway through; internal consistency is the whole point. After the piece, append three or four sentences, out of character, explaining what the satire is doing and what would have to be true for the founder's position to deserve more respect than the reader wants to give it.
