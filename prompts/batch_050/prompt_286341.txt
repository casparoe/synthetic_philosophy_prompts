I sit on the institutional review board of a neurosurgery center that has just completed the first "disembodiment" procedure: to arrest a terminal cranial disease, a patient's brain was removed and is now sustained in a vat of nutrient fluid in our Houston laboratory, where it remains linked to the patient's original body — currently living in Oregon — by a radio connection carrying all sensory and motor signals in both directions. The patient works, visits family, and testifies through the body, and reports feeling entirely located in Oregon. Our insurer disagrees: it classifies the brain in the vat as "the patient" for liability purposes, which makes every treatment performed on the body count as off-site care and excludes it from coverage. The board must now adopt an official position on where the patient is located, for purposes of our consent forms, malpractice coverage, and the design of future protocols. Draft the memo I will circulate before our next meeting: it should recommend one definite position — the patient is where the brain is, where the body is, both, or neither — defend it against the strongest rival view rather than dismissing it, and spell out what the recommendation implies for at least one concrete policy, such as where informed consent must be executed or which location governs insurance coverage.
