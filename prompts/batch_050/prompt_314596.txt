Last month I got into an argument at a family dinner that has been bothering me since. My uncle said Britain should finally separate church and state like civilised countries do — by which he meant disestablishing the Church of England and removing the twenty-six bishops who sit ex officio in the House of Lords. My cousin, who lives in Germany, shot back that Germany is generally described as a secular state even though the German tax authorities collect a church tax from registered church members on the churches' behalf (one can stop paying by formally leaving the church, but the state acts as the collection agent and charges a fee for the service). Someone else brought up France, where laïcité is supposed to be the strictest separation in Europe, yet the French state owns and maintains church buildings erected before 1905 and still pays clergy salaries in Alsace-Moselle under a surviving concordat regime. By the end of the evening, nobody at the table — myself included — could say what separation of church and state actually requires, but everyone kept invoking the phrase as though it settled something.

Here is what I want from you. Take the slogan and lay out the distinct, precise principles it could be standing for, ordered from the weakest (something in the vicinity of: the state may not compel religious observance) to the strongest (something in the vicinity of: religious considerations and religious institutions must have no place in public institutions at all). For each candidate principle, tell me:

- whether it is a near-platitude, a genuinely contested position, or an overreach that proves too much;
- which of the four systems above — established-church England, church-tax Germany, laïcité France, First Amendment America — satisfy it and which violate it, and in the awkward cases, whether the classification should make us revise the principle or instead revise our opinion of the country;
- what practical work the principle does: which arrangements that currently exist in at least one of those four countries it would forbid.

Then — and this is the part I care about most — do not leave me with a tidy menu and a shrug. Commit. Tell me which single precisification you take to be the one worth defending as a matter of political morality, and give your reasons for preferring it to the weaker and stronger alternatives, including your answer to the obvious objection that an established church seems to cost English citizens very little while laïcité-style strictness seems to cost French citizens quite a lot. On your chosen reading, say which of the four countries actually live up to it, and for each that fails, name the specific arrangement — bishops in the Lords, the state-collected church tax, the Alsace-Moselle exception, tax exemptions and publicly paid legislative chaplains in the United States — that constitutes the failure. Finally, rule on the original dinner-table dispute: which precisification did my uncle's case for disestablishment actually need, is that precisification defensible, and does the slogan as he invoked it genuinely support his conclusion or merely borrow the appearance of support?
