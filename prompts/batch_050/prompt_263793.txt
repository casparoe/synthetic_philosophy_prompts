I'm a philosopher of medicine preparing the first session of a graduate seminar on the concept of disease, and I've noticed a recurring problem in past iterations of the course: students declare themselves "naturalists" or "normativists" in week one, and then spend the semester discovering that these labels conceal more than they reveal -- two self-described naturalists disagree about whether a condition no one minds having can still be a disease, and two self-described normativists disagree about whose values are doing the work. I want a one-page decision tree to hand out on day one that sorts my students by their actual commitments rather than by the label they walked in with. Please take on the role of an expert in philosophy of medicine with long experience teaching this debate, and build the tree for me.

Every branch node should be a question built around a concrete case a student can react to without knowing any position's name. Please work in at least the following cases, and add others if they do diagnostic work: a condition present in literally 100% of a population that nonetheless impairs functioning (say, progressive arterial stiffening with age, or dental caries in a community with no dental hygiene); a condition that lowers its bearer's reproductive fitness but causes no suffering and is valued by those who have it; a statistically typical condition that disables its bearer only given the local environment (say, an inability to digest the region's single staple crop); and an asymptomatic condition detectable only through biomarkers, such as untreated hypertension. The leaves must include a Boorse-style biostatistical naturalism, a Wakefield-style harmful-dysfunction view, a Nordenfelt-style view on which health is the ability to realize one's vital goals, a stronger normativism on which disease judgments are wholesale social evaluations, and an eliminativist option on which we'd be better off dropping the concept of disease in favor of talk of specific functional deficits. You may add further leaves if the logical space demands them.

Here is the part I care most about: please do not start from the list of named positions and hunt for questions that separate them. Instead, first brainstorm the logically available diagnostic questions and enumerate the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space -- and think carefully about which distinctions genuinely divide the literature and which are merely verbal. If some combination of answers is unoccupied, note it and briefly diagnose why: is the position incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? If your tree cannot fully separate two of the required leaves, flag that honestly rather than forcing a distinction, and say what further question would be needed to pry them apart. At each leaf, add one sentence stating what verdict that position reaches on the 100%-prevalence case, since that's the case my students always argue about first. Aim for ten to fifteen nodes total.
