I'm on the grants committee of a small family foundation, and we have exactly enough money left this cycle to fund one of three projects. The fight on the committee is about how to choose, not what to value -- we all agree the metric is expected good done for the beneficiaries, roughly measurable.

Option A is a cash-transfer program that's been studied to death. It will do a moderate, well-established amount of good no matter what. Option B is a pilot of a new agricultural intervention: if the mechanism behind it is real, it does vastly more good than A, maybe ten times as much; if the pilot turns out to rest on a measurement error (a live possibility the scientists themselves flag), it does almost nothing and the money is effectively wasted. Option C splits the difference: a program with a decent floor and a decent ceiling, never the best, never the worst.

Here's the thing: nobody will give us probabilities for B's mechanism being real. Not "low confidence" probabilities -- none. The experts say the science is too young and they refuse to put a number on it.

Two committee members are dug in. One says that with no probabilities, expected-value reasoning is dead, and we should pick the option whose worst outcome is best -- that's A. The other says that rule makes us unable to ever fund innovation, and we should instead minimize the most we could end up regretting -- which she argues points to B, since if B works and we passed, we've thrown away an enormous amount of good. A third member keeps saying we should "just assign our best-guess probabilities and maximize," which the first two think is fake precision.

Draft the committee's decision memo: recommend a grant, recommend the decision rule we should adopt for cases like this going forward, and give the strongest honest version of the case for the option you're recommending against, since the losing members have to sign the memo too.
