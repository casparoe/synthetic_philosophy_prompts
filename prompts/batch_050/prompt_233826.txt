In Chapter XVII of An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), Jeremy Bentham divides the agents whose happiness ethics must direct into two kinds, writing: "They are of two sorts: 1. Other human beings who are styled persons. 2. Other animals, which, on account of their interests having been neglected by the insensibility of the ancient jurists, stand degraded into the class of things." In the long note attached to this sentence, Bentham argues that killing animals for food may nonetheless be defensible where the death humans inflict is quicker and less painful than the death awaiting the animal in nature. Suppose Bentham were now confronted with industrial aquaculture: salmon raised for two years in densely stocked sea cages where sea-lice infestations are controlled chemically, female shrimp broodstock whose eyestalks are routinely crushed or severed to induce spawning, and fish harvested by slow asphyxiation in ice slurry even though percussive stunning is available. Working from this passage, his principle of utility, and his felicific calculus, reconstruct how he would judge the industry -- and note that the chapter containing this passage is precisely where he draws the line between what belongs to legislation and what belongs to private ethics. Give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: 1) whether fish and crustaceans count morally for him, and on what grounds; 2) his verdict on the living conditions described; 3) his verdict on the manner of slaughter, in light of his reasoning about quick deaths; 4) whether the sheer scale of modern aquaculture -- tens of billions of animals -- alters his defense of eating animals; 5) whether he would press for legal regulation or leave reform to voluntary private ethics, and why.
