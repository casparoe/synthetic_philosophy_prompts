I'm a software engineer at a company that builds decision-support software for a public health authority, and I've run into a conceptual problem I can't engineer my way around. The system we maintain helps the authority decide which treatments to fund, and it works like this. Patients' health is measured with a standardized questionnaire covering five dimensions — mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain, and mood — each rated at several severity levels. A valuation model then converts every possible health state into a weight between 1 and 0, where 1 is full health and 0 is dead, using survey data from the general population: respondents are asked how many years of life in full health they would give up to avoid spending a stretch of time in some impaired state. Our model multiplies each patient's weight by the time spent in that state, sums the result across everyone affected by a treatment, and the authority funds whatever comes in below a cost-per-unit threshold (the unit being a "quality-adjusted life-year," one year at weight 1).

Two rules are hard-coded and non-negotiable in our contract. First, additivity: improving one patient from 0.4 to 0.7 for ten years counts exactly the same as improving three patients by 0.1 each for ten years. Second, person-neutrality: a unit counts the same whoever receives it, and the model is forbidden from using age, income, or social role.

During a code review, a colleague asked what guarantees that the 0.6 assigned to one patient's year is the same quantity as the 0.6 assigned to another's. I traced the data flow and found something odd: the questionnaire records how each patient describes her own state, and the valuation survey records how respondents trade off their own prospective years. Nothing anywhere in the pipeline ever compares one person's experience with another's. From some reading, I gather that Sidgwick, in The Methods of Ethics, treats comparisons between different people's pleasures as something ordinary practical life forces on us constantly, and as no more problematic in principle than comparing your own present feelings with remembered or anticipated ones — while other writers have held that there is no common unit between minds at all, so such comparisons are impossible.

What I'd like to know:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory of this practice. Does it presuppose that well-being is cardinally measurable and comparable across persons? Or does the anchoring of everyone's scale at the same two endpoints — dead and full health — manufacture a common unit by stipulation, so that the practice rests on a convention rather than a measurement?
2. Which components carry which commitments? The questionnaire, the valuation survey, the choice of anchor points, the additivity rule, person-neutrality, and the funding threshold each seem to assume different things. Which commitments does the practice strictly require, and where is it underdetermined — compatible with several theories of well-being and comparison?
3. Does the practice need Sidgwick's reassurance to be correct? When the additivity rule treats a large gain for one patient as equivalent to small gains for three, does that presuppose that the underlying experiences are commensurable, or only a decision that they count equally? And is Sidgwick right that comparing your own states across time is unproblematic in a way that cross-person comparison isn't — our pipeline certainly relies on the former too?
4. If the skeptical view is right and there is no common unit between minds, what would have to change? Could the authority keep the practice intact by re-founding it on an explicit ethical postulate — a year of full health counts equally for everyone — while abandoning any claim to measurement? Or would the practice have to be restructured or abandoned, and what would each option give up?

I'm not looking for a reading list; I'd like the analysis itself, and where you think the practice is philosophically defensible as-is, I'd like to know that too.
