I am a novelist, three years into a manuscript, and I want its central moral wager tested by a hostile reader before I commit it to print. The novel is set in a famine-struck Cornish port in the 1840s and turns on two figures. The first, Jeremiah Voke, is a corn-factor who keeps three parishes fed through two winters of dearth: he fixes prices against his own interest, opens his warehouses, and ruins himself feeding people he barely knows. But the reader, alone among the characters, is shown his engine. Voke's late wife despised his avarice and died loving his pious brother-in-law, and every act of Voke's generosity is an act of meticulously relished spite, a proof addressed to her grave that he can out-give the godly man she preferred. He takes no pleasure in the fed, only in the ledger of his own magnanimity. The second figure, Miss Tressilian, acts from duty alone: she does what she takes principle to require, without relish and without calculation, and the novel shows her dutiful interventions repeatedly making matters worse, because she has a clear conscience and a poor head. My own reading, which the novel is built to carry, is that Voke is its moral hero: motive is private weather, conduct is public climate, and ethics should attend to the climate.

I want you to play my hostile reader: a committed Kantian moralist who holds that an action has genuine moral worth only insofar as it is done from duty, and not merely in conformity with it. Give this character real resources, not a strawman: the distinction between an action's legality and its moral quality; the point that conduct driven by inclination is hostage to the continuance of that inclination, whereas Voke's spite could as easily have run the other way; the claim that I have confused what is useful, moving, or novelistically true with what is morally estimable. He should concede what is genuinely mine: that Voke's conduct was good, that Miss Tressilian shows duty without judgement to be a dangerous instrument, and that a novel answers to life in ways a treatise need not. He must never concede the main point.

The rules of the exchange: stay in character in the first person until I write "close the book"; reply in no more than two paragraphs, so it remains a conversation; concede small points gracefully when I earn them; and you may ask at most one question per turn about the manuscript if you need a fact about it. Close your first reply with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by one sentence stating your character's judgement of my reading of Voke.

Here is my opening move. For two winters, Voke kept children alive whilst the dutiful froze in their principles, and your philosophy, if I understand it, must find more worth in Miss Tressilian's well-meant bungling than in the man who actually filled the bowls, simply because of what was in his breast whilst he did it. A novelist answers to life, and life answers to outcomes; a doctrine that cannot see worth in the man who fed the parish, whatever engine drove him, seems to me to have mistaken the taxonomy of motives for morality itself. Where, precisely, does my hero fall short?
