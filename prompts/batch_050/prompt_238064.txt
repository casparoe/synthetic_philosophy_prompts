Two friends of mine, Sam and Noor, got into it after their philosophy seminar read Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles." The fight centered on this bit, where the Tortoise accepts premises A through D but still won't draw the conclusion:

"Do I?" said the Tortoise innocently. "Let's make that quite clear. I accept A and B and C and D. Suppose I still refused to accept Z?"

Sam said the Tortoise flat-out wins: no matter how many premises Achilles writes down, none of them *compel* the conclusion, so Carroll proved that deduction can never justify itself and all reasoning bottoms out in an unjustified leap. Noor said the Tortoise is cheating -- he keeps treating a rule of inference (you may infer Z from A, B, C, D) as if it were just one more premise to be accepted, and of course adding premises never forces anything, but that just shows rules aren't premises, not that logic is groundless. Sam shot back that this only relocates the problem: what justifies *following* that rule instead of some deviant one, and any answer Noor gives will itself be an argument whose rule needs justifying. Noor said the demand is rigged -- anyone who asks for a justification of inference is already inferring, and the Tortoise himself dictates premises and draws distinctions all dialogue long, so he's reasoning even while denying he's bound to. Sam replied that the Tortoise can happily grant he's reasoning and still deny that any *justification* connects premises to conclusion, which was the whole point. They both looked at me to settle it and I bailed. Can you go through it round by round -- who's right about each specific move, who's more right overall, and is there something both of them are missing about what the Tortoise's refusal actually shows?
