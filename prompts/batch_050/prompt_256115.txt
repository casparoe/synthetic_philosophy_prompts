Two members of my reading group, Daniela and Marcus, got into an extended argument after we finished Peter Abelard's Ethics, and I'd like it settled. The case they fixed on was this: a man resolves to murder his wealthy uncle for the inheritance, drives to the uncle's house with a weapon fully intending to use it, and discovers that the uncle died of a heart attack that same morning. They contrasted him with an otherwise identical man who arrives to find the uncle alive and kills him, and with a third man who merely nurses resentful daydreams about the uncle's death and feels a quiet satisfaction when the heart attack strikes, having never formed any resolution. The argument went roughly as follows.

(1) Daniela opened by saying Abelard settles the matter cleanly: sin lies entirely in consent to what one knows should not be done, not in the act or its outcome. The first and second men consented identically; the heart attack was wholly outside the first man's agency, so his guilt is exactly equal to the murderer's. Whatever reasons the law has for treating them differently are evidentiary and pragmatic, not moral.

(2) Marcus replied that this proves too much. By the same reasoning the third man, the daydreamer who takes pleasure in the uncle's death, is guilty of the same sin, since he too endorses the death in his heart. But Abelard himself is careful to separate temptation and even pleasure at the thought of a sin from consent to it; consent must be something more than a wish. And the only thing that can mark the difference between a settled consent and a malignant wish is some actual undertaking -- an act begun, or at least an omission where action was possible. So attempts occupy real moral middle ground between fantasy and completion, and Abelard's principle, pressed as hard as Daniela presses it, erases distinctions it was built to preserve.

(3) Daniela answered that Marcus is simply refusing the doctrine rather than refuting it. Abelard's own stock cases are designed to block exactly this move: the man who would commit adultery given the opportunity but is restrained only by circumstance is, on Abelard's view, as guilty as the one who acts, because consent is complete when nothing but external circumstance prevents the deed. Once the will is fully determined -- the man is driving, weapon in hand, committed -- the act adds nothing of moral weight. Marcus's insistence on an act begun just reintroduces outcome-luck under the label of evidence.

(4) Marcus's final move was to press a dilemma. Either consent is an occurrent mental episode, in which case a passing surge of murderous rage counts as consent and Abelard condemns nearly everyone, or consent is a counterfactual about what one would do given the chance -- and such counterfactuals are opaque even to the agent himself. The would-be murderer may sincerely believe he would have gone through with it and yet have faltered at the threshold had he found his uncle alive. If agents cannot reliably know their own consent, the principle cannot guide either self-examination or judgment of others, and everyone, Daniela included, is forced back onto acts as the only workable evidence of will -- which concedes the substance of his position.

I stayed out of this at the time and genuinely cannot decide between them. Please adjudicate the dispute point by point: for each of the four moves, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and justify the verdict in terms of what Abelard's position actually is and what it commits him to. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing -- in particular, whether the notion of consent at issue can do the work the doctrine requires without collapsing into either of Marcus's two horns, and whether anything of moral significance genuinely turns on the act itself.
