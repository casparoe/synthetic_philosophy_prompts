After years of reading rationalist blogs and forums, I thought I understood the unilateralist’s curse. A colleague has now turned it into an argument that seems to prove far too much.

Imagine twelve research groups that independently discover the same dual-use biological protocol. Publishing it might help legitimate defenders prepare, but might also make misuse substantially easier. Each group receives an independent, noisy estimate of the net social value of publication. The estimates are unbiased, the errors have unbounded support, and every group sincerely tries to maximise expected social value. Any group may publish the protocol; publication is irreversible, whereas delay remains possible and reveals nothing.

My colleague argues as follows:

1. A rational group should publish exactly when its private estimate of the net social value is positive. Requiring a higher threshold would mean deliberately declining an option with positive expected value.

2. With \(n\) groups, publication therefore occurs whenever the largest of the \(n\) estimates is positive.

3. If the true net value is negative, increasing \(n\) strictly increases the probability that at least one group receives a misleadingly favourable estimate. As \(n\) grows, the probability of harmful publication approaches one.

4. Consequently, a large consortium is worse than a single custodian: every additional member adds another opportunity for error without adding a corresponding benefit, since publication is a binary outcome.

5. A single custodian nevertheless retains a unilateral power of publication. To remove unilateralism, publication must instead require unanimous consent.

6. Unanimity gives every member a veto. By the same reasoning, when the true net value of publication is positive, increasing the number of members increases the probability that at least one receives a misleadingly unfavourable estimate and blocks a beneficial publication.

7. Thus distributed institutions face a dilemma: permissive rules tend towards harmful action, while unanimity rules tend towards harmful inaction. Since both errors grow with the number of participants, decentralisation itself is an irrational way to govern irreversible technologies.

The conclusion cannot be right as stated. It would make every emergency brake, conscientious refusal and protective veto an instance of the pathology it was designed to prevent, while saying implausibly little about aggregation rules lying between anyone may act and everyone must agree.

Please identify the earliest step at which the argument fails, or, if the reasoning is valid, say which premise is least costly to surrender. Explain why that step or premise initially looked sound, distinguish the genuine unilateralist’s curse from the broader attack on decentralisation, and price the two most plausible repairs. In particular, address whether a positive private estimate can be the wrong decision threshold even for an honest expected-value maximiser, and whether a veto is subject to the same curse when delay is reversible. I should like to preserve the order-statistic warning in steps 2–4, but not at the cost of making every safeguard suspect.
