Exam question: Fuller and Raz each set out a list of principles that make up the rule of law. Their lists look quite similar, but they disagree about what the rule of law is for. Explain this disagreement, and then briefly explain what Waldron thinks lists like these leave out.

Student's answer: Fuller and Raz do give overlapping lists -- laws should be general, public, prospective, clear, stable, possible to obey, and officials should act in line with the law on the books. The real difference is why these matter. Fuller thinks the principles form an inner morality of law: a system that respects them is to that extent morally good, and a system that totally fails them isn't really a legal system at all. Raz basically agrees -- he too takes the rule of law to be an intrinsic moral virtue of legal systems, wich is why he'd say a wicked regime that governs through clear public rules is still, in that one respect, better than one that doesn't. Waldron's complaint about both lists is that they focus only on the form of rules and ignore institutions. On his view the rule of law also demands courts, hearings, and a genuine chance to be heard, and that's why he treats strong judicial review of legislation as a core rule-of-law requirement.
