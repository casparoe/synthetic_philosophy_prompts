I've been working for several years on the ethics of collective responsibility, and I keep finding that my seminar discussions stall on a deceptively simple question: what makes a state apology for a historical wrong a good one? Rather than starting from theory, I'd like to work from my considered verdicts on a set of cases and see whether any principle unifies them. Here they are. First, a government formally apologizes for atrocities committed a century ago, though no one involved in the original wrong is alive: I think this can be a perfectly genuine apology, provided the apologizing state is continuous with the perpetrating one in the relevant institutional respects. Second, a prime minister expresses "deep regret" for colonial abuses but carefully avoids the word apology and any admission of fault: this strikes me as failing as an apology, not merely being a weaker one. Third, a state issues a full-throated apology, names the wrong precisely, accepts responsibility, but simultaneously passes legislation indemnifying itself against any compensation claims: I find this badly compromised, though not worthless. Fourth, a state apologizes sincerely and pays substantial reparations, but only after decades of denying the events and only once the victims' descendants have become an electorally significant constituency: I think the apology still counts as a real apology, even if the state's motives were impure. Fifth, a head of state apologizes on a visit abroad, is widely praised, but her own parliament and public never ratify or even discuss the apology, and the national curriculum continues to present the wrong as a defensive necessity: I think something has gone badly wrong here, yet I can't decide whether the apology was defective as an apology or simply an apology embedded in a hypocritical surrounding practice.

The pair I find hardest to reconcile is the second and the fourth. In the second, I hold that insincere or hedged language defeats the speech act itself -- the speaker never actually apologized. In the fourth, I hold that impure motivation does not defeat the apology -- the state apologized, however cynically. But cynical motivation seems just as much a failure of sincerity as hedged language, and if anything worse. Likewise, my first verdict commits me to the idea that a state can be a proper bearer of responsibility across generations, while my fifth verdict suggests that an apology by a state organ without uptake by the political community falls short -- but if the state genuinely persists across generations, why should one prime ministerial utterance need ratification by a parliament full of people equally uninvolved in the original wrong? Perhaps the answer is that my fifth verdict is really about the wrong being ongoing (via the curriculum) rather than historical, but I'm not confident.

So here is what I'd like: a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible -- ideally distinguishing the conditions under which an apology succeeds as an apology from the conditions under which a successful apology is nonetheless morally defective or insufficient. For each clause of the principle, indicate which case motivates it. Then tell me what the principle says about two cases I haven't settled: an apology issued jointly with the descendants of victims as co-authors, and an apology that is word-perfect but issued by a state that broke institutional continuity with its predecessor (say, through revolution) while still occupying the same territory and benefiting from the wrong. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts, if any, no tolerably simple principle can save, and which one you'd recommend I abandon first.
