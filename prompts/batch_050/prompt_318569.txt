You are a social scientist and philosopher of classification who has spent years thinking about how social kinds are constructed. Think carefully and rigorously before answering; it may help to sketch the correspondence table first, then stress-test it.

A colleague of mine, whenever the conversation turns to "Boomers," "Millennials," or "Gen Z," reaches for the same dismissive analogy: believing in generations, he says, is just astrology for people with spreadsheets. On his telling, generational labels assign personality and fate to arbitrary birth-year cutoffs, the same way zodiac signs assign them to arbitrary dates on a calendar; both give people a flattering story about themselves and a reductive story about everyone else; and both persist because of vague Barnum-style descriptions that anyone can see themselves in. He uses this analogy to argue that we should simply stop talking about generations altogether — that demographic research invoking cohort effects is pseudo-science wearing a lab coat, and that anyone who says "Millennials are more anxious" or "Boomers pulled the ladder up after them" is doing horoscopes with charts.

I have a feeling this analogy is doing a lot of quiet work in his argument, and I'd like you to audit it. First, give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the zodiac sign, the birth-date boundary, the horoscope reading, the astrologer, and the believer, on the generational side of the mapping — and note where the mapping is already strained at this first pass (for instance, is there anything on the generational side corresponding to the celestial mechanism astrology claims, or is the absence of any claimed mechanism itself a relevant difference?). Second, separate the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from the ones that don't: e.g., generational categories are at least in principle anchored to shared historical events and institutional exposure (wars, recessions at career-entry, the arrival of the internet in one's teens) in a way star signs are not — does that difference defeat the analogy, or does the analogy survive it because the *labels* as popularly used rarely cash out in actual cohort analysis? Conversely, consider the respects in which the analogy might cut deeper than my colleague realizes: are there ways generational talk is actually *worse* than astrology (self-fulfilling stereotyping, unfalsifiable trait attributions, motivated boundary-drawing at 1965 or 1981)?

Finally, the payoff: if the analogy mostly holds, his conclusion follows — abandon generational categories. But if it holds only against the *pop-culture* use of the labels and not against serious cohort-period-age analysis, then the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole time and the correct fix is reform, not abolition. Tell me which it is, and if there is a better analogy for what generations actually are — a loose statistical cluster? a marketing category that became a folk kind? something like a "family resemblance" class? — give me that instead, and explain what it licenses that the astrology analogy blocks.
