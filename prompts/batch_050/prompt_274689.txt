I'm giving a paper at a German studies conference in about a month and the Q&A is going to be rough, so help me prep. My claim: the key to Goethe's whole politics, from the Sturm und Drang years to the old privy councillor, is one line from act one of Götz von Berlichingen (1773): "So gewiß ist der allein glücklich und groß, der weder zu herrschen noch zu gehorchen braucht, um etwas zu sein!" — "He alone is great and happy who fills his own station of independence, and has neither to command nor to obey." My argument is that this isn't just drama, it's the personal maxim Goethe actually tried to organize his life around, and that "neither ruling nor being ruled" explains everything people find weird about him: his coldness toward the French Revolution, his refusal of the 1813 war fever, even his whole careful Weimar setup. So my talk definately hangs on treating a character's line as the author's creed, wich I know is the weak point everyone will go for.

The audience: mostly Goethezeit literary historians who think authorial intent is a crime, two or three intellectual historians of the Vormärz whose default view is that Goethe was a courtier who flinched when history knocked, and inevitably one political theory person who reads everything through Arendt or Schmitt.

Write my prep sheet as exactly eight numbered objections, most damaging first, no intro and no wrap-up. Each item gets exactly two sentences: the first is the objection phrased the way this crowd would actually put it, the second is my best compressed reply with a quick flag of where that reply is soft. Make item 8 the question I'm praying nobody asks.
