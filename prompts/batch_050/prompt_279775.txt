ok so a few months ago I kinda accidentally became the person in charge of my town's little historical society (the woman who ran it for 30 years passed away and nobody else wanted it, long story). It's one room in the library basement, boxes of photos, old council minutes, church newsletters, a display case we refresh a couple times a year. I'm a volunteer, maybe 5 hours a month, no budget, and anything I want to do with the display or getting rid of stuff has to get ok'd by a four-person board.

Here's my problem. Every decision I make is basically a decision about what this town remembers and what it forgets, and I have zero framework for it. I've been reading around on my own and I know there's this whole tug-of-war in ethics between the idea that communities have a duty to remember (especially the bad stuff, especially the dead who have no one left to speak for them) and the idea that forgetting is sometimes a gift, that endlessly reliving old wounds keeps people stuck. I buy both sides depending on the day. What I want is not more philosophy, I want a procedure.

So: write me a decision procedure I can actually run on any item or question that comes across my desk -- should this get digitized, should it go in the display, should it be remembered in some active way (a plaque, an annual thing), or should we quietly let it go. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, and where the procedure should just stop and admit this is a values call that a checklist can't make. It has to be runnable in like half an hour by someone with no training.

It needs to handle the cases that actually come up, so work it on these three:

1. The town's most celebrated founder, guy's name is on a park, turns out from his own letters that he did something genuinely awful by any era's standards. People are starting to hear about it. What does the procedure say about the display and the park name?
2. A factory accident in the 60s that killed nine people. One family pushes hard for an annual memorial; two other families who lost people have told me, off the record, that they wish everyone would stop making them relive it every year. This is the one where I suspect the procedure will tell me something I don't want to hear -- do it anyway.
3. Three boxes of meticulously kept records from some guy nobody alive remembers, that I feel guilty even thinking about tossing. Does guilt about forgetting count as a reason to remember, procedurally?

Also tell me honestly where this procedure will misfire -- what kinds of remembering/forgetting decisions it's just not built for. And at the end, say how confident you are in the whole thing overall and single out the single step or claim you're least sure about, because I'd rather know where it's shaky than find out at a board meeting.
