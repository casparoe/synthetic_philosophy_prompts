I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm starting a long feature on a question AI labs mostly treat as an engineering detail: whose values an aligned AI system should actually serve. I don't want another survey of "AI ethics". I want the piece organised around the open conceptual questions on which everything else hangs, and I'd like your help deciding which those are.

To fix the territory: suppose a major lab resolves to align its flagship model not merely to its operator's instructions or to each user's momentary preferences, but to some defensible aggregation of human values. A family of problems immediately appears. There are social-choice-theoretic worries about whether any rule aggregating millions of inconsistent preferences can avoid Arrow-style pathologies. There are liberal-neutrality worries about whether a system that answers moral questions can remain neutral between conceptions of the good the way a liberal state aspires to be, or whether such neutrality is itself a sectarian position. There is a feedback problem: the system's answers will shape the very preferences that later rounds of aggregation measure, so the measured will may be partly the system's own creation. There is the standing question of who counts at all — future people, non-human animals, possible digital minds, dissenting minorities whose values lose every vote. And there are proposals on the table, such as handling the system's normative uncertainty through an internal "moral parliament" rather than committing to one ethical theory, or aiming at an idealised, extrapolated version of what people would value under better information and fuller reflection.

Please give me a defended prioritisation, not a survey. Identify the three or four open questions you would make the spine of the piece, rank them, and justify the ranking against explicit criteria: how much else depends on the answer, how tractable the question is to conceptual progress as opposed to awaiting empirical facts about model behaviour, how neglected it is relative to the attention it currently receives in public discussion, and what concrete developments over the next few years — a lab policy, a result in social choice or moral psychology, a regulatory move — would shift it up or down. Say also which questions dominating current coverage you would demote as overrated or misframed, and why. Bear in mind my constraint: the piece must stay intelligible and gripping to non-specialists, so a question earns its place only if its stakes can be made vivid without distortion.

Please answer in British English, leading with your bottom line in the first sentence — name the question you would put first — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my request.
