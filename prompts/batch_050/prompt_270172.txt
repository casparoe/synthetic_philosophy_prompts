I'm putting together an application for a nine-month postdoc fellowship in political philosophy and need a proposal of about two pages. My half-formed idea: nearly all the philosophical work on state apologies for historical wrongs asks when they're owed and what makes them sincere, and both questions get answered by looking at the moment the apology is delivered -- the words, the speaker, the ceremony, maybe accompanying reparations. I suspect this is a mistake. Whether a state's apology counts as genuine seems to depend at least partly on what the state does afterwards, which would mean later governments can hollow out an apology without ever formally retracting it, and -- weirder -- that an apology could become genuine years after it was issued, or quietly stop being one. If that's right, alot of the standard criteria for a successful apology are at best provisional verdicts. Turn this into a proper proposal: a precise statement of the question, why existing accounts of apology and of collective responsibility leave it open instead of already settling it, what I'd actually do month by month and why this is conceptual work rather than a survey of historical cases, what a result would look like including something usable by people who draft or assess real apologies, and what would count as the project failing.
