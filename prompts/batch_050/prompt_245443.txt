So I never studied philosophy or economics but I've gotten a bit obsessed with the question of how you should make decisions when you genuinely don't know the odds of anything, and after reading around I've written down where I've landed. I'd like to know if the package hangs together before I bore my friends with it again.

My positions:
(1) When there are no probabilities worth trusting, maximin is the rational rule -- pick the option whose worst-case outcome is least bad.
(2) Rawls got it right: behind a veil of ignorance where you don't know who you'll be, you'd choose whichever society is best for its worst-off member, which is basically maximin applied to society.
(3) When I do have rough probability estimates, expected value is the correct rule even for big decisions -- that's why I drive to the airport instead of leaving the night before, and why I never buy extended waranties.
(4) Minimax regret is a bad rule, because it can flip your ranking of two options just because somebody adds a third option to the menu that you'd never pick anyway.
(5) Harsanyi's move of just assigning equal probabilities to everything when you know nothing strikes me as dressing up ignorance as information.

Which of these actually clash, which only look like they clash, and what's the cheapest fix for each real clash? Please rank the clashes by severity instead of treating them all as equal, and at the end tell me which single commitment I'd have to drop or soften to clean up the most. My own guess is that (1) and (4) are the problem since they're both rules for ignorance and I'm keeping one while trashing the other, but I can't tell if that's right.
