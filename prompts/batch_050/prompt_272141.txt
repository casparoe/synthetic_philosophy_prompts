After a forty-year career as an experimental physicist, I retired and found myself, somewhat to my surprise, reading philosophy in the evenings. I mention this because I have a domestic dispute on my hands that I suspect instantiates a problem philosophers have thought about, and I would like advice that engages the principle at stake rather than merely mediating between the parties.

I am seventy-four and I still drive. Last month my daughter, after riding with me to collect my grandson from school, asked me to stop driving at night and to submit to an independent assessment each year. Her evidence struck me as thin: I braked slightly late at one junction and missed a turn I have taken hundreds of times. My position, which I put to her as evenly as I could manage, is that I am the person best placed to judge my own faculties. Impairment, I argued, is not like a crack in a bridge, invisible from the inside. If my attention or judgment were genuinely deteriorating, I would be aware of the deterioration; since I detect no confusion, no hesitation beyond the ordinary, there is nothing to detect. She answered with a sentence I have been turning over since: the trouble is that you would be the last to know.

The empirical facts are not really in dispute between us. My reactions have slowed slightly, as everyone's do, year on year, and neither of us can point to a threshold I have crossed. What divides us, I have come to think, is a principle about self-knowledge: whether a gradual decline in a capacity must announce itself to the person whose capacity it is, or whether there can be a stretch of the spectrum on which one has lost the capacity without being in any position to know that one has lost it. I confess the second possibility unsettles me, though I cannot say it is incoherent. In the laboratory we never trusted an instrument to report on the limits of its own resolution; I would once have said a mind is not an instrument, but I am no longer sure what work that distinction is doing.

A related point arose in the argument. I told her that I know my route home, and that when I know something I am ordinarily in a position to know that I know it — the knowledge is available to me for inspection, as it were. She replied that a feeling of certainty is just another one of my performances, and as fallible as the braking. I found that harder to answer than I expected.

So: whose position is sounder, and why? I would be grateful if your answer took up the underlying principle directly — under what conditions, if any, can a person be wrong about the state of their own mind without being able to detect the error — and then applied it to my case. Please resist the temptation to simply split the difference and recommend the assessment as a compromise; my daughter and I can negotiate the practical arrangements ourselves once we understand who has the better of the argument.
