I run a monthly reading group at my company — we're all engineers, nobody has formal philosophy training, but we've been working through material on irrationality and mental representation. Last month we got stuck on the clinical literature about delusions: Capgras (the conviction that a loved one has been replaced by an impostor), Cotard (the conviction that one is dead or doesn't exist), and similar cases. We learned about the two-factor theory of delusion — roughly, that one factor explains where the strange content comes from (some anomalous experience or perceptual deficit) while a second factor explains why the person adopts the belief and fails to revise it — but our discussion collapsed into an argument about a prior question: is a delusion even a belief at all? Some of us thought the answer is obviously yes (the patient sincerely asserts it, after all), others pointed to the odd ways patients fail to act on their delusions and said these must be something else, like imaginings mistaken for beliefs, and a third faction insisted the whole question is confused. I'd like you to build us a decision tree — a flowchart in text form — that sorts a respondent into a named position on the question "are delusions beliefs?" based purely on how they answer concrete diagnostic questions, not on which label they find appealing.

Here are the constraints I'd like you to respect. The tree should have between seven and ten diagnostic nodes, and each node should be a concrete case or a specific claim that the positions genuinely disagree about — for instance, how to interpret "double bookkeeping" (the Capgras patient who insists the nurses are impostors but cheerfully eats lunch with them), whether sincere assertion plus emotional distress suffices for belief, whether a belief that almost never guides action can still count as a belief, and whether someone can believe something they simultaneously describe as feeling unreal. Please phrase every node so a smart non-specialist can answer it without knowing the vocabulary (so no node that just asks "do you accept doxasticism?"). The leaves must include at least: the standard doxastic view (delusions are genuine, if grossly irrational, beliefs), at least one non-doxastic view on which delusions are some other attitude mistaken for or reported as belief, a hybrid or pluralist view on which the category of delusion splits, and a deflationary or error-theoretic leaf on which the question itself rests on a false assumption. Since our group was also arguing about the two-factor theory, I'd like the tree to include at least one node testing whether a respondent thinks an explanation of a delusion's content and persistence must be an explanation of a *belief's* adoption — i.e., whether adopting the two-factor framework commits you to doxasticism, or whether a non-doxasticist can coherently use it.

Finally, and this is the part I'd most like done carefully: build the tree from the logical space first. Enumerate the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions within that space, and for every unoccupied combination say a sentence about why it's empty — is the position incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored in the literature as you understand it? If two of the leaf positions cannot actually be separated by any behavioral or case-based question and only differ in how they describe the same facts, flag that explicitly rather than papering over it with a fake distinguishing node. End with a worked example: take one fictional respondent with a specified answer pattern, walk them through the tree node by node, and state which leaf they land on and what the tree cannot tell you about their view. The audience is my reading group, so the final artifact should be usable as a one-page handout, with the commentary (empty cells, inseparability flags) in clearly marked footnotes we can discuss after everyone has been sorted.
