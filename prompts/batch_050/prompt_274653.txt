I am advising the constitutional court of a country emerging from two decades of abusive, arbitrary government. The court has limited political capital and cannot contest every defect in the legal system at once; in its first years it must decide which failures of legality to treat as intolerable and which to tolerate for the time being. Take as the menu Lon Fuller's eight principles of legality: generality (conduct governed by announced rules rather than ad hoc orders), publicity, prospectivity (no retroactive rules), clarity, consistency (rules must not contradict one another), practicability (rules must not demand the impossible), stability through time, and congruence between the rules as announced and the rules as actually administered. Produce a tiered ranking of the eight, ordered by how much damage the systematic violation of each does to law's capacity to guide the conduct of ordinary people: the top tier for violations that make it nearly impossible for citizens to plan their lives around the law at all, the bottom tier for violations that degrade law without destroying its guidance function. Give the reasoning for each placement in a sentence or two, and mark explicitly where two principles are interdependent in practice, so that an official violation of one quietly produces a violation of another. If you believe two principles genuinely belong on the same tier, group them rather than forcing an order.

Then stress-test your ranking against two opposing conceptions of what the rule of law is for. On Joseph Raz's view, these principles are instrumental virtues of law as such, in the way sharpness is a virtue of a knife: they make law good at guiding conduct whether its ends are wicked or benign, so a morally repugnant system can in principle satisfy them fully. On Jeremy Waldron's view, at least some elements of legality carry non-instrumental weight, because governing people through reasons, argument, and procedures they can answer is itself a form of respect for their agency. Does either framework reorder your tiers? And does the court's specific mission — consolidating a transition away from arbitrariness in a society where citizens have spent twenty years learning not to plan around official promises — push you toward Raz's instrumental reading or Waldron's dignitarian one, or does the transitional setting change which violations matter most independently of either framework?
