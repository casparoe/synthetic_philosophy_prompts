So I'm a backend engineer and somehow I got voluntold to give a lunch-and-learn at work about why our support chatbot keeps faceplanting on certain kinds of questions. The concrete incident: a user typed "how do I cancel the extra seat I added to my subscription" and the bot happily generated cancel instructions, except the user never added an extra seat, there was nothing to cancel, and the whole conversation spiraled. My pet theory is the bot treats every question as if all its background loading already happened, and humans don't work like that -- we quietly patch in missing context, or push back, and the bot does neither.

While nerdsniping myself on this I fell down a hole and found linguists/philosophers actually have machinery for exactly this: presupposition, accommodation, and Lewis's scorekeeping-in-a-language-game thing. The scoreboard metaphor maps embarassingly well onto stuff I already think about (shared mutable state, transactions, when do you reject a write vs silently migrate the schema). So I want to give my talk on THAT, using the chatbot failure as the hook, to an audience of smart engineers with zero linguistics or philosophy background. ~30 minutes, slides allowed.

Don't give me the talk itself -- plan it. Specifically:

1. The order the three concepts have to come in, and why. My instinct is presupposition first, then accommodation as "what humans do when the presupposition isn't already on the board," then scorekeeping as the frame that makes the first two look like one mechanism instead of two. But maybe that's backwards and you need the scoreboard first for any of it to make sense? Tell me the dependency structure.
2. The one example sentence that should carry the whole talk. I'm torn between "have you stopped cheating on the latency benchmarks" (fun, accusatory) and something quieter like "my sister's coming to visit" said to a coworker who didn't know I had a sister -- because that one seems to show accommodation happening silently with no friction. Pick one (or a better one) and tell me what it needs to demonstrate and what it must NOT contain (e.g. I assume it can't be a case where the presupposition is so weird people would just object, since that derails into a different phenomenon).
3. The misconceptions engineers specifically will form, in the order they'll form them, and where in the sequence to head each off. My guesses: (a) "presupposition" just means any unstated assumption, (b) a failed presupposition makes the sentence meaningless rather than making the move illegal, (c) accommodation is just people being polite / doing charity, not a default mechanic, and (d) the scoreboard is a literal data structure someone maintains, like an actual algorithmic proposal rather than a model. Correct my guesses or add ones I'm missing.
4. One question or exercise I can end with that shows they got it -- ideally something where they have to diagnose a new chatbot failure themselves rather than recite definitions.
5. What I should openly refuse to cover at this level -- I assume the whole Russell vs Strawson fight about what happens on presupposition failure is a trap here, but tell me if I'm wrong and there's a way to gesture at it that actually pays off for this audience.

Bonus points if the plan explains how to use the database/schema-migration analogy without letting it eat the talk, since I can tell it's the kind of analogy engineers will over-extend within 90 seconds.
