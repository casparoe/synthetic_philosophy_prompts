I'm a software engineer leading the choice of a data-storage technology for a system that has to stay reliable for roughly a decade. For the established option, I can give fairly confident probability estimates for the relevant failure modes — hardware churn, scaling bottlenecks, vendor issues — because there's a long track record. For the newer option, I honestly can't: not because the evidence says each outcome is equally likely, but because I have almost no basis for distributing my confidence at all. My tech lead insists this distinction is incoherent. On his view, if I can't produce probabilities, I should treat every outcome as equally likely; if the options then come out tied, I should just flip a coin, and if I refuse to flip, that proves I secretly do have estimates and should state them. But here's what puzzles me: I'd happily flip a fair coin between two options I've researched thoroughly, yet I flinch at flipping between the familiar option and the opaque one — and I'd even prefer that a coin, rather than my own choice, commit me to the unfamiliar one. Is my tech lead right that I'm being irrational, or is his framework flattening a real distinction?
