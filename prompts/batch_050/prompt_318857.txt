Next month I am presenting a paper at a graduate workshop in practical ethics, and I would like a prep sheet for the question period. Here is the paper's position. I defend the moral significance of the distinction between killing and letting die, but not on the usual grounds: I argue the distinction does not rest on agency, intention, or causal involvement as such. My claim is that the two categories typically differ in what they cost and whose resources they commandeer. Letting die almost always means declining to deploy resources that the agent controls and that often have rival claimants (the agent's own time, money, body, or competing duties), whereas killing typically commandeers the victim's body and life as a means to the agent's end. On my view, the standard equivalence cases — the man who lets his cousin drown in the bath to inherit, contrasted with the man who holds the cousin's head under — do not show the distinction is morally inert. They show only that when you zero out the cost asymmetry, both agents act wrongly and equally so. The paper argues that medical ethics has been misled by building its cases in that artificial zero-cost register.

The audience is the complication: mostly consequentialist-leaning philosophers of medicine and a few decision theorists, people professionally suspicious of the doctrine of doing and allowing, who regard the killing/letting die distinction as a vestige that clinical ethics should discard (many of them think the withdrawal/withholding distinction in end-of-life care is equally spurious, and they will hear my paper as smuggling it back in). They are charitable listeners but they will not accept appeals to intuition, and they will test any principle I offer against constructed cases.

Prepare me as follows. List the objections I should expect, ordered by how likely each is to be raised and how damaging it is if I answer badly. Phrase each objection the way this particular audience would phrase it, in their vocabulary — equivalence arguments, counterfactual accounts of causation, demandingness complaints about aid, policy implications for triage and end-of-life protocols — rather than as a generic ethics question. For each objection, give me a reply of two or three sentences that I can actually deliver under time pressure, then a note on where that reply is weakest and what the follow-up attack will be. Tell me explicitly what I should concede at the outset to lower the temperature, and which premise of mine I must never let them reframe as a claim about intrinsic features of acts versus omissions. Include the two or three hardest constructed cases they are likely to improvise on the spot (for instance, cases where the cost asymmetry and the commandeering feature come apart), and rehearse my answers to them. Finally, give me the single objection I am privately hoping nobody raises — I suspect it concerns whether my account can distinguish justified letting-die from mere miserliness when the resources at stake are the agent's own but trivial — along with the best answer I have available and an honest assessment of whether it survives.
