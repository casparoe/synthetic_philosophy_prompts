Twice a year I chair the small grants panel of a learned society: seven of us, and a proposal gets funded only if it passes three yes/no tests -- is the research significant, is the plan feasible, is the budget sensible. We've always voted test-by-test and taken the majority on each. Last round this blew up: a proposal passed each test 4-3, but when somebody grumbled and asked for a straight up-or-down vote on funding it, only three of us said yes -- a different three each time, roughly. I've since learnt philosophers call this the discursive dilemma, and apparently there's no obviously right fix.

Write me a standing procedure the panel can actually adopt: for any given decision, how do we choose between aggregating the test votes and aggregating overall verdicts; how do we spot in advance that the two methods are about to diverge, before a result gets announced; and when they do diverge, what then -- forced discussion, a revote, chair's discretion? Cover abstentions and the member who votes strategically on the tests because they've guessed how everyone else will vote. Mark clearly which steps are real judgement calls dressed up as procedure. Then run the procedure on last round's case above and tell me what it outputs, including if the output is one I won't like.
