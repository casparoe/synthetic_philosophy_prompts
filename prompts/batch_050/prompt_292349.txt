Gettier's 1963 paper ran to about three pages, and the literature attempting to repair the justified-true-belief analysis of knowledge has now run for six decades without convergence: every proposed fourth condition — no false lemmas, indefeasibility, an appropriate causal connection, reliability, aptness of belief — has been met with a fresh counterexample, and general recipes now exist for generating Gettier-style cases against any analysis that leaves a logical gap between justification and truth. Meanwhile, some philosophers have abandoned the project altogether, treating knowledge as unanalysable and making it the starting point of epistemology rather than its object, while others continue to publish new cases and new conditions as though the finish line were real. My working hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but two conducted under a single heading: a substantive dispute about the nature of knowledge and a methodological dispute about what intuitions regarding hypothetical cases are evidence of — and that the second, which is rarely defended explicitly on either side, is doing most of the work. Is that the right diagnosis? If the disagreement is genuinely substantive, locate it precisely: is it about the concept of knowledge, the mental state, or the English word? If it is methodological, explain why the parties do not recognise it as such, and say what could settle it given that experimental data on lay intuitions have not. Either way, I want a verdict on whether anything — a conceptual distinction, a formal result, a change of question — could end the debate, or whether no progress should be expected and why. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. State what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would produce a different diagnosis, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. Please write in British English.
