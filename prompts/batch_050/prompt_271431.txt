Construct a thought experiment in which two agents with common priors, common knowledge of each other's rationality and risk-neutrality, and no liquidity or hedging motives nevertheless trade an asset with each other at prices both find favorable. The catch: exactly one standard assumption behind Aumann-Milgrom-Stokey-style no-trade results must be doing the work, and your job is to find the weakest one. You may not appeal to noise traders, differences in pure time preference, or agents who simply enjoy gambling; the trade has to arise from something structurally more interesting, such as the way information arrives, the partition structure of each agent's knowledge, or what is and isn't common knowledge about the trading mechanism itself. Then do the verification: list the canonical assumptions of the no-trade result, state which one your case violates and show explicitly that the others hold in your scenario, and walk through why the trade is incentive-compatible for both parties rather than merely stipulated. Finish with the strongest objection that a defender of the theorem's generality would raise against your construction — in particular, whether your case quietly smuggles in a second violated assumption — and say how you would respond.
