I am a third-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of language seminar, and I have volunteered to run one session of our department's interdisciplinary reading group, which draws students from linguistics, computer science, and literature, most of whom have no formal philosophy background. Our chosen text is Douglas Hofstadter's satirical essay A Person Paper on Purity in Language (collected in Metamagical Themas). For anyone unfamiliar: the essay presents itself as an earnest defence of a dialect of English in which the words "white" and "black" do all the work that "man" and "woman" do in ours -- one speaks of "chairwhites," of "whites and blacks" as the two natural kinds of person, and of the generic "white" as obviously neutral -- so that the reader slowly realizes the piece is a mirror held up to gendered English rather than a claim about race. The essay's argumentative force depends almost entirely on this delayed recognition: a reader who is told the point in advance experiences it as a gimmick.

The members of the group will not have read the essay beforehand, and I will have sixty minutes. Please plan how I should teach it -- do not simply summarize the essay for me. Specifically, I want: (1) the order in which the component ideas must be introduced and the dependency structure among them -- for instance, what has to be in place about defaults, markedness, or grammatical convention before the satire can do its work, and whether I should let the group encounter the inverted dialect cold or prepare them in some way that preserves the dawning realization rather than spoiling it; (2) the one segment or device of the essay to build the session around, and what that segment must contain and must avoid in order to carry the whole session; (3) the misconceptions I should expect, in roughly the order they will arise -- I anticipate at least the reading that takes the essay to be making a claim about racism, the reading that takes it as an argument for banning particular words, and the dismissal that treats the whole thing as an exercise in political correctness -- and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off rather than merely correcting it afterward; (4) how I should handle the moment of the reveal, including whether to let the discomfort sit and for how long; (5) one question I can pose at the end that would show the group has understood the mechanism of the satire rather than just its conclusion -- ideally something that asks them to reproduce the move in a new domain; and (6) what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so, such as the empirical linguistics of the generic masculine or the later reception and criticism of the essay.
