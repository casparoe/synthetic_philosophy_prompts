Two friends of mine (both engineers, no philosophy training) got into a surprisingly heated argument during a long drive back from a conference, and I got drafted as referee even though I mostly just listened to a podcast about this stuff once. The topic was whether being able to imagine something tells you it's possible. It started when Maya brought up the zombie thought experiment: she said she can clearly conceive of a creature that's a perfect physical duplicate of her -- same brain, same neurons firing, same behavior -- but with no inner experience at all, no "what it's like." Since she can genuinely conceive of it, she said, it must be possible, and if it's possible then consciousness must be something over and above the physical stuff, so physicalism is false. Dev pushed back hard: conceivability is a lousy guide to possibility. People for centuries could "conceive" of water being something other than H2O, but it turns out that's impossible, water just IS H2O. Same with someone before telescopes conceiving that the morning star and evening star could be different objects -- conceivable, sure, but actually impossible since they're both Venus. So her zombie intuition is probably just the same kind of mistake and proves nothing.

Maya's response (this is where it got interesting) was that those cases are all misdescribed. When the ancient person "conceives" of water without H2O, what they're really conceiving is a world where some clear drinkable liquid fills the rivers and that liquid isn't H2O -- and that IS possible, it's just not water. They never conceived of water itself being non-H2O because they never had a full enough grip on what water is. But she claims the zombie case isn't like that: she's not conceiving of something that plays the consciousness role while lacking consciousness, she's conceiving of a full physical duplicate and then just... subtracting the experience itself. There's no descriptive middleman to fix the mistake the way "clear drinkable liquid" fixes the water case. Dev answered that this is overconfident: how does she know she's actually conceiving what she thinks she is, rather than just failing to notice a hidden incoherence? His analogy was a medieval person who "conceives" of a right triangle where the square of the hypotenuse isn't the sum of the squares of the sides -- seems perfectly imaginable until you learn the proof, then it stops being imaginable at all. Conceivability, he said, is just "haven't found the contradiction yet," which tracks the state of your knowledge, not the structure of reality. Maya's last move was that this standard is self-defeating: if conceivability never tracks possibility, then Dev has no grounds for believing the laws of physics could have been different, or even that his own morning could have gone differently, and he'd have to treat every necessity as absolute -- which is a way crazier conclusion than dualism.

So: who's right at each step? I want a move-by-move breakdown, not just an overall winner. Specifically -- does Maya's "no middleman" distinction actually separate the zombie case from the water/morning-star cases, or is Dev right that she's just assuming she sees the physical facts clearly enough to know nothing was subtracted improperly? Does Dev's triangle analogy generalize the way he needs it to, or does it prove too much (Maya's self-defeat charge)? And what, if anything, are both of them missing about what conceivability arguments can and can't show? I honestly can't tell who won and it's bugging me.
