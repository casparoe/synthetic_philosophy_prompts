In the political philosophy seminar I TA for, we just finished three weeks on the liberal–communitarian debate, and the students were left unsatisfied in a way I couldn't fully justify to them. The striking thing is not that the debate was never settled but how it ended: not with a verdict, not even with a stable standoff, but with a quiet loss of interest sometime in the mid-1990s. Rawls recast his theory as political rather than comprehensive, writers like Kymlicka argued that liberalism could absorb most of what communitarians wanted about culture and belonging, and the major communitarian critics — Sandel, MacIntyre, Taylor, Walzer — moved on to other projects. The debate did not conclude; it evaporated.

Here is my working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than confirmed. I suspect the debate stalled because it was never one disagreement but three running on parallel tracks: a metaphysical dispute about whether the self is prior to its ends or partly constituted by them; a methodological dispute about whether principles of justice must be justified universally or can only be interpreted out of a community's shared understandings; and a first-order political dispute about whether the right is prior to the good. Crucially, these tracks do not line up — one can grant the communitarian picture of the embedded self and still insist on liberal neutrality, and one can reject neutrality for reasons having nothing to do with the self. If that is right, the two sides were constantly trading arguments across tracks, meeting a point about the self with a point about state neutrality, which would explain why nothing ever landed.

So: is this diagnosis correct, and if so, what kind of disagreement was the debate really — verbal, substantive but at cross purposes, or something else? If it is wrong, where does the real locus of disagreement lie? And what would have counted as progress: a decisive argument, a widely accepted distinction, an agreed criterion of success? Finally, does the evaporation pattern itself tell us something — is a debate that ends by absorption rather than resolution a sign that the original question was ill-posed, or is quiet absorption a genuine, if unglamorous, form of philosophical progress?
