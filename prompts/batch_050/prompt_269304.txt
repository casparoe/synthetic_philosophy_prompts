I serve on a small advisory panel that has been asked to recommend whether, and how, a national government should apologize for a grave wrong committed by that state several generations ago. The discussions keep stalling in the same way: one faction insists an apology is owed only by the people who actually did the harm, and they are all dead; another insists the state is a continuing institution that can incur and discharge obligations across centuries; a third thinks talk of apology is empty unless accompanied by material redress, and that an apology with money attached is really a settlement wearing ceremonial dress. Everyone reaches for a different historical case as decisive — Australia's apology to the Stolen Generations, the American apology and payments to the citizens it interned during the Second World War, Canada's apologies to survivors of the residential school system, or the states that have flatly refused to apologize on the ground that the present generation bears no responsibility — and each case gets deployed as a verdict rather than a datum. I would like the specification first.

So: what must a theory of state apology actually be able to do to count as adequate? Fix the purpose as this: a theory good enough to guide a real government deciding whether it owes an apology, to whom, in what form, and what that apology would commit it to — not a theory good enough merely to rationalize a verdict already chosen. Give me the requirements list. Which cases must it handle, and what would count as handling them? Which distinctions may it not collapse — apology versus expression of regret versus bare acknowledgment of fact; collective responsibility versus collective guilt; apologizing to the victims themselves versus their descendants versus the dead; symbolic versus material redress? Which constraints must it respect: that a state can persist while every person who acted for it has died; that many current citizens descend from neither perpetrators nor victims; that an apology can be refused by its intended recipients, and it is unclear whether refusal discharges the obligation or merely frustrates it; that an apology costing nothing may be worthless, while one costing something may cease to look like an apology at all?

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether it strikes you as a genuine desideratum or a prejudice of one camp — the "only wrongdoers can apologize" camp, the "continuity of the institution" camp, the "forward-looking repair" camp, or the expressivist camp that treats apology as the wrong tool entirely for events beyond living memory. I am especially interested in whether some subset of the plausible requirements is jointly unsatisfiable; I suspect a trilemma lurking among the claims that only wrongdoers can apologize, that some historical wrongs call for apology, and that states outlive everyone who acted for them, and I would like you to confirm or dismantle that suspicion. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding existing views, and — briefly, in a table if convenient — score the main contenders against the list, keeping the weight on the criteria themselves rather than the scoring.
