So here's the thing. I'm teaching a philosophy of mind seminar next term and I've always been pretty sympathetic to Searle's Chinese Room. But a friend of mine (comp sci background, annoyingly thorough) walked me through an argument over coffee that seems to use Searle's own machinery to prove something insane, and I honestly can't find the loose screw. I want you to tell me where it goes wrong, or if it doesn't, which plank I should saw off.

The bit of Searle my friend kept leaning on is from the rejoinder to the systems reply in "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980), where Searle says that even if the man memorizes the rulebook and works the whole thing in his head:

"If he doesn't understand then there is no way the system could understand because the system is just part of him"

and, a bit later, brushing off the idea that understanding might show up anywhere information processing does:

"there is a level of description at which my stomach does information processing"

OK so the argument, roughly as my friend gave it:

(1) Searle's internalization rejoinder is a general test, not a one-off trick. To check whether some programmed system understands Chinese, imagine one person internalizing the whole thing -- memorizing the rules, doing all the lookups and updates in their head, working outdoors, whatever. If that person still understands nothing, nothing in the setup understands, because the system is just part of them. Crucially the test doesn't care what the program is; Searle is explicit that nothing hangs on the details of Schank's story-understanding program, it would apply to any simulation of any mental phenomenon.

(2) So pick the most unfavorable program for my friend's purposes: a complete neuron-level simulation of an actual native Chinese speaker's brain. A giant book specifying every neuron and its update rule -- given these input firings, fire or don't fire. It's tedious but it's just a rulebook correlating uninterpreted symbols, exactly the kind of thing Searle's test is built for. If anything is "just syntax," a lookup table of neuron states is.

(3) Now run the test: someone (or a patient team, the manpower details are in-principle irrelevant) works through the simulation by hand while a Chinese conversation's inputs are fed in at the sensory neurons and the outputs are read off the motor neurons. By construction the runner understands no Chinese; they're just updating neuron states.

(4) By Searle's test, then, no Chinese is understood anywhere in this process. There isn't anything in the system that isn't in the runner, and the runner understands nothing. Same logic as the original room.

(5) But this process mirrors, transition for transition, the causal structure of the native speaker's brain while she chats with a friend. Every causal event in her brain has a corresponding event in the simulation. There's no level of description at which her brain does something the simulation doesn't also do.

(6) So either the hand-run simulation understands Chinese after all -- in which case Searle's test gives the wrong verdict and the internalization rejoinder collapses -- or the native speaker's brain doesn't understand Chinese either, since nothing distinguishes the two processes except the medium, and Searle's whole point is that the medium is irrelevant.

(7) The second horn is absurd; my grandmother speaks Shanghainese and understands it fine. So Searle's rejoinder proves too much: taken as a general test, it doesn't just refute strong AI, it rules out physically implemented understanding across the board, wetware included.

Now, I can't accept (7). But I also really want to keep the original Chinese Room verdict -- that running the Schank program doesn't amount to understanding -- and I obviously want to keep "my grandmother understands Shanghainese." I suspect the damage is somewhere in (4)-(6) but every time I poke at a step it looks load-bearing. If the argument is actually valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to abandon and what I lose by abandoning it. And definately tell me whether whatever fix you propose reopens the systems reply against the original room, because that's the whole reason Searle needed the rejoinder in the first place.

(yes/no plus a few words). If you think more than one step is fishy, still pick a primary culprit in the prose and say why it beats the alternatives.
