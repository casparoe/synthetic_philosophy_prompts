I write a column on science and ideas, and last month I moderated a panel on governance and growth at a policy institute. Afterward, over coffee, two of the panelists got into a dispute I couldn't settle, and I'd like a careful referee. The first, a development economist I'll call Renata, argued that prosperity and order can contribute to a government's legitimacy but can never ground it. Her core claim was that legitimacy is about the source of authority — consent, accountability, the right of the ruled to replace the rulers — not about outputs. A regime that delivers rising incomes and safe streets, the way China has and Singapore does, is at most a well-performing dictatorship, she said, and calling it legitimate confuses satisfaction with authorization. She added a second point: performance-based legitimacy is inherently unstable, because the moment growth stalls, a regime that rests on nothing else has no residual claim on obedience, whereas an elected government that governs badly is still, in a real sense, owed compliance until it's voted out.

The second panelist, a historian I'll call Marcus, pushed back hard. He argued that Renata was treating one particular legitimacy-conferring procedure — competitive elections — as if it were the concept itself, when really every legitimacy standard is a performance standard of some kind. Elections, he said, are themselves just a ritual we endorse because we believe they tend to produce tolerable governments; if they reliably produced chaos and starvation, we'd stop granting them moral weight. So the real question is always whether a regime delivers what government is for, and by that standard decades of rising living standards, low crime, and effective administration in China or Singapore count heavily. When Renata objected that apparent public acceptance in those countries can't be trusted because dissent is suppressed and surveys are unreliable, Marcus replied that consent is no easier to measure in democracies — low turnout, widespread cynicism, and voters choosing between two candidates they dislike hardly certify authorization either. Renata's rejoinder was that this cuts the wrong way: the point isn't that democratic consent is easy to observe but that free elections and a free press at least make rejection *possible*, so acceptance there is informative in a way that acquiescence under censorship can never be. Marcus answered that Renata had quietly switched from a definition of legitimacy to a method of measuring it, and that even if acceptance is hard to measure, it doesn't follow that outcomes confer no legitimacy — only that we should be cautious in estimating how much.

I'd like you to adjudicate their exchange point by point: for each of the main moves on both sides, say whether it succeeds, fails, or partly succeeds, and why. Then give an overall verdict — who is more right, and on which specific issue — rather than splitting the difference by default. I'd also like you to identify any consideration both of them are missing that would materially change the analysis. Finally, since I'll be quoting your verdict in my notebook: please state explicitly what would change your mind — which argument or piece of evidence, if supplied, would flip your overall verdict, and where in your reasoning you feel least certain.
