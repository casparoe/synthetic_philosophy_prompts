I am a teaching assistant preparing a single 75-minute seminar on vagueness for second-year philosophy undergraduates. The students are comfortable with classical propositional logic and have met the principle of bivalence, but they have done no philosophy of language. The session's aim is narrow but precise: by the end, they should understand epistemicism and supervaluationism as rival diagnoses of the sorites paradox — what each view actually claims, what each costs, and why neither is merely foolish. I do not want the explanation itself; I want a plan for how I should give it.

Please address the following.

First, the order of ideas. What must be introduced before what, and why does each stage depend on the previous one? My instinct is to open with a concrete sorites argument and make the students commit to which premise or inference step they reject before either theory is named, so that epistemicism and supervaluationism enter as answers to a felt problem rather than as doctrines to be memorized — but tell me if this sequencing is wrong. The plan should also cover how to establish that saying 'the predicate is just vague' restates the puzzle rather than solving it, since the paradox is an argument with apparently true premises and apparently valid reasoning, and something specific has to give.

Second, the carrying example. I want one predicate-and-series that runs through the entire session — not 'heap' or 'bald', which the students will pattern-match from popular accounts without thinking. State what the example must have (a sorites series whose adjacent steps are indiscriminable; a setting in which a sharp cutoff would be genuinely shocking rather than pedantic; enough internal structure that the higher-order vagueness objection to supervaluationism can later be exhibited on the very same case) and what it must lack, and then name the example you would actually use and defend the choice.

Third, the misconception map. I expect at least the following: (a) vagueness is ordinary ignorance that better measurement could cure; (b) stipulating a cutoff by fiat dissolves the paradox; (c) supervaluationism says borderline claims are 'neither true nor false, so anything goes'; (d) epistemicism holds that we could in principle locate the boundary by polling enough competent speakers; (e) 'supertrue' is just the supervaluationist's relabeling of true, so the view quietly collapses back into classical bivalence. For each misconception, indicate the point in the sequence where it will predictably arise and how to head it off at exactly that point rather than in a corrective appendix at the end. Add any I have missed.

Fourth, a segment built around a proposal of my own invention, which I will call the Calibration Proposal: convene a panel of one hundred competent speakers, march them stepwise through the series, and declare the boundary to fall at the first step where a majority switches its verdict — matter settled. Plan this segment so that it functions as a live diagnostic: what it should reveal is that the proposal is neither epistemicism nor supervaluationism, that it confuses legislating a usage with discovering one or precisifying one, and that it relocates the vagueness rather than removing it (borderline panelists, borderline majorities, the arbitrariness of the 51 percent threshold). Tell me at what point in the session to deploy it and what student responses would show that it has done its work.

Fifth, the comprehension check. Give me one closing question that discriminates genuine understanding from recitation — for instance, one requiring the students to state, for the carrying example, what each theory says about a specified borderline step and what each theory says we can know about it — and specify what a passing answer must contain.

Sixth, what to leave out. Identify what I should openly refuse to cover at this level (my candidates: three-valued and degree logics, the formal margin-for-error argument associated with epistemicism's defenders, and the forced-march version of the paradox), and advise me on how to flag these omissions without making them sound like evasions.

Structure your answer under the following headings, in this order: 'Goal and prerequisites' (roughly 80 words); 'The sequence' (roughly 450 words); 'The carrying example' (roughly 150 words); 'Misconception map' (roughly 250 words); 'The Calibration Proposal segment' (roughly 150 words); 'Comprehension check' (roughly 100 words); 'Deliberate omissions' (roughly 80 words).
