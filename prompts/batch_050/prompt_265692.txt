So for my philosophy of mind reading group I have to circulate a one-page statement of my positions before our session on consciousness, and I want to stress-test it before people start poking at it. Over the course of the semester I've basically talked myself into epiphenomenalism, and my roommate (an engineering major, annoyingly good at finding the weak point in anything) keeps insisting that my view "explains away the very thing that made me believe it in the first place." I think he's being glib, but I figured I'd write down everything I actually hold and get an outside audit before I have to defend it in front of people who've read more than me. Here it is:

1. Phenomenal properties — the redness of red, the hurt of pain — are not identical to or reducible to any physical or functional property. Zombie scenarios are genuinely conceivable, and I think that teaches us something real about what consciousness is.
2. The physical world is causally closed: every physical event that has a cause has a fully sufficient physical cause.
3. Phenomenal properties never cause anything physical. What makes me pull my hand off the stove, say ouch, and type this sentence is entirely the neural machinery.
4. I'm a causal theorist about knowledge, roughly: for me to know that p, my believing p has to be hooked up in the right way to the fact that p — a merely lucky true belief doesn't count.
5. I know that I'm having an experience right now, and it's not a lucky guess — honestly it's the most certain thing I know, more certain than anything about the external world.
6. Everyday explanations of behavior in terms of feelings are correct and genuinely explanatory, not just figures of speech — he jerked his hand back because the pan was hot and it hurt, she left the party because the awkwardness was unbearable.
7. My introspective reports are extremly reliable indicators of my phenomenal states: when I sincerely judge that I'm in pain, I almost always am.

What I want to know: does this package hang together? Please go through and tell me which commitments are in genuine tension and which only look like they are but actually fit fine. I'd like the real tensions ranked by severity rather than treated as equally bad, and for each one, what exactly would have to give — not just "reconsider your views" but which specific commitment is doing the damage and what the cheapest revision looks like. And if one single change would clean up most of the mess, tell me which one, even if it's a painful one. Also tell me straight whether my roommate has actually landed on something or whether he's just repeating a slogan he heard somewhere.
