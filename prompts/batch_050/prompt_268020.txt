I teach a seminar on formal epistemology, about 14 undergrads, one 80-minute meeting, and we just got to Aumann's agreement theorem. They nod along to the proof sketch fine, but none of them *feel* it. They still walk around thinking "I guess we'll have to agree to disagree" is something two reasonable, fully informed people can say to each other, and the theorem says exactly that this is off the table for agents with a common prior who keep honestly exchanging their posteriors. I want an activity that makes them watch their own disagreement dissolve in real time before I ever put the theorem's name on the board.

Design an in-class game for this. My rough thought is something where there's an unknown quantity in the room (a jar of beads with a mixed composition, a target number generated from some random process, whatever), everyone gets private partial evidence about it, and then they have to publicly announce their current best estimate round after round, updating as they hear each other -- so they can watch a genuinely spread-out set of opening estimates ratchet toward each other even though nobody's private evidence changed. But you should feel free to scrap that if you have a better mechanism. What I need from you:

- Concrete rules and materials, including what the private evidence actually is and how to stop students from just peeking or pooling raw data (the point is they exchange *estimates*, not evidence, or the magic is gone)
- A payoff scheme that rewards accuracy without real money, and that doesn't accidentally reward sticking to your guns or herd behavior
- A timeline for the 80 minutes, including how many announcement rounds to run and when to stop
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the machinery of the theorem -- the common prior, the iterative announcements, the way your estimate of the quantity ends up encoding your estimate of everyone else's rationality
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I assume at least one student will anchor on their first estimate out of stubborness, someone will try to strategically misreport, and some pairs will converge for the wrong reason (just copying the confident person). Tell me how to handle those, and whether any of them are actually features I should exploit in the debrief rather than bugs to prevent.
- One honest paragraph on what the game can't reproduce. My students are not Bayesian updaters, there's no real common knowledge of rationality in the room, and I can't literally hand them a common prior -- so which premise of the theorem am I faking, and what do I need to say out loud in the debrief to paper over it without pretending the game proved more than it did?

No lecture before the game, please. They should be mid-convergence and slightly weirded out by it before they hear Aumann's name.
