I spent forty years as an experimental physicist (condensed matter, national lab) and since retiring I've gotten hooked on philosophy, mostly via a lifelong-learning group that meets at our local library — a dozen of us: retired teachers, a dentist, an accountant, bright people but zero philosophy background. I've volunteered to run a session on a question that itches me personally, because I was once scooped on a result by a group in Japan who published three months ahead of us, completly independently, and I've never fully made my peace with whether that should bother me. The session is on simultaneous discovery — Newton and Leibniz and the calculus, Darwin and Wallace and natural selection, plus dozens of smaller cases — and the two questions tangled up in it: are discoveries inevitable, and does it actually matter who made them.

Don't give me a lecture on the topic; give me the plan for running the evening. Specifically:

- The order of ideas. What has to be in place before "inevitable" is even a well-formed claim for them? My instinct is they first need to see that a discovery isn't a single event — having the idea, working it out properly, and getting it accepted are different things, and simultaneity can strike at different points in that chain. But maybe something more basic has to come first. Tell me the dependency order and why each piece presupposes the previous one.

- The carrying example. I want one story to build the whole session on. My default was Darwin and Wallace, partly because both men were famously prompted by the same book on population, which is a gift for showing how shared conditions do the work. But the Newton and Leibniz case has the ugly priority fight, which serves the second question better. Pick one, say why it beats the other, and tell me what the example must contain — and what it must avoid containing (I suspect that if the room starts wondering about plagiarism, the example is ruined).

- The misconception map. What will a room of smart non-specialists predictably get wrong, roughly in the order it will come up? I'm guessing at least: "simultaneous means identical" (when the two versions often differ in ways that mattered later), a great-man picture of history humming away in the background, and some version of "if it was going to happen anyway then nobody deserves credit," which collapses my two questions into each other. For each one, where in the sequence do I head it off, and how?

- The pivot. These are practical people. I want them to arrive at the idea that credit systems are doing a job — incentives, signalling whose results to build on — rather than being pure vanity, without me lecturing them about it. Where in the evening does this belong, and what is the moment to turn from the history to it?

- A closing question I can put to the room whose answers will show me whether they grasped the distinction between the two questions or mushed them together.

Finally, tell me what to deliberately leave out at this level. I assume the academic sociology of "multiple discovery" is a swamp for a single evening, but correct me if some piece of it is load-bearing.
