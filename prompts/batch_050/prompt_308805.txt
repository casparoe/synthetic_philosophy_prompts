In Chapter II of Pierre-Simon Laplace's A Philosophical Essay on Probabilities (the 1902 Truscott and Emory translation of the sixth French edition, public domain and freely available via Project Gutenberg), Laplace introduces his account of chance by saying, in effect, that the theory of chance consists in reducing all events of the same kind to a number of equally possible cases — cases about which we are equally undecided — and then taking the probability of an event to be the ratio of the cases favorable to it to the total number of cases. Read the original passage and keep it in view throughout what follows; the task turns on it. (The passage itself does not count toward any length limit below.)

Construct a single decision problem under genuine ignorance — the agent is given no probabilities, and the story must make it plausible that none are available — with exactly three acts and a small number of states, engineered so that three rules deliver three different verdicts: (1) maximin picks one act, (2) minimax regret picks a second, and (3) expected-value maximization under Laplacean equiprobability across the states picks a third. Constraints on the scenario: it must be realistic and non-catastrophic (a shopkeeper, a farmer, a clinic administrator; no demons, no predictors, no science fiction); the stakes must be ones an ordinary person could face; the scenario must be stated in one paragraph; no act may be weakly dominated; and the state space must be natural enough that the equiprobability verdict cannot be dismissed as an artifact of an arbitrary partitioning — that is, the case should be one in which Laplace's "equally undecided" condition has at least some prima facie grip, since the interesting failure is not the trivial one where the principle of indifference obviously cannot be applied.

After the scenario, do the following, in order:

1. Give the payoff table explicitly and compute the verdict of each of the three rules, showing the reasoning rather than stipulating it.
2. Interpret Laplace's passage against the case: does the scenario actually contain cases "equally possible" in his sense, or does it merely invite us to treat them so? State precisely what has to be true of the situation for his rule to be an application of the passage rather than an extension of it beyond its intended scope.
3. Use the case to locate exactly where Rawls and Harsanyi part company. Harsanyi holds that a rational agent behind a veil of ignorance converts ignorance into risk by assigning equal probability to occupying each social position and then maximizes expected utility; Rawls holds that the original position excludes such probability assignments and that maximin is the rational choice there, given the conditions he takes to hold (no basis for estimating likelihoods, little to gain above the secured minimum, and grave outcomes in the worst case). Say which of these commitments your case presses on, and whether the three-way split you have engineered amounts to a miniature of the Rawls–Harsanyi dispute or merely resembles it superficially.
4. Identify the single structural feature of the payoff table that is doing the work in forcing each pairwise disagreement, and state what happens to each split if that feature is removed.
5. Give the best counterattack available to a partisan of each rule: the maximin theorist on the neglect of everything but the worst case, the regret theorist on sensitivity to the menu of available acts (the addition of a dominated act can change the regret verdict — show whether it does in your case), and the Laplacean on partition-sensitivity.
6. Close by flagging what is settled versus what is genuinely contested. The formal facts — that the three rules diverge here, that regret violates independence of irrelevant alternatives, that equiprobability is partition-sensitive — are settled; do not hedge them. What remains contested is which rule, if any, is rationally mandatory under ignorance, and whether Rawls's conditions for maximin can be satisfied in anything like the real circumstances of justice; do not present either side of those disputes as the consensus view.
