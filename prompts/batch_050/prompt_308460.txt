I sit on the publications-integrity board of a midsized scientific journal consortium. We run an internal prediction market to decide which accepted papers should receive expensive independent reanalysis before publication. Each paper gets a claim of the form this central result will survive the planned reanalysis, and authors, reviewers, editors, and staff can trade pseudonymously with points convertible into modest cash prizes. The current proposal is to commission reanalysis automatically whenever the market probability falls below 45 percent. Last month, a climate-health paper was trading around 65 percent, then dropped to 31 percent after four small sales. Compliance traced the sales to two reviewers and a staff statistician who had seen confidential reviewer reports, though none disclosed any specific concern publicly. The authors are furious that the journal has effectively attached an anonymous black mark to their work based on secret evidence; our editor-in-chief says that is exactly the point of a market, since it lets dispersed private information become usable without revealing confidential reports.

At yesterday’s meeting, a consultant invoked no-trade theorems and made everyone less certain: if all traders share a prior, know one another are rational, and trade only to profit from information, then an offer to sell should itself make potential buyers update enough that no mutually acceptable trade occurs. Our trading volume may exist only because we subsidize an automated market maker, because participants have different priors, or because some people simply enjoy betting. So perhaps the price is not the clean epistemic signal we assumed. One board member wants to ban anyone with confidential access from trading, another wants a quiet book where trading continues but only volume is published until after reanalysis, and a third wants to keep everything unchanged. Please draft the short decision memo for next week’s board meeting. It needs an actionable recommendation on both the general policy and what to do about this particular paper, including a time frame and safeguards against manipulation. Please address whether low-volume movement under a subsidized market maker should count as evidence, but keep it practical rather than turning the memo into a lecture. The strongest argument on the losing side should get a fair hearing, especially since banning informed traders would exclude the people most likely to know something.
