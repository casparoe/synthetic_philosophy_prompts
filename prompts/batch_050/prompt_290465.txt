Last night I had a long and rather heated argument with a friend who maintains that the very idea of supererogation — acts that are morally good but not morally required — is incoherent. Her view is that if something is genuinely good to do, then you have reason to do it, and if you have decisive reason to do it, then failing to do it is a moral failing; so the familiar middle category of "admirable but optional" is just a confusion we use to excuse our own lukewarmness. I think she is wrong, but I noticed halfway through the argument that I could not explain the idea cleanly, and I suspect she was exploiting that. As it happens, I have agreed to run a session for a local philosophy reading group in three weeks — intelligent adults, but non-specialists, most of whom have done no academic ethics — and I have chosen supererogation as my topic, partly to settle my own thinking. I want you to plan how I should teach it, not to teach it to me.

The plan should cover the following. First, the order in which the ideas must be introduced, with a justification for each step: what has to be in place before the category of "good but not required" can even be stated without sounding like a contradiction, and at what point the friend-objection I describe above (that the category collapses one way or the other) should be acknowledged and dismantled rather than ignored. Second, the single carrying example the session should be built around — I had vaguely imagined a bystander risking serious harm to rescue a stranger, or someone donating a kidney to a person they have never met, but I want you to pick one, say exactly what features it must have to do the work (clear goodness, clear non-requiredness, clear praiseworthiness) and what it must lack (for instance, I worry that cases involving grave danger to the agent muddy things by making the question about reasonable self-sacrifice rather than about the structure of the category itself). Third, the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they are likely to surface, and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off: I anticipate at least these — that supererogation just means "nice extras" that are morally weightless; that it smuggles in the view that morality does not really apply to most of life; and a confusion between supererogatory acts and imperfect duties (where the duty exists but one chooses when and towards whom to fulfil it) — and I would like you to add any others you think a smart lay audience reliably forms, including whether my friend's objection is best treated as a misconception or as a genuine philosophical position the plan must engage.

Finally, specify how I should check at the end whether the idea has landed rather than merely been nodded at — ideally one question or borderline case I can put to the group whose answer would reveal whether they have grasped the distinction between what is required, what is permitted, and what is good beyond requirement — and state plainly what I should openly refuse to cover at this level (I am inclined to exclude the technical question of whether supererogation is compatible with consequentialism, but tell me if you think that exclusion is a mistake). Please write your answer in British English.
