Our department's upper-level undergraduate course on early modern metaphysics needs a final-exam essay question on Hume's account of causation, and I'd like you to draft it, along with everything the graders will need.

The question must be built around the following setup, which should be presented to candidates as part of the question itself. Two worlds are described by stipulation. In World A, every event sequence is perfectly regular — whenever an event of one type occurs, an event of a second type follows, without exception — and the world contains nothing over and above this mosaic of events: no powers, forces, or productive relations. In World B, objects possess genuine intrinsic causal powers, but each power's manifestation is irreducibly chancy: a struck match has a real power to ignite, yet the power manifests only about seventy percent of the time, so exceptionless regularities do not exist. Candidates are asked three things: first, on the traditional regularity reading of Hume, which of these worlds (if either) contains causation, and why; second, on the so-called New Hume reading, which attributes to Hume a belief in real but epistemically inaccessible causal powers, which world contains causation, and why; third, whether the conceivability of World B refutes the regularity theory outright, or merely pushes it toward a best-system formulation, and what hangs on the difference for the possibility of causal knowledge.

Set the difficulty at the crux, not at recall: a candidate should be unable to answer well by paraphrasing Hume's talk of constant conjunction and the felt determination of the mind, and the question should cleanly separate candidates who understand what is at issue between the old and New Hume interpretations from those who have merely memorized the labels. Assume candidates have 75 minutes and that the question is worth 25 marks.

Provide a model answer of roughly 600 words. It should at minimum: distinguish the epistemological and ontological readings of Hume's denial of knowable necessary connection; engage with the fact that Hume offers two characterizations of cause — one in terms of regular succession, one in terms of counterfactual dependence — and the tension between them; state what the New Hume reading gains (making sense of Hume's talk of secret powers and of causes beyond possible experience) and what it costs (strain with his copy principle and his official account of the origin of the idea of necessity in an impression of reflection); deliver a verdict on Worlds A and B under each reading; and take a defensible position on whether chancy powers genuinely trouble the regularity theory or can be absorbed by a best-system account of laws.

Present the marking scheme as a table with exactly five rows, one per must-hit point, and the following four columns: Must-hit point (the specific move a top answer must make) | What full credit requires (the content or reasoning needed for full marks on this point) | Marks (the value of this point; the five values must sum to 25) | How candidates typically lose these marks (the most common confusion or shortcut that should cost marks on this point). After the table, add a note of two or three sentences on how to handle borderline cases in which a candidate defends an original position with thin argumentation.

Do not use any direct quotations from Hume's texts anywhere in the question, model answer, or marking scheme; paraphrase his claims instead.
