Consider three practices that are now routine. A streaming platform infers what a viewer "wants to watch" from click and watch-time data and ranks its catalogue accordingly. A company fine-tuning a language model collects pairwise comparisons from human raters -- of two candidate responses, which is better -- and fits a single scalar reward function to those judgments, which is then treated as the raters' preferences. And a navigation app observes which routes drivers actually take and adjusts its recommendations to match, on the assumption that revealed choices encode genuine route preferences. All three are instances of a general method: infer a person's preferences from their behavior, represent the result as a numerical utility or reward, and optimize future offerings against it.

What view of preference does this method presuppose? Reconstruct its implicit theory carefully. In particular: Does it presuppose that preferences exist prior to and independently of the choices that reveal them -- that there is a fact of the matter about what the agent wants, waiting to be recovered -- or is it compatible with the view, common in behavioral economics and psychology, that preferences are constructed on the spot by the very elicitation procedure used to measure them? Does it presuppose that preferences are stable over time, complete (the agent always can rank any two options), and commensurable (reducible to a single scale)? Which of these does the method strictly require to make sense at all, and which does it merely find convenient -- that is, where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories of preference would license exactly the same engineering?

Then examine an apparent tension. The streaming platform presupposes, when it infers, that watch behavior expresses a settled underlying taste; but it also knows that its recommendations shape what users come to like, and it exploits this. The reward-modeling pipeline presupposes that rater judgments track a stable human preference structure; yet the raters' judgments are elicited by the pipeline's own presentation of options, and the resulting model is deployed to shape the behavior of a system that millions of people then interact with. Can one coherent theory of preference underwrite both the inference direction and the shaping direction, or does the combined practice presuppose inconsistent things -- preferences fixed enough to be read off behavior, yet plastic enough to be manufactured? Consider whether the method quietly rules out certain views: that some preferences are adaptive or deformed by the agent's circumstances and so not authoritative; that some values are incommensurable; that choice can express weakness of will, habit, or defaults rather than preference at all.

Finally: if we replaced the presupposed picture with a thoroughly constructivist one -- preferences are made in the act of choosing, not revealed by it -- what, concretely, would have to change in how these systems are designed and evaluated? Would pairwise-comparison reward modeling still be intelligible, and if so, under what reinterpretation of what the learned reward function represents?
