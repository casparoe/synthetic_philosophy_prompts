I run an undergrad ethics seminar (about 18 students, one 75-minute session) and we're about to get to moral luck -- the idea that we blame people more when their reckless choice happens to cause harm than when the identical choice gets lucky, and that this seems both unavoidable and unfair. I want an in-class activity where they feel that pull themselves before anyone names it. Roughly what I'm imagining: the class makes real decisions under uncertainty with genuine (small) stakes, some randomness (dice, cards, whatever) splits them into the lucky and unlucky even though their decisions were equally defensible or equally negligent, and then the class itself has to assign blame, penalties, or grades -- so that they end up judging the lucky and unlucky differently with their own hands and then squirm when you point it out. Give me the actual rules, the setup, how the randomness enters, what the stakes are (no real money), and how to sequence it so nobody sees the trick coming. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the different kinds of moral luck (resultant, circumstantial, maybe constitutive if you can work it in), and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- e.g. someone saying "it's just a game so the blame isn't real." Also tell me honestly: is there a design choice here where you could be talked out of the whole thing, i.e. where the activity might only demonstrate outcome bias (a well-documented psychological quirk) rather than moral luck as a genuine philosophical problem, and what feature of real moral luck cases no classroom game can reproduce so I'd have to supply it by lecture anyway?
