"Quine's paper attacks two dogmas of logical empiricism. The first is the belief in a fundamental cleavage between analytic truths, which are true by virtue of meaning alone (like 'all bachelors are unmarried'), and synthetic truths, which are true by virtue of how the world is. This distinction goes back to Kant's contrast between judgments where the predicate is contained in the subject and judgments that genuinely extend our knowledge, and the positivists, especially Carnap and Ayer, relied on it to explain how mathematics and logic could be necessary and knowable a priori without any appeal to metaphysics. The second dogma is reductionism, the view that the whole of science is what faces the tribunal of experience. Quine argues that the first dogma is untenable because every attempt to explain analyticity turns out to be circular: analyticity is explained in terms of synonymy, synonymy in terms of definition or interchangeability in all contexts, and these notions in turn can only be understood in terms of analyticity itself, so the whole family of intensional notions stands or falls together. Quine takes this to prove that the analytic-synthetic distinction is meaningless and that the very idea of meaning should be discarded. His holism then connects the two dogmas: because the unit of empirical significance is the whole of science rather than the individual sentence, no statement is immune to revision, not even the laws of logic, which in Two Dogmas Quine shows by arguing that conventional definitions of logical truth collapse into circularity. Grice and Strawson, in their reply, concede the circularity argument but maintain that the distinction is too deeply entrenched in our practice to abandon."
