I write novels, mostly quiet literary stuff, and my last book had a subplot where a guy pours a decade into an online world -- builds a town, breeds these weird digital horses, half his friendships live there -- and then the company sunsets the servers and it's all gone. I thought the grief chapter was the strongest writing in the book, but a solid chunk of readers shrugged it off with the exact same line: 'it's just a game.' One reviewer said my character was basically mourning a screensaver, which stung.

That little sentence is doing a lot of work and I want to know precisely what. Lay out the different claims 'it's just a game' might be making, from the boring to the radical. Some candidates I can dimly see: virtual objects don't really exist; they exist but can't have real value; the harm of losing them is real but always trivial compared to 'real' losses; players consented to the loss by opting into something with an off switch; engagement with fiction/game stuff is by definition not the kind of thing you can be wronged in. There are probably others I'm missing, so add those. For each reading, tell me whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or just false, and -- this is the part I actually care about -- which readings would acctually justify my readers dismissing the grief chapter, versus which ones people lean on without noticing the reading doesn't get them the dismissal.

Keep the tone fairly casual, I'm a novelist not a philosopher and I will glaze over if this turns into a journal article. Menu format please, not one long argument for a single interpretation. At the very end, on its own line, give me the one reading (or combo of readings) the dismissive use of the slogan genuinely depends on, written as \answer{...}.
