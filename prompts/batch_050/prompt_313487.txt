I recently got elected to my union's bargaining committee, and we keep hitting situations where members disagree morally, not just about money -- like whether we should strike over a political issue most of us care about but a big minority finds abhorrent. This got me thinking about when compromising on your values is just smart trading and when it's selling out, and I realized my gut verdicts are kind of a mess. Can you help me find the principle, if there is one? Here's where I land:

- My partner and I take turns picking vacation spots even though each of us thinks the other's taste is genuinly bad. Fine, obviously.
- Two parents who disagree about vaccinating -- one thinks it's a moral duty, the other thinks it's dangerous -- "compromise" by vaccinating their older kid but not the younger one. Appalling. But I can't say exactly why this differs from the vacation case.
- Legislators logrolling: I'll vote for your bridge if you vote for my clinic, on issues where each of us honestly ranks them differently. Fine, maybe even what politics is for.
- Two judges agreeing to trade votes across cases -- each votes against what the law actually says on the case the other one cares about. Repulsive. But structurally it looks alot like the logrolling case, which bugs me.
- Two negotiators disagree about a factual question -- say, what a policy will cost -- and "compromise" by writing down the number halfway between their estimates, each believing it's wrong. This doesn't even seem like compromise, just agreeing to be wrong together.
- A politician quietly softens a position because a donor asked. That's not compromise, that's just corruption -- but the donor might sincerely hold the opposing view, so why does the money change things?
- Two factions on my committee resolve their fight by agreeing to a deal whose costs fall entirely on non-members who aren't in the room. Feels dirty even though both sides "got something."

So: is there one principle that sorts these the way I want them sorted? My hunch is something about trades being legit only when each side gives up something they value less than what they get, where "value" is measured by their own lights -- but I don't think that handles the judge case or the vaccine case, since both sides there could honestly be getting more than they give.

If no single principle delivers all seven verdicts, tell me which one I should give up and why. And whatever you land on, run it against two cases I didn't list: a vegan agreeing to cook meat for their spouse half the week, and two countries settling a border dispute by splitting contested territory even though both think the whole thing is rightfully theirs.
