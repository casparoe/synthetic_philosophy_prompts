So I'm trying to sort out what I actually believe about blame when autonomous systems hurt people, going by my gut reactions to cases, and I want to know if there's a real principle under there or if I'm just inconsistent. Here are my verdicts:

1. Guy texting while driving rear-ends someone. Driver's fault, obviously, open and shut.

2. A warehouse buys an autonomous forklift, runs it on factory settings, maintains it exactly per the manual. A sensor fails in a way that's never been seen before and it kills a worker. My gut says this is just a tragedy. Not the warehouse's fault, not the manufacturer's fault (they tested it more than any human-driven forklift gets tested), nobody's fault. It feels genuinely different from case 1 even though a person is equally dead.

3. A self-driving car owner gets annoyed that the car brakes too much for pedestrians, finds the hidden menu, and turns the pedestrian detection sensitivity way down. Car hits a jogger. Totally the owner's fault, as much as if he'd been holding the wheel. The fact that he was asleep in the back seat doesn't soften it at all for me.

4. A delivery company runs the numbers: their sidewalk robots will, at current tech levels, very rarely fail to detect a small child darting out, but across the whole fleet they're still much safer than human drivers. They deploy. A year later a robot hits a kid. And here's the thing: my gut says the company IS at fault, even though their decision was defensible, maybe even correct. I can't square this with case 2 and it's the pair I keep circling.

5. A hospital's diagnostic AI was built from an open-source model, fine-tuned by a research lab, then adjusted by the hospital's own IT team, and it misses an obvious tumor. Everyone involved followed reasonable procedures, and the error traces to an interaction between the fine-tuning and the hospital's adjustments that nobody could have predicted. Gut says: this one feels like case 2 (nobody's fault), but a lawyer friend tells me in practice the hospital would eat the liability, and part of me thinks that's... right? But then maybe I'm just conflating "someone should pay the victim" with "someone did something wrong," and I'm not sure those are the same question.

6. Military drone flags a target, human operator has 20 seconds to approve, rubber-stamps it, turns out to be a wedding. Everyone says "the human was in the loop" but realistically no human could verify in 20 seconds. My gut says the operator is NOT blameworthy and honestly the whole "human in the loop" framing feels like a excuse factory. But then who is?

So: is there one principle that gets all of these, or at least most? Something like "blame tracks what you could reasonably have foreseen or controlled" handles 1-3 but seems to collapse on 4. Maybe I need two different notions (accountability to victims vs. culpability) and I'm mushing them together? Tell me which verdicts a decent principle forces me to drop, and what it says about cases I haven't listed, like a Tesla on autopilot where the driver was warned to pay attention.

And genuinely, push back if you think the ask is off. If my case reactions are too muddled for any principle to capture, or if I'm asking a legal question dressed up as a moral one, I'd rather hear that than get a tidy theory that papers over the mess.
