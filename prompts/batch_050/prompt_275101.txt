I am a novelist, currently at work on a book whose narrator is a biographer, and the research has forced on me a question I thought I had settled years ago: when does reading about a life actually teach us something moral, as opposed to merely flattering us, softening our judgment, or moving our sympathies around like furniture? Plutarch is candid that he selects and arranges material in his Lives so that readers will come to admire virtue and recoil from vice, and yet I find his open moralizing less objectionable than much modern biography that pretends to neutrality. I have tried to locate my real views by attending to my own verdicts on cases, and I would like to know what principle, if any, underlies them.

Here are the verdicts. Reading Plutarch's life of Cato as a young woman made me measurably more austere in small matters for some months, and I count that genuine moral learning even though Plutarch's hand is everywhere in the arrangement. A childhood book of saints' lives, miracles included, formed my capacity to admire mercy, and I still count it as formation even though nearly every story in it was embellished or false. A recent warts-and-all biography of a statesman I admire left my admiration intact but taught me that greatness is compatible with pettiness; I call that moral learning too, though I could not name a single thing it taught me to do. By contrast, a beautifully written, scrupulously accurate biography of a man I take to have been a monster softened my judgment while I read it, and I resented the book afterwards as a manipulation, accuracy notwithstanding. Then there is my own practice, which embarrasses the rest: writing my villain's chapters from the inside has genuinely made me more forgiving of an estranged friend, while writing my heroine's virtues for two years has not made me one whit more virtuous. Finally, a self-serving political memoir I distrusted taught me something anyway, about self-deception, but only, I suspect, because I supplied the judgment the author withheld.

What I want is a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that distinguishes biography which morally educates from biography which merely manipulates, sentimentalizes, or entertains, stated precisely enough that I can see which of my cases motivates each clause. The pair that troubles me most is the childhood hagiography against the monster biography: if idealizing falsehood can edify while idealizing accuracy can corrupt, then truthfulness is not doing the work, and I would like to know what is. Tell me which of my verdicts, if any, a coherent principle forces me to surrender — I am prepared to lose one, but I want it named rather than quietly abandoned — and tell me what the surviving principle predicts for two cases I have not listed: an admiring biography of a living tech executive read by an adult who ought to know better, and a novel whose author has concealed a moral lesson inside it without ever announcing one.
