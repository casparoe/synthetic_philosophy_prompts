I am a novelist at work on a dinner-table scene in which one character accuses another of a sweeping generalization, and the accused waves it off with the line "the exception proves the rule." Before I put the phrase in his mouth, I want to be sure I know what he could possibly mean by it, because his wife is a philosophy lecturer and she is going to reply in the next line. The generalization at issue is one of those bare generics -- on the order of "ducks lay eggs" or "mosquitoes carry malaria" -- that speakers accept even though counterinstances are not rare: most ducks do not lay eggs (the males and juveniles never do), and most mosquitoes carry nothing. So: what distinct, precise claims could the proverb be making? I can dimly make out several candidates -- an old sense in which "prove" means "test," a legal thought that a stated exception presupposes a rule elsewhere, a pragmatic thought about what uttering the exception conversationally implies, and perhaps a bolder statistical claim that tolerance of exceptions is what distinguishes rules from mere accidents. Lay out the readings as a list; mark which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, and which are simply false; and then tell me which, if any, would actually license my character's move -- namely, keeping the generic on the table and treating the cited counterinstances as confirming it rather than refuting it. If none do, I would like to know what the most charitable version of his position would have to say instead, since I intend his wife to win the exchange but not cheaply.
