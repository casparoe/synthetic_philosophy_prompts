I teach a first-year seminar called "History and its Gaps" at a small liberal arts college, and next week I have to introduce the idea that archives aren't neutral storehouses of the past -- that what gets preserved, and what's missing, is itself a product of power. My students are mostly not history majors, and if last year's group is any indication, they walk in assuming history is basically just whatever got written down, plus or minus some bias in the writing. The idea that the gaps are *made*, not just found, tends to genuinely surprise them, and I want to lean into that.

I want to build the whole session around one carrying example, and I have a candidate. A couple summers ago I spent an afternoon in a county courthouse basement in the American South, and the only trace of enslaved people in the 1840s deed books was as line items in bills of sale, filed between land transfers and livestock mortgages -- while the sellers get full names, signatures, ages, sometimes little biographical asides. I like this example because the archive here is *full*, there's tons of material, and yet something enormous is absent, which feels like the crux. Separately, a historian friend once sketched for me the idea that silences creep in at several distinct moments: when records get created in the first place, when they get kept or tossed, when they get collected and catalogued into an archive, and when historians finally write from them. I'd like those four moments to be the spine of the plan, so work them in.

What I want is a teaching plan, not the lecture itself: the order the pieces have to come in and why (what depends on what), whether my courthouse example can actually carry the load and what it must have and must lack to do so, the misconceptions students will predictably bring (I'm bracing for at least "the archive is biased so we should just distrust everything in it," "digitization will eventually fix the gaps," and "silence just means nobody bothered to write it down") and the exact point in the sequence to head each one off, a check for understanding that would show the idea has really landed rather than been parroted back, and what I should openly refuse to get into at this level (say, whether subaltern voices can ever be recovered at all). Plan for a 50-minute session with a whiteboard.
