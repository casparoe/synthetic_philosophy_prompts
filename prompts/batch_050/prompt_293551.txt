So I'm a backend dev, zero philosophy training, and my brother-in-law did a philosophy minor like 15 years ago and loves to bring it up at family dinners. Last weekend he was explaining this philosopher Bernard Williams to me, from some essay called "Internal and External Reasons," and I nodded along but later something felt fishy about it. His summary went roughly like this (I'm paraphrasing from memory, he definately said it more confidently):

"Williams argued that all reasons for action are internal reasons. Saying 'you have a reason to do X' is only true if doing X connects to something already in what he calls your subjective motivational set — your desires, but also your commitments, loyalties, projects, emotional dispositions, stuff like that. So it's not just whatever you happen to want right this second. But the connection has to hold up under sound deliberation with true beliefs — like if you want gin and you believe the glass has gin in it but it's actually petrol, you don't have a reason to drink it. Reasons track the facts, not your take on the facts. So the upshot is: if there's a person whose motivational set contains nothing that would push them toward, say, keeping promises or not lying, then 'you have a reason not to lie' is just false when said to them, and there is nothing you could ever say to rationally bring them around — they're unreachable by argument, full stop. Williams embraces this. And that means morality has no claim on the amoralist, and the whole project of moral philosophy, of finding obligations everyone is rationally bound to accept, is dead. It's basically Hume's view: reason is the slave of the passions, end of story."

The first half sounded sensible, honestly it reminded me of debugging — like a reason only "compiles" if it links to something in your existing motivational codebase, and false beliefs are like type errors that break the link. But the ending felt like he was speedrunning from a modest-sounding premise to "morality is dead," and the Hume bit sounded like name-dropping. I pushed back a little and he said "no no, this is just what the view is, external reasons people lost this debate."

Can you audit this for me? Which parts of his summary would Williams or a sympathetic reader accept, which parts are him overstating or flattening something, and does the big conclusion at the end actually follow from the accurate parts? Also, what would the "external reasons" side — the people who supposedly lost — say in response, is it really that one-sided?
