I am a novelist at work on a book whose antagonist runs a website that distributes pirated ebooks, and when the authors in the story confront him, his reply is the same one I have heard at conventions and in comment threads more times than I can count: you can't own an idea. I want that line to do real philosophical work rather than serve as a flat villain's catchphrase, and I also want to give it, later in the book, to a sympathetic character who uses it to wave away a young writer's fear that her high-concept premise will be stolen before she finishes her manuscript. So I need to know what the slogan could actually be claiming. Please separate out the distinct precise theses it might be expressing: for instance, the merely legal observation that copyright protects wording rather than premises, the metaphysical claim that ideas, being non-rival and non-excludable, cannot be property the way land or furniture is, the normative claim that legal monopolies over expression are unjust whatever the law happens to say, and the causal claim that no idea is ever one person's unaided creation, since every plot is reworked from earlier plots. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, genuinely contentious, or simply false, and whether it is the sort of thing someone reaching for the slogan in an argument is likely to mean.

Then test the readings against three cases from the novel, because I suspect the slogan's plausibility shifts between them: verbatim copying of an entire ebook, adoption of a book's central conceit retold in different words, and the independent invention of an identical plot by someone who never read the original. Tell me which readings treat all three cases alike and which draw lines between them, and where each reading draws those lines. Finally, here is the dramatic crux: my antagonist is sincerely outraged when a rival site copies his own site's design and catalog, and I want to know whether any single coherent reading of the slogan lets him excuse his piracy while condemning that copying, or whether the two uses force him onto different theses that sit badly together. Likewise, tell me which reading is actually being invoked when the slogan is used to comfort the anxious young writer, since that scene will fail if the comfort only works on a reading nobody could seriously hold.

I would like your answer as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables. Let the readings and the case analysis unfold in the natural order of an explanation, one claim leading to the next.
