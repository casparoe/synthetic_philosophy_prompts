I'm a PhD student in political theory (working on deliberative democracy stuff) and I keep getting pulled into actually running deliberations, not just writing about them -- I help facilitate our department's grad-student town halls and I've been asked to help design a citizen assembly pilot for the city next year. What I want is a usable procedure, based on Habermas's account of communicative rationality and the ideal speech situation, for judging whether a real deliberation is genuinely communicative or just strategic bargaining wearing deliberative clothes. Something I could actually run through after (or during) a meeting: what to check for, in what order -- like, do all affected people actually get to speak, can anyone challenge any claim, are participants free from obvious coercion, does anyone have reason to think people are being sincere -- and what to do when a check fails (pause the process? fix the setup? just note it and lower confidence in the outcome?). The hard part I want you to think through is the translation: Habermas's conditions are counterfactual ideals, so no real meeting fully satisfies them. Tell me which features of the ideal you think have to survive operationalization and which can be dropped without losing the point, and be honest about where the checklist becomes judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run the procedure on two cases: (1) a department town hall where the chair controls the agenda and faculty outnumber students in the room, and (2) a well-resourced citizen assembly with random selection but only two weekends to deliberate on a complex budget question. One of them should probably come out looking better than the other despite both being flawed -- say which and why, and flag what the procedure fundamentally can't tell me.
