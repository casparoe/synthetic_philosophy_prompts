I co-teach a philosophy of economics seminar (mostly upper-year undergrads plus two grad students, 16 people total, 75-minute slot) and next month we hit epistemic game theory. I want to run something live rather than lecture. Here's the specific thing I want them to feel in their gut: in a finite game of perfect information where it's common knowledge that everyone is rational, backward induction forces play to unravel immediately, and yet when you're actually sitting across from someone, going along with the unraveling feels absurd and leaving money on the table. I was arguing about this with a friend who insists the unraveling result is just obviously correct and anyone who deviates is being irrational, and I want the students to discover for themselves why that's too quick — the interesting question is which premise to blame (common knowledge of rationality itself? the assumption that rationality requires best-responding to what you expect? something about how players reason off the equilibrium path once someone has already deviated?).

So: design an activity around the centipede game (growing pot, players alternate choosing to take or pass) that stages this. I want full rules and a payoff scheme using points or chocolate or something, not money, with a couple of rounds so behavior can shift once they've seen each other play. Crucially I'd like a second phase that breaks common knowledge of rationality in a controlled way — my thought is secretly designating one player per pair as a "nice" type with tweaked incentives, or telling the group that some unknown fraction of players will be replaced by a bot that always passes, so that now passing becomes the smart move even for a fully rational player, and they can feel how the whole backward-induction argument depended on something that's almost never actually in place. Then a debrief that maps specific moments (the first take, the person who passes on the last node anyway, the shift after the twist is revealed) onto the formal machinery: iterated dominance, backward induction, and the difference between mutual and common knowledge of rationality.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I'm guessing someone takes on node one out of pure contrarianism, or the points feel too low-stakes to bite) and how to handle it. And be honest about the limits: a classroom demonstrably cannot produce genuine common knowledge of rationality, since everyone can see everyone else is a sleep-deprived undergrad, so tell me which feature of the theoretical result the activity can't reproduce and what I have to just say in words. Finally, in the debrief notes, clearly separate what's settled (the backward induction solution exists, people systematically deviate from it in experiments) from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this (like whether deviation is irrational, or whether common knowledge of rationality is even coherent once someone has visibly deviated) — don't present my friend's position or mine as the consensus.
