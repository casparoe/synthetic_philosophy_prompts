Six weeks ago my father underwent an anterior cingulotomy for otherwise intractable cancer pain, and he now says something I cannot stop turning over: the pain is still there — he can point to where it is, describe its burning character, say when it worsens — but it no longer bothers him in the slightest. Watching him, I've been reading about pain asymbolia and the badness of pain, and I've landed on a set of commitments I'd like audited for consistency before I trust them:

(1) Unpleasantness is essential to pain: a sensation that does not hurt or distress at all is, at most, pain-like, not pain.
(2) My father's post-surgery experience is genuinely pain — he is lucid, linguistically competent, and his reports of a persisting burning sensation should be taken at face value.
(3) His condition is no longer bad for him; whatever his welfare consists in, nothing of it is now lost through this sensation.
(4) The badness of a pain, when it is bad, lies in how the experience itself feels, not in the subject's desires, judgments, or attitudes toward it — which is why pain would be bad even for someone who had been conditioned not to mind it.
(5) Subjects have first-person authority about their own hedonic states: if an attentive, unimpaired person sincerely reports that an experience does not feel bad, that settles the matter.

Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only appear to? Please rank the real tensions by severity, say for each what would have to give and at what cost, and identify the least costly revision overall — noting where the standard move of splitting pain into sensory and affective components helps and where it merely relocates the problem.
