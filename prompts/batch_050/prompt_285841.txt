I’m taking an upper-level seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and I get to interview a professor who defends Frankfurt-style cases against the Principle of Alternative Possibilities. Her basic line is that what matters is the actual causal source of the action, not whether the agent could have done otherwise. I don’t want the interview to turn into the usual intro-philosophy recap where she just describes a counterfactual intervener and declares victory, especially because she’s very polished at answering objections with “that’s just an epistemic problem about how we know what the agent would have done.”

Can you draft a sequence of 10-12 interview questions that gradually makes that move harder to make? Please build the questions around this modified case: Amara deliberates about whether to expose a friend’s plagiarism and eventually does so for moral reasons. Unknown to her, a device implanted years ago would have forced the same decision if she had begun seriously leaning toward staying silent. In one variant, the device leaves a tiny “flicker” of freedom: Amara could have formed the intention to stay silent, but it would prevent her from acting on it. In another, the device works much earlier by quietly removing evidence that would have made staying silent attractive, so it never has to intervene directly. In a third, Amara knows about the device and has incorporated its presence into her practical identity, telling herself that she is the kind of person who would expose the plagiarism anyway.

I’d like each question to come with the reply she will probably give and a follow-up question that presses the weak point in that reply. Make sure the sequence covers at least these pressure points: whether merely possible alternative intentions are morally relevant; whether prior manipulation of the available evidence is really different from blocking the action itself; whether “actual-sequence” explanations can distinguish reasons from causes without circularity; whether the agent’s awareness of the intervener changes responsibility; and what happens if the device was installed by Amara herself as a commitment mechanism rather than by a manipulator. End with a question asking what possible case or result would make her abandon the Frankfurt-style diagnosis, rather than just asking whether PAP “feels intuitive.” I want the questions to be challenging but still fair enough that she wont justifiably accuse me of setting up a strawman.
