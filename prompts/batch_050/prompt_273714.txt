So I TA for an intro ethics course (second-year undergrads, about 18 students, one 80-minute slot) and we're coming off a three-week unit on consequentialism. The problem is that the unit worked too well: my students are now all cheerful little act-utilitarians and they find deontological constraints genuinely unintelligible, not just wrong. When we did trolley-style cases in discussion they all said obviously pull the lever, obviously harvest the organs, and they treated the people who hesitated as just being squeamish. Next week I want to run something that makes them feel the pull of a constraint from the inside before anyone names it -- and then, crucially, feel the weirdness that follows, i.e. the so-called paradox of deontology: if what makes it wrong for you to harm someone is the badness of that person's being harmed, why isn't it morally better, full stop, when fewer people get harmed, even if you have to do the harming yourself? I want them to refuse a trade in the moment and then be unable to justify the refusal in their own consequentialist vocabulary.

So: design me a runnable in-class activity. Some hard constraints. No lecture up front, no terminology, no trolley. It should involve staged forced choices with real but small stakes (no money -- maybe something like control over resources the group actually cares about that week), structured so that at a key moment the arithmetic clearly favors violating the constraint and refusing therefore visibly costs the group something. I'd like participants to be making decisions for each other, not just about abstract cases, so roles or a rotation that puts everyone in the hot seat. Give me concrete rules, a timeline for the 80 minutes, and then the debrief: how to map specific moments of the session onto agent-relative vs agent-neutral reasons, doing vs allowing, and finally onto the paradox itself, ideally so that the students articulate the paradox before I ever state it. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (e.g. someone games the incentives, someone treats the stakes as trivial and defects instantly, the group converges on a norm of always trading) and how to handle each. And be honest about which feature of the real philosophical problem the game structurally cannot reproduce -- my worry is that a constraint about losing points is just not the same animal as a constraint about harming persons, and I don't want to pretend the activity delivers more than it does.

One more thing about how to frame your answer: I don't want false confidence. Point out where the design smuggles in a verdict -- e.g. where the scenario is stacked so that refusing the trade looks noble rather than irrational, which would defeat the purpose since I need them to feel that the refusal is genuinely hard to defend, not just easy to perform. Tell me which single design decision your whole answer most depends on and what would change your recommendation -- like, if you knew something different about how students actually behave in these games, or if you thought the paradox is better understood some other way than the way I've glossed it, which part of the activity would you rebuild?
