So I've been chewing on L.A. Paul's transformative experience stuff — the vampire case, the having-a-kid case — and specifically the claim that when a choice would both change what you know and change what you value, you can't make it rationally in the standard decision-theoretic way, because you can't assign values to outcomes you can't even imaginatively represent.

Objections I already know and don't need rehashed:

1. The testimony objection: just ask people who've done it. Millions of parents (or vampires, in the thought experiment) can report back, and you can defer to the aggregate or to people whose pre-transformation selves resembled yours.
2. The gradualism objection: most real transformations come in steps. You can babysit, foster, live with your neice for a summer. Each step is epistemically accessible from the one before, so there's no single untraversable gap.
3. The non-first-personal-reasons objection: you don't have to decide based on what it'll be like for you. You can decide on moral grounds, prior commitments, duties to a future person, whatever. Paul's gap only blocks one style of deliberation, not deliberation as such.
4. Callard-style aspiration: becoming can be rational without being decision-theoretic. Aspiring toward a value you don't yet fully grasp is its own form of agency, so Paul's fork — rational choice or leap in the dark — is a false dichotomy.
5. The revelation self-defeat objection: Paul's own fix (choose based on whether you want the revelation of what it'll be like) assumes you can know in advance that the experience will reveal something, and by her own lights you can't know that pre-transformation either.

What I want: a sixth objection that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or mashup of any of them. Say briefly why it doesn't collapse into one of the five, and then grade it honestly — does it actually touch Paul's argument, or does she have an obvious reply?
