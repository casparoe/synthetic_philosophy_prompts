I'm on the trust-and-safety team at a mid-size game studio. We make open-world games with pretty realistic NPCs -- they beg, remember what you did, flinch, plead, the works. Lately we keep having to rule on stuff: a player-made mod, a quest a designer pitched, clips going around of players doing horrible things to NPCs in our sandbox. Every meeting turns into the same shouting match. Half the room says it's just code, nothing is actually suffering, get a grip. The other half says something is clearly wrong with a quest where the only way forward is to hurt a character who's begging you not to, even if nobody can say who exactly is harmed.

I want out of the shouting match. Write me a decision procedure we can actually run on a flagged piece of content, in maybe 30-40 minutes, by people who haven't read any philosophy. The hard part, as far as I can tell, is that "the NPC doesn't feel anything" is probably true but doesn't settle the question -- there might be harms to the player, to people watching, to what the game teaches, that don't depend on the NPC being sentient at all. So the procedure needs to force those apart: a step that asks whether the character itself could plausibly be a victim (and what we'd say if we ever shipped an NPC we genuinely weren't sure about), a step about what doing or watching the act does to real humans, and a step about whether some acts are off the table regardless of consequences. Tell me the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a flat no, and where it has to hand off to an actual human judgment call because reasonable people will disagree -- I don't want it pretending to settle values questions.

Also flag where my "it's just code" colleagues are right in a way the procedure should respect, so it doesn't turn into a machine for banning anything anyone finds icky.

Then run it on three real cases from my backlog: (1) a mod that adds a begging street-kid NPC players can hurt, with detailed reactions; (2) a designer's quest pitch where you have to execute a surrendered enemy who pleads, because the story needs you to have done it; (3) a popular clip of players staging elaborate cruel deaths for NPCs and posting them, no mods involved, just systems the game legitimately allows. I want at least one of these to come out the opposite of what I'd personally want, so I can see the procedure has teeth and isn't just rationalizing my gut. And tell me honestly what the procedure can't do.
