At a graduate reading group on moral responsibility, I defended what I thought was a modest claim about moral luck and ended up with an argument that appears to eliminate blame altogether. Consider two hospital administrators in different cities who independently approve the same cost-saving emergency-power policy after reviewing the same risk assessments. In one city, an unpredictable flood disables the backup system and several patients die; in the other, the flood changes course and nothing happens. Suppose their motives, reasoning, negligence, and degree of identification with the decision are exactly alike. Most participants initially said that the first administrator was more blameworthy, but struggled to justify that judgment without making blame depend on mere chance. I then constructed the following control argument:

1. An agent is blameworthy only to the extent that the morally relevant difference between what the agent did and what the agent should have done is attributable to the agent’s exercise of agency rather than to factors outside the agent’s control.  
2. If two agents’ exercises of agency are identical, a difference in outcome produced solely by factors outside their control cannot make one more blameworthy than the other.  
3. Therefore, the two hospital administrators are equally blameworthy, despite the different outcomes.  
4. The same principle must apply generally: an uncontrolled causal contributor to a morally bad result cannot ground a difference in blame.  
5. Every exercise of agency is itself influenced by factors the agent did not choose or control, including temperament, upbringing, cognitive habits, moral education, and the range of situations encountered. Even when agents identify with these traits or endorse their choices, they are not ultimately responsible for acquiring the traits, except through earlier choices that were themselves shaped by still earlier uncontrolled factors.  
6. There is no principled distinction between uncontrolled factors that influence an action’s outcome and uncontrolled factors that influence the agent’s deliberation and choice: in both cases, the factors help explain why the worse alternative occurred, and the agent did not control them.  
7. Hence, if premise 4 is correct, uncontrolled influences on deliberation and character cannot ground blame either.  
8. Therefore, nobody is ultimately blameworthy for anything, and no desert-based distinction between blame and excuse is justified.

The conclusion seems to prove too much. Identify the first step you reject, explain why it initially seemed plausible, and state what rejecting it costs. In particular, clarify whether the argument equivocates between different senses of control, attribution, or grounding; whether there is a principled distinction between resultant luck and constitutive luck; and how a reasons-responsive, deep-self, or quality-of-will account would block the move from premises 4 and 5 to premise 6. If you think the argument is valid, say instead which premise is least costly to abandon. Please preserve, if possible, both equal blame in the two-administrator case and substantial blame for an ordinary deliberate wrongdoer—or explain explicitly why those commitments conflict.
