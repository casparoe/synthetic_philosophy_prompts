Two colleagues of mine disagree about what the AI alignment problem is. One holds that it is a single, unitary problem — roughly, ensuring that an increasingly capable system's objectives continue to track what its designers would want upon reflection — and that distinctions such as outer alignment and inner alignment are bookkeeping conveniences internal to it, much as thermodynamics treats distinct molecular phenomena under one framework. The other holds that "the alignment problem" is a misleading singular label for a family of problems (outer alignment, mesa-optimisation, scalable oversight, corrigibility, interpretability, and the governance questions that presuppose progress on some of these) which differ in their success criteria, their methods, and even the disciplines needed to solve them — so that asking whether "alignment" will be solved is like asking whether "medicine" will be solved. A further fault line, as I understand it: the unitarian expects some invariant that survives increases in capability, whereas the pluralist expects the problem to change character at capability thresholds, so that a solution at one level need not transfer to the next.

Include: (1) a numbered list of claims both accept about what alignment research is asking and about how its sub-questions relate; (2) a table of their remaining disagreements, each formulated in wording the relevant side would accept for its own view; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one of them — with, for each, an observation, result, or conceptual argument both agree bears on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance; and (4) any part of the dispute they conclude is verbal rather than substantive, with reasons. Aim for formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted. Please write in British English.
