I run a small secondary school whose academic policy I have tried, perhaps stubbornly, to keep faithful to the picture of learning set out in Alfred North Whitehead's The Aims of Education and Other Essays. For orientation: Whitehead holds that every genuine act of learning moves through a three-stage rhythm -- an initial stage of romance, in which the subject is encountered whole and with unanalysed vividness; a stage of precision, in which details are mastered and exact formulation is demanded; and a stage of generalisation, in which the now-disciplined knowledge is turned back toward free application. He warns against what he calls inert ideas, knowledge received into the mind without ever being used, combined, or tested; he insists that the rhythm recurs at many scales, within a lesson, within a year, and across the whole arc of schooling; and he treats freedom and discipline as alternating needs rather than rivals, so that freedom without the warrant of prior discipline is as miseducative as discipline that never ripens into freedom.

Over the past five years, my governors have let me rule on curriculum proposals against this doctrine. My record:

1. I approved a languages proposal to postpone all formal grammar instruction in beginners' French until students had spent a full year immersed in stories, songs, and guided conversation, against the objection that examination syllabi would be covered late.
2. I approved a science proposal requiring that the first term of secondary-school physics consist of unstructured laboratory tinkering and field observation, with systematic treatment of theory deferred to the second term.
3. I rejected a mathematics proposal to teach axiomatic Euclidean geometry to a gifted stream of ten-year-olds, even though a pilot showed they could master the proofs; my written reason was that the proposal compressed the opening of the subject into its most exacting terminal form.
4. I rejected a proposal from our final-year cohort to replace set written examinations with self-designed interdisciplinary projects of each student's own choosing, even though such projects sit at the end of the rhythm rather than the beginning; my written reason was that free application must be built upon knowledge whose disciplined acquisition has actually been verified, not merely presumed.

The new proposal before me: a two-year statistics course for fourteen- and fifteen-year-olds, opening with a full year in which students collect and argue over data from their own community -- traffic counts, canteen queues, local sports records -- with formal probability and hypothesis testing compressed into the final two terms, and with assessment wholly by an open-ended project rather than by examination.

What should I decide if I want to remain consistent with my own record? Which earlier ruling is the closest analog, and how should the others bear on it? If my record genuinely underdetermines the answer, set out the distinct principles of Whiteheadian policy that each fit all four verdicts, state what each predicts about the statistics course and why, give your best guess at the verdict I would reach, and name the further hypothetical proposal whose treatment would most efficiently discriminate among the surviving principles.
