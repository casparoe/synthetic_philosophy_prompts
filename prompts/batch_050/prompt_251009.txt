My political theory professor has been giving her verdicts on a series of cases about who should have a say in which collective decisions. Her record so far: ordinary adult citizens resident in the country get the full vote. Short-term visitors and tourists get no vote, even though the country's laws apply to them during their stay. Citizens who emigrated decades ago but retain citizenship, own property back home, and might someday return keep their national vote. Immigrants who have lived in the country for many years without acquiring citizenship — working, paying taxes, raising children there — should in her view be enfranchised at the national level, since the whole legal order governs their lives. Foreigners abroad whose interests are touched only lightly, say through shifts in trade patterns or the spread of the country's cultural products, get no say. Children she excludes on grounds of decision-making capacity, and she insists that this is a separate question from where the boundary of the demos lies, not a verdict about who belongs to it.

Next term she is taking up two harder cases. First: a region of the country is holding a binding referendum on whether to secede and form its own state, and the question is whether the vote should be confined to residents of that region or extended to the entire existing electorate, given that secession would substantially affect everyone else's economy, security arrangements, pensions, and shared institutions. Second: a constitutional provision, to be ratified by referendum, whose costs and benefits fall almost entirely on people not yet born.

Please predict her verdict on each case. I can see at least three candidate principles that might be generating her earlier judgments — one keyed to whose interests are affected by a decision, one keyed to who is or will be subject to the polity's coercive authority, and one keyed to membership in a self-defining political community — so identify which of these her existing record genuinely discriminates among and which it leaves open, and say where the surviving principles converge and where they come apart on the secession referendum. My own sense is that the affected-interests principle and the subjection principle point in opposite directions there, but correct me if I'm wrong. Give your best prediction with reasons, note which earlier verdict does the most work in constraining each new case, and name a further case that would settle which principle she is actually running. Finally, where the democratic-theory literature on this so-called boundary problem has reached something like a settled view, say so explicitly; where competent theorists genuinely disagree, flag the disagreement rather than presenting any one principle as the consensus.
