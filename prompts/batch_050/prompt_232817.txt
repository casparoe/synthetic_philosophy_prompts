I work at a big multinational and this month I had to sit through two mandatory trainings that seem to flat-out contradict each other, and it's been bugging me.

Training one was cross-cultural management. We're about to start working closely with teams in two other countries, so we got slides on how to run meetings differently depending on who you're dealing with: some cultures are more hierarchical so you dont contradict the senior person in public, some communicate indirectly so a polite deflection actually means no, some treat deadlines as firm and some treat them as opening bids. There were literal country profiles with numerical scores, like personality tests but for entire nations, built on decades of employee survey data.

Training two, the following week, was the standard anti-bias module: never assume anything about a person from their group membership, treat people as individuals, stereotypes mislead even when they feel accurate, and so on.

So within a fortnight my employer told me that generalizing about people based on what nation they come from is an essential managerial skill, and that generalizing about people based on group membership is prejudice. When I raised this with HR they looked at me like I'd missed the point of both trainings. Maybe I have. But I'd like to know which point I missed.

So, questions. First: what view of national character does the first training actually presuppose? That nations have something like personalities, i.e. stable shared psychological dispositions that explain individual behavior? Or something thinner, like statistical regularities across a population? Or thinner still, that institutions and norms differ and individuals get trained into them locally? Reconstruct the strongest version of the implicit theory, and tell me whether it's even coherent — can a hundred million people share a character trait in any sense that isn't just metaphor?

Second: can the two trainings be made consistent, or does one of them have to go? I can think of candidate distinctions — nationality versus race or gender as the grouping, generalizations as base rates versus claims about essences, using statistics to interpret behavior after the fact versus pre-judging individuals before it — but I don't know if any of them survive scrutiny. Which, if any, actually does the work? And where is the practice just underdetermined, i.e. compatible with several theories of what national differences amount to, so that the trainings don't really commit us to any one of them?

Third: suppose we concluded national character is basically a myth and within-country variation swamps between-country variation. What would a defensible cross-cultural training even look like then? Presumably you can't tell managers nothing, because the meetings really do go differently in different places. And conversely, suppose national character turned out to be real and robust — would consistency then require the anti-bias training to soften, i.e. to allow more generalization elsewhere, not less?

I'm not asking whether the specific country scores are accurate — take the survey data as given. I want to know what the practice commits us to, and whether a company can run both trainings in good faith or whether one of them has to be hypocritical.
