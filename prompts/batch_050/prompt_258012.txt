I co-host a modest philosophy of science podcast (we get maybe a few hundred listens per episode, so nothing fancy) and in two weeks we're interviewing a philosoper of biology who's spent much of her career defending the propensity interpretation of fitness -- roughly the idea that an organism's fitness isn't its actual number of offspring but its probabilistic propensity to survive and reproduce, given its traits and environment. Our past guests on evolution topics have mostly gotten softballs, and I want to do better this time, but I'm not a specialist and I'm worried my questions will just invite her to re-explain the basics for an hour.

Draft me about eight to ten interview questions that push on the places where the propensity view is genuinely under pressure rather than asking her to restate it. Specifically I want the set to include: a question on the old tautology charge (doesn't "survival of the fittest" become circular if fitness is defined by reproductive success, and does cashing fitness out as propensity rather than actual success actually fix that or just relocate the problem); a question about organisms that buck their own propensities -- the robustly fit creature who gets struck by lightning, the sickly one who lucks into twenty offspring -- and whether the view can say anything about actual evolutionary outcomes rather than expected ones; a question on how to pin down which propensity we're even talking about (over what time horizon, relativized to which environment, and does the notorious reference-class problem for propensities in general resurface here); and a question about the statisticalist challenge, i.e. the claim that fitness is really just a summary statistic describing populations and not a causal property of individuals at all, so the whole propensity interpretation reifies something that isn't there.

For each question, also give me the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not left nodding when she deploys her standard move. And somewhere in the sequence I want one question about what the view implies for how we should distinguish selection from drift empirically -- if fitness differences are probabilistic anyway, when can we ever tell that a trait spread because of selection rather than chance, and is that a problem for her view or just a fact about epistemology. End the list with a question about what consideration or result, if any, would actually make her abandon the propensity interpretation, since she tends to present it as almost definitionally secure. Casual but sharp tone is fine -- our audience is grad students and interested amateurs, not undergrads hearing this for the first time.
