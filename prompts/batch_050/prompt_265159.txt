So here's the situation. I retired from physics three years ago and have been reading moral philosophy ever since, mostly badly and out of order. Last month I stumbled onto this "longtermism" business and, being me, I tried to write down the core of it as a proper argument, the way you'd lay out a derivation. The problem is the derivation seems to prove something I find nuts, and I can't locate the faulty line. Maybe you can.

Here's my argument:

(1) When a person exists doesn't change how much their life matters morally. A child born in 2124 counts for exactly as much as a child born in 2024.

(2) There could be enormously more people in the future than there are alive today. If humanity lasts even a modest fraction of the time it plausibly could, the number of future lives outnumbers present lives by many orders of magnitude -- I ran the Fermi estimate myself and even with conservative numbers it's not close.

(3) So the total amount of welfare at stake in the long-run future utterly dwarfs whatever is at stake in the present.

(4) If one course of action has vastly greater expected moral value than another, you ought to take it.

(5) Even a tiny reduction in the probability of something that forecloses that future -- extinction, permanent collapse, whatever -- therefore beats, in expectation, even large certain benefits to people alive right now.

(6) So basically all of our charitable giving and a big chunk of our career choices should be redirected from helping present people to safeguarding the far future.

Now here's where it runs away from me. By exactly the same logic:

(7) Any scenario someone can describe where the stakes are astronomically large, however speculative the scenario, automatically outranks any concrete present-day benefit. Multiply a one-in-a-million probability by a big enough number and you still beat the malaria nets. So the argument doesn't just prioritize extinction risk -- it prioritizes whatever cause comes packaged with the biggest imaginable numbers. The welfare of quadrillions of possible digital minds, the fates of galaxies we might one day colonize, things nobody has even thought of yet but could write down tomorrow.

(8) Worse, there are lots of these speculative scenarios, they pull in different directions, and we have no robust way of ranking their probabilities. So the argument seems to direct me toward whichever cause has the most imaginative person doing the arithmetic.

(9) And it tells a nurse on a ward that the best thing she can do with her one life is quit and write grant proposals about asteroid deflection, which I simply do not believe.

An argument that lets you justify anything by attaching sufficiently large numbers to it proves too much. So something in (1)-(5) is false, or the steps to (6) and beyond are invalid. I just can't see which.

Two requests. First: be commited. Don't hand me a menu of six options and invite me to pick. Tell me which single line or step you would cut, why it looked so plausible in the first place, and what rejecting it actually costs -- including what I have to give up saying elsewhere if I cut it. Price the runner-up alternatives too, but briefly, so I can see why you didn't pick them. Second, a preference, not a veto: I'd rather keep (1) than almost anything else. The whole reason the argument grabbed me is that discounting people just for being born later seems like pure prejudice, like discounting them for being born far away. If the fix requires scrapping (1), I want to hear exactly why nothing cheaper will do.
