I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind (my main thing is theories of personal identity, though I keep getting pulled into cog sci reading groups) and I've been arguing about this with my partner for weeks, ever since she lost her grandmother and said something like "half of grandma's personality lives in my mom now, you can literally see it." She meant it literally, not as a figure of speech. I pushed back, and then she hit me with Hofstadter -- the idea from I Am a Strange Loop that after his wife died, some coarse-grained, low-resolution version of her self-model genuinely persisted in his brain, because a self is a pattern and patterns can be multiply instantiated at different fidelities. I'd always filed that under "moving but metaphorical." But now I'm not so sure, and I want to pin down my actual credence with the reasoning visible instead of just vibes.

So: please give me your credence that the following claim is true -- when two people have known each other intimately for decades, some non-trivial portion of one person's self (not just memories *of* them, but their dispositions, values, ways of seeing) is genuinely instantiated in the other person's brain, such that when the first person dies, something of *them*, and not merely a memorial, survives. Decompose it. I figure it at least depends on: whether personal identity is a matter of pattern rather than substance/continuity of a particular brain; whether "being the same pattern" admits of degrees, or whether below some fidelity threshold the copy just stops counting as the person; whether what's instantiated in the survivor is the right *kind* of structure (a self-model that can be run from the inside, so to speak) versus a mere predictive model of another person, the same kind I have of my favorite novelist; and whether Parfit-style "what matters is psychological continuity, not identity" defuses the whole question. Give each of those (or your own better terms) a rough probability with a line of justification, combine them, and give me the final number. Then tell me which sub-question the answer is most sensitive to and what would shift it.

One twist I'd like you to apply your decomposition to, because it's my partner's countermove and it stresses the fidelity term: suppose I deliberately train myself for years to simulate a dying friend -- I study their texts, I practice answering questions as them, I get tested on predicting their reactions to novel situations and hit, say, 85% accuracy. Have I become a partial host of their self, or just a skilled impersonator? And if you think the surviving spouse case comes out differently from the deliberate-training case, say which term in your decomposition does the work, because I currently can't see a principled line between them and I suspect my credence is being dragged around by the romance of the spouse case rather than by any actual argument.
