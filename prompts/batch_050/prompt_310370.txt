Last night, over the dregs of a bottle of wine after our philosophy society meeting, my friend Ana and I argued for three hours about whether lying is morally worse than merely misleading, and I would like an outside reconstruction of what her position actually commits her to. Her verdicts, as I wrote them down, were these. The murderer at the door: lying outright ("he's not here") is clearly permissible, and so is the technically true dodge ("I last saw him heading toward the station"), and she insists the two are exactly on a par, without a shade of moral difference. Lying to a spouse about an affair when asked directly: very wrong. But — and this is where the argument turned heated — she maintains that answering the spouse with a technically true statement engineered to leave a false impression ("I was at the office until ten," having stopped by the office for five minutes on the way home) is exactly as wrong as the flat lie; the literal truth of the words does no moral work whatsoever. She also thinks it is fine to mislead a nosy acquaintance who asks what she earns ("less than my brother, sadly" — true, but chosen to suggest she is broke), and fine to lie outright to a telemarketer who asks whether she is the homeowner. So her pattern, near as I can tell: when the asker is a wrongdoer or a busybody, both modes are permissible and equal; when the asker is her spouse, both modes are wrong and equal.

Two new cases arose at the end of the night, and we could not agree on what she is committed to. First: her closest friend, who has no legitimate claim to the information and is not harmed by remaining ignorant, asks her point-blank whether Ana is the author of an anonymous review that mercilessly mocked the friend's new novel; Ana is the author, and no technically-true dodge is available, so concealing it means lying outright to her dearest friend. Second: a security-clearance form asks whether she has used illegal drugs in the past ten years — a question she concedes the agency is entitled to ask — and she answers, truthfully, "I have been entirely clean for over a year," knowing the reader will take it as a no, when in fact she used heavily until thirteen months ago. What would she say about each, and why?

Please structure your answer in four sections, in this order. "Predicted Verdicts" (roughly 150 words): her verdict on each new case, with your confidence in each. "The Rule She's Running" (roughly 200 words): the single principle that best fits all six original verdicts, stated precisely enough to be falsified by a case. "The Rivals" (roughly 200 words): at least two other principles equally consistent with the original six — I have in mind things like a wrongness-tracks-entitlement theory and a wrongness-tracks-betrayed-trust theory, but name the candidates yourself — and what each predicts about the two new cases, since I suspect they diverge there. "The Tiebreaker" (roughly 100 words): the one further verdict, real or hypothetical, that would most efficiently tell the surviving rules apart, and what I should ask her next time.
