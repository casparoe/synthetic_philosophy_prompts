So my seminar has been stuck all semester on the "standing to blame" literature -- the question of whether a hypocrite loses the right to blame someone else for the very thing they do themselves. Two of my students keep going in circles: one is a standing skeptic who thinks hypocrisy genuinely undermines the hypocrite's blame (call her the skeptic), the other thinks the aptness of blame depends entirely on the blamed person's wrongdoing and not one bit on the blamer's moral record (call him the content theorist, since for him blame is basically the assertion of a true moral proposition and true propositions dont stop being true when a hypocrite says them).

Draft the joint statement the two of them could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. Specifically I want:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. These should include things like: cases where even the content theorist concedes hypocritical blame is somehow defective (e.g. it reliably produces resentment, it seems to invite the reply "who are you to talk", the blamee often has a legitimate grievance in response) and cases where even the skeptic concedes the hypocrite's blame can be accurate, first-personally appropriate for bystanders to endorse, and better than silence. Include agreement on what standing challenges even are (hypocrisy, complicity, meddling in affairs that are none of your business) and on the fact that "tu quoque" is normally listed as a fallacy, so any theory that validates it has some explaining to do.

2. A disagreements section where each remaining dispute is stated in wording that side would actually accept for itself. The big one I suspect: whether blame is best modeled as a truth-assessable claim (whose flaw can only be falsity or baselessness) or as a speech act more like sentencing or excommunicating, which requires the speaker to occupy a certain moral position relative to the audience. Also whether the defect in hypocritical blame attaches to the blaming act, to the blamer, or to nothing normative at all but only to how it predictably lands.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions where if my two students settled them, at least one of them would have to move. I suspect one crux is whether there's a real difference between "what you say is false" and "you are not entitled to say it" -- and another is whether the skeptic's view can handle third parties (if hypocrisy defeats standing, why can the hypocrite's equally guilty friend blame you on the victim's behalf?).

4. For each crux, a case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction about it written down in advance. Some candidates to consider: the parent who still smokes telling their kid not to; the reformed criminal condemning his old accomplice; the politician who privately does what she publicly condemns; the blame from someone with a spotless record who nonetheless has no relationship to the victim. If one of the standard cases turns out to cut no ice between them -- i.e. both sides give the same verdict about it for different reasons -- say so explicitly instead of pretending it's a test.

5. Finally, a section flagging any part of the dispute that is merely verbal. I have a hunch that part of the fight is just the two of them using "blame" for different things (the judgement vs. the overt expression of the judgement), so tell me whether that dissolve move works or whether a real disagreement survives once you disambiguate.

Format: numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table (columns: issue, skeptic's formulation of her view, content theorist's formulation of his), then cruxes with predictions, then the verbal-dispute verdict. Keep it in their two voices in the sense that each side's column should be something that person could sign without wincing.
