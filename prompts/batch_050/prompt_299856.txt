I teach an upper-level philosophy of science seminar and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question on Lakatos that can't be answered by reciting hard core / protective belt / positive heuristic. Build it around this made-up episode: a cognitive science programme whose hard core says memories are stored as standing waves in brain-wide electromagnetic fields, not in synaptic weights. Its one genuinely risky prediction -- that magnetic stimulation at certain frequencies would boost recall of emotional but not neutral memories -- showed up in one small lab but then failed in three larger preregistered replications, after which the proponents added a "coherence window" parameter that retrodicts the hit and the failures alike. They also cite a much older, well-known finding (electroconvulsive shock wipes recent but not remote memories) as support for the programme, even though that fact played no role in constructing the theory.

The question should force students to argue whether this programme is progressive or degenerating, and the crux has to be what counts as a novel fact: the ECT result was known long before the programme existed, so on a naive "predicted before observed" reading it can't count as progress. I want them to have to engage with the Zahar-style move that a fact can be novel relative to a theory if it wasn't used in constructing that theory, and with the worry that this makes novelty too cheap -- and too relative to the theorist's own heuristic path -- to do the appraising work Lakatos needs it to do. I also want them pushed on Lakatos's denial of instant rationality: even if the programme is degenerating, could it still be rational to keep working on it, and can Lakatos give any real answer to the person who asks when you finally have to walk away?

Deliverables: the exam question as it would appear on the paper (answerable in about 60 minutes), a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit moves with the marks for each, plus a one-line note per move on how students typically get it wrong (e.g., treating every retrodiction as degenerating by definition, or thinking the three failed replications refute the programme outright).
