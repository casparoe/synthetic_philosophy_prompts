I am an independent scholar drafting an essay on John Stuart Mill, and I keep running into an interpretive situation that I suspect is a recognized type, though I have no name for it. Let me describe the shape of it in the case I know best. For most of his career Mill was received — and presented himself — as the foremost English champion of individual liberty and competitive markets. Yet in his Autobiography he reports that he and Harriet Taylor eventually came to think of their ultimate social ideal as socialist, albeit with heavy qualifications; his treatise on political economy grew friendlier to workers' cooperatives with each successive edition; and after his death a set of unfinished chapters appeared in which he grants the socialist critique of competition far more than his admirers expected, while still declining most actual socialist schemes. His readers have never settled whether this is one mind developing or two minds in one lifetime, and the evidence is genuinely awkward: the key texts are unfinished, posthumous, entangled with a collaborator's influence, and the shift is gradual on the page but sharp in reputation.

What I want to know is whether this predicament — not Mill himself, but the interpretive situation he leaves us in — has an established name or literature. The neighbors I can think of all seem to miss. It is not recantation, since Mill retracted nothing. It does not look like esoteric writing, since he appears to have been as candid as he knew how to be. It is more than the banal fact that people change their minds, because the puzzle is methodological: how much weight should unfinished, posthumously published material carry against a finished lifetime of published work, and who bears the burden of proof between the continuity reading and the rupture reading? Perhaps what I am describing is just a special case of something broader, like the early-versus-late splits imposed on other thinkers, or perhaps Mill's case has features — the collaborator, the edition-by-edition drift, the fragmentary final text — that make it sui generis.

If there is a name, tell me what the literature takes the phenomenon to be and how it adjudicates cases like this one. If there is not, tell me so plainly and coin a term: give it a definition, state what evidence would settle the continuity-versus-rupture question in principle, and mark the edges — does the phenomenon require posthumous publication, unfinishedness, or merely a late revision that outruns the author's ability to defend it? I am after the carving, not just a label.
