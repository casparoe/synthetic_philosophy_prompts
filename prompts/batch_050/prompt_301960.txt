Exam question (students had 45 minutes):

Frederick Douglass spent the first part of his abolitionist career agreeing with William Lloyd Garrison that the US Constitution was a pro-slavery document, then reversed himself. Explain his mature position that the Constitution is an anti-slavery document, giving at least three of his specific arguments. What method of constitutional interpretation does his position rely on, and how did that method lead him to respond to the Dred Scott decision?

Student answer:

Douglass began his career as a follower of Garrison and shared the Garrisonian view that the Constitution was a pro-slavery compact, and he is the one who famously described it as a covenant with death and an agreement with hell. Around 1851, however, he publicly broke with the Garrisonians and announced that he had changed his mind, concluding that the Constitution, rightly read, is an anti-slavery document.

His arguments were mainly textual. First, the Constitution nowhere uses the words slave or slavery, even where it clearly gestures at the institution, which Douglass took to show that the document does not sanction slavery by name. Second, he pointed to the preamble's commitment to securing liberty and to the Fifth Amendment's guarantee that no person be deprived of liberty without due process, arguing these apply to all persons, including the enslaved. Third, he argued that the fugitive slave clause, which speaks of persons held to service or labour, referred to indentured servants and apprentices bound by contract rather than to enslaved people, and so did not constitutionalize slavery at all. He also argued that the three-fifths clause actually worked against slave states by denying them full representation for the people they enslaved.

Methodologically, Douglass argued that we should interpret the Constitution by recovering the anti-slavery intentions of the framers, who he thought were closet abolitionists constrained by the politics of their time. This intentionalist reading is what allowed him to claim the document for the anti-slavery cause. On Dred Scott, Douglass rejected the decision entirely, arguing that Taney's opinion was a misreading of the Constitution rather than a faithful application of it, so accepting the Constitution as anti-slavery did not commit him to accepting the Court's holding.
