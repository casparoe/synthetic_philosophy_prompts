There's a debate in my decision-theory reading group that feels completly stuck and I want a diagnosis, not another round of the same arguments. The case is Quinn's self-torturer: a device strapped to you with a dial of a thousand settings, where each notch up pays you ten grand and increases your pain by an amount too small to consciously notice, and at the top you're in agony and would pay everything you earned to go back to setting one. Half the group says the case shows stepwise-rational choices can land you somewhere it's rational to pay to escape, so rational preferences (or at least rational choices) can be intransitive or otherwise cycle over a spectrum. The other half runs the money pump: intransitive preferences mean you can be cycled A to B to C to A paying a fee each time, so they can't be rational, full stop. The first side replies that the pump cheats -- it assumes you can be walked back to the start through imperceptible steps while keeping your preferences fixed, and once you know you're being pumped the situation (and your preferences over it) has changed. The second side says that's just refusing the thought experiment. A third person keeps insisting the whole thing is really about vagueness of pain comparisons and has nothing to do with rationality.

Everyone has heard every move and nobody has budged in months. So: what kind of disagreement is this actually? Is it verbal -- two camps using "rational" for different things (local choiceworthiness vs. invulnerability to exploitation)? Is there a hidden substantive question doing the work, like whether diachronic exploitability is even the right test of rational preference, or whether preference has to be determinate across imperceptible differences? Or is it bedrock intuitions all the way down? Diagnose it using what each side actually says, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis -- a distinction, a formal result, an agreed test -- or argue that nothing would and why.

At the very end, put your diagnosis as a short phrase in \answer{...}, e.g. \answer{verbal dispute over the standard of rationality} or whatever you actually conclude.
