I recently took a position in my national government's small unit for historical reconciliation, which fields formal requests for state apologies -- from descendant communities, from foreign governments, and occasionally from groups of still-living victims. I joined partly because my own family belongs to a community that petitioned for an apology some years ago and received only a statement of "deep regret," which many of us experienced as worse than silence. The unit currently has no settled criteria: each request is handled ad hoc, and my predecessor's files show the same event being treated as apology-worthy in one year and not the next, depending on the minister.

I would like you to design a decision procedure the unit can apply to any request, grounded in whatever the philosophy of apology, collective responsibility, and intergenerational ethics actually establishes -- translated so that a civil servant without philosophical training can run it. The procedure should address, at minimum, the following problems, and should do so as an ordered sequence of questions with clear criteria for what counts as a satisfactory answer to each:

First, the identity problem: the officials who committed or authorized the wrong are dead, and everyone now in office disavows the act. Under what view of the state as a continuing agent can "we" apologize for what "we" did not do, and what institutional facts (continuity of the legal order, inherited assets, official celebration of the perpetrators) bear on whether that continuity holds?

Second, the standing problem: who is owed the apology -- direct victims, their descendants, the group as such -- and does standing expire? Handle requests concerning events three generations old differently from those with living victims only if there is a principled reason to.

Third, the performativity problem: an apology is something done with words, so it can fail the way a promise made under duress fails. Build in tests for the conditions under which a state apology is defective -- issued under electoral pressure, paired with a denial of legal liability, or accompanied by no change in school curricula or commemorations. Our unit needs to be able to tell a petitioning group, in advance, which of their requested formulations would produce a real apology and which would produce what my family received.

Fourth, the consequence problem: does a genuine apology commit the state to reparations, truth-telling, or prosecutions, or can it stand alone? Say where the procedure must hand the decision to elected officials because the question is one of values or political judgment rather than of fact.

Then run the procedure on three requests: (a) a colonial-era famine in which official policy substantially deepened the mortality, all victims long dead, now the subject of a foreign government's request; (b) a forced-sterilization program ended forty years ago with some thousands of surviving victims; and (c) a wartime alliance with a reprehensible regime that caused great harm but is defended by many historians here as the least bad available option. Please ensure at least one application yields a recommendation I will not want to defend at a public hearing, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot settle and where it is most likely to be gamed.
