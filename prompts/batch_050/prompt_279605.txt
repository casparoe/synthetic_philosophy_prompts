A colleague in my graduate seminar set me a piece of reasoning that I have been unable to fault, though its conclusion cannot be right. It starts from Locke's account of personal identity in An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (Book II, Chapter XXVII). Locke there holds that what makes someone the same person as the doer of some past deed is not sameness of body or of immaterial substance but sameness of consciousness: a person's identity reaches backwards exactly as far as their consciousness of past thoughts and actions extends, and this is why, on his view, person is a forensic notion apt for responsibility, reward and punishment. In the same discussion Locke concedes that consciousness is perpetually interrupted by forgetfulness, so that at no moment of our lives do we have the whole train of our past actions before us in a single view; even the best memories lose sight of one part of the past whilst surveying another. From these materials my colleague builds the following:

(1) I am the same person as the doer of a past deed only if my consciousness extends back to that deed.
(2) For consciousness to extend back to a deed is for that deed to be present to my mind: at any given moment, either I am conscious of the deed or I am not.
(3) At almost every moment of my life, almost none of my past deeds are present to my mind; that is precisely what Locke's concession about forgetfulness amounts to.
(4) Therefore, at almost every moment of my life, I am not the person who performed almost any of the deeds attributed to me.
(5) A promise binds, and a punishment is just, only where the person bound or punished is the very person who promised or offended.
(6) Therefore almost no promise binds anyone for longer than the deed stays in mind, and almost every punishment ever inflicted has fallen on the wrong person.
(7) The obvious repair — say that I am the same person as an earlier self provided a chain of overlapping memories connects us, so that I need only remember someone who remembered the deed — collapses on Reid's case of the brave officer: the aged general remembers the young officer's exploits, the young officer remembered being flogged as a boy, yet the general has no memory of the flogging; so the general is the boy (by the chain) and is not the boy (by the same chain, since identity is transitive and memory-overlap is not), which is a contradiction.
(8) So no criterion of personal identity in terms of memory, whether direct or chained, can underwrite our practices of responsibility; and since the bodily and substance criteria were ruled out by Locke's own arguments, responsibility itself is groundless.

My colleague concludes that we must simply live with (8). I refuse to. Ordinary blame and promise-keeping are non-negotiable for me, and if forced to choose I would rather abandon Locke's memory criterion altogether than accept that yesterday's signature binds nobody today. But I cannot locate the exact point at which the argument leaves the rails, and every premiss looks defensible in isolation. Which step should I reject, why did it look acceptable, and what does rejecting it cost — in particular, does the repair I attempt at (7) fail for the reason given, or only for a reason Reid's case does not actually establish?
