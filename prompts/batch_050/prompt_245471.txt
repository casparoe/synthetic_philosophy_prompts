I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm currently working on a long piece about longtermism and the moral weight of the far future. A contributing writer has submitted a sidebar on the so-called "cluelessness problem" for consequentialism, and before I sign off on it I'd like an accuracy audit, because something about it feels off to me but I can't pin down where. The sidebar runs as follows. Consequentialists hold that we ought to do whatever will produce the best consequences overall. But every action, however small, sets off causal ripples that persist indefinitely: whether I catch a particular train today slightly changes traffic patterns, conversations, and couplings, and over centuries these differences compound into wholly different sets of people being born, and with them different wars, discoveries, and atrocities. Because these remote consequences are so much larger in aggregate than anything in the near term, and because we cannot even assign probabilities to them, we are in the author's words "flying blind": no act can be rationally preferred to any other on consequentialist grounds. He concludes that consequentialism thereby collapses into practical paralysis, and he adds, in a closing paragraph, that this is a problem only consequentialists face, since deontologists judge actions by their intrinsic character rather than their downstream effects and are therefore untouched by the unpredictability of the far future.

What I want to know, essentially, is which parts of this a philosopher who actually works on the topic would accept, which parts are defensible simplifications for a general readership, and which are misrepresentations I should flag to the writer before publication. In particular: is it right that the mere fact of vast, unpredictable downstream consequences entails that no act can be rationally preferred to any other, or does that conclusion conflate "we cannot know which action is best" with "all actions are equally choiceworthy"? Does the argument as stated distinguish between consequences we can conceive of but cannot estimate and consequences we cannot even conceive of, and does that distinction matter for how damaging the problem is? Is the sidebar treating ordinary empirical uncertainty as if it were the special thing at issue here? And is the closing claim -- that deontological views escape the problem because they don't look at consequences -- actually sound, or does it overlook ways in which non-consequentialist reasoning also leans on predictions about what one's acts will bring about?

Finally, a more editorial question: if the accurate core of the problem is as serious as the sidebar suggests, does it follow that the piece's framing of longtermism needs to change -- that is, does cluelessness cut against trying to benefit the far future at all, or only against certain ways of doing it? I'd like a verdict I can act on: keep, revise, or kill each of the sidebar's three main claims, with the reasoning in terms I could relay to the writer without a philosophy degree.
