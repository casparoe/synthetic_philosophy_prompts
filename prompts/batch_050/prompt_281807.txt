Next month I'm presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on aesthetics — roughly half analytic philosophers of art, half psychologists doing empirical work on aesthetic preference and expertise. My thesis is that Hume's standard of taste, properly understood, is not the circular appeal to "true judges" it is usually accused of being: I argue that his five marks of the qualified critic (delicacy of imagination, practice, the making of comparisons, freedom from prejudice, and good sense) describe an ideal of calibrated response that can ground genuinely objective verdicts about beauty, and that cross-cultural disagreement about particular artworks is compatible with this standard rather than evidence against it. Prepare me for the question period. Give me the seven or eight objections I should actually expect from this particular audience — the philosophers will press the circularity and regress worries in their own terms, the psychologists will press cultural variation and the fragility of expert judgment in theirs — ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, each phrased the way that side of the room would actually phrase it. For each, give me what I should concede outright, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a note on where that reply is weakest. End with the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
