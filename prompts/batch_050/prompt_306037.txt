hey, i'm in a second-year philosophy of tech course and i have to write a term paper on griefbots (chatbots trained on a dead person's texts/emails so the family can keep "talking" to them). i've got a draft intro and an outline and i'd love comments before i write the thing properly. here it is:

**Working title: The Dead Don't Reply**

Thesis: most of the debate i've seen is about the dead person -- did they consent, is it dignified, does it disrespect their memory. i think that's the wrong place to look. my claim is the real problem is on the mourner's side, and it would still be a problem even with a fully consenting, fully informed dead person.

Outline of the argument:
1. Grieving is partly *revision work*: your internal model of the person gradually gets re-sorted, softened, edited down into memory. the fading isn't a bug, it's how you get to keep someone without being stuck next to them forever.
2. A griefbot outsources that work. it keeps regenerating the person from a frozen dataset, so every conversation refreshes one fixed version of them and crowds out the version you were building yourself. you're not revising your model anymore, you're renting theirs.
3. Bonus asymmetry: when my own memory of my nan is wrong, i can interrogate it ("no wait, she'd never say that"). when the bot is wrong, it says the wrong thing *in her voice*, and it was trained on more of her messages than i ever saw -- so it epistemically outranks me about my own nan. a wrong "i forgave you" from the bot lands harder than one i only imagined.
