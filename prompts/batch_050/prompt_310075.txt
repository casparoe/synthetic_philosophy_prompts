So I'm a novelist, mostly literary fiction, and my current book has a mid-level bank branch manager as one of the main characters. I keep circling the Wells Fargo fake accounts scandal from 2016 as the skeleton for his storyline, and I want to think about it properly before I write a single scene. I've gotten obsessed with Goodhart's law and specification gaming lately and I suspect this episode is either the best illustration of it ever or a case people lazily cite for it when it doesn't quite fit.

Here's what I want. Take the Wells Fargo thing -- employees opening millions of unauthorized checking and credit card accounts to hit aggressive cross-selling targets, thousands of low-level workers fired, the big fines, the CEO eventually resigning -- and run it through Goodhart's law as carefully as you can. When a measure becomes a target, etcetera. Map it for me: what exactly was the measure, what was it supposed to be a proxy for, why was it a decent proxy at first (or was it ever?), and what did the employees do to the mapping. Be precise about the mechanism, because my character's whole arc depends on me getting the incentive machinery right -- I need to know what the pressure actually felt like from inside a branch, not just that there was pressure.

Then do the harder half: tell me where Goodhart's law strains here. My worry as a writer is that the law describes people cleverly gaming the letter of a metric -- distorting behavior but still sort of playing the game -- whereas Wells Fargo employees often just fabricated the numbers outright, which smells less like specification gaming and more like plain fraud that the metric merely motivated. Is that a distinction the theory can absorb, or does the episode expose a gap in it? Also: does it matter that management kept the target after the gaming was well documented, and that the people punished were mostly tellers? Is there a version of Goodhart that explains who gets blamed, or is that just politics the law is silent about?

End with the two-way verdict: does Wells Fargo confirm Goodhart's law, complicate it, or show it was never really about cases where the gaming crosses into outright fabrication? And one selfish practical ask -- flag the single moment in that mechanism you think a novelist should build a scene around, the hinge where a basically honest person starts sliding. A typo or two in my thinking here I'm sure, but that's the assignment.
