Before I wade any further into the brain death literature, I want the spec first: what would a definition (or criterion, or standard -- feel free to untangle those) of death actually have to get right to count as adequate? Write me the desiderata list. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it -- e.g. the desideratum has to handle whole-brain-dead bodies on ventilators, DCD donors where circulation stops and organs are taken a few minutes later, ECMO cases where you can restart circulation after declaring death, locked-in patients, persistent vegetative state, maybe anencephalic infants. I want to know which cases a theory simply cannot be allowed to get wrong and which are just cases where one camp's intuitions happen to be strong.

A few things I specifically want covered:

- The irreversibility vs. permanence issue. As far as I can tell, DCD protocols only work if you read "irreversible" as "won't be reversed" rather than "couldn't be reversed", and I want to know if that's a genuine desideratum conflict (certify death early enough for donation vs. don't let the definition depend on what doctors happen to intend) or a pseudo-problem.
- Whether the definition is allowed to be technology-relative at all, or whether that alone should disqualify a criterion.
- Whether a good definition has to track the dead donor rule, or whether that's circular -- like, is "our definition must permit standard organ procurement" a legit constraint or is it just rigging the outcome?
- Whether it needs to say anything about whether death is an event or a process, and whether it has to come out the same for all organisms or just humans.

Flag any subset of the desiderata you think is jointly unsatisfiable -- that's honestly the part I care about most, since it would mean the whole debate is partly about which requirement to drop. And mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable (a criterion that counts a locked-in patient as dead is just wrong, presumably) versus which are the distinctive commitments of one side.

Then briefly, and only briefly, score the three main contenders -- whole-brain death, the circulatory-respiratory standard, and higher-brain death -- against your list. The criteria are where the thinking is, so don't spend more than a third of the answer on the scoring. And if some requirement knocks out the most popular position all by itself, tell me which one.
