For a grad seminar on epistemic paradoxes I have to write down where I stand and I'm worried my positions don't cohere. Here they are: (1) I'm a fallibilist about knowledge -- you can know things even though your evidence doesn't guarantee them, and knowledge is compatible with the possibility of error. (2) In the preface paradox, the author is being perfectly rational: she believes each claim in her book and also rationally believes, on her track record, that at least one of them is false. (3) In the lottery case, though, you do not know and cannot rationally believe that your ticket will lose, no matter how long the odds -- purely statistical grounds can't do it. (4) Rational belief is agglomerative: if you rationally believe p and rationally believe q, then you rationally believe their conjunction. (5) Whether a belief is rational is just a matter of whether your evidence makes it probable enough -- some threshold short of certainty does the job, and nothing else about the evidence matters. Which of these genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest fix? Rank the tensions by severity and for each one tell me exactly what has to give -- don't just say they're all a bit awkward.
