ok so I'm taking a seminar on population ethics and value theory this term and we just spent two weeks on the torture vs dust specks thing (the whole "would you rather one person be tortured for 50 years or some astronomically huge number of people each get a dust speck in the eye" case). Our prof kept pressing us on whether tiny harms can aggregate into something that outweighs one catastrophic harm, and I realized I still can't decide what I think because I keep flip-flopping depending on how the argument gets framed.

So here's what I want: write a dialogue between two equally sharp philosophers who disagree on this. One of them defends the view that at some (absurdly large) number, the dust specks genuinely do outweigh the torture -- aggregation all the way down. The other holds that there's something like a discontinuity between trivial harms and serious ones, so no number of specks ever beats the torture, and that this isn't just squeamishness.

Some constraints on the dialogue, because I've seen bad versions of this:

- The aggregationist shouldn't just repeat "numbers go up." They should actually use the spectrum/sequence move -- like, if you prefer 1 torture over 10^30 specks, what about 1 slightly-less-bad thing over 10^30 slightly-more-bad specks, and chain down from there -- and the anti-aggregationist has to say exactly where that chain breaks for them and why that break isn't arbitrary.
- The anti-aggregationist has to deal with the awkward cost of their view: sometimes refusing to aggregate means letting way more total suffering happen (e.g., in triage cases where you can only help one group). Make them bite that bullet explicitly or show how they avoid it.
- At least once, they should clash over whether a dust speck even counts as a harm at all (is something you barely notice and forget in a second still "bad for you"?), because I suspect half the disagreement is hiding there.
- Neither should strawman the other. The anti-aggregationist isn't allowed to be a sentimentalist who just says "torture is icky," and the aggregationist isn't allowed to be a spreadsheet-brained caricature.
- I'd like them to actually move each other a little -- by the end each should concede one genuine weakness in their own view, not reach agreement, just acknowledge the strongest pressure point.

Keep it maybe 800-1200 words, and give them names so it's easy to follow. No need to cite anyone, I know the literature, I just want to see the strongest live version of both sides argued out so I can figure out where I land.
