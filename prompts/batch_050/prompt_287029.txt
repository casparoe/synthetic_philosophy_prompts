My roommate and I keep arguing about forgiveness, and I've started tracking her actual verdicts because I think she's running a rule she hasn't said out loud. Her calls so far:

1. A mutual friend kept borrowing her car without asking and only apologized, kind of sarcastically, after getting caught — she says there's no obligation to forgive, and doing it anyway would be a doormat move.
2. An ex lied to her about something serious, then confessed unprompted a year later, was visibly wrecked about it, and went out of his way to make things right. She forgave him and says it was the right thing to do — but when I asked whether someone in that situation must forgive, she said no, that's their call, it's just good if they do.
3. Her grandfather was genuinely cruel to her mom for decades, never once acknowledged it, and now has dementia too advanced to understand an apology. She says her mom has zero obligation to forgive, though if she did it would be, in her words, saintly.
4. A coworker sabotaged her project, apologized tearfully and seemed to mean it, she forgave him — and then a year later he did almost the same thing again. She says there was no obligation the second time and maybe forgiving the first time was a mistake.

New case: her cousin cut off his mom (our aunt) five years ago over something the aunt has now fully and unpromtedly repented of — real amends, therapy, years of demonstrated change, and everyone including the cousin agrees she'd never repeat it. The cousin still won't forgive, and the fallout now lands on innocent people: the kids never see eachother, holidays get split, the grandmother is heartbroken. What will my roommate say — obligation to forgive, or not?

I'm less interested in the verdict than in the rule. As far as I can tell, at least three rules fit her four verdicts: (a) forgiveness becomes obligatory exactly when there's sincere unprompted repentance plus amends plus solid evidence of lasting change; (b) forgiveness is never obligatory, it's always supererogatory, and cases like 2 are just where the supererogatory is most clearly called for; (c) obligation tracks the forward-looking costs of continued estrangement, not anything about the wrongdoer's repentance. Tell me which rule she's most plausibly running, what each of the three predicts for the cousin case, and give me your actual prediction with the reasoning. Note that at least two of the rules seem to land on the same answer here, so also give me the one further case I could put to her that would best tell your favored rule apart from the runner-up.

And one last thing: on whichever rule you pick, forgiveness seems to be conditional on repentance almost by definition. So is what her mom might do for the grandfather even forgiveness, or is it some other thing — letting go, moving on, ceasing to resent — that we've just been slapping the same label on? Does the answer change anything about the cousin case?
