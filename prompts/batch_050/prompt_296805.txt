I'm writing a short paper on the ethics of lying versus misleading and before I commit to any view I want the full spec first. So: what would a theory of the moral difference between outright lying and merely misleading actually have to get right to count as adequate? By misleading I mean stuff like saying "I had oatmeal for breakfast" knowing full well the hearer will conclude you didn't take the last cookie, when you did — literally true, deliberately deceptive. Versus just saying "I didn't take it," which is the lie.

Give me the requirements list. The cases it has to classify and get intuitively right should include at least: bald-faced lies where nobody is expected to believe the speaker (so no one is actually deceived); half-truths where every clause is literally true; misleading by presupposition rather than assertion; saying something you believe is false that turns out to be true (failed lie or successful lie?); and cases where the hearer is already mistaken and you just let them stay mistaken without saying anything. Then the distinctions a theory can't collapse: asserting vs. implicating, intending to deceive vs. merely foreseeing deception, and — this one seems underappreciated — the line between lying and misleading vs. the line between permissible and impermissible, since a theory that equates "it's a lie" with "it's wrong" seems to get both worse. Also the external facts it has to stay consistent with, like what linguistics says about how implicatures actually work, and how the law treats active fraud differently from nondisclosure.

For each requirement, say what motivates it and whether it's genuinely non-negotiable or just a prejudice of one camp (the Griceans, the assertion-first people, the consequentialists who think the whole distinction is morally inert). The part I most want: is some subset of these desiderata jointly unsatisfiable? For instance, it seems hard to simultaneously (a) count bald-faced lies as lies, (b) make an intention to deceive central to what's wrong with lying, and (c) keep a clean assert/implicate boundary doing the lying/misleading work. If the list itself forces a conclusion like that, say so explicitly. And which single requirement knocks out the most standard views at a stroke?

Keep the weight on the criteria, not on scoring existing accounts — I can do that myself. One constraint on the whole exercise tho: the theory has to be usable, because my endgame is applying it to whether an AI assistant that gives literally-true-but-carefully-misleading answers is lying to users or doing something morally lighter. So flag which requirements matter most once you move from human speakers to a system that maybe can't "assert" or "intend" in the ordinary sense, and say whether the lying/misleading distinction even survives that move.
