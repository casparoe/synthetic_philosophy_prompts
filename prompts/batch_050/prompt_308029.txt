I work on the trust-and-safety team of a large online marketplace, and one of my recurring jobs is ruling on seller disputes under our honesty policy. The policy bans lying but says nothing explicit about technically-true statements designed to create false impressions: a listing that says the watch was serviced by an authorized dealer when the seller knows the dealer replaced nothing but the battery, a seller who answers a question about water damage by noting the item was stored in a climate-controlled facility, and so on. I make about twenty of these rulings a week, each under time pressure, and my decisions feed into account suspensions, so I need something more systematic than my current sense of what feels slimy.

Write me a decision procedure I can apply to a disputed communication, grounded in the philosophical distinction between lying and misleading: the ordered tests to run on a statement (what was literally said, what a reasonable buyer would take away, what the seller could reasonably have expected them to take away, what the seller knew), what each test establishes and what it cannot establish, and where the procedure must hand the call to my judgment rather than deliver a verdict. I especially want the procedure to tell me what to do in the hard middle cases: statements that are literally true but would be false if the obvious conversational inference were added, statements whose truth depends on an unusually strict reading the seller can plausibly claim to have intended, and sins of omission where the seller simply never mentioned a defect nobody asked about. Then apply the procedure to the three cases above, including at least one where it yields a ruling I will find uncomfortable -- either clearing someone who is obviously gaming the policy or condemning someone whose conduct looks innocent -- and say honestly whether that discomfort indicates a flaw in the procedure or a flaw in the policy it serves.
