In 1930 Keynes predicted that, within about a century, productivity growth in advanced economies would shrink the working week to roughly fifteen hours and free people to cultivate the art of living. Suppose Keynes were shown today's richest countries: output per hour has multiplied several times over, broadly in line with his projection, yet working hours have fallen only modestly and many people report chronic time scarcity despite material abundance he would have found unimaginable. Explain, on his behalf, what went wrong — or whether, by his lights, anything did. In particular: how does his distinction between absolute needs and relative needs (those we feel only insofar as satisfying them lifts us above our fellows) bear on the persistence of long hours? Would he diagnose continued overwork as evidence that relative, positional needs are effectively insatiable in a way he underestimated, as a political and distributional failure to convert productivity into leisure, or as a shift in what work itself means to people? And given his own account of the good life, would he regard modern consumption and career ambition as an immature stage on the way to it, a distraction from it, or a phenomenon his framework simply failed to anticipate?
