Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether there is anything genuinely objectionable about the kind of continuous performance monitoring my employer has introduced — keystroke logging, attention tracking, algorithmic productivity scoring — given that we all agreed to it in our contracts and are free to leave. My friend held that calling such monitoring oppressive is confused on broadly Foucauldian grounds: power, he insisted, is not something anyone holds or imposes but something that circulates through all social relations, so there is no power-free standpoint from which my complaint could even be stated. When I pushed back, he wrote out the following summary of Foucault to show that I had misunderstood the view:

"Foucault's core claim in Discipline and Punish is that power is not a thing possessed by a person, a class, or the state but a relation circulating through every social encounter; it is everywhere, exercised from below as much as imposed from above. Its distinctively modern form is discipline: where sovereign power terrorized bodies from outside, discipline trains them from within — timetables, drills, examinations, surveillance — until people monitor themselves, with Bentham's Panopticon as the diagram of this mechanism. Because discipline has replaced sovereign power, the state and its laws matter far less than the micro-practices of schools, hospitals, and workplaces. Power and knowledge, moreover, are two names for one and the same thing: every regime of knowledge is a machinery of domination, which is why truth itself is an effect of power. It follows that there is no standpoint outside power from which to resist it, and the honest response is to document the machinery rather than pretend one can step outside it."

Something here felt wrong to me, though I could not articulate it in the moment. Foucault was a political activist, and I seem to remember that he treated resistance as something internal to power relations rather than rendered impossible by them, which sits oddly with the quietism my friend draws. I am also suspicious of the step from the claim that power is everywhere to the conclusion that no criticism is possible — but I cannot tell whether the problem lies in his Foucault or in my recollection of mine.

Please audit the summary carefully: which parts are accurate, which are defensible simplifications, which misrepresent Foucault's position, and which attribute to him claims he explicitly denied? And once the summary is corrected, does my friend's use of it in our argument — that there is no coherent standpoint from which consensual workplace monitoring can be called oppressive — still stand?
