I am developing the research strategy for a foundation that can support eight researchers for three years on conceptual and legal-theoretical work about self-amending institutions, inspired by Nomic but extending beyond games to constitutional amendment clauses, international organizations, professional bodies, decentralized digital governance, and private platform rules. The foundation will not fund empirical surveys, litigation strategy, or bibliographic mapping; it wants work that clarifies which legal or institutional outputs should count as valid when the rules for changing rules are themselves subject to change.

The central problem family includes amendment regress, where a higher-order rule authorizes amendment but can itself be amended; self-entrenchment, where a rule prohibits its own repeal and protects that prohibition; recognition gaps, where an amendment satisfies the text of the relevant procedure but officials or participants cease to treat it as authoritative; forking, where the same procedure appears to generate incompatible successor rulebooks; and re-founding, where a purported amendment is so extensive that the successor is better understood as a new institution than as the old one continuing. Assume these distinctions are defined for present purposes, even though their boundaries are contested.

Which open conceptual questions should the foundation prioritize, and in what order? Please rank the five most important questions, but make clear that the reasoning matters more than the list itself. For each, assess how many other problems depend on its resolution, how tractable it is through conceptual and legal theory rather than empirical research, whether progress would matter to real institutional design, and what makes it superior to the questions ranked immediately below it. Also identify several plausible research questions that should be deprioritized because they are technically interesting but unlikely to change downstream design or adjudication.

Structure the response in exactly these sections and in this order:

1. **Bottom line** — approximately 150–250 words: the top three priorities and the main rationale for treating them as the core agenda.
2. **The field of open questions** — approximately 350–500 words: at most seven questions, each formulated as a question whose resolution would make a practical or theoretical difference.
3. **Defended ranking** — approximately 800–1,100 words: the five priorities in order, with separate treatment of dependency, tractability, practical stakes, and the decisive comparison with the next question on the list.
4. **Dependency map** — approximately 250–400 words: explain in prose which questions are upstream of which others, including one case where a superficially urgent question depends on prior work.
5. **Weak investments** — approximately 250–350 words: the questions or lines of work you would not fund, and why their interest does not translate into priority.
6. **Update conditions** — approximately 250–350 words: the theoretical result, counterexample, or institutional development over the next three years that would most change the ranking.
7. **Risks to the agenda** — approximately 200–300 words: how this research program could become sterile, overly abstract, or irrelevant despite answering its stated questions.

Do not provide citations, a bibliography, or a reading list. Ground the answer in the distinctions above and in general philosophical reasoning rather than in claims about particular named theorists.
