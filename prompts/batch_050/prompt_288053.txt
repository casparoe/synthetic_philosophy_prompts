I am a PhD student in philosophy, writing a thesis on the moral status of non-human minds, and I need your help anticipating a verdict. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher specialising in animal ethics and comparative cognition, with a solid working knowledge of how brains scale across species. Think carefully, and approach this as a puzzle in rational reconstruction rather than as a literature survey.

The situation. My supervisor has a settled pattern of intuitions about how much moral weight the interests of different creatures deserve, and my next chapter builds directly on her framework. She has never, to my knowledge, pronounced on the case I now need to place, and I would rather predict her verdict properly than simply ask her. Below are her past verdicts, graded on her own informal scale (full, very high, high, substantial, modest, negligible), together with the facts she takes to be relevant. Treat all figures as stipulated approximations for the purposes of the exercise; nothing is meant to turn on their precision.

1. Human (~86 billion neurons, ~16 billion in the cerebral cortex; encephalisation quotient around 7): full standing.
2. Chimpanzee (~6–7 billion cortical neurons; EQ around 2.5): very high standing, just short of human.
3. African elephant (~257 billion neurons in total, but only ~5.6 billion cortical, the great majority residing in the cerebellum; EQ around 1.5): high standing, but explicitly below the chimpanzee. Pressed on why a brain with three times our neurons ranks lower, she cited the distribution of the neurons, not the total.
4. Grey parrot (a brain of only ~9 grams, containing ~1.5–2 billion neurons packed into the pallium at densities rivalling primates): substantial standing, comparable in her view to the cow, on the strength of demonstrated problem-solving and vocal learning. When someone objected that the parrot possesses a fraction of the cow's neurons, she replied that what mattered was what the tissue was doing.
5. Cow (~3 billion cortical neurons; EQ well under 1): substantial standing.
6. Common octopus (~500 million neurons, roughly two-thirds of them located in the arms rather than the central brain): modest but genuine standing. She once described it as an alien mind with the evidence to match.
7. Honeybee (~1 million neurons): negligible standing, though she concedes that recent work on insect nociception makes her less confident than she once was.

The new case is the orca: a brain of ~5–8 kilograms, among the largest on Earth; ~43 billion cortical neurons, more than twice the human figure; EQ around 2.5–3; a highly convoluted cortex with an unusually elaborate paralimbic region; stable matrilineal societies, vocal dialects and cooperative hunting; but no manipulative appendages, almost no tool use, and contested evidence on mirror self-recognition and theory of mind.

Here is what I would like from you, in order:

First, brainstorm at least three distinct principles that could rationalise the full pattern of verdicts — candidate dimensions might include total neuron count, cortical neuron count, encephalisation quotient, demonstrated cognitive sophistication, degree of neural integration, or others you consider more faithful — and say which candidates the track record straightforwardly rules out and which survive.

Secondly, for each surviving principle, state what it predicts for the orca, and demonstrate that at least two of them diverge on the case.

Thirdly, give me your best prediction of her verdict and of the rationale she would offer for it, identify which earlier verdict is the closest analog, and explain why you weigh the evidence that way. My own suspicion is that her remarks on the parrot and the elephant are the most diagnostic items in the record; say whether you agree, and if you think I am overweighting two offhand comments, tell me so.

Finally, describe one further case, real or hypothetical, whose verdict would discriminate between the principles you were unable to separate, stating what each principle would predict for it.

One constraint: I am less interested in whether her view is correct than in what her view is. Keep any normative defence of the principles brief; the deliverables are the competing rationalisations, a defended prediction, and the discriminating case.
