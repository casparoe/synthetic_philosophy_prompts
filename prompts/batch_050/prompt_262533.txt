You are a philosopher of science specializing in statistical inference, retained as a consultant by a university committee on research-methods reform. Before drafting its recommendations, the committee wants a conceptual audit of two norms that the replication-crisis reform movement has made standard, and I have been asked to put the problem to you. Work through it carefully: begin by brainstorming a list of candidate presuppositions (aim for at least six), then narrow it to the ones that survive scrutiny, and only then compose your final answer. Mark clearly where you are reconstructing a position rather than stating one of your own.

The two norms, described concretely:

First, registered reports. A growing number of journals allow researchers to submit only an introduction and a methods section, before any data have been collected. Reviewers judge the question and the design; if the paper is accepted at that stage, the journal commits in advance to publishing the eventual results whatever they turn out to be, provided the announced protocol was followed. The pitch is that this removes publication bias and the incentive to massage data into significance.

Second, the condemnation of optional stopping. A researcher who collects data in batches, reruns the significance test after each batch, and stops as soon as the p-value dips below .05 is treated as having committed malpractice — even if she reports every observation honestly, and even if her final dataset is identical, number for number, to one she would have obtained under a fixed-sample-size plan announced in advance. The sin lies in the stopping rule, not in the data.

Here is the puzzle I want audited. A statistician on the committee — call her a thoroughgoing likelihoodist — insists that everything a body of data has to say about a hypothesis is contained in the likelihood of the observations actually obtained. On her view, what the researcher intended, what she would have done had the data come out differently, and whether the hypothesis was written down before or after the analysis make no difference whatsoever to the evidence. If she is right, optional stopping is blameless whenever the data are honestly reported, since identical datasets warrant identical conclusions; and the registered-reports apparatus looks like a ceremony for regulating researchers' intentions, which are on her account evidentially inert.

Answer the following, in this order:

1. Reconstruct the implicit epistemology of the two norms. What conception of statistical evidence must a practice be operating on for it to make sense to treat two numerically identical datasets as differing in evidential weight because of how they were generated, or for an advance commitment about methods to raise the value of results that do not yet exist? Identify the family of views the practice sits most naturally with, and state its central evidential notion precisely.

2. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely harmonizes with. In particular: can the likelihoodist coherently endorse registered reports on the grounds that publication practices determine which results she ever gets to see, so that the selection regime is itself part of her evidence? If so, does that rescue the practice or quietly change its subject? And does an analogous move work for the condemnation of optional stopping, or is that a norm she simply must reject?

3. Say where the practice is underdetermined. Which features of the two norms would be licensed by several rival epistemologies at once, so that observing the norms tells us nothing about which view the reform movement actually holds?

4. Play out the counterfactual: suppose the committee converts wholesale to the likelihoodist position. Which elements of the two norms lose their rationale and must be abandoned, which could be retained but only after being re-justified on different grounds, and what new practices, if any, would a consistent likelihoodist have to introduce to handle the problems the old norms were meant to solve — selective reporting, data-dependent hypotheses, and incentive-driven analysis?

Constraints: reason from the descriptions given above; I am not asking for references, the names of particular journals, or a history of the reform movement. Where an argument turns on a contested premise, flag it rather than resolving it by fiat. Keep both of the committee's audiences in view: working scientists who want rules, and methodologists who want to know what those rules commit them to.
