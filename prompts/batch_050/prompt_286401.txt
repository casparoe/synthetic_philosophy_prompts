As a retired research biologist, I have in the course of my career overseen the humane killing of a great many laboratory animals, and I have lately been reading the philosophical literature on euthanasia and assisted dying for humans. One analogy recurs constantly, in op-eds and ethics seminars alike: we consider it a mercy, indeed a moral duty, to end the suffering of a terminally ill animal rather than let it linger, so refusing competent, suffering humans the same release treats them worse than we treat our dogs. Proponents deploy this to argue that opposition to assisted dying is either sentimentality or speciesist squeamishness. I would like the analogy audited rather than simply endorsed or dismissed. Please give me, first, an explicit mapping: what plays the role of the owner deciding for the animal, of the veterinarian, of the animal's incurable condition, of its inability to comprehend its own prognosis. Second, identify where the mapping breaks, and distinguish the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the intended use from those that are merely cosmetic. In particular, note that the analogy seems to cut in both directions at once: an animal cannot consent, yet we euthanize it anyway, while a human patient can consent and is refused; does this strengthen or undermine the comparison? Third, say what the analogy quietly imports — for instance, assumptions about who may judge that a life is no longer worth living, and about whether the decision-maker's interests can conflict with the patient's — that does not survive transfer to the human case. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion the analogy is made to carry (that opposition to assisted dying is incoherent for anyone who approves of euthanizing animals) actually survives the audit, or whether the analogy was doing illegitimate work; and if a better analogy for the human case exists, propose it.
