Next month I'm presenting at an interdisciplinary workshop on AI and agency, defending the claim that the standard case for instrumental convergence is overstated. My argument: the familiar line that almost any sufficiently intelligent agent will converge on self-preservation, resource acquisition, and goal-content integrity shows only that these subgoals are *available* to nearly any agent, not that a highly capable agent will actually pursue them, because the argument silently assumes an instrumental-reasoning disposition that has to be built in or selected for -- and nothing guarantees that this disposition co-varies with raw capability. An agent could be extremely good at achieving whatever it does pursue while simply not pursuing much. The audience is split: two AI safety researchers who know the classic convergence arguments (Bostrom, Omohundro's basic drives) inside out and will defend them; several machine learning researchers who doubt that philosophical talk of "agents with final goals" applies to systems produced by gradient descent at all; and two philosophers of action who suspect the whole debate equivocates on "goal" and "instrumental reason." Prepare me for the Q&A: give the objections each group will actually raise, phrased the way that group would phrase them, ordered roughly by likelihood and by how much damage they do; say what I should concede up front and what I should hold; flag the weakest step in my own argument (the move from "the disposition isn't guaranteed" to "convergence claims are overstated"); and tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
