Since retiring from chemistry I've been reading philosophy for fun, and I picked up a popular book on Chinese thought at the library. Its summary of Zhuangzi on death goes roughly like this:

'Zhuangzi's response to death was one of cheerful acceptance. When his wife died, a friend who came to offer condolences found him banging on a tub and singing. Zhuangzi believed that just as the body is assembled out of the four elements, it dissolves back into them at death, while the soul passes on to a new form — so death is just a change of address, like moving house. For that reason he held that mourning is irrational: he himself felt no grief at his wife's death once he understood the principle involved. On his own deathbed he told his students not to bury him, asking what difference it makes whether crows or worms get the corpse. The sage, Zhuangzi says, treats life and death as one, so the wise person should look forward to death as a release from the toil of living.'

Something about this feels off to me, though I couldn't say exactly where — maybe I've been trained to be suspicious of tidy stories. Which parts of this would someone who knows the Zhuangzi actually accept, which are imports from other philosophies dressed up as Zhuangzi, and does the final conclusion follow from the parts that are accurate? I'd also like to know whether the wife and deathbed anecdotes are in the text at all or just legends. End your reply with a last line of the form: Verdict: fair / mostly fair / mixed / mostly misleading.
