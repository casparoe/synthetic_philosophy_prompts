I serve on the board of a small charitable foundation with nine trustees. Our charter contains an emergency clause: any single trustee may, acting alone, commit the foundation to a grant of up to $50,000 when they judge that a time-sensitive opportunity would be lost under our ordinary (and much slower) full-board process. The clause was adopted on the theory that we all trust one another's competence and good faith, and that it would be a shame to let good opportunities die in committee.

Three years in, a pattern has emerged. Grants made under the emergency clause have a noticeably worse track record than grants approved by the full board -- not catastrophic, but consistently disappointing, and several have been outright regretted. Yet when I examine each case individually, I cannot find fault with the deciding trustee's reasoning. Every one of them is experienced, sincere, and acting on information that genuinely seemed to warrant the grant at the time. Nobody is reckless, and nobody is abusing the power. Some of my fellow trustees conclude that we have simply been unlucky, and that the clause is fine.

I am inclined to disagree, but I cannot fully articulate why. My suspicion is that the problem lies not in any trustee's judgment but in the structure of the rule itself: that something about letting the most enthusiastic among nine reasonable people act for the whole would produce systematically overoptimistic decisions even if every trustee were perfectly calibrated and perfectly honest. I would like to know whether this suspicion can be made precise. Is there a mechanism by which a unilateral-action rule degrades collective decision quality without requiring anyone to be biased or careless? If so, does the rational response lie with the individual trustee (for instance, discounting one's own enthusiasm before invoking the clause, and by how much), or with the institution (for instance, requiring a second signature, a mandatory waiting period, or advance notice that lets others object)? Finally, does the logic of the problem change as the board grows -- and does it generalize beyond our case to other settings where any member of a group can irreversibly commit the whole, such as spokespeople, whistleblowers, or gatekeepers with release authority?
