So I recently came across a paper by David Lewis (yes, the modal realism guy) where he argues that we all actually do believe in penal substitution, whether we admit it or not. His line is roughly: we accept practices where the cost of wrongdoing lands on people who didn't do the wrong — corporations get fined and the hit falls on innocent shareholders and customers, we tolerate collective punishments and reprisals where a group suffers for what some member did, and so on. His conclusion, as I read it, is that Christians don't need to be embarrassed about the doctrine of the atonement, because the idea that one person can rightly suffer for another's sins is something every functioning legal system already presupposes. It's basically a tu quoque against people who call the doctrine barbaric.

My question is: doesn't this settle the debate about punishing the innocent? If even our own considered practices commit us to penal substitution, then retributivists and liberal legal theorists who insist that only the guilty may be punished are just being inconsistent with their own societies, right? Is there any principled reason left to hold that punishment must track actual guilt, or should we just accept that desert-based punishment was always a bit of a fiction?

And a follow-up for the theology side: once you grant Lewis's point, is there anything substantive left to object to in the atonement, or were all the remaining objections really just the one he defused? I keep seeing philosophers of religion go on about consent, about who arranged the suffering, about whether guilt is even the kind of thing that transfers — but if the practice is fine, why do any of those details matter?
