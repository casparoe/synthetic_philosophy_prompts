I'm in a reading group on philosophy of machine learning, and last week we got into the no-free-lunch theorems and I think they've kind of broken my brain. As I understand it, Wolpert's results show that averaged over all possible problems, every learning algorithm performs identically — gradient descent is no better than random guessing, a fancy neural net is no better than a lookup table that always outputs 7. And people connect this to Hume's problem of induction, that there's no non-circular justification for expecting the future to resemble the past.

So here's my question: given that the theorems prove no learning algorithm can be better than any other, how come machine learning actually works? If every algorithm is equally good, isn't the whole field basically superstition that happens to have paid off so far — no different from a gambler on a lucky streak who thinks hes figured out the dice? And doesn't it also show that Occam's razor is arbitrary, since preferring simple hypotheses is just one inductive bias among infinitely many, all equally unjustified? I brought this up in the group and one person said the no-free-lunch theorems actually "dissolve" Hume's problem by making it precise, but to me it seems like they make everything way worse — they turn Hume's worry into an actual mathematical proof that science and ML rest on nothing. Who's right here, and if it's me, why does anyone bother tuning hyperparameters at all?
