Two colleagues of mine, Maya and Leo, both work through probability puzzles for fun, and last week they got into a disagreement I couldn't settle. The setup: a patient is screened for a disease with a known prior of 10%, using a rapid test with sensitivity 90% and specificity 80%. The complication is that the technician read the test strip under poor lighting, and afterwards said she was only 70% confident that the positive line actually appeared -- she isn't sure whether the result was positive or negative. Maya said the correct response is Jeffrey conditionalization: don't conditionalize on 'positive' or 'negative' (since neither is known), but shift the credence in 'the test was positive' to 0.7 and update the disease probability to 0.7 times P(disease | positive) plus 0.3 times P(disease | negative), which comes out to roughly 24%. Leo objected that this is a shortcut around proper Bayesianism. What you actually learned with certainty, he said, is a proposition -- that the technician reports 70% confidence in a positive line -- and rational updating means ordinary conditionalization on that proposition, using the likelihood of such a report given disease and given no disease. Jeffrey updating, he claimed, is what you do when you can't be bothered to model your own perceptual process, and it hides assumptions rather than avoiding them.

Maya replied that Leo's approach is a mirage: the technician's 70% is a credence, not an asserted proposition, and to conditionalize on the report you'd need P(tech reports 70% | disease) and P(tech reports 70% | no disease), which nobody has; insisting on those likelihoods doesn't eliminate assumptions, it manufactures numbers. Jeffrey's rule, she said, exists precisely for cases where experience directly shifts credences without delivering any proposition as certain. Leo conceded the likelihoods aren't available, but countered with two problems he said were worse. First, rigidity: Jeffrey's rule holds the conditional probabilities P(disease | positive) and P(disease | negative) fixed through the update, yet learning that the strip was read in bad lighting arguably should change those very conditionals, so the rule assumes away part of what was learned. Second, order-dependence: successive Jeffrey updates on different partitions don't commute, so if the technician's confidence had been formed in stages, the final credence would depend on the order in which the stages are processed, and he said a genuine learning rule shouldn't care about the order in which you feed it the same total evidence. Maya answered that non-commutativity is a feature, not a bug: when later evidence bears on the reliability of earlier evidence, order ought to matter, and ordinary conditionalization only appears order-free because it pretends all evidence arrives as propositions. At that point they turned to me, and I realized I didn't actually know who was right about any of the individual moves, let alone overall.

Please adjudicate their exchange.
