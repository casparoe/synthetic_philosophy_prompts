So I'm in a philosophy of physics seminar this semester and we just spent two weeks on Einstein's hole argument -- the 1913 thing where he convinced himself that generally covariant field equations would leave the metric inside a matter-free "hole" underdetermined, and he nearly gave up on general covariance over it. Then I was grabbing coffee with a friend from the physics department who works on quantum gravity, and she mentioned the "problem of time" in canonical quantum gravity: the Wheeler-DeWitt equation (which dates from 1967, so well after Einstein died in 1955) has no time variable in it at all, the quantum state of the universe just sits there, static, and people argue about whether time is fundamental or somehow emergent. We ended up in a half-serious fight about what Einstein would have made of this, and I want to settle it properly.

The reason I think it's not obvious: in his 1916 paper "The Foundation of the Generalised Theory of Relativity," Einstein wrote: "The law of causality has the sense of a definite statement about the world of experience only when observable facts alone appear as causes and effects." So on one reading, the frozen formalism is just the hole argument's logic carried through -- of course there's no fundamental time, because bare spacetime points and coordinate evolution were never observable in the first place, and Einstein's own point-coincidence escape from the hole argument already said only meetings of things are real. On another reading, a theory where nothing ever happens seems like exactly the kind of acausal nightmare that made him panic in 1913, and the determinism worry would come roaring back.

So: if Einstein were somehow brought back and briefed on the problem of time, what would his position actually be? Would he read it as vindicating his 1916 move, or as proof that general covariance was a mistake after all? Would he see the disappearance of time as a symptom of quantizing gravity the wrong way, or as a hint about what a future theory should look like? Please give me your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Ground the prediction in the passage above and in what he actually did during the hole argument episode, not in generic "Einstein loved determinism" vibes.
