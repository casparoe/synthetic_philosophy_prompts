I was recently appointed to chair my city's Commemorative Naming Committee, and our first case is harder than the standard ones I prepared for. In 1911 the council named a downtown street after Thomas Hale, a machinists' organizer who led the 1909 foundry strike and later served on the council himself. In 1938, three years after Hale's death, the council renamed the same street for the foundry owner whose company had broken the strike; the surviving minutes make clear the renaming was a deliberate act of retaliation, meant to blot Hale from public memory. The foundry owner himself was, by the standards of his era, unremarkable — no scandal attaches to him personally.

Our city code permits renaming only where the current honoree is discovered to have committed acts that disgrace the name. Hale's descendants, joined by the local historical society, have petitioned to restore the original name, arguing that the 1938 renaming was itself a civic wrong that restoration would partially repair. Two committee members take the opposite view: the current name, paired with an interpretive plaque, honestly preserves a shameful episode, whereas swapping the names back lets the 1938 council's malice vanish from the record entirely — a commemoration, they say, functions as evidence and not merely as honor. A third faction wants a fresh name honoring no one.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before our vote. It should recommend one of the three options as a concrete course of action, with a process and time frame; it should state whether the code's disgrace clause can cover this case or must be amended, given that the present honoree is blameless; and it should engage seriously the strongest consideration on the losing side — in particular the claim that erasing an erasure destroys the proof that the original wrong occurred.
