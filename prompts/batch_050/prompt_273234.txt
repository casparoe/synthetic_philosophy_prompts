I have been keeping a list of my considered judgments about mental privacy and cognitive liberty as neural interfaces improve, and I would like to know whether any principle organizes them, or whether the list is just a pile of intuitions. The cases, with my verdicts: (1) A logistics firm requires its drivers to wear EEG headbands that detect attention lapses and early signs of drowsiness; the flags are stored, appear in personnel files, and serve as grounds for dismissal. Not permissible. (2) The same headband, same drivers, except that it only vibrates to alert the driver and records nothing; nothing leaves the device. Permissible, possibly even good. (3) A defendant is offered a choice between standing trial on weak circumstantial evidence and undergoing a neural test that purports to detect whether he recognizes details of the crime scene that only the perpetrator would know; he takes the test and is exonerated. Not permissible, because the consent was coerced by the alternative. (4) A court orders the same test over the defendant's objection. Not permissible either -- and yet I regard a court-ordered blood draw, fingerprint comparison, or handwriting sample as plainly permissible, and I cannot say why decoding recognition from brain activity differs in kind from decoding identity from a fingertip. (5) A woman with locked-in syndrome communicates through an implanted interface; under a broad consent clause, the manufacturer logs all of her neural activity, including signals corresponding to inner speech she never chose to transmit, and uses the logs to improve its products. Not permissible; consent to the channel is not consent to the archive. (6) A consumer voluntarily buys a headset that records and reconstructs her own dreams, and she shares selected recordings with her therapist. Permissible. (7) The therapy platform then sells those recordings, anonymized, to an advertising firm for market research. Not permissible -- though I cannot say whether the wrong lies in the sale, in the imperfection of anonymization, or somewhere else. (8) Parents fit their eight-year-old with a neurofeedback device, encouraged by the school, to improve classroom attention; the child dislikes wearing it. Not permissible, or nearly so -- whereas I think both ordinary tutoring and, on a physician's advice, stimulant medication for the same purpose can be permissible, and the difference among these three eludes me.

My tentative principle is what I will call the externalization account: mental privacy protects only content, and it is wrong to extract from someone's neural activity any content that the person has not chosen to externalize, while anything goes with data the person has knowingly externalized or with neural measurements that yield no content at all. It handles some of my cases cleanly -- it blesses (2) and (6), condemns (3), (4), and (5) -- but it appears to bless (7), since the dreams were knowingly externalized to the therapist, and it gives me no verdict at all on (8), where the child externalizes nothing but something still seems wrong. It also leaves the (4)-versus-fingerprints pair untouched, since it does not explain why compelled externalization of recognition is worse than compelled externalization of a fingerprint. Please do the following: assess whether the externalization account, or some repair of it, can capture my verdicts; if not, propose the principle or small set of principles that comes closest, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is motivated by which case; say what your best candidate implies about cases I have not listed -- a subpoenaed private diary, a smartwatch that infers stress from heart rate variability and sells the inferences, and a witness whose memory has been refreshed through hypnosis; and state honestly which of my eight verdicts the simplest adequate principle forces me to abandon, and whether you think I should abandon them or complicate the principle.
