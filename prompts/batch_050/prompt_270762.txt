I've been a reader of LessWrong and the EA-adjacent blogosphere for long enough to have absorbed the standard arguments that wild animal suffering is large, neglected, and morally urgent. Recently I tried to reconstruct, as steel-manned as I can manage, an argument that seems to follow from premises I find plausible, and it appears to prove far too much. I'd like a diagnosis.

The argument:

(1) Population arithmetic: in most wild species, especially r-selected ones, far more individuals are born than can possibly survive to reproduce. The excess die young, mostly of starvation, exposure, predation, or parasites. So the modal wild animal life is short and ends badly.

(2) A short life consisting mostly of hunger, fear, and pain, ending in a painful death, is plausibly worse than never having existed at all. So a large fraction of wild animals — plausibly the majority — have lives of net-negative welfare.

(3) All else equal, a state of affairs containing less aggregate suffering is morally better than one containing more.

(4) Habitat destruction — draining a wetland, clearing a forest, paving a meadow — reliably and durably reduces the number of wild animals who will ever exist in that place.

(5) Even granting that the act of destruction itself inflicts acute suffering on the animals currently present, that is a one-time cost, whereas the averted future lives form a stream extending indefinitely into the future. On any plausible weighting, the stream dominates the one-time cost.

(6) Therefore habitat destruction is, considered purely in terms of its effects on wild animals, morally good, and habitat conservation and restoration are morally bad.

I take (6) to be a reductio, not a discovery. And the argument seems to prove adjacent absurdities if it proves anything: that climate change is good insofar as it reduces total wild biomass; that rewilding projects are atrocities; that a benevolent steward of nature should aspire to a parking lot. If I'm right that it proves too much, something in (1)–(5) has to give, or the inference between them does.

Two constraints on your answer. First, I would rather not be told that wild animals simply fall outside the moral circle — a diagnosis that rescues conservation by declaring the entire problem illusory feels like winning by forfeit. Second, of the premises, (3) is the one I'm most reluctant to abandon; if the flaw lies elsewhere, so much the better.

So: which step is the weakest, why did it look plausible, and what does each available repair cost? If the argument is actually valid, tell me which premise I should surrender and what I give up by surrendering it.
