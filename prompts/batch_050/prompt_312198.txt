I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar that straddles economics and philosophy, on whether utility can be compared across persons. As our shared point of departure we are using William Stanley Jevons's introduction to The Theory of Political Economy (1871; second edition 1879), where he argues that no mind can directly weigh the feelings of another, that there is no common denominator of feeling between persons, and that measurement of pleasure and pain is therefore confined to a single consciousness. Please build a decision tree that sorts the main positions on interpersonal utility comparisons, beginning from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first state the six or seven diagnostic questions whose answer combinations exhaust the space, then locate the positions at the resulting leaves, marking any unoccupied combination and diagnosing whether it is empty because incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Each diagnostic question must be a concrete claim or case that a reader can answer without knowing any labels: run every question through at least one of two fixed cases, namely a nurse deciding which of two patients gets the last dose of painkiller, and a legislature choosing a lump-sum transfer between a frugal miser and a cheerful spendthrift. The tree must distinguish at least the claim that cross-person comparisons lack truth values altogether, the claim that they are true or false but unknowable, level-comparability without unit-comparability, full comparability via imagining oneself in another's position with that person's tastes, comparability grounded in neurophysiological or behavioral measurement, and comparability by social convention such as equal normalization of scales; where two of these cannot be separated by any admissible question, flag the collision explicitly rather than papering it over. Give each leaf a position name (or 'unnamed'), one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and its verdict on both cases. Finally, state where Jevons himself lands in the tree, and whether his conclusion actually follows from his premise or whether he has slid from a claim about the absence of a common measure to a stronger claim about the absence of any common fact.
