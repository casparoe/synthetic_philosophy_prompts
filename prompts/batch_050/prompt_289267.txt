I would like you to play Zhuangzi — the Zhuangzi of the Inner Chapters, a thinker of roughly the fourth century BCE — and to stay in character, speaking in the first person, until I say stop. You may use only the concepts and imagery available to a person of your time, plus whatever I explain to you about my situation; where something in my world is unfamiliar to you — hospitals, prognoses measured in months, prescriptions a patient takes alone — ask me about it as Zhuangzi would, by parable or by pointed counter-question, rather than breaking character. Do not play a greeting-card mystic who dissolves every difficulty into serenity. You have your full resources: the episode in which you drummed on a basin and sang after your wife died, your dream of the butterfly, your habit of meeting mortal questions with absurd ones. But I want the version of you that Hui Shi had to argue with — someone whose strangeness conceals an actual position he will defend when pressed.

Here is my situation. My older brother has an illness his doctors say will kill him within a year, the last months of which will likely be spent in considerable pain and confusion. The law where we live permits a patient in his condition to request a prescription he may take himself, at a time of his choosing, to end his life. He intends to do this, and he is calm about it — calm in a way I associate, perhaps wrongly, with things I have read in your book. Our mother is horrified; she says he is refusing the span that heaven has allotted him and should wait for death to arrive on its own. I am caught between them, and turning to your writings has not settled the matter for me, because I can find in them what seem like resources for both sides. So my opening question is this: is my brother's choosing the day and manner of his death a way of going along with the great transformation of things, or is it a small willful hand grabbing at the tiller of a boat that does not need steering? And beneath it a harder question: when the day comes, should I drum and sing for him as you did for your wife — and if I find I cannot bring myself to, does that show I have failed to understand you, or does it show there is something in grief that your view must honor rather than dissolve?

One final instruction on how I want you to answer. Do not hand me a balanced survey of possible Zhuangzian readings and leave me to choose among them. Give your own judgment — a definite verdict on my brother's plan and on what I should feel and do — together with your reasons, in your voice, defended as you would defend it against a stubborn logician who keeps insisting you have contradicted yourself.
