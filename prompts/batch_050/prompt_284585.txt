In two weeks I'm presenting a paper at our philosophy of physics colloquium, and I want a proper prep sheet for the question period rather than the vague worry-anticipation I usually do the night before. The paper defends a strong reading of what effective field theory teaches us: that the autonomy of scales is a genuine, non-merely-pragmatic feature of the world. My claim is not just that effective theories are convenient fictions we resort to out of ignorance, but that for a wide class of phenomena the lower-scale theory explains and predicts without any input from a more fundamental theory, and that the renormalization group — in particular the way irrelevant operators wash out microscopic detail at accessible energies — shows why this had to be so, rather than merely noting that it happens to be. I got into this after a long argument with a condensed matter colleague over coffee about whether fluid dynamics "needs" molecular theory to be explanatory, and I came away convinced the standard reductionist reading of EFT gets the explanatory direction of the formalism backwards.

The audience worries me. There will be two particle theorists who regard EFT as reductionism's crown jewel rather than its refutation — they will know the Wilsonian picture and the decoupling of heavy physics cold, and one of them works on beyond-Standard-Model physics, so naturalness and the hierarchy problem are live issues for her. There is also a philosopher who works on inter-theory relations and asymptotic reasoning, who I suspect thinks the singular character of the relevant limits (thermodynamic, continuum) means the microscopic theory is doing indispensable work precisely where my autonomy claim needs it not to. My condensed matter friend will be sympathetic, and the rest are grad students. Prepare me: the objections I will actually get, roughly in the order they'll come and phrased the way these particular people would phrase them — including, if it belongs on the list, the worry that anomaly matching and fine-tuning puzzles are cases where ultraviolet information demonstrably leaks into infrared predictions, which strikes me as the most technically serious threat to my thesis. For each objection, give me what I should concede outright, what I should hold, and a two-minute reply, with an honest note about where the reply is weakest. Rank the objections by how much damage they'd do to the talk if I fumble them, tell me which one I'm secretly hoping nobody raises, and flag anything in my framing — especially the move from "the formalism permits ignorance of the UV" to "the explanation doesn't involve the UV" — that a careful listener could drive a wedge into.
