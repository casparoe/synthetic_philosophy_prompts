So, a bit of context: I'm a novelist (literary fiction, one book with a philosopher character in it) but I also moonlight teaching an intro philosophy of mind seminar at the college in town, and I'm writing the final exam. I want one essay question on conceivability as a guide to possibility.

The thing I *don't* want is a question students can answer by reciting the usual gloss — Descartes thought he could conceive of mind without body, therefore dualism is possible, and then some vague handwaving. I want them to wrestle with something I run into constantly as a writer: I can spin a coherent-feeling, richly detailed story about things that are almost certainly impossible (a mathematician proving there's a largest prime; a world where water turns out to be a basic element), and meanwhile I often can't vividly picture things that are perfectly possible. So the question should force them to seperate "I can tell a story about it" from actually conceiving a complete scenario, and to grapple with why conceivability might still count as *evidence* for possibility even once you grant it's defeasible (the water/H2O-type cases should come up, but they should have to explain the problem, not just name-drop Kripke).

Deliverables, please: (1) the exam question itself, written the way I'd put it on the paper — one essay, ~45 minutes, intro level, worth 30 marks; (2) a model answer of about 500 words; (3) a marking scheme out of 30 that names the specific moves an A answer must contain versus a B answer — e.g., whether the student distinguishes detailed imagining from conceiving, whether they explain what work conceivability could still do if it isn't proof, whether they connect it back to why arguments like the zombie argument for dualism live or die on this.

Please act as an expert philosopher of mind who's also an experienced exam designer. Think carefully, and brainstorm two or three candidate questions before settling — show me the rejected ones briefly and tell me why you ditched them.
