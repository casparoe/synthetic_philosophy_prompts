Here was the exam question:

Does that second point tell against utilitarianism specifically, or against impartial moral theories more broadly?"

And here's the student's answer, which struck me as pretty good on a first read, but something nags at me:

"Williams's integrity objection is developed in his debate with Smart. Jim is a botanist travelling in South America who comes across a military captain, Pedro, about to execute twenty captured Indians as punishment for recent protests. Pedro offers Jim a 'guest's privilege': if Jim shoots one of the prisoners himself, the other nineteen go free; if he refuses, all twenty are shot. Utilitarianism clearly says Jim must shoot, since one death is far better than twenty. Williams argues this demand assaults Jim's integrity: the utilitarian calculus treats Jim's own deepest convictions against killing as merely one psychological input to be weighed against others, and so alienates him from the actions and commitments that constitute who he is. Williams concludes that Jim should refuse to shoot, and that this shows utilitarianism simply gives the wrong answer in such cases.

The wife example works like this. Williams imagines a man who can rescue only one of two people in danger, one being his wife. If the man's motivating thought, fully spelled out, runs 'she's my wife, and in situations like this morality permits me to save my wife,' then he has what Williams calls one thought too many. Williams's point is that the man shouldn't be deliberating at all in such a moment — the very fact that a thought crosses his mind before he acts shows that impersonal morality has already corrupted his response, and what love requires is that he act instantly and unthinkingly. This objection tells against utilitarianism specifically, because utilitarianism uniquely demands that partiality toward one's loved ones be justified from an impersonal standpoint, whereas a Kantian agent who acts from duty toward a particular person faces no comparable problem."
