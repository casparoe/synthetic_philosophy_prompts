I've drafted a short essay for an online philosophy forum and I'd like your comments before I post it. Here is the essay:

The standard definition of corruption, the abuse of entrusted power for private gain, is usually criticized for being too Western: it presupposes a sharp line between public office and private interest that many societies draw differently or not at all. I think this criticism misfires, because the definition is flawed in a deeper way that has nothing to do with cultural difference. Consider the police officer who waives a friend's fine, openly, in a village where everyone regards helping kin as a duty, and compare her with the officer who quietly sells waivers to strangers. The standard definition either condemns both (and so condemns practices the first officer's whole community endorses, which is what invites the charge of parochialism) or lets us relativize "abuse" to local norms (and so lets the second officer off wherever tolerance is high enough). I want to propose a different criterion: an act is corrupt when the benefit it generates depends on concealment from the people to whom the discretion-holder is accountable. Corrupt transactions are ones that could not survive being known by the principal. On this test, the first officer's act is not corrupt, however much it offends an auditor's instincts, because it can bear publicity; it belongs to the category of reciprocal obligation, not corruption. The second officer's act is corrupt in any culture, because its value evaporates the moment it is disclosed. Gift-giving is likewise cleanly separated: a gift that creates a publicly understood obligation is a different social technology from a payment that must be hidden. Clientelism is the interesting borderline case. Patronage networks are often semi-public, and I argue that they become corrupt precisely at the point where the patron must conceal the quid pro quo from the wider public whose taxes fund the favors. This also yields a more defensible universality thesis than the usual one. Every society distinguishes dealings that can bear publicity from dealings that cannot; what varies is only where the boundary of accountability is drawn, to family, to village, to state. Western anti-corruption standards are therefore not wrong in substance but in application: they assume one particular answer to the question of who the principal is. The obvious objection is lobbying and campaign finance, which are legal and often fully disclosed, yet which many people call corruption. I bite the bullet here: disclosed influence is a real pathology but a different one, and calling it corruption borrows rhetorical force from an intuition that applies only to concealment.

I'd like comments of three kinds. First, assess the concealment criterion itself: are there acts that are plainly corrupt despite being done openly, or plainly innocent despite requiring concealment from a principal, and does the criterion collapse if "could not survive being known" is read too literally? Second, check the dialectic: does the essay's criticism of the standard definition actually motivate the new criterion, and does the universality thesis in the final paragraph follow from the criterion or is it an independent claim bolted on? Third, tell me honestly whether the clientelism passage and the bullet-biting on lobbying are the strongest or weakest parts, and where you would cut. Please give your comments in continuous prose, the way you'd write an email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
