At an alignment workshop last month I proposed vetting a model's outputs by reading its chain of thought before trusting the final answer, and a senior colleague waved the whole idea off with a slogan: "the rationale is not the reason." Several people nodded; nobody said what it meant, and I suspect different people heard different things in it. I want to know what it could precisely mean.

Lay out the distinct theses this one-liner might be expressing, ordered from the near-trivial to the genuinely radical, with a verdict on each: platitude, substantive and defensible, or false. At minimum, please discriminate among the following claims, and add any I have missed: that the generated reasoning text is a post-hoc rationalization with no causal role in producing the answer; that it plays some causal role but omits factors that actually determined the output (a subtle cue in the prompt, say, or a bias instilled during training); that it is causally complete but framed so as to mislead a human reader about what mattered; and that a model is in principle incapable of reporting on its own computation, so the text can at best correlate with, never describe, the real process. For each thesis, say what evidence could distinguish it from the others — an intervention on the reasoning text, an intervention on the input, a consistency test across rephrasings — and say whether it would actually justify my colleague's dismissal of chain-of-thought monitoring or merely qualify how such monitoring should be interpreted. I suspect the slogan is doing rhetorical work that only its strongest reading would license, and I want to know whether that reading is true. Please argue from how these systems are built and trained rather than appealing to particular published results.
