Ok so my grandpa died about a month ago and I've been messing around on his old upright piano, and I ended up writing this little instrumental piece - no lyrics, no words at all, just chords and a melody. Everyone I've played it for says it sounds absolutely heartbreaking, even people who don't know the backstory. Which got me thinking: how can a bunch of sounds *be* sad? There's no person in the music, nothing sad is happening in it, it's literally just air vibrating.

Write a dialogue between two friends arguing about this. One of them defends the view that the sadness is genuinely *in* the music: the piece somehow resembles or mirrors the way sadness presents itself in people (the slow tempo, the drooping melody, the low register - a bit like how you can hear sadness in someone's voice or see it in their posture), so perceiving sad music is like perceiving an expression. The other one thinks that's confused: sounds can't resemble feelings because feelings aren't visible or audible things at all, and really when we call music "sad" we're talking about what it *does* to us - it stirs up something sad-ish in the listener, so the sadness is a reaction we project onto the sounds rather than a property of them.

Make sure they actually respond to each other's points instead of giving parallel monolgues. Have the resemblance person deal with the objection that the same piece played at double speed or in a different cultural tradition might not sound sad at all, so maybe the "resemblance" is just learned convention. And have the arousal person deal with two problems: first, listeners are often totally calm and even enjoy sad music while still insisting it's sad (so the description doesn't track what they feel), and second, if it's all about the listener's reaction, then it seems like no one could ever be *wrong* about a piece being sad, which seems off.

Both characters should be equally sharp, and it's fine if the dialogue ends without a winner - but have each of them state at the end what they'd need to see to change their mind.
