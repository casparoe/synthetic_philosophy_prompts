So two of my grad students have been at it all semester and I'm tired of refereeing. One is a convinced abolitionist in the David Pearce / hedonistic imperative mold: suffering is an engineering problem, and once biotechnology (genetic recalibration of hedonic set-points, better drugs, eventually rewriting the brain's reward architecture) lets us phase out aversive experience entirely, we are morally required to do it, for humans and for all sentient animals. The other thinks this is a kind of cheerful catastrophe: she doesn't defend agony, but she thinks negative affect carries information, structure, and motivation that a world of "gradients of bliss" would lose, and that the abolitionist's confidence rests on treating a contested theory of value as settled science.

I told them no more seminar-room sniping; they have to produce a joint statement in the style of an adversarial collaboration. Write the document they should hand me.

What I want in it:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign. I suspect these include things like: extreme suffering is extremely bad and reducing it is urgently valuable; present-day neuroscience doesn't yet deliver anything like the abolitionist program; some interventions (better pain treatment, ending factory farming) are overdetermined by both views. But don't just take my guesses -- figure out what they'd actually agree on, including about what each side's strongest argument is.

2. A table of the residual disagreements, each stated in wording the relevant side would accept as a fair statement of its own position. No strawmen on either row.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move one of them. Here are three I expect to show up, though they may reframe or add to them: (a) whether suffering is bad only because of how it feels, or also because of what it does (alerting, teaching, motivating), and what follows if the functions can be offloaded onto non-aversive signals; (b) the wireheading worry -- whether the abolitionist's talk of information-sensitive gradients of bliss genuinely escapes the objection that hedonistic engineering collapses into blissed-out stupor, or just rebrands it; (c) consent and future people -- whether phasing out suffering by germline or otherwise involuntary means can be justified toward people who will never miss a capacity they never had.

4. For each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. E.g. they might both accept that how patients on certain antidepressants or after certain brain lesions actually fare (motivationally, cognitively) is evidence about (a) and (b) -- so say what each side predicts the evidence shows.

Also: if part of their dispute turns out to be verbal (I have my suspicions about "abolishing suffering" vs "abolishing pain"), identify which part and why.

Format: agreed-claims section first, then the disagreements table, then cruxes with the pre-registered predictions. Last line should be a single sentence starting "Bottom line:" naming the deepest genuine disagreement once the verbal ones are stripped out.
