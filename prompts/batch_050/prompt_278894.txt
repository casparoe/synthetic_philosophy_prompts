I want a thought experiment where Avishai Margalit's ethics of memory and David Rieff's case for forgetting give opposite verdicts about the same commemorative practice, with the disagreement being genuine rather than coming from strawmanning either of them. (Context: I'm trying to figure out whether the Margalit/Rieff tension is a real clash of frameworks or just a difference in which real-world cases each of them happened to be picturing, and a well-built case should help settle that.)

Rough versions of the two positions, in case the details are hazy: Margalit thinks memory obligations belong to ethics rather than morality, meaning they grow out of thick relations -- you owe remembering to your dead parent or your people in a way you don't owe it to strangers, and communities of memory are partly constituted by shared remembering handed down the generations. Rieff thinks collective rememberance is wildly overrated as a good: the never-forget imperative mostly keeps old wounds open, fuels grievance politics, and bequeaths hatreds to people who never suffered the original wrong, so a decent amount of forgetting is often what actually makes living together possible.

Specs for the case:

1. Realistic setting: a town, a family, a veterans' group, a congregation. No amnesia pills, no memory-erasing tech, no thought-experiment gadgets.
2. The remembered events really happened, roughly as remembered. The conflict is over whether to keep ritually remembering, not over the facts -- if the memory were false the case would be boring and both sides would just agree to drop it.
3. The people keeping the memory have to stand in genuine thick relations to the people remembered (kids and grandkids of survivors, say) -- not professional grievance merchants with no personal stake, because that makes Margalit's verdict too easy to dodge.
4. The harms of continuing to remember must be concrete and foreseeable, and land mostly on people who had nothing to do with the original events (the next generation, the neighboring group that's since intermarried, whatever). The costs of stopping must be concrete too. Neither side should obviously win on net consequences, because then the frameworks aren't what's doing the diverging.
5. The scenario itself in one or two paragraphs, and don't just stipulate that each theory gives its verdict -- the verdicts have to fall out of the setups.

After the scenario, the demonstration:

- Each view's verdict, with the reasoning run from inside that view -- which feature of your case Margalit is latching onto and which feature Rieff is latching onto.
- Which single feature is doing the work of forcing the split, and what happens if you remove it: do the two views collapse back into agreement, and in whose favor?
- Then the counterattacks. How would a Margalitian argue the case doesn't actually generate a duty of memory (or that his framework already lets the consequences override it), and how would a Rieffian argue this particular remembrance isn't really the harmful grievance-stoking kind he's targeting? Do either of those attacks work against your case as built?
