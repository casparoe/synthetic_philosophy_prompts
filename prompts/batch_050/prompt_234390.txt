I am a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, where teleportation, uploading, and fission are often treated as the central cases in debates about personal identity. I am now grading an undergraduate answer that focuses instead on Locke and Reid. Please act as a careful philosophy grader, not as an advocate for any favored theory of persistence.

Exam question, worth 10 points:

State a broadly Lockean memory criterion of personal identity. Reconstruct Reid’s brave-officer objection as a logical problem for that criterion. Then assess whether replacing direct memory with ancestral psychological continuity solves the problem. Your answer should distinguish a criterion of identity from evidence for identity, explain the role of genuine as opposed to merely apparent memory, and say whether branching or fission introduces an additional difficulty.

Student answer:

A broadly Lockean memory criterion says that a later person is numerically identical with an earlier person just in case the later person can remember, from the first-person point of view, an experience or action of the earlier person. Reid’s brave-officer case puts pressure on the transitivity of identity. A young boy is flogged; as a brave officer, he remembers the flogging; as an elderly general, he remembers having been the officer but no longer remembers the boyhood episode. If direct memory constitutes identity, then the officer is identical with the boy and the general is identical with the officer, but the general is not identical with the boy. Since numerical identity is transitive, the first two claims imply that the general is identical with the boy. The criterion therefore gives inconsistent verdicts.

The standard repair is to replace direct memory with psychological connectedness: a later person is identical with an earlier person if they stand in an overlapping chain of direct memory connections. Because connectedness is transitive, the general is directly connected to the boy through the officer, and Reid’s case no longer produces a contradiction. This revision preserves the transitivity of identity while retaining memory as the basis of personal identity.

The case sometimes motivates a circularity objection: before we can classify a memory as genuine, we must already know whose past experience it was, so memory appears to presuppose personal identity. That objection is weak, however, because genuine memories can be identified independently through their neural traces and reliability. The circularity concerns only our evidence for identity, not the constitution of identity.

Branching creates an additional difficulty. If one person’s memories were duplicated into two later subjects, ancestral continuity alone would relate the original person to both successors. Numerical identity cannot branch in the same way without violating transitivity. The continuity criterion therefore needs a non-branching restriction, a closest-continuer rule, or some further account of why one branch has priority. This does not undermine the response to Reid, but it shows that replacing direct memory with a chain of memories does not settle every problem for psychological theories.
