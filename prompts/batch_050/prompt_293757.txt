I am a retired condensed matter physicist who has, in the last few years, acquired a serious amateur interest in the philosophy of biology. I have been reading around the "adaptationism debate," and in particular the spandrels critique that Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin published in 1979 — the argument, as I understand it, that evolutionary biologists too readily explain each trait of an organism as the product of selection for that trait, neglecting drift, developmental and architectural constraints, correlated growth, and features that arise as by-products and are only later put to use. The architectural metaphor itself I find lovely: a spandrel is the tapering triangular surface one gets necessarily, and not by design, when a dome is set upon rounded arches.

Here is my difficulty. My native conceptual vocabulary is that of variational principles, constrained optimization, energy landscapes with flat directions, symmetry and conservation laws, and the distinction between what a Hamiltonian explains and what boundary conditions explain. I would like you to act as a philosopher of biology who is also genuinely at home in that vocabulary, and to restate the spandrels critique — both its positive catalogue of alternatives to adaptationist explanation and its methodological complaint that adaptive "just-so stories" are unfalsifiable — in my terms, as faithfully as you can. Then do the same for the standard adaptationist rejoinder (that the critique attacks a straw man and that testing adaptive hypotheses is simply sound method).

Take your time and think carefully: begin by brainstorming candidate correspondences between the two vocabularies (for instance, is a spandrel a constraint-determined feature of configuration space? is neutral drift diffusion along a flat direction of the fitness landscape? is the just-so-story objection a claim about underdetermination of the objective function by the data?) before settling on your translation.

The part I care most about is the residue report. Tell me which claims of the critique translate cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and which — if any — cannot be expressed in the optimization vocabulary at all. For whatever does not survive, give me your judgment of whether the failure exposes a poverty in my physicist's framework or instead reveals that the original biological claim was less precise than it appeared. I would also like your assessment of whether the translation, once made, quietly favors one side of the dispute.

Close with a last line beginning "Bottom line:" followed by a single sentence naming the most important claim, from either side, that does not survive translation.
