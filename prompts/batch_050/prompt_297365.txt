Okay so I'm a science/ideas journalist working on a piece about whether economics really deserves to be called a science, and I keep running into the same wall. Back in the 1930s Lionel Robbins apparently showed that comparing utility between people is impossible — you can't meaningfully say my gain from getting a coffee is bigger or smaller than your loss from spilling yours, because there's no common scale and no way to ever measure it. Here's where I'm stuck though: if that's right, doesn't all of welfare economics just fall apart? When a government runs a cost-benefit analysis on a new highway, when economists say trade deals raise total welfare, when anyone talks about maximizing social wellbeing full stop — aren't they all quietly assuming exactly the kind of interpersonal comparison that was shown to be impossible? A couple of people I've interviewed said the Pareto criterion sidesteps the problem, but I honestly don't see how, since any real policy has losers, and "nobody is worse off" never actually applies. So should my article basically say that wellfare economics rests on a known fallacy that everyone has agreed to politely ignore, or am I missing something about what the impossibility claim actually rules out? Give me your take as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up — I'm going to structure the piece around them.
