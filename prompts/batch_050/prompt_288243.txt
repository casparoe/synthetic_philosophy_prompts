I'm a university student taking a seminar in political philosophy, and we've just finished a unit on paternalism and the ethics of nudging — Mill's harm principle, the "libertarian paternalism" of the nudge literature, and the standard objections to both. I'm trying to figure out what I actually think, so here are my gut verdicts on a series of cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, lies behind them.

1. The university dining hall puts fruit and salads at eye level and moves chips and desserts to a back corner. Fine — this barely registers as an intervention at all.
2. An employer automatically enrolls new hires in a retirement plan at 5% of salary, with an easy opt-out. Fine, and clearly good for most employees.
3. A state moves its organ donor registry from opt-in to opt-out. Fine, though I feel a faint unease I can't articulate.
4. Heavy excise taxes on cigarettes, with revenue directed to cessation programs. Fine.
5. Graphic warning images on cigarette packs. Fine.
6. Mandatory helmet laws for adult motorcyclists. Fine.
7. A city bans the sale of soda in cups larger than 16 ounces. Not fine — this is where I balk, even though the health rationale is essentially the same as in 4 through 6.
8. A ban on trans fats in restaurant food. Fine — and I realize this sits badly with my verdict in 7, since both are outright prohibitions on unhealthy food options.
9. A surgeon, knowing framing effects, describes an operation as having a 90% survival rate rather than a 10% mortality rate. Fine — arguably obligatory.
10. A casino uses the same framing techniques and loyalty nudges to keep habitual gamblers at the table. Not fine.
11. A friend takes my car keys at a party when I'm clearly drunk, over my protests. Fine, obviously.
12. The same friend, after I'm diagnosed with diabetes, quietly replaces the regular soda in my fridge with diet soda without asking. Not fine — even though she'd have been right about the stakes.

The questions: First, is there a single principle — or a small set of principles — that delivers all twelve verdicts? Some candidate considerations I've noticed in my own reactions: whether the person can easily opt out; whether the intervention is transparent or covert; whether it serves the chooser's own ends or the intervener's; whether the burden it imposes is trivial or blocks an entire ordinary choice; and whether the targeted feature of the situation is something the chooser could even identify on their own (trans fats are invisible in a way a giant soda cup is not). I'd like the principle stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it.

Second, whichever principle you settle on: what does it predict for cases I haven't listed? I'm especially curious about (a) mandatory calorie counts on restaurant menus, (b) a national tax on red meat, (c) defaulting patients onto generic drugs unless they actively object, and (d) an employer auto-enrolling staff in a wellness program whose participation data is visible to HR.

Third, and most importantly: if no plausible principle captures all twelve, tell me honestly which verdict(s) I should give up, and why that revision is cheaper than the complexity needed to save it. I'd rather be shown where my intuitions are inconsistent than be handed an ad hoc principle that flatters them.
