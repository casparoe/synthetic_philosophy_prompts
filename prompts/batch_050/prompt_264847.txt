So I'm putting together a syllabus for a "game theory at the movies" night class and the centerpiece is Dr. Strangelove. I want to read it through the lens of commitment devices and, more specifically, commitment races. Quick gloss of what I mean by that since the term gets used loosely: a commitment device is when you bind yourself now to restrict your future options (Cortés burning his ships, Odysseus tying himself to the mast), and a commitment race is the adversarial version where each side gains by visibly and irreversibly committing first -- in chicken, if I throw my steering wheel out the window before you do, you have to swerve. The nightmare case is both sides racing to lock into incompatible demands, so the individually smart move guarantees mutual disaster. My students will have done the standard Schelling-style treatment where commitment is modeled as removing your own options.

Read the film against this. The doomsday machine looks like the perfect commitment device -- automatic, no human in the loop, can't be begged or bribed -- and the film has Strangelove himself point out why it fails: a deterrent commitment is worthless if the other side doesn't know it exists. So the Soviets ran the race and skipped the publicity leg. Meanwhile Ripper runs his own one-man commitment race by sealing the base, monopolizing the recall code, and issuing the attack order, and the horror is that his commitment outlives him -- Mandrake can piece the code together, but one bomber's radio is shot up and the device does its job anyway. And in the War Room nobody can stop racing: Turgidson wants to commit to a full first strike while the opportunity is there, and the film closes on the mineshaft gap, i.e., precommitting to the next arms race before this one has even finished ending the world.

What I want from you: does the film actually claim commitment devices fail only when they're secret, or is there a deeper claim that in any real commitment race there's always some human who can't commit credibly, or can't stop committing? Is Ripper the counterexample to the whole logic -- the one man whose commitment device works flawlessly, and that's exactly the catastrophe? And what does the film add that the formal models don't -- my sense is it thinks the real danger isn't commitment per se but commitment entangled with accident, bureaucracy, damaged radios, and male ego, which the models abstract away. But don't flatten the movie into a lecture: where does it resist this reading? One of my sharper students is definately going to argue the doomsday machine isn't a commitment device at all but just a booby trap, since deterrence was never its actual function once built -- tell me whether that objection lands, and if it does, what it does to the rest of the reading.
