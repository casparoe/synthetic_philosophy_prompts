I have been keeping a record of my intuitive verdicts about when flat-out assertion is proper, and I would like to know whether any single norm of assertion stands behind them, or whether I am secretly committed to several. Here are the cases and my reactions.

First, the easy ones. When I assert something I know outright -- say, that my office keys are in my coat pocket, having just checked -- the assertion is unimpeachable. When someone asserts a true proposition on the basis of a coin flip, or because a horoscope said so, the assertion strikes me as improper even though it happens to be true. So mere truth is not sufficient, on my view. And when someone asserts "p, but I don't know whether p" -- for instance, "it will rain tomorrow, but I don't know whether it will" -- the utterance feels defective in a way that goes beyond awkwardness, even when the first conjunct turns out true.

Now the harder ones. Consider a meticulous scientist who asserts that her samples are uncontaminated, on the basis of careful, state-of-the-art testing, when in fact an unforeseeable equipment fault has contaminated them. Her assertion is false, yet I cannot bring myself to call it improper; she asserted exactly as she should have. That verdict seems to favor a justification or reasonable-belief norm. But consider the lottery case: holding a ticket in a million-ticket lottery, having read the odds but before hearing the results, I judge that it would be improper for me to assert flat-out, "my ticket didn't win." Yet my justification here is far stronger than the scientist's -- a probability of 0.999999, higher than the reliability of her testing equipment. If justification suffices, I should be permitted to assert; but I am not, or so my intuition says.

A further case pulls in a third direction. Suppose I assert something on excellent evidence, and later learn it was false. I feel I am now obliged to retract it -- not merely to stop asserting it, but to correct the record. That obligation seems to track the truth of what I said, not the quality of my evidence, which points back toward a truth norm and sits uneasily with my verdict on the scientist.

Two more. When stakes rise sharply -- suppose a friend will bet his house on whether the bank opens Saturday, and I saw it open on a Saturday two months ago -- I become reluctant to assert "the bank is open" without double-checking, even though in a low-stakes version of the conversation I would assert it without hesitation. And yet I do not think the bare fact that stakes are high makes my earlier low-stakes assertion retroactively improper; it seems rather that the standard for proper assertion itself has shifted. Finally: repeating what a trustworthy colleague told me -- "the meeting was moved to three" -- seems proper even though I lack firsthand knowledge, which suggests that whatever the norm demands, testimony can supply it.

My tentative principle was a knowledge norm: assert only what you know. It handles the coin-flip, the lottery, and the Moore-paradoxical case neatly. But it appears to condemn the conscientious scientist, whose assertion I want to excuse, and it says nothing obvious about the retraction obligation, which tracks truth rather than knowledge. So here is what I am asking. First, state the strongest single principle you can find that accommodates as many of these verdicts as possible, precisely enough that each clause can be tied to the case that motivates it -- including whether "excusable violation" versus "no violation at all" is a distinction the principle should build in. Second, tell me plainly which of my verdicts your principle cannot save, and which verdict you think I should surrender if forced, with reasons. Third, apply your resulting account to two cases I did not list: asserting something one knows to be true but knows one's audience will reasonably misunderstand, and asserting, under oath in court, a proposition one justifiably believes but does not know.
