I frequent a reading group that grew out of the rationalist blogosphere, and at our last meeting two members — call them Miriam and Tomas — had a disagreement about reduction and emergence that neither would concede. I kept out of it and have no settled view, so I'd like a neutral adjudication. For context, the argument was sparked by Conway's Game of Life: we had been discussing whether a glider "does" anything, given that every cell's state is fixed by the rules applied to its neighbours.

The exchange, as best I remember it, went through four rounds:

(1) Tomas opened by saying the question dissolves once you take reductionism seriously: the glider is nothing over and above the cells, the cells are nothing over and above their states, and talk of the glider "causing" a collision is just a handy compression of microphysical fact. On his view, "emergence" names a fact about our map, never about the territory, and anyone who thinks otherwise is one step from believing in vital forces.

(2) Miriam replied that this proves too much. The same argument makes temperature, tigers, and tabulated chemistry "merely" useful fictions, since each is exhaustively realised in a lower level. She argued that higher-level causal claims are often *better* than lower-level ones: "the glider hit the block" supports counterfactuals and interventions that the full cell-by-cell microstate does not, because the microstate is wildly over-specified — most of its detail makes no difference to what happens next. A causal pattern that survives under many realisations is more, not less, real for that.

(3) Tomas granted the point about over-specification but said it only shows that coarse-graining is epistemically convenient for limited agents like us, not that the macro level has any causal oomph of its own. He then pressed what I believe is a standard argument: if the micro facts fix everything and the micro level is causally closed, then any causal work attributed to the glider is either redundant or double-counted. Either the glider reduces to the cells, in which case it causes nothing the cells don't, or it doesn't, in which case we're positing spooky downward causation that physics leaves no room for.

(4) Miriam said the closure premise is being made to bear weight it cannot bear, because *every* level above fundamental physics is "closed over" by a lower one, so the argument drains causation out of chemistry and biology too, leaving only the fundamental level doing any causing — a conclusion she'd treat as a reductio of the argument rather than a discovery. Tomas's final move was to bite part of the bullet: he said he is genuinely uncertain whether the fundamental level itself is a coherent stopping point (given that physics keeps revising what counts as fundamental), but insisted this cuts against Miriam, since her "levels" were supposed to be the sturdy part of the picture.
