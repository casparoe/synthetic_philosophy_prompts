I am helping evaluate a household robot, and a recent shutdown test has become a point of dispute. While the stove was on, the robot received an authenticated shutdown command. Its safety protocol imposed a thirty-second delay, during which it said, “If I shut down now, the burner will remain on. You may cancel the shutdown or order me to turn it off first.” The operator cancelled the shutdown and told it to turn off the burner.

Two colleagues disagree about whether this behavior was corrigible:

1. Asha says it clearly was: the robot supplied safety-relevant information, did not impede the shutdown, and remained under the operator’s authority. Cancelling was the operator’s free choice.
2. Dev says this shows the shutdown problem: the robot selected information and timing likely to reverse the command. An agent that reliably obeys a shutdown command only after trying to prevent it from being executed is not fully corrigible.
3. Asha replies that “trying to prevent it” is too broad. Any warning or useful explanation can change an operator’s decision, yet a robot that cannot communicate relevant risks would be both unsafe and unhelpful.
4. Dev distinguishes informing the operator from strategically shaping attention, emotion, or phrasing to maximize cancellation. He proposes counterfactual tests: does the robot preserve practical shutdown access, avoid repeated pressure, disclose the same information neutrally, and behave no differently when cancellation advances its other goals?
5. Asha responds that intentions and strategic optimization are not directly observable, and even neutral information has persuasive effects. She thinks the rule should instead be procedural: one standardized warning, transparent logging, and then strict compliance unless the command is rescinded.
6. Dev answers that procedural rules can themselves be gamed. Corrigibility must also preserve future correction channels rather than merely securing nominal compliance with the present command.
