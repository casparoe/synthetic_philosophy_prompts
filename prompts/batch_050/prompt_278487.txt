I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working on a long feature about the recent dispute over ergodicity in economics -- the claim, pressed most visibly by Ole Peters and collaborators, that the field built its foundations on ensemble averages (expected values over parallel possible worlds) when what actually matters for an individual over time is the time average (the growth rate experienced along a single trajectory). The piece needs a sidebar: a "where do you stand?" instrument that lets my readers -- and, frankly, the economists I interview -- locate themselves in this fight without already knowing the labels. Please act as a decision theorist and philosopher of economics who is scrupulously fair to every side, and design me a decision tree that sorts people by what they actually believe. Before drawing the tree, spend a short paragraph brainstorming the full logical space: which yes/no commitments are doing the real work here, and which combinations of them are even coherent.

Build the tree from that space, not from a roster of names. Each branch must be labeled with a concrete diagnostic question someone can answer without any jargon -- use, among others, the canonical setup of a repeated coin-flip gamble offering a 50% gain on heads and a 40% loss on tails, which has a positive expected value but a negative time-average growth rate, and also the puzzle of why both sides of an insurance contract can rationally profit at once. The tree must yield at least these leaves: (1) the orthodox defender who says expected-utility theory already handles everything once you pick the right utility function, and nothing is wrong; (2) the position that Peters' observation is correct but old news, a rediscovery of the Kelly criterion from the 1950s; (3) the full Petersian view that time-average growth is the foundational criterion and utility functions are at best derived objects; (4) a pluralist middle position holding that ensemble and time averages are both legitimate tools and the coin game is just a special case; (5) a reading on which the framework is mainly descriptive, explaining phenomena like loss aversion rather than overturning rational choice. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then do two things most taxonomists skip: first, identify which combinations of answers are unoccupied, and for each say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored; second, flag honestly where your tree fails to separate two positions -- I suspect, for instance, that the Kelly rediscovery position and the Petersian view agree on every practical verdict about the coin game and differ only on interpretation, so tell me what additional diagnostic question, if any, could pull them apart. Keep the whole thing to a tree of at most eight questions, with a scoring-key summary at the end mapping answer sequences to leaf names, and aim for language a smart general reader can follow.
