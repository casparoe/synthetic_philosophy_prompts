I'm a novelist midway through a book in which the narrator, an archivist losing his memory, comes to inhabit something very close to what I take to be Augustine's account of time in Book XI of the Confessions: that the past and future do not exist at all, that there are really three times only in a manner of speaking — a present of things past, a present of things present, and a present of things future, which are memory, attention, and expectation — and that time itself is a distension of the mind rather than something out in the world that the mind happens to register. I've grown attached to this reading, both philosophically and as the spine of the novel, which makes me suspicious of myself. What I want is a map of the view's exposure: what would actually count against it?

I'd like the answer on two levels, kept distinct. First, as an interpretation of Augustine: which textual features of Book XI itself (the measurement of syllables, the eternity-and-creation material, the prayer that opens the book) sit most awkwardly with the pure 'presentism of the soul' reading, and how seriously should I take the rival suggestion that Augustine's real subject is the created world's relation to eternity rather than the phenomenology of time-consciousness? Second, as a free-standing philosophy of time: which phenomena would be genuine defeaters — the physics of relativity and the relativity of simultaneity, cases of memory that seem to reach past events rather than present impressions, the intuition that the past is fixed in a way the future is not — and which of these would a committed defender of the Augustinian view concede as damaging, versus dismiss as merely changing the subject from experienced time to physical time? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely inconvenient, and say whether the view, suitably qualified, risks becoming unfalsifiable — and if so, whether that is a vice for what may be a phenomenological claim rather than an empirical one.

Two requests about form. Please write in British English throughout. And since I gather that scholarship on Confessions XI is genuinely divided — on how much Augustine owes to Plotinus, on whether distentio animi should be read as 'distension' or 'attention', on whether the account is reductive or merely descriptive — please separate clearly what is settled from what remains contested among people who know the text, and do not present a contested reading as the scholarly consensus. If the honest answer is that my narrator's philosophy rests on a disputed interpretation, I'd rather know before the book comes out.
