Next month I am giving a thirty-minute colloquium paper in a philosophy faculty, followed by an hour of questions, and I want a proper Q&A prep sheet. My paper argues that the eleventh book of Augustine's Confessions is systematically misread when it is treated as a treatise in the metaphysics of time — as though Augustine were an unwitting contestant in the presentism-versus-eternalism debate, or an anticipation of McTaggart. My claim is that the celebrated analysis of time is a confessional exercise from first to last: Augustine's puzzle about what time is arises inside, and is governed by, a prior question about how a creature scattered across time ought to spend it. My textual anchor is a passage near the start of Book XI (chapter 2), in E. B. Pusey's public-domain translation, well before the famous 'what, then, is time?' discussion begins: "And if I suffice to utter these things in order, the drops of time are dear to me. Long time have I burned to meditate in Your law, and in it to confess to You my knowledge and ignorance, the beginning of Your enlightening, and the remains of my darkness, until infirmity be swallowed up by strength." My paper leans hard on the fact that Augustine pronounces time precious — rationing his hours against competing duties of body, mind and office — before he finds it puzzling. The metaphysical aporiai, I argue, are generated by a man anxiously auditing his time, and the book closes not with a definition of time but with an image of the soul stretched thin and gathered up, which is the answer to the auditor's question rather than to the metaphysician's.

The argument runs in roughly four moves: first, the framing chapters show that the inquiry's stakes are practical and devotional, not theoretical; second, the 'what is time?' puzzles function within the confession as a demonstration of the mind's distension rather than as steps towards a theory; third, the measurement discussion — time measured in the mind — is the pivot at which modern readers most egregiously detach Augustine from his frame, reading a phenomenological observation as a metaphysical thesis; fourth, the closing movement gathers the whole inquiry back into the question of how a temporal being relates to an eternal God. I am not claiming Augustine has no views with metaphysical consequences; I am claiming that mining Book XI for a position on the A-series is a genre mistake, like reading a diary for its meteorology.

The audience will be a mixed philosophy faculty colloquium, and it is the mixture that worries me. Roughly half will be analytic metaphysicians, several of whom work on the philosophy of time and have views about presentism, the passage of time, and McTaggart; there will be two or three historians of philosophy, including one genuine Augustine specialist who works in Latin; a philosopher of religion who suspects secularising readings of Augustine of being anachronistic in the opposite direction; and a scattering of graduate students who have read Book XI once, in translation, for a seminar. Nobody in the room is hostile, but the metaphysicians will suspect I am draining the text of philosophical interest, and the Augustine specialist will know the text far better than I do.

Please prepare my prep sheet, in British English, of roughly 1,200–1,500 words (the quoted passage does not count towards this). I want:

1. The eight or so objections this specific audience will actually raise — phrased the way each constituency would phrase them, so that I can hear the metaphysician's idiom, the philologist's, and the philosopher of religion's as distinct voices — ordered by a combination of how likely each is and how much damage it would do if I answer it badly.
2. For each objection, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, followed by a candid note on where that reply is weakest and what I should do if the questioner presses the weak point.

4. The single reply that has to land cleanly if the paper is to survive the hour — identify which objection it answers and why the whole reading depends on it.
5. The question I am hoping nobody asks, with the best available answer to it anyway.
6. Advice on one foreseeable technical moment: the Augustine specialist, or a classicist, may challenge my reliance on Pusey's rendering of the anchor passage and ask what the Latin is and whether 'drops of time' is faithful. I do not read Latin well. Tell me how to handle that without either bluffing or capitulating.

Two constraints: do not pad the sheet with objections I would get from a different room (pure theologians, physicists, continental phenomenologists are not the worry here), and do not invent quotations from Augustine beyond the passage above — paraphrase anything else you need.
