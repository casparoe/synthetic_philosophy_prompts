So I just spent about three hours in the pub arguing with a mate of mine who's doing a PhD in decision theory, and I need someone to either vindicate me or tell me where I'm exposed, because honestly I can't tell anymore.

The argument was about money pumps. His line was the standard one: if your preferences are intransitive (you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A), then someone can trade you round in a circle, charging you a little each time, until you're back where you started minus money — therefore intransitive preferences are irrational, full stop, and the transitivity axiom is just what rationality is. My position, which I ended up defending more dogmatically than I maybe should have, is roughly this: being money-pumpable is a *practical liability*, not a failure of rationality in the strict sense. Being exploitable by a cunning trader shows something's wrong with you the way being easy to beat at chess shows something's wrong with you, but neither shows you hold contradictory attitudes. On top of that, I reckon the argument smuggles in a bunch of premises nobody defends explicitly: that the agent accepts every individually favourable trade, that she can't foresee the whole sequence and adopt a policy of refusing, that the trades are costless, that she never just gets fed up and walks away. A sophisticated agent with intransitive preferences who knows her own dispositions could surely plan around them — which suggests to me that whatever irrationality there is lives in the *pattern of choices over time*, not in the preferences themselves, and maybe diachronic requirements like "don't get pumped" reduce to being synchonically rational at each moment plus having decent self-knowledge.

What I want to know is: what would actually count against my view? Not just my mate restating the pump slower and louder, but which arguments, cases, or results should genuinely move me. For instance: is there a version of the pump argument that works against agents who *can* see the whole sequence coming? Does the "it's merely a practical flaw" line prove too much — would it also excuse violations of dominance, which even I want to call irrational, and if so is there a principled line between the two? Are there cases where the intransitive agent's escape routes (policies, resoluteness, self-binding) themselves cost something, so that she pays a tax the transitive agent doesn't, and does that re-establish the conclusion by the back door? And on the other side: what would defenders of *my* position themselves concede as damaging, versus what they'd bat away as question-begging? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether my view has quietly been qualified into something that can't lose — if "irrationality is always really diachronic and situational" turns out to be compatible with every possible pattern of choices, tell me, because that's the sort of thing my mate will say next time and I'd rather hear it from you first.
