I'm TAing an intro course on 19th century political thought and grading short-answer responses. The exam question was:

In the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844 ("Estranged Labour"), Marx writes: "Political economy starts with the fact of private property; it does not explain it to us. It expresses in general, abstract formulas the material process through which private property actually passes, and these formulas it then takes for laws. It does not comprehend these laws – i.e., it does not demonstrate how they arise from the very nature of private property. Political economy throws no light on the cause of the division between labor and capital, and between capital and land. When, for example, it defines the relationship of wages to profit, it takes the interest of the capitalists to be the ultimate cause, i.e., it takes for granted what it is supposed to explain." What exactly is Marx's methodological complaint against political economy here, and how does his own account of estranged labour in the manuscript try to do better?

Here's one student's answer:

"Marx's complaint is that Smith, Ricardo etc. treat private property as a brute, timeless fact instead of explaining where it comes from, so their 'laws' are really just descriptions of contingent arrangements dressed up as eternal necessities. It's like explaining evil by the fall of man -- assuming as a fact what needs to be explained. Marx's alternative is his theory of alienation: he shows that private property is the root cause of the worker's estrangement -- because the capitalist owns the means of production, the worker is separated from the product, from the activity of labour, from his fellow workers, and from what Marx here calls commodity fetishism, his 'species-being' (a concept he takes over from Hegel). So instead of presupposing private property, Marx explains alienation through it, which political economy failed to do."
