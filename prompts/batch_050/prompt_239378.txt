I retired three years ago after four decades doing experimental physics, mostly instruments and control systems, and lately I've fallen down the philosophy-and-politics rabbit hole the way a lot of retired scientists seem to. Here's where I'm stuck. In a discussion group I'm in, someone defended presidential systems (roughly the American sort: separately elected executive, fixed terms, legislature that can't just fire the boss) with an analogy I recognized from my own field: a good constitution is like a well-designed fault-tolerant machine. Separation of powers is redundancy -- independent components so one failure doesn't take down the whole system. Checks and balances are interlocks and circuit breakers. Fixed terms are like scheduled maintenance intervals. The parliamentary alternative, where the executive is just a committee of the legislature and falls the moment it loses a confidence vote, is supposedly like a tightly coupled machine: efficient when all parts are aligned, but one jammed gear and the whole thing seizes or you get constant crashes and restarts (Italy was mentioned, someone had the number of postwar governments handy). The conclusion being pushed was that presidentialism is therefore the more stable, failure-resistant design, full stop.

My engineer's gut likes the analogy but my physicist's gut distrusts it. Can you audit it properly? Give me the explicit mapping -- what plays the role of the redundant components, the interlock, the coupling, the load, the operator -- and then where it breaks. Some disanalogies I've half-noticed myself: the machine's parts don't each want to be in charge, the machine's designer isn't one of the parts, and machines don't have norms that everyone quietly agrees to follow until one day they don't. But I'm not sure which of these actually matter for the stability conclusion and which are just pedantic. In particular: does fault-tolerance engineering even predict that redundant independent components are more stable, when the components can actively work against each other (in my old line of work redundant sensors that disagree are a solved problem only because they can't scheme)? And does the analogy smuggle in the assumption that gridlock is a failure mode to be prevented rather than, sometimes, the system working as intended? Finally: if the machine analogy can't carry the pro-presidential conclusion, is there a better analogy lurking nearby -- maybe from a different field entirely -- that captures whatever real difference there is between the two system types, or should we just drop analogy-talk here and argue the question directly? A correspondence table would be great, plus a verdict on what the analogy can and cannot support.
