I run a small interdisciplinary reading group on AI safety (philosophers plus a couple of ML postdocs who humour us), and I've just been put on the advisory board of a modest research fund -- enough to support about four philosophy projects of two years each on the conceptual foundations of AI control, i.e. safety measures that are supposed to work even if the model is scheming against you, as opposed to alignment work that tries to make the model not want to scheme in the first place.

Here's my problem. When I look at what philosophers are actually writing in this space, most of it is either rehearsing the control/alignment dichotomy at a very high level or doing armchair versions of things the labs already do better empirically. I want to spend the fund on questions where philosophical methods genuinely have an edge. Help me set the agenda.

Give me the five or six open conceptual questions you'd rank highest, in order, and defend the ordering. Some angles I've been chewing on, which you can engage with or push back on: whether "we never caught it attempting anything" can ever constitute evidence of alignment rather than evidence of competence (and what a principled answer would even look like); what the epistemology of a *successful* control regime is, given that success means you never get the feedback signal that would tell you it was needed; whether principal-agent theory and the old literature on deterrance actually transfer or just gesture; and whether the whole control-vs-alignment framing is itself confused, since most real protocols seem to assume partial alignment in the monitoring machinery.

For each item: why it's upstream of other work, why it's tractable for philosophers specifically rather than something empirical ML will settle anyway, and how neglected it is. Also tell me which popular question in this area you think is overrated and would get cut from my list, and what development over the next two years -- a result, an incident, a technique -- would most change your ranking. I'm less interested in the list than in the criteria and the cruxes underneath it.
