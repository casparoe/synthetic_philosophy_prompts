You are a poet with professional training in game theory and behavioral economics, equally at home in the auction house and the sonnet. I want you to write a villanelle about the dollar auction: the game in which a single dollar bill is auctioned off under the rule that both the highest bidder and the second-highest bidder must pay what they bid, though only the winner receives the dollar. The game's infamous logic is that once two people have bid, the second-highest bidder always has a reason to bid again rather than concede a certain loss, so the price can climb past one dollar and both players can end up losing money to avoid losing more.

Before writing, brainstorm briefly (three or four bullet points): sketch which moments in a typical escalating auction correspond to which stanzas of the villanelle, and how the form's machinery can do philosophical work. Then write the poem, following these constraints:

- Standard villanelle: nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, two refrains and two rhymes.
- The two refrains should voice the two competing reasons a bidder tells themselves -- something like the pull of the prize on one side and the fear of the sunk loss on the other -- so that their relentless alternation across the stanzas, and their uneasy union in the final couplet, enact the trap itself rather than merely describe it.
- Let the implied bid amounts rise across the poem, and make the volta-like moment where rational play tips into mutual ruin recognizable to anyone who knows the game: the point where winning and escaping can no longer be told apart.
- It should still work as a poem for a reader who has never heard of the game.

After the poem, add a short note of three or four sentences explaining your formal choices.
