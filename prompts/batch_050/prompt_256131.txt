I spent forty years in public health (epidemiology, mostly infectious disease) and since retiring I've been reading my way into the free will debate. I've got to the sceptics -- Caruso and the like -- who say nobody basically deserves punishment, and I've noticed the argument keeps leaning on an analogy from my own old field: the idea is that treating offenders should be modelled on quarantine. Just as we can restrict a carrier of a dangerous pathogen without thinking they *deserve* it, purely to protect others and with a duty to make the restrictions as mild as possible, so (the thought goes) we can detain dangerous offenders without any notion of just deserts. This is doing real work: it's meant to show that dropping desert doesn't leave us with nothing, that a humane non-desert-based system is already familiar from medicine.

I'd like a proper audit of how far this actually holds, and -- since I still think in British journals -- please answer in British English.

Concretely: give me the correspondence table first. What plays the role of the pathogen, the carrier, the exposed public, the incubation period, the least-restrictive-means principle, the duty to compensate the quarantined for a burden they didn't choose? Then the places where the mapping strains or breaks. Some that nag at me as an old hand: quarantine targets a condition, not a deed, and nobody asks whether the carrier *intended* anything -- but criminal justice seems to need mental-state judgements even without desert (you handle the accidental poisoner differently from the deliberate one), so has the desert really been removed or just relocated? Quarantine is usually short and ends when the infectiousness does, whereas criminal risk doesn't come with a test that tells you it's cleared. And victims: nobody feels wronged *by* a virus in a way that calls for acknowledgement, so does the analogy quietly delete the victim's claim rather than answer it? There will be others you've spotted that I haven't.

For each disanalogy, say whether it actually matters for the conclusion the analogy is carrying (that a desert-free system is workable and humane) or whether it's just cosmetic. And finally: if the quarantine picture turns out to be doing more rhetorical than argumentative work, is there a better model from medicine or elsewhere that does the job more honestly -- or does the sceptic's case just have to be argued without borrowed stethoscopes? Be frank; I've had my analogies savaged by referees before and I can take it.
