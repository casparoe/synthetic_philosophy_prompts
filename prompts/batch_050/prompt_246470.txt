His summary reads as follows: "The famous Blue Bus problem teaches that courts, wisely or not, refuse to ground findings of fact in bare statistics. When a plaintiff is sideswiped by a bus on a road where the Blue Bus Company operates four-fifths of the traffic, proof of that market share alone cannot establish that a Blue Bus vehicle injured her, even though on the numbers it is four times as likely as not that one did. The same logic resolves the gatecrasher paradox: if a thousand people attend the match but only five hundred tickets were sold, the bare probability that any given attendee did not pay cannot justify holding that particular attendee liable. The deeper lesson is that statistical evidence is second-rate evidence, inherently less trustworthy than direct testimony, which is why Anglo-American law insists that civil plaintiffs, like prosecutors, must prove their cases beyond a reasonable doubt rather than merely tipping the balance of probabilities."
