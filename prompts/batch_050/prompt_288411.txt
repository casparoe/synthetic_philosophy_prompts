I'm a data analyst at a mid-size company and I've been going back and forth with a coworker for weeks about when you're allowed to split your data up before drawing a conclusion. He says there are cases where the disaggregated numbers are the real ones and cases where they're a trap; I said I'd write down my gut verdicts on some examples and figure out whether I actually have a consistent view. Some of these are reworked from things I've read, some from our own work, numbers rounded for simplicity. Here they are:

(1) A migraine drug trial. Among mild cases, the drug group recovers at 90% and placebo at 80%. Among severe cases, drug 50%, placebo 20%. But the drug was given mostly to severe patients, so overall the drug group recovers at 63% and placebo at 70%. My verdict: the drug works, the subgroup numbers are the right ones, the overall comparison is garbage because sicker people got the drug.

(2) Exact same trial, exact same table of numbers, but now the split isn't severity at intake -- it's blood pressure measured six weeks in, after treatment. Same percentages, high-BP subgroup and low-BP subgroup. Now my verdict flips: look at the overall number, because if the drug changes your blood pressure that's part of how it works (or hurts you), and slicing on it cuts the effect in half. My coworker says holding both (1) and (2) is incoherent since the tables are identical. I say they're obviously different cases. Neither of us can articulate the rule though.

(3) A hitter has a better batting average than a teammate in both day games and night games, but a worse average overall, because he plays mostly night games where everyone hits worse. My verdict here is a shrug: there's no fact of the matter about which number is "real," it's just arithmetic, it depends what question you're asking. This one feels different in kind from (1) and (2) but I can't say why.

(4) Our hiring data: women were hired at a higher rate than men in both engineering and sales, but at a lower rate company-wide, because they disproportionately applied to engineering where hardly anyone gets hired. My verdict: no evidence of bias against women, the department-level numbers are the ones that matter.

(5) Gym members have more heart attacks than non-members overall, but fewer within every age band, because members skew much older. My coworker says trust the age bands. Here's where I might be inconsistent: I want to trust the overall number, because slicing your data until the result reverses smells like p-hacking to me, and I think if you let people condition on whatever they want they can make anything come out either way. But I notice this sits badly with (1) and (4).

So: is there a principle that captures verdicts (1) through (4)? Please state it precisely enough that it actually decides cases -- what it is about the third variable that tells you whether to condition on it or not -- and flag which clause of the principle is motivated by which case. I expect it will have to overturn my verdict in (5), and I want you to be honest about whether it can really reconcile (1) and (2) or whether one of those has to go too. Finally, tell me what the principle says about a case I'm genuinely unsure about: same drug trial setup, but the third variable is something measured at the same time as treatment started, like a biomarker, and nobody knows yet whether the drug influences it or it influences who got the drug.
