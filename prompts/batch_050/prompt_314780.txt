I'm helping put together the final exam for a third-year course on contemporary moral problems, and the week on individual contributions to collective harms always goes badly. Students memorize Parfit's harmless torturers and the standard line about how one chicken purchase doesn't matter because producers respond in thresholds, then parrot it back without being able to reason about any case that isn't in the readings. I want a question that forces them to actually run the machinery rather than recite it.

Here's the seed I'd like built in, in some form. Imagine a food company that orders chickens from its supplier in batches of 1,000, where the ordering system places a new batch order whenever cumulative purchases cross the next multiple of 1,000 -- but the running counter was seeded at an unknown offset somewhere between 0 and 999 when the company was founded, and nobody can inspect it. So any given purchase has roughly a 1-in-1000 chance of triggering 1,000 more birds being farmed. The thing I want students to wrestle with: the expected harm of one purchase comes out to about one bird's worth of suffering, so expected-value reasoning seems to deliver the "don't buy" verdict -- yet most of the time your purchase causes literally nothing, and occasionally it causes an enormous amount, and it's not obvious that the average settles the moral question. I also want the question to ask how the analysis changes if the offset isn't uniformly distributed (say internal documents show the seeding procedure made offsets near 0 and 999 about twice as likely as middle values), because that seems like it should matter and I'm not sure the students will see why. And a part comparing the consumer case to voting, where the chance of being pivotal shrinks with the size of the electorate but the stakes scale up in a way that arguably cancels out -- is the chicken case structurally the same as voting, or is there an asymmetry?

So: draft one exam question on this material that a strong student could answer in about 45 minutes, worth 20 marks total. It should be impossible to answer well by summarizing the readings -- they have to deploy the inefficacy objection on a case they havent seen. Give a model answer and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth, plus a one-line note on the way students typically fumble each point. Assume they've read the standard fare on this topic (threshold/slack arguments, expected-utility defenses of individual purchases, and at least one non-causal or complicity-style account of why participating in a collective harm can be wrong even when it causes nothing) but nothing fancier than that.
