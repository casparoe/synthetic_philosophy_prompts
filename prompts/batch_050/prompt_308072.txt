I run a small philosophy reading group, and we have been working through Leibniz's Theodicy. At last week's meeting someone read aloud a sentence from one of Leibniz's shorter texts (English translation in The Shorter Leibniz Texts, ed. Lloyd Strickland, p. 193), in which Leibniz writes that "even God discovers that some things are good, or better than others […] for God is endowed with will [and] the will is under the rule of the good". That sentence set off a prolonged argument between two members of the group — I will call them A and B — about whether Leibniz's famous claim that the actual world is the best of all possible worlds is a substantive claim at all. I deliberately stayed out of it, and I now want an outside verdict.

Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. A opened by declaring the thesis empty: if "best" simply means "the world God in fact chose," then no world could fail to be best, and the thesis is a tautology in metaphysical dress, so Voltaire's mockery was fully deserved; and if Leibniz instead has a standard of goodness independent of God's choice, nobody can apply it, since we cannot survey infinitely many possible worlds and compare them — so the claim is either vacuous or uncheckable, and neither is worth defending. B replied that the quoted sentence shows Leibniz grasped the second horn deliberately and rejected the first: God discovers the good rather than inventing it, and his will is ruled by the good rather than legislating it, so "best" is not defined by the choice — the choice is explained by an antecedent standard, and A's "vacuous" reading is a plain misreading. A answered that this relocates the problem rather than solving it: a standard only God can apply gives the rest of us no checkable reason to believe this world is best, so our only ground remains prior trust in God's goodness — the very thing the thesis was supposed to establish — and a substantive claim that can never be verified is, for us, epistemically on a par with a vacuous one. B countered that this conflates a claim's truth-conditions with our access to them (many substantive claims outrun our checking), and that A had quietly retreated from "vacuous" to "unverifiable," a different and weaker charge that in fact concedes the original point. A then pressed what struck me as the strongest move: the sentence cuts against Leibniz himself, for if God's will is ruled by the good, then either the best compels the choice and God could not have done otherwise — collapsing the contingency of creation into something like Spinozistic necessity — or God could have chosen a lesser world, in which case the good does not explain the choice after all. B's final answer was that for Leibniz the best inclines God without necessitating him: the choice is certain but not necessary, and whether that distinction can survive scrutiny is the real question. There the meeting broke up.

Please adjudicate the dispute as actually conducted, under the following headings, in this order: "The actual fault line" (about 120 words — what the disagreement is really about once both positions are stated at their strongest); "Rulings, round by round" (about 400 words — take each of the six moves above in turn and say who has the better of it and why); "What the passage settles" (about 150 words — which reading of the quoted sentence is correct, and how much argumentative weight it can bear); "What both are missing" (about 200 words — distinctions or considerations neither party raised that would sharpen the dispute); and "Final verdicts" (about 100 words — one sentence each for A and B, stating what each won and what each lost).
