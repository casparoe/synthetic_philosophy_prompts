I am a retired experimental physicist and have, over the past two years, been making my way through the philosophy of mind with a reading group I now help to organize. We meet through a university extension program: twelve to fourteen adults, mostly with scientific rather than philosophical training, and we have ninety minutes per session. Our next meeting concerns Thomas Metzinger's self-model theory of subjectivity, and in particular the notion of phenomenal transparency -- the claim that we characteristically look through our representational states at the world they present, rather than at the states themselves, so that the medium of representation does not appear as a medium, and the experienced self strikes us as something we are rather than something the brain constructs.

I have concluded that simply explaining this will not work. My co-members are courteous skeptics; told that their sense of being a self is a model whose modelhood is invisible to them, they will nod and remain unpersuaded. What I want instead is a structured in-class activity that makes them feel the phenomenon before it is named. The analogy that first made the idea click for me is that of tinted glasses one forgets one is wearing -- one notices the world's tint, not the lens -- and I wonder whether something along those lines, or along the lines of simple perceptual manipulations (I am open to the rubber-hand illusion or cheaper variants requiring no laboratory equipment), could be staged so that participants first take some feature of experience to belong to the world or to their own nature, and are then brought to see it as a contribution of the representational machinery they were looking through all along.

Please design the activity in full: the sequence of stages with a timeline, whatever simple materials are required (we have a seminar room, paper, and ordinary objects; nothing electronic beyond a projector), precise instructions for what participants are to do and report at each stage, and a mechanism by which their own reports -- recorded before they know the point -- become the evidence that convicts them. There should be no lecture beforehand, and the terms transparency and self-model should not be uttered until the debrief.

Then give me the debrief itself: how each stage maps onto a specific component of the theory (the transparency of perceptual representation, the transparency of the phenomenal self-model, the difference between the content of a model and the fact of its being a model), and where the mapping is tight and where it is merely suggestive. I would also like your honest forecast of what will go wrong the first time I run this -- I anticipate at least one participant who reports seeing the trick from the start and another who finds the whole exercise a parlor game -- and how to handle both. Finally, tell me plainly which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by argument: my suspicion is that any staged demonstration can at best show that some representations are transparent, whereas the theory's stronger claim is that phenomenal experience is transparent in principle, because a system that could represent its own representational medium as such would need resources evolution had no reason to provide. If the activity overshoots or undershoots that claim, I want to know exactly where.
