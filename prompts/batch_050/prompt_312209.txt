Here's the students answer:

Locke's view is that what makes a person the same person over time is memory. A person, for Locke, is a thinking thing, and personal identity consists in consciousness: as far back as your consciousness can reach, that's how far your identity extends. So the person who did your childhood misdeeds is you only because you now remember doing them. Locke is careful to say this isn't about having the same body or even the same soul-substance, since consciousness could in principle be transferred between substances.

Reid objects with the brave officer case. Imagine a boy who is flogged at school, then grows up to be a brave officer who remembers the flogging, then becomes a general who remembers his deeds as an officer but has forgotten the flogging. Reid's point is that this shows Locke's theory is circular: memory already presupposes personal identity, so you can't define identity in terms of it without going in a circle. Reid also notes that since memories are often unreliable or confabulated, basing identity on memory makes identity depend on something shaky.

On Locke's view the general is the same person as the officer (he remembers the deeds), and the officer is the same person as the boy (he remembers the flogging), but the general is not the same person as the boy (he's forgotten it). Since being the same person as is supposed to be transitive, the theory generates a contradiction, and this shows memory can't even be a necessary condition for personal identity.

Is it decisive? I think mostly yes. Locke might reply by saying identity only requires an overlapping chain of memories rather than direct memory at every step, but this is really just changing the subject, since Locke himself defines identity by present consciousness.
