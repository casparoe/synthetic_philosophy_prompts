I've been reading LessWrong and the adjacent decision-theory literature for over a decade, and I'm drafting a post staking out my position on fanaticism — the thesis that expected-value reasoning can rationally require accepting arbitrarily improbable chances of sufficiently large payoffs. Before I publish, I want an audit of whether my commitments are jointly tenable. Here they are:

1. Maximising expected utility is the correct standard of rational choice under uncertainty. The representation theorems show that agents whose preferences satisfy completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence can be represented as expected-utility maximisers, and that violating these axioms leaves one inconsistent or exploitable; so any departure from expected-utility maximisation needs extraordinary justification.

2. Utility is unbounded in the goods themselves. A world with twice as much flourishing is twice as good, full stop; there is no cap. Money's diminishing marginal utility is purely instrumental and reflects no bound on value.

3. Case verdicts. I would refuse a Pascal's mugging — a stranger offers me 10^100 happy lives in exchange for $50, on a story I can assign only a minuscule credence — however high he scales the promised payoff, and I regard refusing as rational, not merely excusable. Likewise, offered a genuine St. Petersburg game by a demonstrably trustworthy casino, I would not pay more than about $30, and I don't think that's irrational either.

4. Probabilistic hygiene. I follow Cromwell's rule: no empirical proposition gets credence exactly 0 or 1. I also reject rounding tiny credences to zero for decision purposes; if a hypothesis is coherent, my credence in it is small but positive, and my decision theory has to live with that rather than pretending otherwise.

5. Bounded utility is not an acceptable fix. Any cap strikes me as ad hoc, and it generates its own absurdities — indifference between outcomes that differ astronomically once one is near the cap, with the cap's location doing no principled work.

6. Everyday rationality runs on heuristics. I make nearly all real decisions by habit and rough rules of thumb, almost never by explicit expected-value computation, and I regard this as fully rational rather than a falling-short of my own standard.

7. Existential-risk reduction is worth substantial resources even when the probability that any given intervention makes a difference is low, because the stakes are astronomical. I take this verdict to sit comfortably with (3): the probabilities involved in x-risk work, while small, are nowhere near mugging-small.

Please tell me: which of these commitments are in genuine tension, which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and how the real tensions rank by severity. For each genuine tension, state exactly what would have to give and at what cost. Where I have claimed that two of my own commitments sit comfortably together, check whether my stated grounds actually deliver that. Then tell me which single revision would restore the most consistency for the least damage, and what my position looks like after making it. I want a ranked audit, not a survey of decision theory.
