I've volunteered to run one session of an interdisciplinary reading group at my university (mix of comp-sci, linguistics, and psych grad students, so they all half-know each other's jargon and are overconfident about it) and I got assigned the symbol grounding problem. Here's my worry: the comp-sci folks will arrive convinced the problem is already solved, because, in their words, modern language models clearly learn word meaning from text plus a bit of multimodal training, so what's left to puzzle over? Meanwhile some of the humanities-adjacent people will arrive thinking the Chinese Room already settled that computers can't mean anything, full stop. I think both of those are ways of not seeing the actual problem.

So what I want from you is a plan for the explanation itself, not the explanation: what has to be in place conceptually before the grounding problem can even be felt as a problem rather than as a triviality or a mystery (I'm guessing the distinction between a symbol system being interpretable by us and its symbols meaning something to the system itself has to come early, but maybe that's wrong?). Give me the order of ideas and why each depends on the last. Then suggest the one carrying example to build the session around — I've considered the merry-go-round of looking up a word in a monolingual dictionary of a language you don't know, and also Searle's room, but the room comes with so much baggage and pre-formed opinions that I'm tempted to avoid it or only bring it in late. Tell me which example actually earns its keep and what properties it needs to have (and which tempting features it should lack, e.g. I suspect any example involving a robot with sensors too early will let the comp-sci people say "fine, solved" and stop thinking).

Also give me the misconception map: the wrong turns each constituency in this audience will predictably take, in roughly the order they'll take them, and at what point in the sequence I should head each one off versus let it get voiced first. I'm especially unsure how to handle the "LLMs ground symbols in other symbols and that seems to work okay, so grounding must be a spectrum not a binary" move, which is smart and which I half-believe myself. And what should I deliberately leave out at this level (the frame problem? embodiment? teleosemantics?) and flag as deliberately excluded. Finally, how do I check at the end that they've grasped the problem as a problem — e.g. what's a question or prompt I could give them where the right response shows they get it, and where the confident "it's solved" / "it's impossible" answers both reveal they don't?
