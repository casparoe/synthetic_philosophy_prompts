For the final meeting of my upper-level undergraduate seminar on metaphysical modality, each student presents a paper and then fields questions from the class. Mine defends transworld identity against Lewis's counterpart theory. The core of the paper: counterpart theory systematically mistranslates de re modal claims — my sense that I could have become a nurse is about me, not about some sufficiently similar person in another world — and the standard reply, that context fixes which similarity relation is in play, makes modal facts about me partly facts about speakers' interests, which I argue is a higher price than the problems counterpart theory was built to solve.

The audience is our instructor, a sympathetic Lewisian who taught us the argument from accidental intrinsics and Lewis's own insistence that the counterpart relation is context-dependent, plus classmates who mostly lean the same way. Prepare my question-period prep sheet: the six to eight objections this audience will actually raise, ordered from most to least likely, each phrased the way the instructor would put it (he favors concrete cases — Humphrey's five fingers, Humphrey winning the election — over abstract formulations). For each objection, give me what I should concede up front, a reply of roughly two minutes, and a note on where the reply is weakest. At minimum, cover the argument from accidental intrinsics, Chisholm's problem about chains of gradually differing possibilities, and the symmetrical-candidates case in which two otherworldly individuals are equally good matches for being me. Close with the question I am hoping nobody asks, and why it is the hardest one for my position.
