I'm taking an upper-level undergraduate seminar in social epistemology, and our final project is to produce a model adversarial collaboration. I've chosen the debate between reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimony. Please draft the joint statement that a committed reductionist (who holds that justified testimonial belief requires positive, non-testimonial grounds for the speaker's reliability) and a committed anti-reductionist (who holds that hearers have a defeasible default entitlement to accept testimony without such grounds) could both sign after a good-faith collaboration.

To keep the collaboration concrete, the document must apply both positions to two stipulated cases I invented for the project, which neither classic position was formulated with in mind:

1. Vouching chains: A hearer receives a claim from a stranger, but a friend the hearer knows to be highly reliable has certified that the stranger is trustworthy. The hearer has no independent information about the stranger, only the friend's certification.
2. Synthetic aggregation: A hearer reads a factual summary produced by a system that averages together the reports of many anonymous human informants, none of whom the hearer can identify, but whose aggregate outputs have been statistically validated. It is contested whether this counts as testimony at all.

The statement should contain: (a) a numbered list of agreed claims — substantive points both sides can genuinely endorse, including at least one about what testimony-based knowledge does not require, one about what goes wrong in paradigmatic cases of gullibility, and one about how each side classifies the vouching chain case; (b) a table of disagreements, with each position stated in words its own defenders would accept (no strawman formulations, and no disagreement that is merely terminological — if a disagreement dissolves once both sides fix their terms, move it to the agreed section with a note explaining the dissolution); (c) a list of cruxes — sub-questions such that answering them would oblige at least one side to revise its view; and (d) for each crux, a test, argument, or case both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance, including their diverging verdicts on the synthetic aggregation case.

Please do not resolve the debate; the goal is a document both parties could sign, with the residual disagreement made exact.
