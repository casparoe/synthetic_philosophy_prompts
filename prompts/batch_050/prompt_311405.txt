You are a philosopher specializing in rational choice theory and diachronic rationality — someone equally at home with the formal structure of preference logic and with the philosophical literature on plans, precommitment, and practical reasoning over time. I would like your help with a piece of reflective equilibrium: I have recorded my verdicts on a set of cases involving cyclic preferences and foresight, and I want to know what principle, if any, unifies them. Please think carefully. Begin by brainstorming two or three candidate principles, each stated precisely enough that it could be applied mechanically to a new case, before defending one of them (or explaining why none survives).

First, so that you know where I stand: I understand the standard money-pump argument against cyclic preferences, the distinction between synchronic requirements on an agent's preferences at a time and diachronic requirements on an agent's deliberation across time, and — in rough outline — the idea that a rational agent might be resolute, sticking to a plan even when reconsideration at the later time would favor abandoning it. What I lack is a principled view of my own. Here are my cases and verdicts.

Case 1. Petra prefers the Monet print to the Kandinsky, the Kandinsky to the Mondrian, and the Mondrian to the Monet. A dealer offers her swaps at a dollar apiece, and she accepts each one as it comes, cycling through all three prints and ending with the print she started with, three dollars poorer. My verdict: this is irrational — the paradigm case.

Case 2. Dieter has exactly Petra's preferences, but he knows that he has them. Offered the first swap, he declines, reasoning that accepting it would launch him onto a path whose steps he can already foresee. My verdict: fully rational. Note the tension I already feel: Petra and Dieter have identical preferences at every moment, so if irrationality resides in the preferences, both are irrational; if it resides in the pattern of choices, then Case 1's verdict cannot be about preferences at all.

Case 3. Lena's tastes over restaurants cycle — each week she prefers to move on to the next one, in a fixed rotation. No one offers her trades; no plan is at stake; nothing is lost. My verdict: no irrationality whatsoever. Cyclical tastes with no structure that could exploit them seem to me entirely innocent, which suggests the cycle alone is not the sin.

Case 4. Tomasz has Petra's cyclic preferences and, unlike Dieter, would accept each swap if offered — but he lives where no one ever offers trades. My verdict: I genuinely wobble here. Half of me says he is just as irrational as Petra and merely luckier; half of me says rationality cannot depend on what offers the world happens to put in front of you.

Case 5. Marcus prefers, at 6 p.m., that he leave the party early; he also knows that at 10 p.m. he will prefer to stay, and that 10 p.m. Marcus will act on that preference. So at 6 p.m. he hands his car keys to a friend with instructions. My verdict: rational — even admirable. But notice that his earlier self is deliberately frustrating his later self, and I am unsure on what grounds the earlier self gets to govern.

Case 6. Sofia is offered the same sequence of swaps as Petra, accepts all three, and loses the three dollars — but she tells us, sincerely, that she enjoys the ritual of trading enough that the exchanges themselves were worth the money. My verdict: not irrational after all, which makes me suspect that Case 1's verdict secretly depended on an assumption about what Petra cares about, not on the cycle.

My questions:

1. State the principle — or the small set of candidate principles — that best captures these six verdicts. For each clause of the principle, tell me which case motivated it.
2. Apply the principle to cases I have not considered. Two in particular interest me: a person whose cyclic preferences concern only trivial goods versus one whose cycle governs life-defining choices; and a committee whose majority preference cycles and which is pumped by a patient outsider.
3. Be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and tell me which verdict you think I should drop if I refuse to accept the principle's complexity. I would rather sacrifice one verdict than adopt a principle with a clause bolted on for every case.
4. Finally, tell me what would change your mind. Which argument or consideration, if it were put to you, would most plausibly overturn your deliverable here? Where in your reasoning do you judge the risk of failure to be greatest?
