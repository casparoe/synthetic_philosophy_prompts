"In your own words, explain the Frege-Geach problem for expressivism. Why does it arise specifically for non-cognitivist views, and what resources do expressivists have for responding? (approx. 300 words)"

Here's what the student wrote:

The Frege-Geach problem is about how moral sentences behave when they're embedded in larger sentences rather than asserted on their own. Expressivists like Ayer and Stevenson say that "stealing is wrong" doesn't state a fact, it expresses disapproval or an attitude. That works okay when the sentence is asserted by itself. But consider: "If stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong. Stealing is wrong. So getting your little brother to steal is wrong." This is a valid argument. But in the antecedent of the conditional, nobody is actually expressing disapproval of stealing — the speaker might be agnostic. So the expressivist can't say the sentence means the same thing in both places, and the argument comes out as a straightforward fallacy of equivocation on their view. The problem was introduced by Geach in his 1965 paper "Assertion," crediting a point Frege made in his essay "The Thought" about negation.

Expressivists have resources to respond though. The main one is that once we accept minimal or deflationary truth-aptness, moral sentences can be true or false in the same thin way as any other sentences, so conditionals and inferences work exactly as they do for factual claims. This means quasi-realism essentially dissolves the problem, because if "stealing is wrong" is truth-apt, modus ponens is valid for it automatically. So the problem is only a problem for crude old-style emotivists who denied truth-aptness altogether, not for sophisticated modern expressivists like Blackburn.
