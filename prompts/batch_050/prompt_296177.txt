I'm designing a unit on democratic backsliding for a graduate seminar in comparative politics, and before I assign any of the literature I want to fix what a defensible concept of backsliding actually has to do. Write the desiderata: the requirements any adequate conceptualization of democratic backsliding must satisfy if it is to function as something more than a label for 'politics I dislike,' with the case or consideration motivating each requirement.

Please address at least the following issues, each as a desideratum with its motivating case:

1. **The baseline problem.** Backsliding implies movement away from something. What must the concept say about the reference point — a country's own prior state, an ideal type of consolidated democracy, a contemporaneous cross-national standard? What goes wrong with each choice (e.g., a country that was never fully democratic cannot 'backslide' from it; an ideal-type baseline risks classifying stable mediocrity as decline)?

2. **The substance–procedure boundary.** The concept must distinguish erosion of democratic competition from ordinary partisan policy change one happens to oppose — a government packing courts, capturing media regulators, or rewriting electoral rules versus a government passing tax or welfare policies its opponents consider disastrous. Say what criterion does this work, and flag the obvious danger: that any bright line here will be contested precisely by the actors the concept is meant to describe.

3. **Gradualism.** Modern cases rarely involve coups; they involve sequences of individually legal, individually minor moves. Must the concept be able to aggregate incremental steps into a verdict of backsliding, and what desideratum does that impose (a threshold? a trajectory measure? a test of cumulative effect on the competitiveness of future elections)? Note the tension: any rule sensitive enough to catch slow erosion will generate false positives on vigorous-but-legitimate democratic hardball.

4. **Symmetry across ideologies.** The concept must apply equally to left-wing and right-wing governments. State what this requires concretely and whether it rules out defining democracy partly by policy outcomes.

5. **Who judges.** Must an adequate account specify whose assessment counts — expert coders, domestic courts, citizens, international monitors? Consider that expert ratings are themselves contested and potentially ideological, while citizen perceptions are blunted by polarization: many voters do not register erosion when their own side does it. Can the concept remain neutral about the authority question, or is that a desideratum in its own right?

6. **The hybrid-regime boundary.** Must the concept distinguish democratic backsliding from movement within, or out of, an already-competitive-authoritarian regime — and does anything of substance turn on the distinction, or is it bookkeeping?

For each desideratum, mark whether you take it to be non-negotiable or instead a preference of a particular camp (minimalists who want a thin electoral definition, maximalists who build liberal rights in, disaggregators who reject any single verdict). Then the part I care most about: identify any subset of these desiderata you believe cannot be jointly satisfied — for instance, I suspect full ideological symmetry, sensitivity to novel erosion tactics that no checklist anticipates, and complete coder neutrality pull against each other — and say why. Close with a brief scorecard: how a minimalist electoral definition, a thick liberal-democratic definition, and an institution-by-institution disaggregated approach each fare against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their rationale; I can do the scoring myself in seminar.
