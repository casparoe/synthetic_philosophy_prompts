I work as a software engineer (backend stuff, zero philosophy background) and I keep getting into this arguement with a coworker about whether politics would be fixed by just picking random citizens like we do for juries. I realized my gut verdicts don't add up to anything, and I want to know what principle I'm actually running on, if any. Here are the verdicts, honestly reported:

- Jury duty, random people decide the verdict while a judge runs the procedure: totally fine, actively good.
- Replace the whole House of Representatives with 435 randomly drawn citizens serving four-year terms: I flinch. Feels like a disaster.
- My company randomly rotates three engineers onto a committee that reviews big architecture proposals for six months at a time: works great, nobody complains, I like it.
- Two-term limit for the US president: obviously good, removing it sounds insane to me.
- Term limits for state legislators: I think these actually backfired, power just moved to lobbyists and permanent staff who never leave, so I'm against them.
- Lifetime appointments for Supreme Court justices: bad, seems to warp everything around it.
- A citizens' assembly, random people paid to study one question for months and then issue a recommendation that voters or the legislature still have to approve: seems genuinely great.
- Sortition for picking the Fed chair, or a CEO: laughable. Terrible idea.
- A senator who's held the seat for 30 years, is good at it, keeps winning by big margins: I can't point to anything broken, but something still feels off to me.

The tension as I see it: I like random selection in cases 1, 3, and 7 but hate it in 2 and 8; I like term limits in 4 and 6 but hate them in 5; and case 9 just confuses me. I suspect the real variable is something like whether the job is a judgment task or a skill task, or maybe whether power sits in one person vs. being spread out, but I can't make any version of that fit all nine.

What I want is a numbered list with exactly six items, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion: (1) the best single principle you can state that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, precise enough that I could apply it to new cases myself; (2) which of my verdicts it can't save, and which one you'd actually advise me to drop; (3) what it predicts about term-limiting congressional committee chairs; (4) what it predicts about electing sheriffs and judges, where the US is weird by world standards; (5) whether my gut feeling in case 9 survives contact with the principle or gets explained away as status quo bias; (6) one case I haven't thought of where the principle gives a verdict I'd probably find surprising, and why.
