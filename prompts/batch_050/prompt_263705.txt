So I'm a PhD student in moral psychology, and I'm writing a dissertation chapter on whether psychopaths *know* that what they do is wrong. The usual framing bores me because both sides have a clean answer to the empirical data: the "they know" camp points out that psychopathic offenders can recite moral norms, grade the seriousness of transgressions, and often cover their tracks (which suggests they know what others will condemn); the "they don't know" camp replies that parroting norms isn't moral knowledge, it's like knowing the speed limit in a country whose traffic laws mean nothing to you, and they point to the emotion deficits and to the finding that psychopaths tend to treat moral rules more like arbitrary conventions.

Here's what I want: write me a dialogue between two (equally sharp, equally charitable) proponents of these two views, arguing about a specific test I've been toying with. Call it the Rule-Flip probe: you ask subjects whether a harmful act (say, one kid hitting another) would still be wrong *if the teacher, or the law, or God, announced that it was now permitted*. My thought is that someone who flips their verdict when authority flips only ever knew the rules, whereas someone whose verdict is authority-independent has something closer to genuine moral knowledge. One interlocutor should think Rule-Flip performance settles the question (or nearly does); the other should argue the test is badly contaminated -- e.g., that it measures deference to authority rather than moral understanding, that a non-psychopathic sadist would pass it while knowing full well, and that psychopaths' real deficit might be in *why* they hold wrongness fixed when they do (rule-worship vs. harm-based reasons), which Rule-Flip can't see.

Requirements: real back-and-forth where each responds to the other's actual last point, no strawmen, no narrator declaring a winner. Both should draw on the standard moves in this debate (the moral/conventional distinction, the motivation-vs-judgment issue, the "they hide their crimes" argument) but push them against my Rule-Flip idea specifically rather than rehashing the textbook. Somewhere in the dialogue, have each character say explicitly what *empirical result* would make them abandon their position -- not a vague "if evidence came in," but a concrete finding (e.g., a particular pattern of Rule-Flip answers plus reasoning transcripts from diagnosed psychopaths). And keep both of them honest about where their own view is shakiest; if one side's best argument turns out to rest on a contested assumption about what knowledge requires, I want that flagged in the dialogue, not smoothed over.

Length-wise, maybe 600-900 words of dialogue. Casual-ish register between the two of them is fine, they're grad students hashing it out, not giving papers at a conference.
