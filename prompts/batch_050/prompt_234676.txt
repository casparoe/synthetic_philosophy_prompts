bit of backstory first. my mom was diagnosed with early-onset alzheimers about a year and a half ago, and ever since, my family keeps saying things like "she's basically already gone" or "the woman in that bed isn't really her," and it got under my skin in a way I couldn't articulate. I did two years of a philosophy PhD before jumping ship to nursing (long story), so I went back to Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature, book 1, part 4, section 6, "Of personal identity," and this passage is now the spine of a talk I'm giving:

"The mind is a kind of theatre, where several perceptions successively make their appearance; pass, re-pass, glide away, and mingle in an infinite variety of postures and situations. There is properly no simplicity in it at one time, nor identity in different; whatever natural propension we may have to imagine that simplicity and identity. The comparison of the theatre must not mislead us. They are the successive perceptions only, that constitute the mind; nor have we the most distant notion of the place, where these scenes are represented, or of the materials, of which it is compos'd."

My rough pitch: Hume is right that the self is a succession of stages strung together rather than one simple thing that sits there unchanged, and if you buy something in that neighborhood (I gesture at Lewis-style perdurantism here -- persons as temporally extended things with stages, the way a road has segments), then "that's not really her anymore" is a category error. The family is mistaking one late stage for the whole person, and my job as their clinician is to gently redirect them toward caring for the whole temporally extended person instead of mourning the slice in front of them. I also want to say this reframes advance directives in a good way: the woman who signed that form years ago isn't some stranger bossing the current patient around.

The venue is our hospital's monthly ethics grand rounds. Audience: mostly palliative and ICU nurses and docs, our two staff bioethicists (both basically Beauchamp-and-Childress principlists), and -- the part that's keeping me up at night -- a philosopher from the university who works on personal identity and Parfit-style reductionism is coming, because the talk somehow got cross-listed with their med humanities series.

prep me for the Q&A. What objections am I actually going to get from this crowd, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do, phrased the way each of these people would really phrase them (an exhausted ICU nurse does not talk like a Parfit scholar)? For each one: what should I concede up front, whats my two-minute reply, and where is the reply soft. Also be straight with me about the passage itself -- does it actually support what I'm building on it, or is the philosopher going to point out that Hume is warning against exactly the move I'm making with the theatre line, and that I've turned a bundle theorist into a perdurantist?
