I am the honorary secretary of a university debating society whose constitution is, by historical accident, fully self-amending: any clause, including the amendment clause itself, may be altered by the same two-thirds vote. Roughly once a month a member tables a self-referential proposal — last term it was a motion that, if passed, may never be repealed — and I have perhaps twenty minutes before the meeting to rule on whether it may even be put to a vote, with the president's signature required on any refusal.

Please write me a vetting procedure I can run on any proposed amendment: the questions to ask, in order, such as whether the amendment applies to itself, whether it survives being applied to itself, whether it destroys the machinery that would be needed to repeal it, and whether it smuggles an amendment of the amendment clause into apparently ordinary business. For each question, state what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates in a refusal, and where it must hand the question to the membership because the issue is one of values or political judgement rather than of coherence. Mark honestly which steps are genuine logical tests and which are judgement calls dressed up as procedure, and say what the procedure cannot settle at all.

Then run it on three cases: the unrepealable motion above; an amendment replacing the two-thirds vote with a dice roll, complete with a grandfather clause exempting the amendment clause from being dice-rolled away; and a bland housekeeping amendment — correcting an archaic quorum figure — which your procedure nonetheless refuses, giving me a ruling I will not enjoy delivering.
