Okay so I've been reading LW-adjacent stuff about Kolmogorov complexity and Solomonoff induction for years, and I keep bouncing off the same wall: everyone says a finite sequence can be random in the sense of being incompressible, and everyone also admits that a fair coin could literally produce any finite sequence, including a million zeros. So either "random" applies to sequences or to the processes that generate them or to... something else, and I want the spec before I pick a side.

Write the desiderata list for a theory of finite-sequence randomness. Not "what is Kolmogorov complexity" -- I know that part -- but what ANY adequate account has to deliver to count as solving the problem, with the case or consideration motivating each requirement. The things I'm hoping it covers, roughly:

- the π digits problem: the first billion digits of π (in binary) pass every statistical test I can think of but are generated by a short program, so a theory has to say whether they're random and why that's not embarrassing either way
- the "every string is equally likely" problem: under a fair coin, a string of 500 alternating 0s and 1s has the exact same probability as a typical-looking string, so what work is "random" doing if probability can't distinguish them
- the reference machine problem: incompressibility depends on the choice of universal machine (up to a constant, fine, but for a *finite* string the constant is the whole game), so does the theory need to tolerate that or explain it away
- the betting/martingale criterion: a sequence is random if no computable betting strategy gets rich against it -- is that a definition, a consequence, or a desideratum?
- whether a theory has to extend to infinite sequences cleanly or is allowed to be finite-native
- what it should say about physically generated sequences (quantum measurements etc.) vs. purely mathematical ones

For each desideratum give me the motivating case, mark which are genuine constraints vs. which are just prejudices of the algorithmic-information-theory camp or the probability-theory camp, and flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied -- especially anything where making randomness a property of the string itself collides with making it well-defined for short strings. Then briefly: how do Kolmogorov/incompressibility, Martin-Löf style tests, von Mises's old frequency approach, and a pure Bayesian "randomness is just model-relative surprise" view do against the list. Keep the scorecard short; the requirements list is the actual deliverable.
