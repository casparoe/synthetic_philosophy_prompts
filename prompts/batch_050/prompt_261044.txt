So here's my situation. I spent forty years as a biochemist (protein folding, mostly), retired five years ago, and since then I've been reading philosophy the way other people take up golf. Right now I'm stuck on hedonism about wellbeing -- roughly the view that a life goes well in proporton to its pleasure. My snag isn't the usual objections. It's more basic: what do all the things we call pleasure actually have in common? The pleasure of a hot shower after a long run, the pleasure of finally seeing why a proof works, the pleasure of my grandson laughing, whatever morphine does -- introspectively these feel wildly different, and I can't locate a shared ingredient the way I could point to what all acids have in common at the bench. I'm told philosophers call this the heterogeneity problem.

My scientist's instinct is to say: fine, maybe there's a common neural mechanism underneath -- some shared opioid or dopamine signal that plays the role of "the same underlying stuff," the way water is H2O everywhere even though jade turned out to be two different minerals. But from my reading I gather philosophers are split on whether a result like that would even settle the question, and I honestly can't tell who has the better of it.

So before I read another theory of pleasure, I want the spec first. Write the desiderata: what must any adequate theory of pleasure (as the hedonic component of wellbeing) be able to do? For each requirement, give the case or phenomenon that motivates it. Here's what I'd expect to see on the list -- correct me where I'm off:

- It has to take a stand on whether pleasure is a distinctive feeling at all, or instead an attitude (liking, wanting, being pleased) directed at experiences -- and handle cases where those seem to come apart.
- It has to deal with the apparent fact that pleasures differ in quality and not just in intensity and duration, without either collapsing quality into quantity or quietly smuggling in non-hedonic values (that second failure mode is what I'd watch for, it's exactly the kind of move we'd catch in a referee report).
- It has to handle false or baseless pleasures -- pleasure taken in something that isn't actually happening -- and say whether they count the same, less, or not at all.
- It has to draw a principled line between pleasure and desire-satisfaction, since those get conflated constantly and the whole debate seems to hinge on keeping them apart.
- It has to say what the neuroscience is even for: can a theory of pleasure simply wait on the lab results, or is there a conceptual residue no brain scan will ever settle?
- And presumably it has to survive the experience machine without just defining the objection away.

On top of the list itself: tell me which of these requirements are genuine constraints and which are really just the prejudices of one camp; flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied (I have a hunch the "distinctive felt quality" requirement and the "one thing in common" requirement are in tension, but maybe that's naive); and say which single desideratum knocks out the most theories currently on the market. Then, briefly -- a paragraph or two, not a full scorecard -- how the main contenders do against the list: the felt-quality view, the attitudinal view, and whatever the "common neural currency" position is properly called. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases. The scoring I can redo myself; the criteria are where the thinking is.

A table for the desiderata is fine. Skip the bibliography -- no point, I can chase down the reading on my own. One last thing so the spec is aimed right: I want a theory good enough to evaluate hedonism about wellbeing, not one good enough to write a psychology textbook, in case adequacy depends on the purpose.
