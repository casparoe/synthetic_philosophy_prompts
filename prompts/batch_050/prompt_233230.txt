I'm preparing a session for a graduate seminar on the unity of consciousness, and I want a thought experiment that pushes against the standard way commissurotomy cases get interpreted. In the classic discussions of split-brain patients — Nagel's well-known paper on brain bisection, and the literature that Bayne surveys in The Unity of Consciousness — the default interpretive fork seems to be: either there are two subjects of experience, one per hemisphere, or there is one subject whose experience is somehow fragmented, partial, or switching between streams. What I find underexplored is the middle position: exactly one subject, but two concurrent streams of experience that are not phenomenally unified with each other — the subject undergoes both streams, yet there is nothing it is like for the two streams to be experienced together.

Construct a thought experiment that makes this one-subject/two-streams reading as strong as it can be made, i.e., maximally resistant to being redescribed in the more familiar two-subjects way. Constraints: (1) Keep the science within realistic commissurotomy — callosotomy patients, lateralized stimulus presentation, and the known cross-cuing strategies are all allowed; no fictional implants, hemisphere transplants, or telepathy. (2) The crux must not rest on the patient verbally reporting something ambiguous like "I feel divided"; whatever does the work should come from behavior and experimental results. (3) One scenario, a paragraph at most, with the relevant variable isolated explicitly rather than smuggled in.

After the scenario, do the demonstration. (a) State precisely what in the setup is supposed to block the two-subjects redescription, since that theorist will simply assign each stream to its own subject and deny there is any common subject who has both. (b) Walk through the verdicts of at least three positions — a Nagel-style reading, Bayne's treatment (his distinction between the duality and switch models and his own considered view), and a one-subject-with-a-disunified-phenomenal-field view — and show that the case genuinely separates them rather than being one they all absorb in the same way. (c) Identify the single feature of the scenario that is doing the work, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed. (d) Give the strongest attack a two-subjects theorist could mount against the case and assess whether it succeeds. The case is for seminar discussion, so one rule about what counts as cheating: don't stipulate the target verdict in the setup ("imagine a subject who experiences both streams without their being unified") — the verdict has to fall out of the scenario and the theories, not be asserted by it.
