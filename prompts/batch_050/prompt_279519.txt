I recently started helping out with my uncle’s aquaculture co-op, which raises tilapia and shrimp, because a grocery chain wants us to produce an animal-welfare plan before they’ll renew the contract. At our meeting yesterday, two people got into a surprisingly heated argument about what should count as good welfare. I stayed out of it and honestly don’t know who had the better case.

Please act like a philosopher of animal ethics who also understands aquaculture science. Think carefully, and first steelman both sides before reaching a verdict.

1. Mara said welfare is basically health and functioning. If mortality is low, growth and feed conversion are normal, lesions are rare, and water quality is in range, then the animals are doing well. Those measures are objective and hard to game, unlike guesses about what fish or shrimp supposedly feel.

2. Jules said those are mostly production metrics, not welfare metrics. Animals can grow adequately while living under chronic stress, and farm-wide averages can hide a miserable subgroup. We should also look at animal-based evidence, such as abnormal swimming, repeated escape attempts, loss of appetite, shelter use, and recovery after handling.

3. Mara said this is where people start anthropomorphizing. Shrimp do not even have a cortex, and fish brains are organized very differently from mammal brains. A startle response or escape behavior may just be nociception or a reflex, not suffering, so we should not impose costly changes based on projecting human feelings onto them.

4. Jules said lacking a cortex does not settle whether an animal has valenced experiences, because the question should be what the whole nervous system can do, not whether it contains one mammalian structure. Persistent avoidance, learning from aversive events, and trade-offs between avoiding harm and seeking food would all be evidence that something matters to the animal.

5. Mara replied that this proves too much. Thermostats, plants, and simple robots can respond adaptively to damage or negative stimuli. Unless Jules can state a principled threshold, precaution becomes open-ended: every response can be reinterpreted as possible suffering whenever the initial intervention fails to improve production.

6. Jules said the relevant threshold should not be any one behavior. What matters is a pattern of flexibility, persistence, integration across goals, and relief-seeking. Also, uncertainty cuts both ways: not having conclusive proof of suffering is not evidence that there is definitely no suffering.

7. Mara said even granting some uncertainty, the proposed fixes can backfire. Lower stocking density can increase aggression in some systems, added substrate can worsen water quality or disease risk, and extra handling for welfare inspections can itself be stressful. Good intentions do not guarantee better lives for the animals.

8. Jules said that is exactly why we need controlled trials and a welfare dashboard rather than relying on either intuition or productivity. The right measures may differ between tilapia and shrimp, but mortality alone is far too coarse.

9. Mara answered that a dashboard only pushes the problem back. If nobody knows how strongly each proxy tracks felt welfare, then assigning weights is arbitrary, and certification will encourage farms to optimize whatever is easiest to measure rather than whatever animals actually experience.

10. Jules said every welfare measure is a proxy, including mortality and growth. A transparent dashboard can at least be revised as evidence improves, whereas treating productivity as the sole safe measure hides its own huge assumptions.

Please adjudicate move by move. For each numbered move, say whether it is clearly mistaken, mostly right, or right only with major qualifications, and explain why. Then say what both Mara and Jules are missing, and who is more right overall. Don’t just tell me what our welfare policy should be; I mainly want to know which arguments actually work.
