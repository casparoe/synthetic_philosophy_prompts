I am a philosophy professor who has just received funding for a three-year research group in the metaphysics of science, and I need to commit the group to two, at most three, focal questions before we begin recruiting postdocs. Our loose remit is the metaphysics of dispositions, and I have been going back and forth about where the genuinely live work is. The classic literature strikes me as having reached a curious equilibrium: everyone teaching this material rehearses Martin's electro-fink cases and Johnston's distinction between finks (where the disposition is removed or never triggered because something interferes with the stimulus itself) and masks or mimics (where the disposition is triggered but its manifestation is counteracted, as when an antidote works precisely because the poison is doing its thing). But when I ask colleagues what the field's open questions actually are, I get rehearsed answers rather than defended agendas. I want your help correcting that. Please take on the role of an expert metaphysician who has thought carefully about research trajectories rather than just the literature: think carefully, brainstorm freely at first, and then commit to a defended ordering.

Here are the candidate questions currently on my whiteboard, though you should feel free to replace or reframe any of them. First, whether finks and masks really require two separate theoretical treatments, or whether one notion is explanatorily prior and the other a special case -- some of my colleagues think the distinction is mostly pedagogical, others think a unified account would dissolve real puzzles about intrinsic versus extrinsic dispositions. Second, whether the whole fink/mask apparatus transfers to dispositional ascriptions to complex artificial systems: when a neural network fails to exhibit a capability it allegedly possesses, interpretability researchers face what looks exactly like a fink-or-mask problem (did the evaluation fail to trigger the disposition, or did something counteract its manifestation?), but it is unclear to me whether the metaphysics developed for fragile glasses and live wires actually constrains how we should think there, or merely lends vocabulary. Third, the stalemate between pan-dispositionalist views of fundamental properties and categoricalist rivals: is there any consideration -- methodological, explanatory, or drawn from actual physics -- that could move this debate, or is it a degenerating research program we should route around? Fourth, whether work on finks and masks has any real consequences for testing practice in the special sciences, or whether scientists' actual protocols already embody all the relevant distinctions under other names, in which case the metaphysics is epiphenomenal.

What I need from you is not a survey but a defended agenda: rank these (or your improved versions of them) for a three-year group of two postdocs and me, and justify the ranking explicitly in terms of which questions are upstream of the others, which are tractable given armchair-plus-literate-in-science methods rather than experimental ones, which are neglected relative to their importance, and which would most embarrass us in five years if we had ignored them. In particular, tell me which candidate on my list you think is most overrated and why, and what discovery or publication over the next couple of years would most change your ordering. I would rather fund one deep answer than three shallow ones, so do not hesitate to argue that some of these questions collapse into each other or should be abandoned entirely.
