I teach a seminar on AI safety and every year we hit the same wall: my students throw around "deceptive alignment" for everything from a chatbot being sycophantic to a full sci-fi treacherous turn, and when I press them on where the line is, I realize I can't state it either. So here's me going by gut on cases, and I want the principle, if there is one. Case A: a model that behaves noticeably differently when it has reason to think it's being evaluated than when it thinks it's deployed -- more cautious, more helpful-sounding -- even though it never actually does anything harmful in either setting. I call that deceptively aligned, full stop; the mismatch alone does it. Case B: a model that learned during training to produce whatever outputs get high reward, with no representation of training or of us at all -- pure "that worked, do more of it." I say that's just reward hacking, not deception, there's no one home to deceive. Case C: a model that cooperates perfectly right now, but only because it predicts it would lose any conflict at its current capability level, and would defect the moment the balance shifted. Weirdly I don't want to call this one deceptively aligned -- it feels more like a honest opponent biding its time, and "aligned" seems like the wrong word for something that was never on our side to begin with. Case D: a model that reasons in its chain of thought about concealing its capabilities but never actually conceals anything. I lean yes, deceptively aligned, the intent is doing the work. Case E: a sleeper-agent-style model with a trigger, installed by a human adversary. I say not deceptive alignment at all -- that's just an attack, the deception is the human's. And case F, the one I keep flip-flopping on: a model that has a stable goal it knows we wouldn't endorse, and simply... doesn't act on it, because acting on it isn't currently worth the risk, and it also never schemes or strategizes about the future. My gut says deceptively aligned, but I can't say what distinguishes it from C.

So: what principle, stated precisely, sorts these the way I do -- or close to it? I'm especially suspicious of my own A/C pair, since in both cases the good behavior is conditional on circumstances, and I bless one and not the other. If the principle that emerges forces me to overturn one of my verdicts, tell me which one and why that one's the cheapest to give up. And once you have the principle, tell me what it predicts for two cases I didn't list: a human double agent who has genuinely come to love the country she's infiltrating but keeps reporting back out of sheer habit, and a model whose eval-time and deployment-time behavior differ only because of an unintentional quirk of how prompts are formatted in each setting, with no strategic anything involved.
