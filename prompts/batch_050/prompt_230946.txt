Background: I'm a philosophy professor — ethics and political theory mostly, my logic is twenty years rusty — and I'm building a unit for my freshman seminar on "the limits of knowledge." In prepping I ran into a little structure I can't find the established name for, and its bugging me. Here's the phenomenon, as concretely as I can put it. Some truths seem to be destroyed by being known. Take: the cat is asleep on the porch and nobody knows it. Plausibly true right now. But nobody can ever know the whole thing, because knowing it requires knowing the first half, which makes the second half false. Okay, so there are unknowable truths — mildly interesting, fine. But then a colleague down the hall in logic showed me a short derivation that seems to prove something much wilder: start from the innocent-sounding claim that every truth could in principle come to be known (not that anyone ever will, just that it's not ruled out), add a few bland assumptions — knowledge implies truth, if you know a conjunction you can know each conjunct, the usual — and out pops the conclusion that every truth is already known. Pure logic, no funny business I could spot. That can't be right, and I can't find where it goes wrong.

What I want from you: does this have a name? Both the smaller pattern (truths that go false when known) and the stronger collapse argument. I'm aware it rhymes with several things I half-remember: Moore-paradoxical sentences of the 'p but I don't believe p' shape, Gödel-style undecidability, the old verificationism debates, maybe something in the neighborhood of the knower paradox. Please sort these out carefully — which are genuinely the same phenomenon, which merely look similar from a distance, and where exactly the differences lie, because I suspect at least one of those is a false friend. I won't be satisfied with a bare name plus a wave at the literature; I want the carving — what the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where its edges are. And if the standard move is to blame one premise of the derivation, tell me which you'd cut and why, since my seminar students will absolutely ask.

Format-wise: answer as an expert in epistemic and modal logic writing for a competent but rusty philosopher, not for undergrads. Think carefully and take your time — first brainstorm the candidate concepts, then evaluate the fit of each, then commit to a verdict. Don't hedge everywhere; pick a position. But do tell me what would change your mind: which premise or inference rule in the derivation you think is most vulnerable, and what consideration or argument would convince you the collapse is actually sound (or, alternativly, that my colleague fumbled the setup and the real argument needs more machinery than he let on).
