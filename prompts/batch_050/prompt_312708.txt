I recently took a job on the standards desk of a mid-sized news organization, and within my first month I was asked to help revise our internal guidelines for photographic images. The existing rules struck me as oddly patterned. We permit photographers to crop, to adjust exposure and color balance globally, and to convert an image to black and white, but we flatly forbid removing or adding objects at the pixel level, even trivial ones like a stray plastic bag in a landscape shot. We treat a staff photograph as near-sufficient documentation of an event, whereas a skilled artist's drawing of the same event would run only as an illustration, explicitly labeled as such, no matter how faithful it was. And we apply the same anti-manipulation rules to images that were never really 'taken' in any simple sense: our photographers' phones already merge several frames for night shots, synthesize a depth-of-field effect that no lens produced, and in some modes select a different facial expression from a burst of frames to substitute into the final picture.

When I pressed my colleagues on why the rules draw the lines where they do, the answers were inconsistent. Some said photographs earn their evidential status because the image is counterfactually dependent on the scene -- it would have looked otherwise had things been otherwise -- and pixel-level edits break that dependence while global adjustments merely restyle it. Others said viewers literally see the event through the photograph, the way one sees through a telescope or a mirror, and that this is what distinguishes a photo from a drawing however accurate the drawing is. Still others shrugged and said the rules are just about preserving audience trust, which is a pragmatic matter with no deep theory behind it. Nobody could say what our policy implies about the phone-camera cases, where the 'manipulation' happens automatically, before any editor could forbid it.

What I'd like from you is a reconstruction: what philosophical account of photography, if any, does a policy like ours actually presuppose? Does it rest on a transparency thesis about seeing through images, on a causal or counterfactual-dependence theory of photographic evidence, or merely on conventions of audience trust that could survive without either? Please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where it is genuinely underdetermined -- where several rival theories would license exactly the same rules. Then assess whether the policy is internally coherent: do the allowances (cropping, tonal adjustment, in-camera computational processing) and the prohibitions (object removal, unlabeled illustration) presuppose the same theory, or different ones that happen to coexist? Finally, if we adopted the most defensible account you identify, which of our current rules would have to change, which could stay as they are, and what should we say about images that were computationally synthesized before any human made an editorial choice?
