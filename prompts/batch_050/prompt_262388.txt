So I've been reading Hume on causation and there's a specific thing that's bugging me that I can't find a clean answer to. Hume seems to give two different definitions of cause: one in terms of constant conjunction (A-type events are always followed by B-type events), and one in terms of a counterfactual (roughly, if the first thing hadn't happened, the second wouldn't have either). On the surface these don't look equivalent at all.

Please explain, in detail, what's going on here. Specifically:

1. Why are the two definitions not equivalent? Walk me through the ways they can come apart -- both extensionally (cases where one applies and the other doesn't) and conceptually (what each one is actually claiming about the world versus about us).

2. Here's the part that really confuses me: the counterfactual definition seems to smuggle in exactly the kind of necessary connection that Hume spends the whole rest of his treatment arguing we have no idea of. If all we ever observe is regularity, and our "idea of necessity" is really just a feeling produced by habit, then where does the counterfactual definition even come from? Is Hume being sloppy, or is the counterfactual definition supposed to be a report about what our minds do rather than about what's out there?

3. Does the fact that Hume offers both definitions help or hurt the "New Hume" reading -- the view that Hume was actually a realist about genuine necessary connections in nature and only skeptical about our epistemic access to them? It seems like a realist wouldn't need a mind-dependent second definition at all. But maybe that's too quick?

4. Bonus: what happens to both definitions in cases of causal overdetermination, where two potential causes each would have sufficed? It seems like the counterfactual fails (the effect would have happened anyway) and the regularity might too (no general pattern where this exact conjunction holds). Does Hume have the resources to handle that, or is this just a spot where both definitions break down and later counterfactual theories had to patch things up?

I want the actual conceptual machinery here, not a summary of "Hume is a skeptic about causation" -- I already know that part.
