I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on moral psychology and the reactive attitudes, and this term I'm TAing an upper-level undergraduate seminar on forgiveness, apology, and blame. I've been asked to contribute one essay question to the final exam, and I'd like your help drafting it, together with the materials my professor expects exam questions to come with: a model answer and a marking scheme.

The question should press on a tension my students kept circling all semester: whether forgiveness can ever be owed, and if it can, whether it still counts as forgiveness. Two pressure points came up repeatedly in discussion. First, several students argued that forgiveness is essentially gratuitous -- a gift rather than a debt -- so that describing someone as obligated to forgive changes the subject rather than identifying a genuine duty. Second, less often articulated but more interesting: even if such a duty existed, it might be undischargable, because forgiving seems to involve the overcoming of resentment, and resentment is not obviously something we can shed at will -- which threatens to make "you ought to forgive" the kind of ought that implies a can we lack. I want a question that forces candidates to take both pressure points seriously and to construct the strongest defensible version of an obligation to forgive: perhaps by relocating the duty to something other than the bare act of forgiving, perhaps by challenging the voluntariness worry head-on, perhaps by questioning whether forgiveness really must be gratuitous to be forgiveness. What I don't want is a question answerable by reciting the conditional-versus-unconditional taxonomy from week three; the marking scheme should make it easy to spot a student who merely reproduces that taxonomy and thinks they've answered.

Practical constraints: candidates have 90 minutes, the question is worth 20 marks out of 100 on a three-question paper, and answers typically run 800 to 1,000 words. Please provide (1) the question as it would appear on the exam paper, phrased so a strong student immediately sees the crux; (2) a model answer of roughly 600-700 words written at the level of our best students; and (3) a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, noting the marks each carries and, in one line each, the characteristic way weaker candidates bungle that point. The scheme should also state explicitly what separates a first-class answer from a good upper-second -- not in terms of polish, but in terms of the specific moves the better answer makes that the merely good one misses. One final request: if you think the tension I've described rests on a confusion -- if, say, the two pressure points collapse into one on closer inspection, or some obvious move dissolves the puzzle and makes my intended question trivial -- please tell me so plainly rather than designing around a defective brief. I'd rather have my framing corrected than receive a polished question built on a muddle.
