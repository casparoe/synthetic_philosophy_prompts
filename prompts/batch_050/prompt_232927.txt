Write a dialogue between two adult siblings, both philosophically sharp, whose father has just been moved into assisted living in the early stages of dementia. He was never cruel or neglectful, but he was cold and joyless throughout their childhood; he also worked two jobs for fifteen years to put both of them through university, and neither has had a real conversation with him in over a decade. One sibling takes Jane English's friendship model of filial obligation: grown children owe their parents roughly what friends owe friends, parental sacrifices are gifts freely given rather than debts to be repaid, and where no friendship exists — and where, as with dementia, the very possibility of friendship is fading — special obligations lapse. The other defends a robust duty of gratitude and filial care that survives estrangement and cognitive decline. Each should press the other on the hardest point of their view. The friendship theorist must confront the charge that her model licenses abandoning parents precisely when they need their children most, and must explain why walking away now would not simply be contemptible. The obligation theorist must confront the charge that his view turns love into debt collection, and must explain why we owe a distant father more than we owe, say, a devoted and generous former teacher to whom we also owe a great deal. Neither side should be a strawman: the siblings should respond to each other's actual arguments, and the dialogue should end either in genuine movement on at least one side or in a crisply articulated point of irreducible disagreement — not in an amicable shrug.
