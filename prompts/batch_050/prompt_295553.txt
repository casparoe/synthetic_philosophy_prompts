I've been reading the longtermism literature for years — the old LessWrong posts, the GPI working papers, the 80,000 Hours material, the back-and-forth with critics — and I've noticed that nearly every debate about "strong longtermism" (roughly, the thesis that the primary determinant of an action's value is its effect on the very far future) is really a proxy fight over which demands a theory of far-future value must satisfy. Nobody writes the spec first. I'd like you to write it.

Write the desiderata a theory of the value of the far future must meet to count as an adequate basis for strong longtermism — adequate, specifically, for the purpose of guiding actual resource allocation by a well-funded altruist today, not for winning a seminar. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. The list should cover, at minimum, the following, plus whatever I'm missing: (a) what the theory says about the sheer scale of possible future populations, including whether it can resist being driven by the astronomical-waste argument without simply accepting it; (b) how it handles arbitrarily small probabilities of astronomically large payoffs — whether it has a principled response to fanaticism and mugging-style cases, or just bites the bullet; (c) whether it can survive the non-identity problem, since nearly everything we do changes who will exist; (d) how it treats pure time discounting versus extinction-risk discounting, and whether it can distinguish them cleanly; (e) what it does with deep cluelessness — the worry that the sign of our best interventions' effects on the far future (not just their magnitude) is unknowable; (f) which population-axiology commitments it is hostage to (totalism, critical-level views, person-affecting restrictions), and how much of the longtermist conclusion survives swapping those out; (g) whether it can accommodate the possibility that some far-future outcomes are of overwhelming negative value rather than merely foregone positive value (suffering risks, value lock-in of bad trajectories), without collapsing into paralysis; and (h) whether it yields any tractable decision procedure at all under ordinary empirical uncertainty, or only a verdict about an idealized agent.

For each desideratum, mark it as non-negotiable, negotiable, or a prejudice of one camp, and defend the marking — I expect the interesting disagreements to be here. In particular, I want your honest view on which requirements are in joint tension: for example, whether a theory can simultaneously avoid fanaticism, respect something like an independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives intuition, and still deliver the strong longtermist conclusion, or whether that triad is provably or plausibly unsatisfiable. If a subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, that is the part I most want stated explicitly, with the conflict laid out rather than gestured at.

Close with a brief scorecard — no more than a few sentences each — on how the main contenders (expected total utilitarianism with no time discount, the same with a modest pure discount rate, person-affecting views, critical-level utilitarianism, and deontologically constrained versions) fare against the list, and name the single requirement that excludes the most contenders. Keep the weight on the criteria themselves; I can do my own scoring. No citations or reading list needed — I already have the books.
