Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Russell's theory of descriptions is motivated by puzzles about sentences containing definite descriptions that don't refer to anything. One puzzle is about identity: George IV wanted to know whether Scott was the author of Waverley, and if 'the author of Waverley' just means Scott, then this amounts to asking whether Scott is Scott, which is trivial. Another puzzle is how a sentence like 'the current king of France is bald' can be meaningful at all, given that there is no king of France. Russell's key idea is that definite descriptions are not referring expressions at all but 'incomplete symbols' that only get their meaning from the whole sentence they occur in. So 'the current king of France is bald' gets analyzed as: there is at least one thing that is a king of France, there is at most one such thing, and whatever is a king of France is bald. Since France is a republic and the first conjunct is false, the whole sentence is neither true nor false — it simply fails to say anything, because the description fails to pick anyone out. This shows how the sentence can be meaningful without there being a king of France for it to refer to. As for 'the current king of France is not bald,' Russell's analysis makes this come out true, since it's just the negation of the earlier claim, and we know there is no bald king of France. So the theory lets us deny that the king of France is bald without committing ourselves to a nonexistent bald object, which solves the puzzle of negative existentials as well."
