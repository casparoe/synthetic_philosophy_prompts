I work in machine learning -- mostly training reinforcement learning agents and, lately, trying to interpret what large models are actually doing internally. In a reading group on philosophy of mind, we got into a debate about Daniel Dennett's idea of free-floating rationales: his claim that there can be reasons -- real, genuine reasons why a trait or behavior exists -- without anyone, anywhere, ever having comprehended or represented those reasons. Natural selection, on his telling, is a process that discovers and tracks reasons (why the giraffe's neck is long, why the cuckoo's egg mimics the host's) with zero comprehension on anyone's part; evolution exhibits what he calls competence without comprehension. Some of us found this obviously right, others found it borderline incoherent -- how can there be a reason if no mind has ever grasped it, and isn't talk of evolution "finding reasons" just a façon de parler that we strip away when being precise?

Here is what I'd like you to do, acting as a philosopher of mind and biology who is also genuinely conversant with modern machine learning. Restate the free-floating rationales thesis and the core of that debate in the vocabulary I actually work in: optimization processes and loss landscapes, learned policies, selection pressure over populations of candidates, the distinction between a system that achieves a task and a system that has an internal, inspectable representation of why its solution works, mechanistic interpretability, mesa-optimizers and the inner/outer alignment gap. Be as faithful as you can -- when Dennett (or his critics) makes a claim, find its best counterpart in my terms rather than the most convenient one, and flag explicitly where you are interpreting rather than translating. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the thesis map cleanly onto things like gradient descent discovering solutions no engineer understands, which parts you had to distort to make them fit, and which parts -- if any -- simply cannot be expressed in the vocabulary of optimization at all. I'm especially interested in whether the notion of a reason adds anything beyond "this structure was selected because it increased fitness/lowered loss," and whether the normativity of reasons (they don't just explain, they purportedly justify) survives translation into purely descriptive talk of selection pressures, or quietly evaporates.

Please think this through carefully before writing: first sketch, for yourself, a rough mapping table of source concepts to target concepts, and only then write up the translation and the residue analysis. Conclude with a verdict on the reading-group disagreement: is the residue a defect of my optimization vocabulary (something real I can't yet say in it), or is it evidence that the philosophical debate rests on a way of talking that feels substantive but isn't -- and does the inner alignment literature, where we worry that a trained system pursues objectives nobody intended or represented, sharpen this question or just re-raise it in fancier dress?
