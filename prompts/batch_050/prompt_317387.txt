I'm trying to get my head straight on when it's fine to just multiply probabilities -- i.e. presume independence -- and when doing that is a blunder. My tentative principle is: you're allowed to presume independence unless the sources actually communicated with each other. Here are my gut verdicts on some cases. Tell me what principle I should actually be running, fix mine or replace it, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save.

1. Two eyewitnesses who were sequestered separately right after the robbery, never met each other, both describe the getaway car as a green hatchback. Verdict: multiply away, two of them is way stronger evidence than one.

2. Same setup, except the two witnesses sat together in the lobby for an hour before giving their statements and definitely chatted. Verdict: barely better than one witness, don't multiply.

3. Two polling firms, no collusion, never talk to each other, both show the candidate up 4 -- but they both read the same public fundamentals, use similar likely-voter models, and herd toward the same industry consensus. Verdict: I still think this is nearly as strong as case 1. Multiply, mostly.

4. That famous kind of court case where an expert squares the odds of one infant dying unexpectedly to get the odds of two dying in the same family. Everyone now agrees this was the big statistical sin, since whatever genetic or enviromental thing caused the first death can cause the second. My head knows this. But my gut keeps whispering that two incredibly rare things happening to one family is still suspicious, and it won't shut up. I'd like the principle to either tell my gut to be quiet or vindicate it.

5. My spam filter treats "FREE!!!" and "wire transfer" as independent and just multiplies, even though those words obviously travel together, and it works great. Verdict: the sin is excused in practice, maybe whenever you only need to rank things rather than get calibrated numbers out?

6. Two lab tests for the same disease, run on different machines but on the same blood sample from the same patient. Verdict: leaning "don't multiply," but I honestly can't say why, and it bothers me that I can't.

Cases 2 and 3 seem to be where my principle does all the work, and I'm not sure it gives the answers I want, since nobody talked to anybody in case 3 either. So: what's the right principle? Please state it precisely enough that I could apply it myself, say which clause each of my cases is motivating, and tell me which of my verdicts has to go if the principle is going to stay simple. Then apply it to a case I haven't listed: three friends independently recommend the same restaurant to me, but it turns out all three of them follow the same food account on instagram. How much evidence is that, on the repaired principle?
