In American debates over money in politics, one analogy does nearly all the argumentative work: spending money on politics is speech, so restricting political spending is like restricting speech itself. Courts have relied on versions of it, advocates and columnists repeat it constantly, and it is almost always deployed to reach a deregulatory conclusion — that limits on campaign contributions and independent expenditures are censorship by another name, suspect in exactly the way any speech restriction would be.

I would like you to audit this analogy rather than accept or reject it wholesale.

First, give the mapping explicitly. What, on the analogy, plays the role of the speaker, the utterance, the audience, and the public square? Which feature of money — its fungibility, its power to purchase amplification, printing, airtime, staff, or access — is doing the work of the "speech" it is claimed to be? A correspondence table is fine.

Second, identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that matter from the ones that don't. Candidate disanalogies I want assessed, not assumed: money is instrumental rather than expressive, since a donation does not say anything by itself the way a pamphlet does; speech is non-rival while audience attention and media inventory are scarce, so one spender's "speech" can crowd out another's rather than merely adding to the conversation; contributions are transferred to and spent by someone else, which arguably makes them more like gifts than like utterances; and large expenditures create dependencies and incentives for officeholders that pure expression does not. Which of these are fatal to the analogy's intended use, and which are incidental?

Third, say what the analogy smuggles in. Does it presuppose a speaker-centered picture of political communication in which the interests of listeners — and the interest in some rough equality of influence — simply disappear? Does it quietly extend the protection we grant the content of a message to the sheer volume of its amplification?

Fourth, test the conclusion. If the analogy holds only partially, does anything interesting survive of the deregulatory argument — perhaps for independent expenditures but not direct contributions, or for spending limits but not disclosure rules? Or was the analogy carrying the entire load from the start?

Finally, if the analogy is defective, propose a better one — money as a megaphone, political influence as an auction, contributions as gifts, or something else — and demonstrate that your replacement actually does better: that it preserves what the original captured while not concealing what the original obscured.
