I am a novelist drafting a book set in an invented Aegean port city, and I have given it a worked-out economy: stamped bronze coin, grain measures, moneylenders on the harbor front, a civic treasury. An early reader has told me the economy feels incoherent — the same coin seems to obey different laws in different chapters — and rather than patch scenes one by one, I want to fix the underlying theory first. I would like to anchor it in Aristotle. In the Nicomachean Ethics, Book V, chapter 5 (1133b, in H. Rackham's translation), he writes:

"It is therefore necessary that all commodities shall be measured by some one standard, as was said before. And this standard is in reality demand, which is what holds everything together, since if men cease to have wants or if their wants alter, exchange will go on no longer, or will be on different lines. But demand has come to be conventionally represented by money; this is why money is called nomisma (customary currency), because it does not exist by nature but by custom (nomos), and can be altered and rendered useless at will."

Treat this passage as the load-bearing text and write the desiderata for an adequate theory of money and exchange: what such a theory must be able to do if Aristotle's claims here are to come out true, or as near to true as the behavior of actual economies permits. For each requirement, give the case or phenomenon behind it. I expect the list to cover, at minimum: what the "one standard" could possibly be measuring when a house and a pair of shoes share no physical property; why it is demand, rather than labor, cost, or scarcity, that is said to hold everything together, and what that predicts when wants alter (a siege trebling the price of grain, a glut, an unfamiliar import); why exchange binds together people who are different and unequal — Aristotle's own example in the same chapter is a physician and a farmer, not two physicians — rather than merely duplicating what each could do alone; and how one and the same thing can serve as a stable common measure while also being a creature of convention that a ruler may alter, debase, or render useless at will while the underlying demand persists.

Then tell me which desiderata are non-negotiable — those whose denial would make the passage simply false — and which are the preferences of particular camps (commodity or metallist theories, state or chartalist theories, credit theories, subjective-utility accounts). If any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — the pairing of money as a fixed measure with money as conventionally revisable strikes me as the obvious candidate — say so plainly, since for my purposes that is itself the most useful result. Also say which single requirement excludes the most contenders, and note where Aristotle's own hedge in this chapter (he concedes that things differing so greatly cannot strictly be made commensurate, only sufficiently so for exchange) belongs in the list: requirement, concession, or escape clause. Close with a brief scoring of the four camps above against the desiderata, keeping it short — the specification is the deliverable. The standard of adequacy is this: a theory good enough that my city's monetary rules never contradict themselves when a character hoards coin, the treasury debases it, or a lender prices a loan.
