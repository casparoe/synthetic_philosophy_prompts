You are a philosopher of AI safety with a strong background in decision theory and game theory, and you have been asked to act as the neutral drafter for an adversarial collaboration. Before drafting, brainstorm freely: list candidate claims both sides might accept, candidate formulations of their disagreements, and possible cruxes. Then produce the polished document described below. Think carefully about each formulation -- the entire value of the exercise lies in wording that neither side would feel misrepresents it.

Here is the situation. Two researchers in my AI-safety reading group have been arguing for months about the shutdown problem for advanced agents. The first, whom I'll call the design optimist, holds that shutdown-ability is an engineering-tractable property: the formal results of the past decade show that an agent can be constructed so as to have no incentive either to resist shutdown or to seek it, with uncertainty about the human's objective doing the key work. The second, the structural pessimist, holds that consequentialist agency is inherently shutdown-averse, and that every formal fix achieves its result only inside idealizations -- a fixed off switch, a fixed action space, no channel by which the agent shapes what the human knows or believes, no self-modification or successor agents -- that a sufficiently capable system will violate precisely because it is capable. On her view the published models don't solve the shutdown problem; they relocate it into their assumptions.

Their argument keeps turning on a passage from Dylan Hadfield-Menell, Anca Dragan, Pieter Abbeel, and Stuart Russell's paper "The Off-Switch Game" (2017):

"Our observation is that this assumption is fundamentally violated when we consider an agent's off switch -- an agent that does not treat a 'switch-off' event as an observation that its utility estimate is incorrect is likely to have an incentive for self-preservation or an incentive to switch itself off."

(Note: in the passage above I have rendered the source's em dashes as double hyphens and its quotation marks as straight quotes; treat the content as verbatim.)

The optimist reads the passage as stating the solution in outline: make the agent treat a switch-off event as evidence about the true objective, and both horns of the dilemma are avoided. The pessimist reads the very same sentence as a confession: the dilemma -- resist shutdown or seek it -- is the default, and the proposed remedy hands the agent a new and dangerous object of optimization, namely the human's evidence, which a capable agent will want to manage rather than merely observe.

Draft the joint statement these two could both sign. It should contain:

1. An agreed-claims section, numbered. I expect it to include at least: what creates shutdown aversion in a naive reward maximizer (and what does not -- e.g., no survival instinct need be posited); why a naive shutdown reward instead produces a shutdown-seeking incentive; what utility-indifference constructions do and do not achieve; what the off-switch-game result establishes and on which assumptions it rests; and what both sides agree would count as the problem being genuinely solved. Every claim must be one both researchers would endorse without silent qualifications.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one disagreement in neutral wording, then give each side's position in that side's own preferred formulation -- wording you are confident the side in question would sign. I suspect the real disagreements include: whether the informativeness requirement (the human's press being genuine evidence about the objective) can survive an agent able to influence the human's information state; whether the models' idealizations are harmless abstractions or load-bearing in a way that breaks at high capability; and whether corrigibility-by-construction is in tension with the agent being a strong optimizer at all. Confirm, correct, or extend this list.

3. A cruxes section. For each crux, identify the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side, then name a concrete case, formal extension, or argument that both sides accept as bearing on it -- for instance, variants of the game in which the robot can act on the human's beliefs, or can build successors, or in which the human is known to be irrational -- and record in advance what each side predicts that case or extension would show. If for some crux no such shared test exists, say so explicitly rather than forcing one.

4. A final short section identifying any part of the dispute that is verbal -- for example, whether "corrigible" denotes observable shutdown-permitting behavior or a property robust to capability gains -- with a brief justification for classifying it as verbal rather than substantive.

The statement itself should run roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words; the quoted passage does not count toward this length. Do not adjudicate the dispute or declare a winner. The deliverable is a document both researchers could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact.
