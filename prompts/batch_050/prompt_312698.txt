A colleague and I co-direct the forecasting group at a policy institute, and he has begun using the following argument to settle hiring and staffing disputes. I find the conclusion impossible to accept, yet each step looks defensible in isolation, and I want a precise diagnosis rather than a vague feeling that something slipped.

(1) Rational agents update their degrees of belief by conditionalising on incoming evidence.
(2) There is a theorem in the Bayesian literature — the merging-of-opinions result associated with Blackwell and Dubins — which says, roughly, that if two such agents' priors are mutually absolutely continuous (neither assigns probability zero to any event the other treats as possible) and both observe the same growing stream of evidence, then with probability one their predictive distributions come arbitrarily close to one another.
(3) Mutual absolute continuity is a modest requirement: it asks only that an agent not rule out a priori anything a competent peer regards as a live possibility, which is simply a minimal condition of intellectual modesty that any decent analyst satisfies.
(4) Therefore, given enough shared data, priors wash out: the forecasts of competent analysts converge, and their initial disagreement leaves no trace in the long run.
(5) So when two of our analysts — both competent, both drawing on the same evidence feed — disagree persistently about some observable matter, such as whether a technology will be deployed within a decade or whether a recession is coming, at least one of them must be either dogmatic (violating (3)) or failing to update properly (violating (1)). Persistent disagreement among informed peers is always a symptom of a defect somewhere.
(6) Hence we should treat durable internal disagreement the way we treat arithmetic errors: hunt down the faulty prior or the faulty update and correct it, rather than staffing so as to preserve a diversity of prior views, which (4) shows to be epistemically inert in the long run anyway.

The conclusion strikes me as proving too much. Taken seriously, it implies that all long-run disagreement among informed peers — economists divided over inflation theory, physicists over the interpretation of quantum mechanics, our own analysts over nearly everything that matters — is a pathology that enough shared evidence must eventually close; yet such disputes show little sign of closing, and I doubt the explanation is that everyone involved is dogmatic or innumerate. Still, I cannot locate the weak step. (2) is a theorem, so I cannot touch it; (1) is close to definitional for a Bayesian; which leaves (3), (4), or the inference from (4) to (5), each of which looks innocuous to me.

Which step do you reject, and why did it look acceptable? If more than one step bears weight, say so. In each case, tell me what rejecting it costs: in particular, whether I can keep the modesty condition in (3), or the diagnostician's ambition in (6) that disagreement is always traceable to a fault, without being forced into the pathologising conclusion. If I am forced to choose, I would rather abandon (6) than (3), but I want the price of every option made explicit, including the option of accepting the argument wholesale.

Please answer in British English, in no more than 600 words.
