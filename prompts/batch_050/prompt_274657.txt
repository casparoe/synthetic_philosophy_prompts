I teach a seminar on Indian philosophy of language, and I'd like to close our unit on Mīmāṃsā with a poem to hand out at the final meeting. Write a sestina that takes up the school's two most distinctive commitments and lets the form argue for the first of them.

The first commitment: the Veda is apauruṣeya — authorless and beginningless. The seers did not compose the mantras; they perceived an order of sound and meaning that was always already there, as one might overhear rather than invent. There is no first speaker to whom the text can be traced, and the school takes this not as a defect but as the ground of its authority. The second commitment: testimony is intrinsically valid. A cognition or a heard sentence is true unless something defeats it; truth needs no external corroboration, while error always requires an extrinsic explanation — a defect in the instrument of knowledge, not in the word itself. The demand to know who first spoke, before trusting what was said, mistakes the situation.

Let the sestina's machinery do the philosophical work. Choose six end-words that can carry the first commitment across the fixed permutation — words for hearing, breath, fire, sight, sequence, and sound would serve, though you may substitute better ones — so that across the six stanzas the reader experiences a text whose words keep returning in an order no one chose, a structure preceding any speaker. The envoi should then turn on the second commitment: the speaker realizes that they have been trusting these words all along, and that the poem will not supply the one guarantee being asked for — an author, a first occasion, an original voice. Do not resolve whether that refusal is serene or vertiginous; it should land as both.

Constraints: no Sanskrit terms inside the poem itself ("the seers" is acceptable, technical vocabulary is not); no stanza may lecture — the ideas should surface through concrete images, such as a fire burning before any match was struck, or a sentence heard before any mouth; and the closing three lines should earn, rather than assert, the thought that a word can be true without anyone having said it first. A reader who knows the Mīmāṃsā position should recognize it in the poem's architecture, and a reader who doesn't should still find it a good poem.
