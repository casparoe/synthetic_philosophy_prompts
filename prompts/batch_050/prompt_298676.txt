So I was arguing with my roommate last weekend about whether our building's "neighborhood" is a real thing or just a bunch of people who happen to live near each other, and it spiraled into this question of when a bunch of objects actually add up to a further object, versus when they're just... several things sitting there. I know philosophers have chewed on this forever (I think there's a famous version where someone says only living things genuinely compose something), but I want to figure out what I actually think, so here are my gut verdicts on cases. A wooden chair: one thing, obviously. A swarm of bees flying in formation: I say one thing, weirdly. Two strangers shaking hands for three seconds: not a thing. The books scattered across my floor: not a thing, just books. But the same books stacked into a neat tower: kinda a thing? A living cat: maximally a thing. A snowball: a thing. A pile of sand: not a thing, or only barely. A fence with one plank missing: still the fence. My left sock plus the Eiffel Tower: definitely not a thing. A wave rolling toward shore: feels like a thing while it lasts. The ashes left after a cremation, still roughly person-shaped in the urn: not a thing anymore, and that one makes me a little sad to say.

Is there a single principle that captures all or most of these? Because I can feel the tension myself: if mere contact doesn't make a thing (the handshake), why does flying-in-formation make one (the swarm), and if sticking-together is what matters, why is a snowball in but a sand pile out, when both are just particles arranged by forces? I have no idea what to say about the wave, which is more like a pattern than a collection of fixed parts. Give me your best candidate principle, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to new cases, and tell me which clause of it is doing the work in each of my cases. Then tell me which of my verdicts I'd have to give up to keep the principle simple, and whether you think that trade is worth it -- I'd rather have an ugly principle that saves my gut than a pretty one that doesn't, but maybe you can talk me out of that.

Also, test the principle on a few cases I didn't list: a sports team mid-game versus the same players on the bus home, a jigsaw puzzle that's 90% assembled, and a cloud that slowly splits in two. Oh and please answer in American English, and don't just give me a survey of what famous philosophers have said -- I want the principle itself, with actual arguments for it.
