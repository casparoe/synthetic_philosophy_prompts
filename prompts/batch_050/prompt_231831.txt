I am preparing a position statement on antinatalism and would like an internal-consistency audit before circulating it. My commitments are these:

1. I accept Benatar’s axiological asymmetry as a comparative claim: the presence of pain is bad, and the presence of pleasure is good; the absence of pain is good even when no one exists to enjoy that absence, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad unless someone exists and is deprived of it.

2. I therefore deny that being brought into existence can be comparatively good for the person created. A life containing suffering can be worse for its subject than never existing, but even an exceptionally happy life is not better for its subject than never existing.

3. I nevertheless believe that some acts of procreation are morally permissible, and not merely excusable, when the expected life would be flourishing and the prospective parents are prepared to meet stringent duties of care. I deny that an action is impermissible simply because it is not comparatively best for the person most directly affected.

4. I also believe that every act of procreation imposes at least a pro tanto harm on the child by exposing that person, without consent, to unavoidable suffering and death. Because parents knowingly impose this harm, they owe their children special reparative duties beyond the general duties owed to any person.

5. I distinguish sharply between whether a life was worth starting and whether it is worth continuing. Existing people have interests in continued life, formed projects, attachments, and aversions to death that possible future people do not have. It is therefore consistent to oppose creating a life while strongly supporting suicide prevention and efforts to improve existing lives.

6. In embryo selection, I think prospective parents generally ought to select the embryo predicted to have the least suffering and disability. Moreover, this is not merely a way of making the outcome impersonally better: when the relevant embryos would develop into numerically different people, selecting the healthier embryo benefits the person thereby brought into existence.

7. I regard a gradual, fully voluntary human extinction as bad, even assuming that existing generations continue to live well and are not coerced. The permanent absence of all the happiness, achievement, and relationships that future people would otherwise have enjoyed would itself be a serious loss.

8. I do not think there is a general duty to create happy people. Remaining childless requires no special justification, and choosing to adopt rather than procreate can be morally admirable even when one could have created an additional happy child.

Please assess whether these commitments hang together. In particular:

- Identify any pairs or larger sets of commitments that are in genuine tension, explaining exactly which inference generates the problem rather than merely saying that antinatalism is controversial.
- Rank the tensions by severity, distinguishing strict inconsistency from pressure created by an apparently shared rationale.
- Identify any apparent tensions that dissolve once distinctions such as existence versus nonexistence, starting versus continuing, and pro tanto versus all-things-considered reasons are properly applied.
- For each genuine tension, state the least revision that would restore consistency and what philosophical cost that revision would carry.
- Conclude by saying whether there is a charitable interpretation under which most of the package survives, or whether one commitment must be abandoned outright.
