i work for a nonprofit that certifies workplaces, sort of like a fair trade label but for labor conditions. we run maybe two audits a month, and the certificate matters -- unions use it in organizing fights and two big retail buyers wont sign contracts without it. our checklist covers the measurable stuff (wages, hours, safety, harassment policies) but the workers we interview keep describing something it doesnt capture, and the closest thing to it that ive found is marx on alienation in the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844. the passage from the estranged labor section i keep coming back to is this one:

"The product of labor is labor which has been embodied in an object, which has become material: it is the objectification of labor. Labor's realization is its objectification. Under these economic conditions this realization of labor appears as loss of realization for the workers; objectification as loss of the object and bondage to it; appropriation as estrangement, as alienation."

here's what i take the crux to be: making something external isnt itself the problem, since every worker in every system objectifies their labor -- a co-op member baking bread she co-owns and a factory hand baking the same bread both do that. the bad part is a specific inversion where the made thing turns back against the maker. so my instrument has to operationalize that distinction or it will flag literally all work as alienated, which is useless.

what i want: an audit procedure my team (not philosophers -- organizers and ex-HR people) can run over a three-day site visit with interviews and floor observation. it should score a workplace on each of marx's four modes of alienation (product, activity, species-being, relation to other workers, or whatever the standard breakdown is), giving for each one the observable indicators and two or three interview questions, what counts as enough evidence to score it high or low, and the order to run the tests in. mark explicitly the points where the procedure has to stop and hand the call to a human because its a values question -- e.g. im guessing control over the work vs. legal ownership of the product is one of those. and be honest about what it cant do: i doubt you can detect exploitation in the surplus-value sense from interviews alone, so say what falls outside the instrument's reach.

then run it on three cases: (1) a fulfillment warehouse where the routing and pacing software was trained on the workers own movement data and now disciplines them; (2) a worker-owned bakery co-op everyone loves where the actual work is repetitive, loud and dull; (3) a well-paid game studio engineer with huge creative freedom who owns none of what she makes and crunches 70-hour weeks before launch. at least one of these should come out with a score i wont like -- tell me which and why.

last thing: where people who actually know this literature disagree -- like whether alienation is an objective fact about the labor relation or a psychological state of the worker (my whole instrument rides on that one), whether marx's exploitation is a moral accusation or a neutral description, whether the 1844 manuscripts even represent the mature marx -- flag those as genuinely contested and dont present one reading as the consensus. where something is just settled, say so plainly.
