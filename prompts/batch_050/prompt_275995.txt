I am writing a paper on the paradox of deontology — the charge that a constraint against killing is irrational, or at least morally self-defeating, if it forbids killing one person even to prevent five killings of the very same kind. Before I assess existing replies, I want the specification they should be measured against. Write the desiderata an account of deontological constraints must meet to count as resolving the paradox rather than merely restating it: the cases it must get right (trolley-style variants, threshold cases where the number of killings prevented grows enormous, and the difference between intending harm and merely foreseeing it), the distinctions it must either vindicate or explicitly dissolve (doing versus allowing, using someone as a means, agent-relative versus agent-neutral value), and the results it must avoid (collapsing into a duty to kill whenever the numbers favor it, or moralizing every personal preference). For each requirement, give the consideration that motivates it, and mark which desiderata you take to be jointly unsatisfiable — in particular, whether any account can both justify the constraint from the standpoint of the potential victim and permit a threshold override in catastrophic cases without becoming consequentialism under another name. Then, briefly, score the main families of reply against the list: constraints as inviolable moral statuses, agent-relative reasons grounded in one's special relation to one's own acts, and the claim that the paradox dissolves once the value of persons is understood as something other than a quantity to be maximized. Fix the purpose: the account should be good enough to underwrite constraints in criminal law, not merely to comfort moral philosophers.
