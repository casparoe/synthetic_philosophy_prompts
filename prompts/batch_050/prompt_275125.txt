I have been reading rationalist blogs and EA-adjacent forums for well over a decade, which means I have now sat through what feels like two hundred iterations of the same exchange about Singer's drowning child: one side wheels out the pond, the other side wheels out demandingness, and everyone leaves with the views they arrived with. I would like you to produce something more constructive. Draft the joint statement that a serious, good-faith adversarial collaboration between the following two camps could actually sign:

- Camp A: a Singer-style consequentialist of the effective-altruist sort. This camp holds that the pond case generalizes: if you can prevent a death or a comparably grave harm at a cost to yourself that is insignificant by comparison, you are morally required to do so, and it does not matter whether the victim is in front of you or on another continent, whether they are identifiable or statistical, or whether the threat is a one-off emergency or a standing condition.
- Camp B: a moderate deontologist who fully accepts the duty to wade into the pond but denies that the principle behind it generalizes into anything like Camp A's demanding conclusion. On this view, duties of rescue are real but limited and agent-relative, sensitive to features Camp A dismisses as noise: cost to the agent, special relationships, the difference between emergencies and ongoing conditions, and the claims of one's own projects.

The document should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. These must be neither vacuous nor slanted. Include at minimum: a formulation of what the pond case itself establishes that both camps accept; an acknowledgment from Camp A about which costs genuinely count as morally significant; and an acknowledgment from Camp B that some of the features they appeal to (physical distance taken entirely on its own, say) are hard to defend as intrinsically morally relevant.

2. A disagreements table with columns: the crux; Camp A's formulation of its own position; Camp B's formulation of its own position; the case or argument both accept as bearing on the crux; and each side's prediction, recorded in advance. Each side's position must be phrased in wording that side would actually accept — no strawman versions the other camp would disown. The table must address at least: (a) whether an indefinitely repeated condition of preventable suffering is morally equivalent to a one-off emergency, i.e., the pond that keeps refilling; (b) the coordination problem among many potential rescuers, each reasoning that others will step in; (c) whether the difference between rescuing an identifiable victim and averting a statistical death is a moral difference or merely a psychological one; and (d) whether an institutional division of moral labor could discharge what would otherwise be an impossibly demanding individual duty.

3. A short final section in which each side states what, if anything, would change its mind. If either position turns out to be movable by no case or argument at all, the document should say so plainly rather than papering over it.

Where a disagreement appears to dissolve on analysis into two claims each side could separately accept, flag it as verbal and show the decomposition. And please go beyond the standard forum talking points; I have read them all.

Two epistemic requests. First, attach to each agreed claim a confidence level (high, medium, or low, with a sentence of justification wherever it is not high) that fair-minded representatives of both camps would in fact sign it. Second, at the end, single out the one element of the whole document — an agreed claim, a formulation of a disagreement, or a proposed test — that you are least confident both sides would accept, and explain why.

Format: the numbered agreed-claims section, then the disagreements table, then the what-would-move-us section. The very last line of your response must take the form: Verdict: <one sentence naming the single crux that would do the most to move the debate if settled, or stating that no such crux exists because the disagreement is a bedrock clash of values>.
