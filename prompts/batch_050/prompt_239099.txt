You are a professional philosopher specializing in effective altruism, uncertainty, and institutional decision-making. Think carefully, and start by briefly brainstorming the hidden assumptions before giving your main answer.

Our donor commitee has been trying to take the measurability-bias critique seriously. The worry is that funders systematically favor interventions with crisp, near-term metrics over things like policy reform, community building, or reducing long-run catastrophic risks, where the causal chain is diffuse and success may not show up for decades.

So we proposed an “opacity premium.” A highly measurable project gets its ordinary expected-value estimate. A moderately hard-to-measure project gets multiplied by 2. A genuinely opaque project gets multiplied by 10, using the best available reference-class estimate. If there is no quantitative estimate at all, we do not assign zero; we assign the average value of similarly ambitious projects already in our portfolio. The rationale is that the measurable estimate is only a lower bound, because it leaves out unmeasured benefits by construction, and refusing to correct for that would recreate the original bias.

A critic says this just treats ignorance as evidence of huge upside. But that seems question-begging. If we know our measurement process is systematically blind to certain kinds of value, shouldn’t a rational decision procedure adjust upward when those kinds of value are especially likely to matter? Isn’t using the unadjusted estimate effectively pretending the unmeasured effects are zero?

What exactly is wrong with our reasoning, if anything? Please distinguish evidence about an intervention from artifacts of how interventions are selected and reported, and distinguish uncertainty inside a model from uncertainty about which model is even appropriate. Also explain whether an unmeasured effect should be treated as symmetrically positive and negative, as zero, or as a reason to widen the uncertainty range without changing the central estimate.

Finally, propose a better grantmaking policy for three cases: a cash-transfer program with good measurement, a governance reform with plausible but hard-to-model effects, and a movement-building project whose main value is an option on future opportunities. I’m looking for a principled framework, not citations or a reading list.
