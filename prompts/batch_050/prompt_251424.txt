I teach a third-year module in environmental and applied ethics, and for next term I'm bolting on a week about the ethics of space colonisation, partly because my students keep derailing seminars with Mars questions and I'd rather channel it than fight it. I want to build the week around a real case rather than the usual terraforming thought experiments, and the obvious candidate is the Starlink saga. Since 2019 SpaceX has put thousands of satellites into low Earth orbit, with tens of thousands more authorised or planned; astronomers found their images streaked with satellite trails almost overnight, radio astronomers have their own grievances, there have been near-misses and formal complaints from other spacefaring states, and the whole thing was green-lit by one country's regulators without anything resembling global consultation. Meanwhile the night sky itself, which was arguably the one bit of 'space' every human being had in common, now visibly belongs in part to a private company.

Analyse this episode through the lens of the ethics of space colonisation, or through the question of whether space is a commons, if you think that's the more fruitful frame. Some things I want the analysis to do: who had standing to object here, and who was actually asked? The 1967 Outer Space Treaty says the exploration and use of outer space "shall be the province of all mankind" and shall be carried out "for the benefit and in the interests of all countries" — did that language do any work in this episode, or does the episode show it was always empty? Does the colonisation literature even apply, given nobody is settling anything and the 'colony' is a swarm of robots a few hundred kilometres up — or is that precisely the point, that the first large-scale appropriation of space happened in Earth orbit while everyone was busy arguing about Mars habitats and planetary protection? And please run it the other way too: what does the episode show about the theory? My hunch is the space-ethics literature was caught flat-footed by a case about orbits and sky access rather than settlement, contamination and indigenous rights analogies, but I'd like that hunch tested rather than confirmed.

Also, be disciplined about where the framework is genuinely silent instead of forcing it to pronounce. The collision-risk and Kessler-syndrome worries, for instance, look prudential rather than ethical at first glance — are they, or does the commons frame recapture them? If some objections (aesthetics, astronomy, safety, equity of access) turn out to need different theoretical treatment from each other, say so rather than lumping them. British English please, and keep it to a length I could actually hand out as a seminar reading note.
