Okay so I'm in this reading group on decision theory and effective altruism, and last week's session kind of broke my brain. We were discussing "fanaticism" — the idea that expected value reasoning apparently says that a tiny probability of an astronomically huge payoff (saving 10^40 future lives, creating a galaxy of utopia, whatever) can outweigh a guaranteed large good, as long as the probability isn't literally zero. Someone brought up the Pascal's mugging thought experiment as the reductio: a stranger on the street claims that unless you give him five bucks, he'll use his magical powers to torture trillions of people, and naive EV math says hand over the money since even a microscopic chance of that threat being real dwarfs the five dollars.

Here's what's bugging me. Everyone in the group treated this as a fatal objection to expected value maximization — basically, "EV says pay the mugger, paying the mugger is absurd, therefore EV is false, case closed." But then, no joke, twenty minutes later the same people were arguing that we should prioritize existential risk reduction over global health charities *because* when you multiply even a small probability of averting extinction by the staggering number of future lives at stake, the expected value wins going away. And I raised my hand and said, isn't that just... the mugger's argument again, structurally speaking? A tiny probability times a vast number of lives dominating the calculation? I got some hand-wavy responses about how x-risk probabilities are "more credible" but nobody could cash that out.

So which is it? If fanaticism refutes expected value reasoning, why is it still legitimate for my groupmates to use EV arithmetic to pick cause areas — and if it doesn't refute it, why were we all so confident the mugger should be refused? I genuinely can't tell whether my group was being inconsistent, or whether there's some principled line between "legitimate" tiny-probability-huge-stakes arguments (extinction risk) and "illegitimate" ones (muggers), and if there is, what it's actually doing the work — something about how the probability estimate was generated, whether the claimed probability shrinks as the claimed payoff grows, bounded utility functions, game-theoretic incentives about making yourself immune to threats, or what? Please untangle this for me, because right now it seems like everyone just accepts longshot EV arguments when they like the conclusion and calls them "fanatical" when they don't.
