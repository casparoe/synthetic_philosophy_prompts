I want to run a session on the old claim that history is written by the victors, but I want my students to watch the skew happen inside a record they themselves produced, rather than hear it argued. The setting: a second-year undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of history, twenty students, two ninety-minute meetings exactly one week apart, plus a short written reflection after the second meeting. I give no lecture beforehand, and the slogan itself is never mentioned until the final debrief.

Design the activity to the following specifications.

The overall shape I have in mind: in the first meeting, the class generates a contested event together with its surviving record; in the second, after the week's gap, a subgroup that witnessed none of it must reconstruct what happened from what survived. You fill in the game.

First requirement: phase one should produce a genuine winner and loser rather than assigned ones — some contest or structured struggle whose outcome is in doubt until it ends, so that the winners feel a real, earned sense of entitlement to the story. The losers should accumulate actual grievances in play (moments where the winners cut corners or benefited from a ruling), because I want the record's eventual skew to be created by the participants' own choices, not scripted in.

Second: between the event and the archive, include a documentation phase with binding resource constraints — only so many minutes of testimony recorded, only so many items preserved — such that the winners end up controlling what is kept, and such that even a scrupulously honest archivist in their position must make choices that quietly favor the winners' framing. The intended discovery is that an archive can be badly distorted without anyone having told a single lie.

Third: the historian subgroup works a week later, from the archive alone, under an incentive that rewards a clean and coherent narrative (their account will be judged by a neutral audience on clarity and plausibility). This is the third pressure I want made visible, alongside deliberate spin and structural selection: the narrative demands of later telling. Ideally the three mechanisms are traceable to distinct moments in the game, so the debrief can point at each one separately.

Fourth: the debrief. Map particular moments from the session onto the three mechanisms; state the two or three ways this will predictably go wrong when actually run — students who see through the design and ham up their villainy, historians who smell a trick and overcorrect into paranoid readings, winners whose genuine righteousness makes the debrief feel like an accusation — and how I should handle each. Be frank about which features of the real phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce: real stakes, generational time, power that enforces itself rather than being assigned by a facilitator. Tell me what I must therefore supply by ordinary talk afterwards, and how to keep that talk from undoing what the game taught.

Fifth: state concretely what participants should come away seeing. My targets: that a skewed record requires no bad faith; that the winners' perspective becomes the neutral default against which the losers' account reads as merely biased; and that later reconstructors are typically rationally constrained by what survives rather than gullible.

Keep the contest itself low-stakes and non-political — no reenacting real oppression; invent something neutral. Provide timings, materials, role instructions, the exact rules of the archiving constraint, and the spine of the debrief script.
