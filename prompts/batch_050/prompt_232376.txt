I am a PhD candidate in philosophy, working on the epistemology of historical knowledge, and I am drafting a dissertation chapter that tries to give a principled answer to the question of what separates legitimate historical revisionism from denialism. My working proposal is that the distinction cannot rest on conclusions (how heterodox a claim is) but on patterns of reasoning, and I have a tentative taxonomy of denialist signature moves: selective quotation and decontextualization of sources, impossible or asymmetric standards of proof (demanding a single smoking-gun document while accepting far weaker evidence for claims one favors), systematic shifting of the burden of proof, conspiracy explanations for the consensus of the relevant experts, and a self-presentation as the persecuted Galileo figure. Against this, revisionism is just the ordinary self-correction of historiography -- new archives, revised estimates, overturned consensus views -- and I want to insist that revisionists and deniers often dispute the very same propositions, so the criterion has to be diagnostic of method rather than of content.

I would like you to stress-test this framework against the Irving v. Lipstadt libel trial, decided in London in 2000, which I take to be the best-documented real-world case of the revisionism/denialism line being drawn in practice. The facts as I understand them: the writer David Irving, who had done genuine archival work on the Second World War and enjoyed a measure of mainstream respect early in his career, sued Deborah Lipstadt and her publisher over her book's characterization of him as a Holocaust denier; because English libel law placed the burden of proof on the defendants, Lipstadt's legal team had to demonstrate the truth of the claim, and their historians conducted an exhaustive audit of Irving's published work, documenting mistranslations, omissions, and misrepresentations of source material; Irving represented himself and cross-examined the expert witnesses; the court found decisively for the defendants. Please map my framework onto this episode carefully: which of the signature moves were actually on display in the courtroom and in the audit, and which were absent or inapplicable? Then press on the strain points. First, Irving had made some real archival discoveries, so if my criterion is about method, how does the framework handle a mixed case -- does the defense's finding that his errors systematically ran in one political direction suggest that directionality of error, rather than error itself, is the real diagnostic, and is that a coherent epistemic criterion or a disguised appeal to motive? Second, my account says denialism is diagnosed by reasoning patterns, yet the trial proceeded largely by forensic fact-checking of particular claims; does the episode show that the content-test and the method-test coincide in practice, or that they can come apart?

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode reveal about the framework itself? The verdict depended on establishing that Irving's distortions were deliberate, which suggests that any workable revisionism/denialism distinction may need a motive condition -- but motives are notoriously hard to prove, and I am unsure whether that is a fatal cost or a virtue of the account. I would also like your judgment on whether a courtroom, with its adversarial structure and winner-take-all verdict, was a good instrument for drawing this line at all, or whether the trial's success is ungeneralizable; a colleague argues the distinction is purely sociological -- deniers are simply those the expert community excommunicates -- and I want to know whether the trial vindicates an epistemic criterion over her view, or accidentally supports her. If space permits, say a word about whether an analysis built around an individual litigant like Irving transfers to state-sponsored denial, as with the Armenian genocide, where the structure of the enterprise looks quite different.
