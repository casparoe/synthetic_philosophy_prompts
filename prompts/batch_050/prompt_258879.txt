Take the placebo-controlled AZT trials for preventing mother-to-child HIV transmission, run in Thailand and parts of sub-Saharan Africa in the mid-1990s, and analyse them through the lens of clinical equipoise. The set-up as I understand it: a 1994 US trial had already shown that a long course of AZT cut transmission by roughly two-thirds, and the African and Thai trials then randomised women to shorter, cheaper regimens or to placebo, on the grounds that the long regimen was unaffordable locally and nobody yet knew whether a short course worked. Critics said the placebo arm was indefensible once an effective treatment existed; defenders said the local standard of care was nothing at all, so placebo was fine. What I want: where exactly equipoise was supposed to be doing the justificatory work, whether the local-standard defence actually invokes equipoise or quietly swaps in a different principle, what the episode did to the concept itself (did it refine it, expose a fatal ambiguity, or show it was never the right tool for international research), and whether the fact that the trials eventually did find a working short-course regimen changes the verdict at all. Also, please be clear about what's now broadly settled in research ethics versus what's still genuinely contested — don't present one side of the standard-of-care debate as the consensus if it isn't.
