I am a PhD student in philosophy of language, serving as the conceptual consultant to an interdisciplinary project that studies possible communication systems. Roughly twice a year, the group must rule on whether some newly recorded stream of behavior — animal vocalizations, the outputs of an artificial system, exchanges within an uncontacted human community — should be treated as a language and subjected to interpretation, or modeled purely statistically as structured behavior. Each error is expensive: premature interpretation can waste years of fieldwork, while premature refusal can close off a genuine discovery. The principal investigator has asked me to write a standing decision procedure, grounded in Davidson's account of radical interpretation, specifying what evidence must be in place, and in what order, before the group is licensed to assign meanings and beliefs to the behavior at all.

Write that procedure. It should spell out the tests to run in sequence — for example, establishing stable behavioral correlates of the signal, finding a common worldly cause that signaler and interpreter can be triangulated on, applying the principle of charity as a working policy rather than a discovered truth, and managing the holism that ties meaning attribution to belief attribution — and state what counts as passing or failing each test. It should mark where the procedure must stop and defer to human judgment because the question is constitutive rather than empirical, and it should say what it cannot settle (for instance, choosing between rival interpretation schemes that fit the evidence equally well). The procedure must be usable by field researchers who have not read Davidson, fit on about a page, and flag its judgment calls rather than hiding them inside checklist items.

Then apply it to two cases our group currently faces: (1) a marine mammal's vocal repertoire with rich, compressible internal structure but no observable correlation between parts of the signal and anything in the animals' environment or behavior; and (2) the fluent, open-ended text produced by a large generative model trained on human writing, which answers questions plausibly but has no ongoing causal contact with the subject matter it describes. For each case, give the procedure's verdict, and flag any point where that verdict is one my collaborators will resist — for instance, where the procedure withholds the status of speech from a system that seems to talk.
