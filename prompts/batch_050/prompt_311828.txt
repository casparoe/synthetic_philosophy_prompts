I'm a PhD student working on the intersection of philosophy of mind and population ethics, and I've been circling a question that came up during a reading group on panpsychism and that I can't seem to settle for myself. Suppose some form of panpsychism or micropsychism is true: the fundamental physical entities posited by our best physics possess proto-experiential or phenomenal properties of some kind. My question is not whether that thesis is true in general, but something more specific: what is your credence that there is morally relevant suffering at the level of fundamental physical processes -- that is, states of microphysical systems that are intrinsically bad in the way paradigmatic suffering is bad, such that a universe containing them is, other things equal, worse for containing them?

Please don't answer with a bare number or with the usual move of saying it depends on your theory of consciousness. I want the decomposition. Break the question into the sub-judgments your final credence actually rests on -- I'd suggest at minimum: the probability that some micro-experiential view of consciousness is correct; the probability, conditional on that, that micro-experiences have valence at all rather than being affectively neutral proto-phenomenal qualities; the probability, conditional on valence existing at that level, that it is negative often enough to matter rather than rare or symmetrical with pleasure; and the probability that micro-level valenced states aggregate or persist in a way that is ethically significant rather than being so fleeting, fragmented, or subject to combination-problem worries that they wash out. Give a rough probability for each term, a sentence of justification for each, and show how you combine them. You are welcome to add or replace terms if you think I've carved the space wrongly.

Two further requests. First, tell me which term in your decomposition is doing the most work -- the one where reasonable disagreement would most change the final number -- and what kind of argument or evidence, if any, could actually shift it, given that we plainly cannot run experiments on micro-phenomenology. Second, my own credence is quite low, around 0.05, mostly because I think conditional on panpsychism the valence and aggregation terms are each quite unlikely; a colleague puts hers at 0.4, largely because she thinks any sufficiently general theory of consciousness makes valenced micro-states probable. Diagnose where our disagreement most likely lives, given your decomposition, and say which of us is making the more defensible judgment at each contested step.
