I run the corrections desk at a national news organization, and each week my team fields dozens of reader challenges to our reporting. Some dispute particular evidence (a misquoted official, a misread statistic); others deny that the kinds of evidence journalism rests on — government records, photographs, expert testimony, on-the-ground reporting — could ever establish anything. Our current policy of treating every challenge as an ordinary factual dispute is failing: the second kind of challenge is never settled by supplying more citations, and answering it that way seems to make things worse. I've been reading about hinge epistemology — the idea, developed from Wittgenstein's On Certainty, that doubt and inquiry only make sense against a background of commitments that stand fast without themselves being justified, so that challenging a hinge is not a move inside the evidence-game but a demand to change games — and I want it turned into a triage procedure my editors can run on any challenge in under fifteen minutes. Give the questions to ask in order, what counts as a settling answer to each, where the procedure hands off to individual judgment because no rule applies, and how the response should differ between an ordinary dispute and a hinge challenge, including what a good-faith response to the latter even looks like. Then apply the procedure to three cases: a reader who says we misquoted a mayor; a reader who holds that no mortality figure from any government can be trusted; and a case of your own choosing where the procedure gives an answer I will not like.

Structure the response as: The Distinction, Briefly (about 150 words); The Triage Procedure (numbered steps, about 400 words); Worked Applications (about 350 words); Where the Procedure Fails (about 150 words).
