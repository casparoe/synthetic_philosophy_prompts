In the novel I am drafting, a junior senator defends her minimal-tax platform in a televised debate by retelling Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain story: a million spectators each freely pay a small surcharge to watch the great man play, he ends the season far richer than anyone else, and since every transfer was voluntary, the resulting inequality cannot be unjust — so redistributive taxation must be an interference with free choices. Her opponent, an economist, needs a rebuttal that engages the argument rather than sneering at it, and I will not write the scene until I know where the argument actually buckles. Audit the thought experiment as an analogy for a modern economy: what maps onto what — the surcharge, Chamberlain's talent, the pre-game distribution everyone supposedly started from — and which features of real markets have no counterpart in the story, such as inherited endowments, market power, luck, cumulative advantage, and the background rules that define what counts as a voluntary transfer in the first place. Then tell me whether the conclusion the senator draws — that any patterned distribution requires ongoing coercion, so liberty and equality of outcome cannot both be honored — survives once those disanalogies are on the table, or whether it holds only for one-shot exchanges between equally situated adults and collapses when generalized to a whole society. I want the economist's reply to strike the argument's true weak point, not a strawman, so be exact about which premise does the failing.
