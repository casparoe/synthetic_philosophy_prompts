Question: State Stalnaker's Thesis concerning indicative conditionals, and explain how Lewis's triviality result threatens it. Does the result show that indicative conditionals do not express propositions?

Student's answer:

Stalnaker's Thesis says that for an indicative conditional "If A, then B," the probability of the conditional equals the conditional probability of B given A. Lewis (1976) showed that if this equation is required to hold not just for a probability function P but also for functions obtained by conditionalizing P, triviality follows: applying the thesis to P(A→B | C) and partitioning on B and not-B forces P(A→B) to equal P(B), so conditionals become probabilistically idle, and iterating the argument collapses the probability function to only a few distinct values. Stalnaker himself later proved this result, which is why he abandoned the thesis.

The triviality argument also refutes the material-conditional analysis of indicatives, since P(A ⊃ B) = P(¬A ∨ B), which is always at least P(B | A) and usually greater.

Lewis concluded from the result that indicative conditionals are not propositions and lack truth values, a conclusion Adams developed with his assertibility account of conditionals. The only remaining alternative is a dynamic or contextualist semantics, but those approaches are also subject to the triviality proof, since they still assign conditionals probabilities.
