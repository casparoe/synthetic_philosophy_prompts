so I'm trying to figure out whether modern Stoicism's favorite slogan, the dichotomy of control, can be turned into an actual theory rather than a poster quote, and before I read anyone's version of it I want the spec first. here's the passage that convinced me the slogan undersells the thing. Epictetus, Enchiridion ch. 4 (George Long translation), on how to approach an ordinary trip to the baths:

"If you are going to bathe, picture to yourself the things which usually happen in the bath: some people splash the water, some push, some use abusive language, and others steal."

and then the kicker, what you say to yourself when the bath goes wrong:

"It was not only to bathe that I desired, but to keep my mind in a state conformable to nature; and I will not keep it if I am bothered at things that happen."

notice what's going on here: not a sorting of the world into two boxes, but one action carrying two nested goals with different success-conditions, where the outer goal can't be taken from you. ok so: what would a theory of the dichotomy of control — better, of what's "up to us" — have to get right to be adequate? adequate for actually guiding what a person should try for, want, and hold themselves responsible for, not just for winning arguments. give me the desiderata as a numbered list, each with the case or consideration that forces it onto the list. some candidates I suspect belong there, but push back if any of these are prejudices I've picked up:

- it has to explain the bathhouse move: how a single action can have an external goal (getting clean) and an internal one (keeping the mind conformable to nature), and why the theory isn't just the advice to care about fewer things
- it has to say something non-embarrassing about cases where even judgments and intentions don't feel up to us — depression, intrusive thoughts, addiction, manipulation. does the boundary of the self do the work there, or the boundary of control, and are those the same line?
- it must not conflate three things people constantly merge: what I control, what I merely influence, and what I only care about. Irvine's "trichotomy of control" is an attempted fix here — is that an improvement or an admission the original dichotomy was wrong?
- it has to say what the dichotomy is even a theory OF — responsibility, attention, or value — because a theory of one of these can't quietly do duty as a theory of the others
- it should make contact with the archer-style point that doing everything right and hitting the target come apart, without making trying and succeeding totally unrelated (otherwise why practice archery)
- it has to deal with the fact that "whatever are our own actions" arguably includes basically everything I deliberately do, including bathing badly

then: which single desideratum knocks out the most versions people actually defend — the bumper-sticker version, the therapy/self-help version, the scholarly fate-and-assent version? and flag any pair of requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied. my hunch is that "give clean verdicts about what I'm responsible for" and "respect how messy agancy looks from the inside" pull against each other, but tell me if I'm wrong about that. you can score two or three live versions against the list briefly at the end, but the list itself is the deliverable.
