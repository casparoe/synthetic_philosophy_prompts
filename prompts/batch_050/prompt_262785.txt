Last night I had a long argument with a friend that ended in stalemate, and I'd like an outside adjudication — or at least a clearer map of the territory.

The setup: my employer offers a free genetic screening panel covering several late-onset conditions. It is genuinely free, the results are confidential, and there is no obligation to act on them. I am inclined to decline. My friend finds this position absurd, and her argument took the form of an analogy she kept returning to: turning down free information is exactly like turning down free money. Nobody tears up a check made out to them; if you don't want to spend it, put it in a drawer, but refusing it outright is leaving value on the table. On that basis, she says, my decision should be automatic — take the test, then decide later what to do with the results. When I objected that information is not like money, she raised the stakes and claimed that formal decision theory actually proves her side: an ideally rational agent, she said, can never be made worse off in expectation by accepting costless evidence, so my reluctance must be a failure of rationality rather than a genuine consideration.

My own hesitations, for what they are worth: I am about to apply for life insurance and have a vague worry that test results could complicate that; I suspect I would spend my remaining healthy years dreading a bad result in a way that would make my life worse whether or not the disease ever arrives; and I find her suggestion that I can just put the results in a drawer naive, because you cannot un-know something the way you can leave a check uncashed.

Here is what I would like from you, in the following sections and in this order. Please write in American English.

1. Bottom line (three to four sentences): does the free-money analogy, together with the decision-theoretic argument she bolted onto it, settle the question in her favor?

2. Correspondence table (a compact table): what plays the role of the money, of accepting the check, of the drawer, of the option to spend it later — lay out explicitly what her analogy maps onto what in my situation.

3. Where the mapping breaks (roughly 250 words): the disanalogies between free money and free information. Separate the ones that matter for her intended conclusion from the ones that are true but irrelevant, and state which of my three hesitations track real disanalogies and which dissolve under scrutiny.

4. The theorem behind her claim (roughly 200 words): she gestured at a result to the effect that costless evidence can never harm an ideal decision-maker in expectation. State what such a result actually requires — its assumptions about the agent, about the costs of acquiring and processing information, about what the information is allowed to affect, and about the surrounding environment — and assess whether my situation meets those assumptions. If the result has a standard name or origin, mention it, but do not rest anything on my having relayed her version correctly.

5. Does her conclusion survive? (roughly 150 words): with the disanalogies on the table, should I take the test? If you think she was right for reasons her analogy was not, say so plainly.

6. A better analogy (two to three sentences): if the free-money picture fails, propose a comparison that fits free information better.
