I overheard two grad students in my department (history) arguing in the break room last week and it's been stuck in my head since. The question was whether historians can fairly judge people of the past by today's moral standards. I'll reconstruct the exchange as best I remember it -- I'd like you to referee it move by move.

(1) Priya opened with: you can't morally condemn, say, an 18th-century slaveholding planter, because he was a man of his time. Judging him by 21st-century standards is anachronism, no better than faulting medieval surgeons for not sterilizing their instruments. The moral framework simply wasn't available to him in the way it's available to us.

(2) Marcus replied that "the standards of his time" is doing a lot of sneaky work, because his time contained more than one standard. Abolitionists existed back then -- people who were writing, publishing, petitioning, and in some cases freeing slaves and saying loudly that the whole institution was a crime. So it isn't that the man lacked access to the anti-slavery position. He lived alongside it and chose the position that happened to be profitable. "His time" had both standards; he picked one.

(3) Priya gave two replies. First: the abolitionists were a small, marginal, often religiously eccentric fringe, and you can't blame someone for failing to join the fringe of their era -- there are fringes right now whose arguments you and I don't take seriously, and future people might be wrong to blame us for that. Second: even if abolitionist arguments were physically available to him -- pamphlets existed, speeches were given -- available isn't the same as accessible. Whether an argument strikes you as compelling depends on your whole background picture of the world. The pamphlet was on the shelf the way a math proof is "available" to someone who never learned the prerequisites.

(4) Marcus: fine, then don't judge him by our standards -- judge him by his own. This is a man who professed the universal brotherhood of man, natural rights, equality before God, whatever the era's version of that was. He fails by internal standards. Condemning him isn't anachronism, it's just noticing hypocrisy. And historians do exactly this with non-moral failures all the time: we don't say a doctor who ignored the best evidence of his own era is beyond criticism just because germ theory hadn't won yet. We say he fell short of the methods his own time made available. Why should moral failures get an exemption intellectual failures don't?

(5) Priya's last move: the internal-inconsistency standard proves way too much. Literally everyone is inconsistent with their own professed values -- that's a boring fact about humans, us included. If falling short of your own stated ideals is blameworthy, then everyone in every era is blameworthy and the standard stops discriminating between anyone. You've just traded anachronism for a razor that condemns the whole species equally, which is useless for a historian trying to say anything about this man in particular.

At that point they both had to run to a seminar. Please adjudicate: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification for each rating. And if there's something both of them are missing -- some distinction neither one drew -- tell me that too, since I have a feeling the real answer is hiding in a place niether of them looked.
