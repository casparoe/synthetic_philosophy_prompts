Draft: "Saying Sorry for the Dead"

1. In 2008, Australian Prime Minister Kevin Rudd stood before Parliament and apologized to the Stolen Generations -- Aboriginal children removed from their families under government policy for much of the twentieth century. The same year, Canada's Prime Minister Stephen Harper apologized for the residential school system. Two decades earlier, the United States had passed the Civil Liberties Act of 1988, pairing an apology for Japanese American internment with $20,000 payments to surviving internees. State apologies have become something of a genre. This essay asks what, exactly, they are -- and whether they can possibly succeed at what they claim to do.

2. An apology, in ordinary life, is a speech act with strict conditions. The apologizer must be the wrongdoer, or at least answerable for the wrong. The addressee must be the wronged party. The apologizer must acknowledge the wrong, accept responsibility, and express something like regret. Strip away any of these and you get the notorious non-apology: "I'm sorry you feel that way," which performs the grammar of apology while evading its substance.

3. State apologies violate the first two conditions by construction, and this is what I want to call the double impersonation problem -- though it really splits in two. The agent problem: the government of 2008 is not the government of 1910. Different people, different voters, arguably a different moral agent altogether, even if the legal person of "the state" persists. When Rudd apologized, he was speaking for an institution, not confessing a sin, and institutions (one might think) do not have consciences to confess from. The addressee problem is worse: most of the direct victims are dead. You cannot apologize to the dead. So the apology is delivered to descendants and to surviving communities, who were wronged in derivative ways, if at all. The apology is thus performed by a stand-in, addressed to a stand-in. Two impersonators meeting on a stage.

4. Here is where I depart from the standard cynicism. The cynic says state apologies are cheap talk -- costless symbolic gestures that substitute for material redress, and are sometimes deliberately offered instead of it. But I think the double impersonation is not a bug; it is the entire point. What a state apology does is redefine the institution's self-description. When a state apologizes, it publicly reclassifies a past policy from "regrettable necessity" or "best intentions" to "wrong." That reclassification is something only the institution itself can perform -- no victim, no historian, no court can do it for it -- and it has downstream consequences: textbooks change, commemoration changes, and future policymakers inherit an official record in which the thing is marked as wrong. The apology is less like one person apologizing to another and more like a legislature amending its own constitution. The impersonators on stage are not pretending to be the wrongdoer and the victim; they are performing an institutional act that only impersonators could perform, since the original parties are gone.

5. If this is right, it explains some puzzles. It explains why "sorry" versus "apologize" matters so much politically (opponents of reparations often support apology precisely because they sense it commits the institution to less, while victims' groups push for the strongest wording for the opposite reason). It explains why apologies without any material accompaniment feel hollow -- the institutional self-redescription is not matched by any institutional action. And it explains why some states refuse outright: Turkey's refusal regarding the Armenian genocide, or Japan's endlessly contested series of statements on wartime conduct, where each new formulation is parsed for whether it advances or retreats from the last. If apologies were mere words, nobody would fight over the wording for seventy years.

6. Two objections I don't have good answers to yet. First: on my account, the value of the apology is entirely forward-looking (it shapes the institution's future self-understanding), but victims seem to be owed something backward-looking, and my account makes them props in the state's self-improvement project. Second: if the state of today is really a different moral agent than the state that did the wrong, why should its reclassification carry any more weight than anyone else's opinion? I gesture at legal continuity but I know I'm hand-waving.

One more thing: in section 5 I rely on the idea that the fierce political fights over apology wording only make sense if apologies do real institutional work. But someone could just say the fights are pure tribal signaling and the wording matters because the *audience* cares, not because the institution changes. If you've got a thought on how to tell those apart, I'd take it.
