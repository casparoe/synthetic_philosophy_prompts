I'm a second-year physics PhD student and I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science seminar this term, partly because my office mate (who does condensed matter, very anti-string-theory) keeps taunting me that elegance talk is just astrology for mathematicians. I tried to defend the pro-elegance side by building what I thought was a solid little argument, but when I wrote it out I realized the same argument seems to prove something I absolutely don't believe, and now I can't figure out whether I'm bad at arguments or whether the pro-elegance position is just bad. Here's the argument:

(1) A consideration used in theory choice counts as genuine evidence if theories selected by it systematically go on to be confirmed more often than theories it ranks lower.
(2) History passes this test for mathematical elegance: Dirac stuck with his equation even when it seemed to give wrong answers because it was too beautiful to discard, and it won; Einstein credited formal aesthetic considerations over the experimental situation of his day and general relativity won; every time physicists have trusted the elegant option over the ugly patch-up, the elegant option ended up on top.
(3) Therefore elegance is evidence -- not just a tiebreaker or a personal taste, but actual evidence for truth.
(4) Evidence doesn't expire: if some consideration counts in favor of a theory, it counts in favor of that theory regardless of whether we currently have better (empirical) tests available.
(5) So the elegance of a theory is evidence for it even in domains where we have no empirical access yet -- e.g. quantum gravity.
(6) Evidence rationally raises credence, so we're rationally required to raise our credence in elegant untestable theories over their uglier rivals.
(7) And since credence guides resource allocation, funding agencies are rationally required to weight elegance when deciding between theorists, even ahead of experimental programs that could, in principle, settle things empirically.

And (7) is nuts. I do not believe the NSF should start weighting the beauty of Lagrangians against building telescopes. But each step looked innocent when I wrote it. If I had to choose, I'd most want to keep (2) -- the historical track record seems like the one thing the elegance side genuinely has going for it -- and I'd be pretty happy to ditch (7) if there's a principled way to stop the argument before it gets there without special pleading. So: which step is the culprit, why did it look fine, and what do I have to give up by rejecting it? Also, if the fix is something like "elegance is a heuristic, not evidence," I'd like to know what work that distinction is actually doing, because right now it feels like a slogan.
