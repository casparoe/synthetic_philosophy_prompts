I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a long essay on decision-making under moral uncertainty -- the situation where you're not just unsure what the facts are, but unsure which moral theory is correct. The position I've provisionally adopted, and want stress-tested before I commit it to print, is a fairly standard one in the literature: when facing normative uncertainty, a rational agent should treat moral theories the way decision theory treats empirical hypotheses -- assign them credences, represent each theory's verdict on the options as a degree of choiceworthiness, and maximize expected choiceworthiness across theories. Call it MEC. I'm aware this requires intertheoretic comparisons of choiceworthiness (a utilitarian's "very wrong" must be placed on a common scale with a deontologist's "very wrong"), and I'm treating that as an engineering problem to be solved rather than a fatal objection.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure -- what would count against it, and how badly. Specifically:

1. The structural objections first. Does MEC face a genuine regress problem (we seem to need a higher-order principle to tell us that maximizing expectation is the right response to uncertainty -- and what justifies that principle?)? Is the intertheoretic comparison problem worse than an engineering problem -- is there a principled reason to think the comparisons are not merely hard but undefined? I want to know whether these are the kind of objection a committed defender can deflect with technical machinery or the kind that dissolves the project.

2. Which concrete possible findings would actually move me. For instance: if it turned out that every proposed common scale for choiceworthiness smuggles in substantive moral assumptions, is that a defeater or just a disappointment? If plausible moral theories turned out to be non-consequentialist in a way that resists representation as maximizing anything at all, what happens to the framework? What if agents following MEC reliably end up paralyzed or fanatical in realistic choice situations?

3. What the position's own defenders would concede counts against it, as opposed to what only critics press. I'm interested in whether there are acknowledged costs that insiders treat as open wounds versus ones they've quietly written off.

4. An honest verdict on insulation. Has MEC been qualified into near-unfalsifiability -- with every counterexample absorbed by refining the choiceworthiness scale or the credence function -- and if so, is that a vice for a normative proposal of this kind or just what maturation looks like?

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say what the world -- or the philosophy -- would have to look like for it to land. I don't want citations; I want arguments. End with a single line of the form: Verdict: [fatal / seriously damaged / bruised but standing / well insulated], choosing whichever grade the totality of your analysis supports.
