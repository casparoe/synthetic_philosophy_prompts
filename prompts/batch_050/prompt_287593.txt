Christopher Nolan's The Prestige poses the problem of identity twice over. In one strand, the Borden twins live a single public life, alternating in the role of the magician and his ingenieur, and the film is at pains to show that the two are not interchangeable: one loves Sarah, the other Olivia, and their temperaments diverge enough that Sarah can sense, without being able to prove, that the man before her varies from day to day. In the other strand, Angier's machine produces each night a qualitatively perfect duplicate of himself, memories included; one man drowns in a tank beneath the stage while the other appears across the theater to take the applause, and Angier confesses at the end that he never knew, stepping onto the platform, which one he would be. Read the film through the identity of indiscernibles -- the principle, associated with Leibniz, that things sharing all their qualitative properties are numerically one -- and through the standard objection to it, associated with Max Black, of a universe containing nothing but two perfectly identical spheres. Do the twins vindicate the principle by never quite managing perfect qualitative identity, or does the film suggest that their oneness and twoness are social achievements rather than metaphysical facts? Does Angier's horror presuppose a haecceity, a thisness that survives duplication and settles which man is really him, or does the film dramatize precisely the absence of any such further fact -- the drowned man and the applauding man having equal claim, the question of which one survived having no answer? Consider too the staging of the trick itself: the duplicate is always produced at a distance, so that the two men differ at least in position. Does that relational difference do the work Leibniz's defenders assign it in the spheres case, or does the film show that being here rather than there is too thin a difference to ground the asymmetry everyone feels between the man in the tank and the man taking the bow? I want a reading that is faithful in both directions -- that neither flattens the film into an illustration of a metaphysics paper nor bends the principle and its objection to fit the plot -- and that ends by saying what the film contributes to the debate that argument alone does not: for instance, whether the first-personal dread of the man on the platform shows that our concern for our own survival outruns anything the metaphysics of identity can underwrite. I am using the film in a personal identity unit and want to know where the analogy with Black's spheres genuinely holds and where I should warn my students that it breaks.
