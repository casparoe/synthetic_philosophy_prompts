So I'm prepping for a discussion section I'm TAing on hedonism, and the students keep running together totally different answers to the question of what, if anything, all pleasures have in common. One said pleasure is obviously a feeling, another said eating fries and being proud of your kid have no shared feeling so pleasure must be an attitude, and a third just said the word is a mess and there's nothing there. I want to give them a handout next week: a decision tree (or taxonomy, whatever works better) that sorts the main positions on the heterogeneity problem for pleasure -- roughly six to eight leaves. The audience is smart undergrads who've read a bit but don't know the terminology, so each branching question should be a concrete case or intuition pump they can react to without knowing any labels, and no question should just ask "do you think pleasure is a sensation or an attitude?"

A few things I specifically want in there. First, the leaf positions should include at least: the felt-quality view (all pleasures share one phenomenal feel), the hedonic-tone view (pleasantness is a dimension or modifier of sensations rather than a separate feeling), attitudinal hedonism in a simple liking/enjoying form, a desire-based attitudinal variant (pleasure as wanting the experience to continue, intrinsically), a functionalist or motivational-role view, and a disjunctivist/family-resemblance view that says 'pleasure' names nothing unified. Second, some cases I think do real diagnostic work and should show up at the branch points: the contrast between the pleasure of a massage and the "pleasure" of hearing that a distant war has ended; the warm bath you're too absorbed in conversation to notice but would protest having ended; pleasures you feel while being ashamed of having them (does that refute felt-quality or attitudinal views?); relief-from-pain pleasures where it seems like nothing positive is present at all, just subtraction; and the difference between background contentment and a sharp thrill. Third, build the tree from the logical space if possible -- i.e., start from the yes/no questions and the answer-combinations they allow, then place the actual views in that space, and flag any combination that nobody seems to occupy with a one-line diagnosis of whether it's incoherent, just unattractive, or weirdly unexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves the tree can't cleanly separate rather than forcing a distinction.

Feel free to note where my case-list is redundant (if two cases separate exactly the same positions, tell me and I'll cut one) and where a question I think is diagnostic actually fails because all the views give the same answer. A scoring-key style summary at the end mapping answer patterns to the named positions would be great, since I want to print it as a one-pager.
