Since retiring from a career in physics, I have been working my way through the phenomenological tradition, and I recently came across the following summary of Husserl's method in a general introduction to continental philosophy. I would be grateful for an accuracy audit.

The author writes: "Husserl's epoché is essentially a revival of Cartesian doubt: the phenomenologist is to suspend belief in the existence of the external world, treating it as possibly illusory, in order to find an indubitable foundation in inner experience. The phenomenological reduction then performs what the name suggests -- it reduces the world to the subject's private mental contents, since those are all we can be certain of. What remains after this procedure is a pure ego that constitutes reality out of its own representations, which is why Husserl's mature position is generally classified as a subjective idealism not far removed from Berkeley's, and why his later students, who wanted to study the concrete world rather than consciousness, had to abandon the method."

Something in this sits uneasily with what little I have read elsewhere, but I cannot put my finger on it. Which parts of this characterization are accurate, which are common simplifications, and which would Husserl or a careful Husserl scholar object to? In particular: is the epoché a form of doubt, does the reduction "reduce" the world in the way described, and does the conclusion about why his students broke with him follow from the accurate parts?
