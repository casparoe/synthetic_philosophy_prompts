I'm putting together a set of teaching cases for a graduate seminar on collective wrongdoing, and I've been dissatisfied with the standard examples: they almost always let the participant's contribution be causally relevant, so students never have to confront the harder question of what to say when someone's involvement makes no difference whatsoever to the outcome. I'd like you to construct a thought experiment to order for me. The scenario should involve two employees at the same company who are participating in the same wrongful venture -- something realistic and everyday (a firm quietly shipping a product it knows to be defective, or falsifying routine compliance paperwork), not a war crime or a trolley case. The specifications: both employees perform the same concrete task; both know full well what the venture is and that it's wrong; and crucially, both are causally redundant in exactly the same way, so that the harmful outcome would have been identical, in timing and magnitude, had either of them refused -- their replacement or an existing colleague covers the work seamlessly. The only difference between them is their orientation toward the venture: one identifies with it, takes pride in the project succeeding, and would be genuinely pleased by the harm's occurrence; the other is entirely indifferent to the outcome, participates solely to keep the paycheck, and quietly hopes the whole thing fails. Keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph, and do not stipulate any verdicts -- the verdicts must be generated by the theories, not asserted.

After the scenario, run the case through three families of views, stated in general terms rather than by name: first, accounts on which complicity is exhausted by causal contribution to the outcome; second, accounts on which complicity depends on the agent's intentions or practical identification with the collective project, regardless of difference-making; third, accounts on which wrongful participation "taints" the agent or makes the wrong partly theirs even absent contribution. Show precisely which verdicts each family delivers for each employee, and confirm that the first family cannot distinguish the two employees while the second and third can (or, if you think one of them can't, explain why). Then identify which single feature of the case is doing the work, and tell me what happens if it's removed: if both employees are made indifferent, does the intuition of differential blameworthiness collapse entirely, and if so, what does that show? Finally, steelman the response of a defender of the causal-contribution view, who will presumably argue either that the two employees really are equally blameworthy and our differing intuitions track character rather than complicity, or that the intending employee's stance is itself causally relevant in ways my stipulations haven't ruled out. Tell me honestly whether either move succeeds against the case as constructed.
