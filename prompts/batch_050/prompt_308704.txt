My archaeology tutor keeps banging on about this idea that inference from objects runs up a kind of ladder of difficulty: what a thing is and how it was made is easy, what people ate and traded is harder, how their society was organised harder still, and what they believed is basically off-limits. Her line is that most confident claims in popular archaeology are just people forgetting which rung they're standing on.

Now the episode I want analysed through this: Ötzi, the Copper Age mummy found frozen in the Alps in 1991, about 5,300 years old. The basics as I understand them: he was found with a copper axe, an unfinished bow and arrows, a flint dagger, a fire-lighting kit, birch-bark containers and a pouch containing birch fungus; he had dozens of tattoos, mostly short parallel lines and crosses on his lower back, knees and ankles; his last meals included ibex, red deer and einkorn; and for roughly the first decade everyone assumed he'd been caught in a storm and frozen to death, until newer scans turned up a flint arrowhead lodged in his shoulder with a torn artery, plus a deep defensive cut on his hand, and the whole story flipped to murder.

What I want is three things. First, grade the claims that have actually been made about him against the ladder: that the copper axe means he was high-status; that the tattoos sit over arthritic joints and roughly match acupuncture points, so his people practised medicine; alternatively that the tattoos are clan marks or protective magic; that he was a shepherd, or a shaman, or a man on the run; that his community had metallurgy and long-distance trade. For each one, say which rung its really on and what the inference actually leans on -- experiment and replication, present-day analogy, ethnographic analogy, or not much at all.

Second, and this is the part I care about more: what does the case do to the ladder? Cause of death should be the easiest rung going, the pure physical stuff, and it got completely overturned ten years in by a better scan. Meanwhile the top-rung claims about belief and status don't seem to get more wrong over time, they just stay speculative. So maybe the ladder fails to keep two things seperate -- how directly the evidence constrains a claim versus how much interpretation is piled on top -- or maybe the bottom rungs only feel safe because we don't notice the assumptions doing the work. Does Ötzi confirm the ladder, break it, or show it was never really a theory of evidence but a warning about overconfidence? And do the overconfident claims in his case actually cluster where the ladder predicts they will?

Don't re-tell his life story, I know it; I want the epistemology of it. And if any of the facts I've given are off, say so rather than playing along. British English please.
