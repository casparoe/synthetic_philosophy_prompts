I've spent years reading arguments about group differences on rationalist blogs and forums, and the national character question is where those arguments go to die. One side insists that aggregate differences between populations are just base rates, and that refusing to use them is epistemic malpractice. The other side insists that "the French are like X, the Japanese are like Y" is pre-scientific essentialism dressed up in statistical vocabulary. Both sides talk past each other constantly, and I suspect it's because nobody has stated what an adequate account of national character would even have to do.

So before I read or write another take, I want the spec. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of national character has to deliver for it to count as a coherent notion at all, and then separately, what it has to deliver before it may legitimately be used in everyday generalizations about people. Give me the case or consideration motivating each requirement. Concretely, I'd like the list to address at least the following pressure points, though you should add whatever I've missed:

- The observation that within-country variance on most psychological traits swamps between-country mean differences, and whether a coherent notion survives that fact.
- Cases of rapid apparent change (a country's reputation for some trait shifting within a generation or two) alongside cases of striking persistence across centuries and across diasporas. A theory that explains one but not the other is incomplete, and I'd like persistence-versus-change flagged as a candidate pair of desiderata that may not be jointly satisfiable.
- The distinction between national character as a cause of observed outcomes and national character as a label we stick on outcomes actually produced by institutions, incentives, climate, or selection effects (e.g., who chose to emigrate).
- The distinction between a descriptive claim about a distribution and a license to infer something about an individual member — the place where these discussions reliably collapse into reference-class tennis and a motte-and-bailey between "groups differ on average" and "so I may treat you accordingly."
- Measurement problems: self-reports versus observer ratings versus behavioral measures diverging, and the possibility that survey responses track local reference groups rather than absolute levels.

For each desideratum, mark whether it's non-negotiable for coherence, non-negotiable only for the practical-use question, or a preference of one camp that the other camp can reasonably reject. If some subset of the desiderata can't be jointly satisfied, say so explicitly — that would itself be the most interesting result.

Then, briefly and secondarily, score four rough contenders against the list: old-fashioned essentialism (character flows from shared ancestry or spirit), the statistical-profile view (national character is real but consists of modest, overlapping aggregate distributions), eliminativism (the whole notion is projection plus outgroup homogeneity bias), and an institutional-constructivist view (the observed regularities track incentives and institutions, not character). Keep the scoring short; the criteria are the deliverable.

Finally: state how confident you are in the overall framing and in each verdict about which desiderata are negotiable, and single out the single point in your answer you are least sure of and why.
