Alright, here's my situation. I spent fourty years as a field biologist (mostly corvids and gulls, a stint with lab rats early on) and since retiring I've fallen down the philosophy of mind rabbit hole. One thing I keep tripping over is Morgan's canon -- the old rule that you shouldn't explain an animal's behaviour as the product of some higher, fancier psychological faculty if it can be explained by a lower one further down the scale. Sounds sensible, sounded sensible to me for decades. But then I read people pushing back: the "higher/lower" ranking is doing a lot of unargued work, parsimony about mechanisms isn't the same as parsimony about entities, and the critical anthropomorphism crowd reckon you're allowed to start from a human-like hypothesis so long as you discipline it with what you know about the species' sensory world, ecology, and evolutionary history.

What I'd like from you is a case built to order. Construct a scenario -- and since I can't help myself, make it an actually doable experiment, not a sci-fi setup -- meeting these specs:

1. It involves an ordinary, observable behaviour in a common species (something I could plausibly watch in a garden or a field station), and it must NOT be one of the famous warhorses: no mirror self-recognition, no Clever Hans, no ape sign language, no scrub-jay caching. I want a fresh behaviour, invented by you but biologically plausible.
2. Applying Morgan's canon straightforwardly delivers a clear verdict: the behaviour should be chalked up to some lower process (stimulus-response, simple association, hardwired disposition, whatever fits). Spell out exactly what the canon-endorsing interpretation is.
3. The critical anthropomorphism approach delivers a different, richer interpretation. Spell that out too.
4. Here's the kicker: the scenario must include a cheap follow-up manipulation -- something a stubborn retired guy could run with binoculars, a notebook, and maybe a confederate with a van -- whose outcome would show the canon's lower-faculty interpretation was wrong and the richer one right. So this is a case where the canon, applied as a default rule, actively steers you away from the truth, not just toward less colourfull language.
5. The difference in verdicts must come from the methodological rules themselves, not from one side having extra evidence the other lacks. Both parties see the same behaviour.

After the scenario, do the bookkeeping: state the verdict each approach gives and why, tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work of making the canon misfire, what happens to your argument if that feature is removed, and how a defender of the canon would attack the case (e.g. by arguing my manipulation doesn't actually rule out the lower explanation, or that I've snuck the richer interpretation in by stipulation).

For context on what counts as cheating: I want to use this in a talk at my local natural history society, where half the audience will be canon loyalists. If the case just stipulates that the animal has the fancy mental state rather than letting the experiment's outcome establish it, they'll eat me alive, and I'd deserve it.
