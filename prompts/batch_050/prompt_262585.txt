I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and occassionally I have to write short explainers about disputes where scientists say two rival theories account for the same evidence. A philosopher I interviewed recently warned me that journalists constantly blur three different things: theories that are merely compatible with the data gathered so far, theories that make all the same observable predictions, and theories that can be made to fit anything because they have enough adjustable assumptions. I believe him, but my usual response—adding a line saying more research is needed—is not exactly an epistemic triumph.

Please turn the underdetermination problem into a practical worksheet I could run in about 25 minutes before filing a story. Assume I have the researchers’ claims but not the mathematical expertise to check every derivation, that an editor has to approve any strong claim, and that I might use this several times a month. The worksheet should give me questions in order; say what counts as an adequate answer; distinguish evidential equivalence from temporary lack of discriminating data, accommodation from prediction, and theoretical parsimony from merely having fewer named components; and explain how auxiliary assumptions and free parameters change the assessment. Include a simple device called a counterfactual evidence ledger: for each theory, list what observation or intervention should come out differently if that theory rather than its rival is true. Mark where the procedure must stop and defer to an expert, where editorial judgment remains unavoidable, and which common virtues—simplicity, unification, explanatory depth—are legitimate tie-breakers but not extra evidence.

Then apply the worksheet to three invented cases: (1) two models of inflation fit the same twenty years of data, but one treats expectations as backward-looking and the other as forward-looking; (2) decay and interference theories of forgetting both fit standard laboratory recall curves, while making different claims about relearning after delay; and (3) two accounts of a newly observed pattern in animal behavior both explain every observation already collected, but one predicts the pattern should persist after individuals are moved to a new environment and the other predicts it should disappear. At least one application should produce a conclusion an ambitious reporter would dislike—say, that the honest story is merely that the current evidence does not discriminate, even though a scientist is presenting the result as a dramatic empirical tie. Finally, list the five most likely ways the worksheet could mislead me, and say what final wording would be justified in each of the three cases.
