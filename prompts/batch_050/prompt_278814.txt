In doing so, distinguish carefully between points that are settled (what Locke actually claims, what Reid's example is, the logical structure of the objection) and points that are genuinely contested among commentators (for instance, how damaging the objection is to Locke's underlying project, and which repairs, if any, would have been available to Locke himself rather than only to later neo-Lockeans). Exam question:
Set out Locke's account of personal identity in terms of sameness of consciousness. Present Reid's brave officer objection and assess whether it refutes Locke's account. In your answer, consider whether a defender of Locke has any reply available.

Student's answer:
Locke distinguishes the identity of a man, the living human organism, from the identity of a person. He defines a person as a thinking, intelligent being with reason and reflection, one that can consider itself as the same self across different times and places. Personal identity over time, on his view, consists in sameness of consciousness: because consciousness always accompanies thinking and is what makes each of us a self, a later person is the same person as an earlier one just in case the later person's consciousness extends back to the earlier person's thoughts and actions, which in practice means remembering them from the inside. The identity of the underlying substance is beside the point: the same immaterial soul without shared consciousness would not make the same person, and in principle the same person could pass from one substance to another. Locke's account is driven by his insistence that person is a forensic term: we hold people responsible only for what they can appropriate in consciousness, which is why Locke thinks a man should not be punished for what he did while drunk or asleep if he genuinely cannot remember it.

Reid's brave officer objection runs as follows. Suppose a boy is flogged for stealing apples from an orchard. Later, as a young officer, he bravely captures an enemy standard, and at that time still remembers being flogged as a boy. Later still, as an old general, he remembers capturing the standard but has entirely forgotten the flogging. On Locke's account, the officer is the same person as the boy, because the officer remembers the flogging; and the general is the same person as the officer, because the general remembers capturing the standard; but the general is not the same person as the boy, because he has forgotten the flogging. Reid concludes that this shows identity is not transitive, and hence that personal identity cannot be analyzed in terms of memory.

Reid also objects that Locke's account is circular, since genuinely remembering an action already presupposes that one is the very person who performed it, so memory cannot be what constitutes that identity in the first place.

My own view is that the objection succeeds against Locke's theory as Locke stated it, but that a natural repair is available. We can define personal identity not by direct memory but by an overlapping chain of memory connections: the general need not remember the boyhood episode provided that each stage in the chain remembers the stage before it. Locke himself would clearly have accepted this modification, since it preserves the centrality of consciousness to his account. Because the repaired theory keeps everything Locke cared about, namely accountability and self-appropriation, Reid's objection shows only that Locke's formulation needs refinement, not that his approach fails.
