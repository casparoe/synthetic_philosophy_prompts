I'm a second-year undergraduate reading Philosophy, Politics and Economics, and I'm currently taking a course on rational choice theory and democratic institutions. We've just spent two weeks on the paradox of voting — the puzzle that, given the minuscule probability of any single vote being decisive in a large electorate, instrumental rationality seems to recommend staying at home — and we've worked through the standard replies. Whilst preparing for my tutorial, I came up with an objection I haven't encountered in the readings, and I'd like to test it against those replies. I'm calling it the "unknowability twist": even in the astronomically rare event that an election comes down to a single vote, no voter could ever be in a position to know that theirs was the pivotal ballot. Recounts shift totals, margins of administrative error comfortably exceed one vote, and attributing the outcome causally to any particular ballot is hopeless. So, the objection runs, any justification of voting that leans on the chance of being pivotal is not merely a bet at terrible odds, but a bet on an event whose occurrence is in principle unverifiable by the person placing it — which arguably makes the bet irrational even if the odds were better than they are.

The five responses to the paradox we've covered in seminars are these: (1) the expected-benefit calculus, where a tiny pivot probability is multiplied by the enormous stakes of the election; (2) expressive voting, where casting a ballot affirms one's identity or values rather than aiming at an outcome; (3) duty-based accounts, where voting is a deontological obligation irrespective of consequences; (4) legitimacy-based accounts, where voting sustains the practice and authority of democratic institutions; and (5) minimax regret, where one votes to avoid the worst-case scenario of having stayed home when one's vote would have mattered.

Please rank these five responses from most damaged to least damaged by the unknowability twist, giving two or three sentences of justification for each placement, and noting any cases where you think two responses are roughly equally affected. Then, as a final step, tell me whether you think the twist genuinely adds anything to the bog-standard objection that pivot probabilities are vanishingly small, or whether it merely repackages that objection in more dramatic language. I'd like the answer written in British English, and structured clearly enough that I could adapt it into a tutorial essay introduction.
