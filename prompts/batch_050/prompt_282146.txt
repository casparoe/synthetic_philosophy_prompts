I'm a high school junior and I run our school's philosophy club; we're doing a unit on AI safety and I've been arguing a position that I want stress-tested rather than applauded.

My position: the safest and most realistic path for advanced AI is to deliberately build tool AI rather than agent AI — systems that produce outputs (plans, proofs, designs, answers) when asked and then stop, with no persistent goals, no autonomous action in the world, and no open-ended task pursuit. I claim that most of the classic failure modes people worry about (power-seeking, self-preservation, resisting correction) arise specifically from agency, so a well-designed tool sidesteps them, and that the standard reply — "people will just give the tool autonomy anyway" — is a sociological worry about users, not a technical objection to the design philosophy itself.

What would count against this? I want the strongest defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd like you to go beyond the two objections I already know (agents are more profitable so someone will build them; a tool that's good enough at planning is basically an agent wearing a disguise). Specifically:

1. Are there failure modes that don't require agency at all — ways a pure tool could still produce catastrophic outcomes even with a careful, non-malicious user? Give concrete mechanisms, not just gesturing at misuse.
2. Is the tool/agent distinction actually load-bearing, or is it a continuum where the safe region is much smaller than I assume? For example, does adding memory, a search subroutine, or self-critique to a tool quietly convert it into the thing I said we shouldn't build?
3. Does my dismissal of the "users will add autonomy" objection hold up? My instinct is that a design philosophy can't be refuted by predicting that people will abandon it, but maybe that's wrong if the economic pressure to add agency is so strong that the philosophy is only stable under conditions we'll never have.
4. What would defenders of my own position concede counts against it — as opposed to what its critics say?

Also: tell me whether my position has been insulated against refutation. If every proposed counterexample gets answered with "then it wasn't really a tool," that's a warning sign, and I want to know if I'm doing that. Finally, say how confident you are in your overall verdict, and single out the single point in your response where you're least sure you're right.
