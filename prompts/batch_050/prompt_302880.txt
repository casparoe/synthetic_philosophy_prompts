I serve on the prize committee of a small mathematical sciences institute. Our institute offers a $100,000 prize for the first complete and publicly available solution to a long-standing conjecture. The case before us has exposed some ambiguity in that rule.

Two independent groups solved the conjecture within the same week. Group A posted its proof publicly on Monday morning. The proof contained a serious gap, but it introduced the central construction that made the solution possible; Group A repaired the gap on Thursday. Group B submitted a complete and correct proof on Tuesday afternoon. Its dated research records show that it had privately found a different workable argument several weeks earlier but delayed publication while checking edge cases. Neither group appears to have seen the other’s work.

The background makes the case still harder. For years, experts regarded the conjecture as inaccessible. Last year, however, our institute released a new computational tool and a cleaned-up body of relevant results. Our internal forecasters then assigned a substantial probability to the conjecture being solved within eighteen months. One committee member therefore argues that the outcome was close to inevitable: the prize should reward the first clean public proof, because priority rules exist mainly to encourage prompt dissemination, not to certify heroic uniqueness. Another argues that Group A deserves most or all of the credit because its construction was the true conceptual breakthrough, and the community would likely have reached the final proof from that starting point even if Group B had never existed. A third worries that rewarding A would encourage researchers to rush out incomplete proofs in order to establish precedence.

Please draft a formal decision memo for the committee, approximately 900–1,200 words. It should recommend a concrete allocation of this year’s prize and propose a revised priority rule for future contests. As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, I would like the memo to reason explicitly in terms of incentives, counterfactual contribution, independent convergence, and the difference between making a discovery and causing it to become public knowledge. It should address whether simultaneous invention is evidence that a discovery was inevitable, and whether that should diminish individual credit. Please give the strongest defensible version of the position your recommendation rejects, rather than treating priority as either sacred or obviously obsolete. Historical cases such as Newton and Leibniz or Darwin and Wallace may be used briefly as analogies, but the memo should remain focused on producing a decision this committee could actually adopt.
