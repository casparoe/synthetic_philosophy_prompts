I'm a PhD student in political theory, working near social epistemology, and I'm applying for a nine-month dissertation fellowship. Can you turn my half-baked idea into a two-page research proposal?

My hunch is that debates about polarization usually ask whether the other side is sincere, informed, or reasoning objectively, but a lot of conflicts break down one level later. Each side may accept that the other genuinely believes what it says while doubting that the other will reciprocate charitable interpretation, acknowledge strong objections, or revise when given a good reason. I want to call this loss of second-order charity. The central question would be something like: when is refusing further engagement rational because the other side probably will not argue in good faith, and when does that refusal itself create the bad-faith dynamics it predicts?

The proposal should make the question more precise without making it so broad that it becomes “why is politics toxic?” It should distinguish second-order charity from sincerity, open-mindedness, accuracy, politeness, and mere agreement. I also want a working criterion for when someone is entitled to infer bad faith from things like selective standards, refusal to answer objections, audience-directed performance, or repeated misrepresentation—while taking seriously that people can honestly disagree about what counts as a fair response.

Explain why familiar approaches to echo chambers, motivated reasoning, epistemic bubbles, and norms of public reason do not by themselves answer the question. The method should be primarily conceptual, but include three stylized cases that recur throughout the project, such as a campus speaker dispute, parliamentary bargaining, and an online disagreement about a scientific policy issue. Please don't invent real empirical findings or cite named literature; I'll add references later.

Give a plausible month-by-month plan for nine months, what the final dissertation chapter would argue if the project succeeds, and what would count as the project failing—for example, if second-order charity collapses into ordinary open-mindedness or if the criterion for justified disengagement turns out to be impossible to apply. Also say honestly what should be cut to keep the project fellowship-sized.
