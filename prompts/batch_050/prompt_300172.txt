Since I became engaged last spring, I have been attending my fiancée's church and meeting regularly with her priest, and our conversations keep circling back to one question: whether morality stands on its own or depends on God. Rereading Plato's Euthyphro in the Jowett translation, I was struck less by the famous dilemma than by an earlier passage (7b–d), where Socrates asks what sorts of disagreement make enemies of us:

"Let us look at it in this way. If you and I were to disagree about number, for instance, which of two numbers were the greater, would the disagreement about these matters make us enemies and make us angry with each other, or should we not quickly settle it by resorting to arithmetic? ... Then, too, if we were to disagree about the relative size of things, we should quickly put an end to the disagreement by measuring? ... And we should, I suppose, come to terms about relative weights by weighing? ... But about what would a disagreement be, which we could not settle and which would cause us to be enemies and be angry with each other? Perhaps you cannot give an answer offhand; but let me suggest it. Is it not about right and wrong, and noble and disgraceful, and good and bad? Are not these the questions about which you and I and other people become enemies, when we do become enemies, because we differ about them and cannot reach any satisfactory agreement?"

In preparation for a discussion evening at the parish, I have written down where I currently stand, and I would like to know whether my positions hang together before I present them. I hold all of the following:

1. Moral objectivity. Some things, such as cruelty inflicted for its own sake, are wrong, and would remain wrong even if every human being approved of them.
2. The priority of the good. God loves and commands what is good because it is good; if the order ran the other way, his commands would be arbitrary and calling him "good" would say nothing with content.
3. The bindingness of divine commands. God's commands impose genuine moral obligations on us, over and above the standing obligation to do what is good anyway; we owe him obedience, not merely prudent compliance.
4. Divine sovereignty. Everything that exists or obtains, apart from God himself, exists or obtains dependently on him; nothing is independent of his creative will.
5. The accessibility of moral knowledge. A conscientious nonbeliever can know right from wrong as reliably as a devout believer, without recourse to revelation.
6. Socrates' diagnosis. The quoted passage is correct: disagreements about right and wrong differ from disagreements about number, size, and weight in that no common measure exists by which they can be settled, and this is what makes them turn us into enemies.

Please audit this set. Which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, and how severe is each? I am particularly unsure whether endorsing Socrates' diagnosis in (6) undercuts my claims to moral objectivity in (1) and to accessible moral knowledge in (5), or whether the passage, read carefully, says something narrower than I have assumed -- so please begin by stating precisely what Socrates does and does not claim there. For each tension you find, name the cheapest revision that would resolve it and what that revision costs; for any apparent tension that dissolves on inspection, show why it dissolves rather than merely asserting that it does. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and conclude by telling me which single revision would clean up the most. I have no preference among the commitments, so do not soften the verdict to spare any of them.
