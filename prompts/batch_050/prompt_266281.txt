A close friend of mine has no training in biology or philosophy, but she has a longstanding habit of sorting anything clever into one of four boxes, based on where the cleverness came from. Her boxes, in her own words: (1) born-with-it — the competence was built into the thing over generations, with no learning involved at the individual level; (2) learned-the-hard-way — the competence came from the individual's own trial, error, and feedback during its lifetime; (3) thought-it-through — the individual tests candidate actions internally, in some kind of mental rehearsal, before committing to one; (4) borrowed — the individual's competence depends on tools, symbols, or know-how produced by other minds and taken over ready-made.

Her verdicts so far, which I've collected over years of arguing with her: a spider's web-building is born-with-it. A cuckoo's egg mimicry is born-with-it. Ants following pheromone trails are born-with-it, and when I objected that the trail itself is information laid down by other ants, she held firm on the grounds that the trail-reading machinery is inborn. A rat learning a maze for food rewards is learned-the-hard-way. Her own dog, who stopped begging at the table only after months of being consistently ignored, is learned-the-hard-way. An experienced nurse she knows, who can tell at a glance that a patient is about to crash but cannot articulate how she knows, is learned-the-hard-way rather than thought-it-through, precisely because the nurse can't rehearse her reasoning — it's compiled experience, not deliberation. My friend herself, planning three moves ahead in chess, is thought-it-through. A chimpanzee that surveys a room, then stacks crates to reach suspended fruit, is thought-it-through. A schoolchild solving long division by the memorized algorithm is borrowed. My friend driving with satellite navigation is borrowed, and she cheerfully admits her own unaided sense of direction has atrophied as a result. She also insists, when pressed, that most humans are several boxes at once, but she'll always name the one she takes to be doing the real work in a given case.

Three new cases have come up between us, and before I ask her I want to test whether her track record actually pins down what she'll say. First, a honeybee forager reading the waggle dance of another bee: the dance-decoding machinery is innate, but the specific information — where the flowers are — was produced by another individual and taken over ready-made. Second, a large language model of the current generation: it never acted in a world, never lived a trial-and-error life, and acquired virtually everything it knows from text written by humans — yet the process that got it there consisted of astronomical amounts of iterative adjustment against feedback. Third, a slime mold that finds an efficient route through a maze toward food despite having nothing resembling a nervous system.

Please do the following. Predict her verdict on each of the three cases and give your confidence for each. Identify the single rule you take her to be running — by my lights, the candidates include classifying by the historical mechanism that built the competence, classifying by what happens at the moment the competence is exercised, and classifying by whether the individual could in principle have worked it out alone — and say which of these, or which other rule, best fits all of her earlier verdicts. Where two candidate rules both fit the track record but diverge on a new case, say so explicitly rather than forcing a prediction. And finally: if her boxes turn out to be underdetermined by everything she's said so far, tell me which further case, with which pair of possible verdicts from her, would most efficiently settle which rule she's actually using.
