I am a novelist preparing a chapter in which an elderly philosopher must explain Avicenna’s flying man argument to a young neuroscientist during a long power cut. I do not want the dialogue itself, but a plan for the explanation the philosopher should give, one I can later dramatize without making either character sound like a textbook.

The neuroscientist is intelligent but philosophically untrained and has aphantasia: she cannot voluntarily produce sensory imagery. She therefore assumes that any argument beginning from an imagined, disembodied person merely describes a private mental picture and cannot establish anything about self-awareness. Design the explanation so that this feature becomes useful rather than incidental.

Please specify:

1. The order in which the ideas must be introduced, with the dependency at each step made explicit. In particular, distinguish self-awareness, knowledge of one’s bodily states, memory-based personal identity, and the stronger metaphysical claim that the self is immaterial. Indicate where Avicenna’s argument is strongest if treated as an argument about immediate self-awareness, and where it becomes contestable if pressed into a proof of an immaterial soul.

2. The single carrying case to use. Should the philosopher retain the suspended, sense-deprived figure, or replace it with a more controlled example involving temporary loss of bodily sensation, proprioception, autobiographical memory, and sensory imagery? Explain what the case must preserve, what it must omit, and how to prevent the aphantasia from being mistaken for a refutation of the argument.

3. The misconceptions the neuroscientist is likely to form, in the order she is likely to form them. Include at least: confusing imagining oneself with being visually represented; treating self-awareness as just another neural representation of the body; assuming memory is necessary for every form of first-person awareness; and concluding that, if the argument works, it has already established an immortal soul. For each misconception, identify the exact point in the sequence where the philosopher should anticipate it rather than merely answer it afterward.

4. A small number of probing questions the philosopher can ask to test whether the neuroscientist has grasped the distinction between having direct awareness that one exists and inferring the existence of a body or continuing person. The questions should expose confusion without relying on specialized philosophical vocabulary.

5. What the philosopher should deliberately leave unexplained in this first conversation—such as the details of Avicenna’s psychology, later interpretations, or the full transition from epistemology to metaphysics—and what he should openly mark as unsettled rather than conceal for dramatic neatness.

End with a brief self-critical note: state which interpretation of the flying man your plan presupposes, what philosophical or textual consideration would lead you to reorder the explanation or choose a different carrying case, and where your proposed misconception map is most likely to fail.
