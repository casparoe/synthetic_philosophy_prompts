A colleague and I are writing a paper on the ethics of rescue, and he has produced an argument that purports to show there is no stable position short of counting numbers — that the Taurek-style view, on which one may save the one rather than the five, collapses under the weight of its own commitment to equal respect. The conclusion seems to me to prove too much: if sound, it would not merely overturn one controversial verdict but would show that an entire tradition of fairness-based thinking about rescue was never coherent to begin with, and that the long dispute between aggregators and their critics was no real dispute at all. I cannot locate the misstep. Here is the argument.

The standard case: six people will drown, one on a rock to the left and five on rocks to the right, and I can save only one side.

(1) Each of the six has a claim to be saved, and the claims are equal: each stands to lose exactly the same thing, a life, and equal respect forbids counting anyone's peril for more or less than anyone else's.

(2) When equal claims conflict and not all can be satisfied, treating the claims equally requires giving each claimant an equal chance of being aided. A procedure that awarded some claimants a better chance than others would, by that very fact, be weighting their claims more heavily.

(3) Fairness therefore requires a lottery in which each of the six has a one-sixth chance: the five are saved with probability 5/6, the one with probability 1/6.

(4) Notice what fixes these odds: each person is counted exactly once, and the larger group wins more often precisely because more persons are counted. The numbers are doing all the work in determining what fairness demands.

(5) So the Taurekian who accepts (3) has already conceded that numbers count. His position now differs from outright aggregation only in declining to save the greater number with certainty — he accepts a procedure that saves fewer lives on average than always saving the five, while gaining nothing in fairness, since the lottery's fairness consisted entirely in counting each person once.

(6) The remaining retreat — a fair coin between the two sides — is no better: it gives the lone person a 1/2 chance of rescue and each of the five only 1/10, thereby counting his claim at five times each of theirs, contrary to (1).

(7) Hence either the numbers count (3) or equal respect is violated (6). There is no coherent view on which they do not count; the only question is whether one counts them with or without added randomness.

Every premise has a plausible motivation, yet I distrust the destination. Which step should I reject, why did that step look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like, if possible, to keep (1), and also some version of the verdict that saving the one is at least permissible — though I care more about getting the diagnosis right than about protecting either commitment. In particular: if the fault lies in (2), explain what treating equal claims equally could amount to instead, and whether any such account survives the pressure of (6); if it lies in (4)–(5), explain how a procedure can have the numbers doing all the work in fixing fair odds without the position behind it collapsing into aggregation. Finally, tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis overall, and single out the point in your answer that you are least sure of.
