I am a PhD student in philosophy of science, and I contribute each year to our graduate journal's satire issue. This time I want a piece targeting the dark forest hypothesis — the proposed answer to the Fermi paradox on which the cosmos appears empty because rational civilizations keep their heads down. The underlying reasoning, as I want it satirized: intentions cannot be verified across light-years, technological growth is exponential and unpredictable, chains of suspicion make trust unstable, and the cost of being discovered may be extinction; so a civilization that detects another must either strike first or hide, and the only civilizations one could ever notice are the ones that have already made a fatal mistake.

Write the piece as an in-universe orientation pamphlet titled something like "So You've Invented Radio," issued by an ancient civilization — give it a bland institutional name along the lines of the Office of Long-Term Quiet — welcoming a young species to the galactic community and explaining, in the soothing deadpan of a workplace safety manual, how things work around here. The pamphlet should cover, in its own bureaucratic idiom, the actual logic of the hypothesis: why a civilization's declared peaceful intentions are worthless as evidence, why a harmless neighbor today is a potential threat two centuries of exponential development from now, why silence is the only policy with a track record, and why the Office's authors have survived precisely by never doing anything like what the reader's species just spent eighty years doing.

Three constraints matter most. First, the satire must exaggerate real features of the reasoning rather than invent absurd ones — a specialist should be able to reconstruct the underlying argument from the jokes alone, and ideally judge the satire fair. Second, the Office must obey its own logic throughout: a pamphlet is a broadcast, and the piece should find the funniest available way to acknowledge that tension without letting the Office lapse into hypocrisy it wouldn't notice. Third, include at least one passage in which the Office warmly congratulates the reader's species on its scientific achievements, where the congratulations read unmistakably as a threat — accomplished through tone alone, since that inference from admiration to danger is the hypothesis's central move.

Aim for around 800 words, broken into sections with bureaucratic headings (a FAQ is welcome). Deadpan throughout; no winking, no breaking character. Do not reference any specific novel, author, or fictional universe — treat the hypothesis as the common property of the astrobiology seminar circuit, and satirize the game theory rather than any proponent. The Office may have a founding date and a charter; invent them freely.
