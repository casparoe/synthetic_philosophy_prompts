You are a professional epistemologist with experience facilitating adversarial collaborations between entrenched camps. Think carefully before drafting; brainstorm candidate formulations internally, and avoid strawmen of either side.

Some background: I recently started teaching my teenage niece to navigate our city without her phone, and I found myself insisting that she understand *why* the bus routes run where they do, not merely memorize which bus to take — even though, as she correctly pointed out, either method would get her to school just as reliably. That exchange left me stuck on the Meno problem: why, exactly, is knowledge worth more than mere true belief, when both guide action equally well?

I want you to draft the joint statement that two camps could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on this question. Camp A ("practicalists") holds that whatever extra value knowledge has over true belief is exhausted by practical benefits — chiefly that knowledge is stable, tied down, and resistant to being lost under pressure or misleading evidence. Camp B ("epistemicists") holds that knowledge has value beyond the practical: that it constitutes a genuine achievement creditable to the agent, or embodies a relation to the truth that lucky true belief lacks, even when the practical payoffs are identical.

The statement should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. These must be non-vacuous. Include at least one concrete case (you may construct one) in which both camps concede that knowledge intuitively seems superior to true belief, and record each camp's agreed interpretation of that intuition. Also include any agreements about cases where knowledge seems *not* superior.
2. A disagreements table: each row states one remaining disagreement, phrased in words the side holding the position would accept as accurate, plus a column flagging whether the disagreement is substantive or, in your judgment, merely verbal.
3. A list of cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp.
4. For each crux, one thought experiment or case both camps accept as bearing on it, with each camp's predicted verdict written down in advance.

Keep the whole statement under 900 words and do not cite published philosophers by name; frame everything in the camps' own terms.
