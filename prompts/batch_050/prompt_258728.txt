I teach history at a secondary school (well, I teach a bit of everything, but history is my favourite) and I'm putting together a unit on the French Revolution. While reading up on it I keep noticing a pattern that I can't find a proper name for, and I'd love to hand the students an actual concept rather than just a pile of dates.

Here's the pattern. Each group that comes out on top does it by accusing the previous lot of being secretly against the revolution — the Girondins go down as covert royalists, then the Hébertists get it for being too extreme and supposedly plotting, then Danton of all people goes down for being too soft, and a few months later Robespierre himself is denounced as a traitor to the revolution by men using the exact vocabulary he'd been using all spring. What gets me is that nobody ever manages to be loyal enough. The proof of loyalty that worked last year — denouncing the king, say — becomes baseline, then insufficient, then weirdly becomes grounds for suspicion itself (you denounced too late, or too eagerly, or you were standing near the wrong person in 1792). And it's not even a simple ratchet in one direction, because Hébert was destroyed for excess and Danton for indulgence within weeks of each other — it's like whatever the current centre is, deviation in either direction counts as treason, and the centre keeps moving. So the pool of acceptable people shrinks and shrinks until it swallows the people running the thing. Crucially the real enemies are basically backdrop here: the émigrés and the Austrians matter, but the people doing the destroying and the people being destroyed are all on the same side, and nobody, not even Robespierre, seems to have sat down and planned it.

I've heard the line about revolutions devouring their own children, but that just names the outcome — it says nothing about the mechanism, and it makes it sound like the revolution is some hungry beast rather than a lot of people each doing something that sort of made sense at the time. One of my students said it sounds like a "purity spiral," which she'd heard on a podcast about online communities, but that seemed to be about small groups competing to be the most morally scrupulous for status, and I don't think it predicts the details that interest me: the state violence, the two-directional thing, and above all the way the denouncers reliably become the denounced. So: is there an established concept for this specific dynamic — a loyalty standard that keeps tightening past the point anyone can satisfy it, with strategic denunciation baked in — whether in the historiography of the Terror or in political science generally? If there is, I want more than the label: tell me how the mechanism is supposed to work and where its edges are. And if there isn't a clean one, tell me which nearby concept fits best and exactly where it stops fitting, or coin me a term with a definition tight enough that I could test other revolutions (Russia, Iran) against it.
