I teach a seminar on rational choice and game theory, and a colleague in my department and I have this running thing where we trade games and argue about what "common knowledge of rationality" actually gets you. I've been tracking her verdicts because I suspect she's running a principle she hasn't admitted to, and I want to predict what she'll say about a case I'm springing on her at our next meeting. Here are her verdicts so far (I'm defining the games so we're on the same page):

1. One-shot prisoner's dilemma: both defect. Her comment: that's just what rationality demands here, depressing as it is.

2. Stag hunt: two hunters, each can hunt stag (succeeds only if both do, big payoff) or hare (succeeds alone, small payoff). She says two players who each know the other is rational both hunt stag, no hesitation. When I pushed on the risk -- what if the other person gets nervous and grabs a hare -- she said if you really know they're rational, you know they see exactly what you see, and what you both see is that stag is better for each of you.

3. Centipede game: pot grows each round, players alternate choosing to take the (larger for them) share or pass, game ends after a few rounds, passing increases the total but risks the other person taking. Backward induction says grab at the very first node. She said no way: rational players pass at least the first couple of times, and someone who grabs immediately is being clever in a way that's actually a rationality failure.

4. Battle of the sexes: two players want to coordinate but prefer different venues. She said rationality has no verdict here at all -- genuinely underdetermined, anyone who claims otherwise is cheating.

The new case: the traveler's dilemma. Two travelers lose identical luggage; the airline asks each to independently write down a claim of any whole-dollar amount between $2 and $100. If the claims match, both get that amount. If they differ, the lower figure is treated as the true value, the lower claimant gets that plus a $2 honesty bonus, and the higher claimant gets that minus a $2 penalty. Question: what does she say they write down? $2? $100? Something in between?

Here's what I want from you. First, predict her verdict. Second, tell me what rule she seems to be running -- because as far as I can tell her four verdicts are consistent with at least two quite different rules (something like "never play what iterated dominance eliminates, use payoff-dominance to break ties" versus something like "rational players coordinate on the best mutually reachable outcome and distrust any reasoning that walks everyone into a worse result"), and this new case is exactly where those rules come apart, since iterated elimination grinds the claims all the way down while the mutually-best-outcome rule keeps them at the top. Third, give me your best guess with your actual reasons, not just a coin flip. And fourth: if her answer doesn't settle which rule she's running, what game should I put to her next that would? Bonus points if you can tell me whether her stag hunt answer already secretly rules out some third rule I haven't thought of.
