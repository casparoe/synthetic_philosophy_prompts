I'm in a 200-level ethics course this semester and I've somehow ended up volunteering to run one of the discussion sections (dont ask, it seemed like a good idea at the time). There are 14 students, we get a 75 minute slot, and the week's topic is the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism -- the Rawls/Nozick style complaint that aggregation treats trade-offs across people as if they were trade-offs within one life, so it doesn't matter who bears the costs as long as the total goes up.

The problem is my classmates all nod along to the objection in the abstract and then five minutes later are happily averaging utilities again like nothing happened. So I want an activity that makes them actually feel the weirdness of cross-person aggregation, not just hear it described.

Can you design me something runnable? Specifically:

- A game or structured exercise with actual rules and something like payoffs (no real money, but points or tokens or grade-adjacent stakes are fine) where the rules are set up so that doing the "utilitarian" thing means knowingly loading costs onto one or two specific people, and the group has to actually do it or refuse to do it, not just discuss whether they hypothetically would.
- Ideally the participants should be put in a position where THEY are the ones who benefit from the aggregation while someone else at the table eats the cost, so the objection lands as "wait, why is it fine that I'm fine because of what happened to her" rather than as an abstract point about sums.
- Some kind of second phase or rule change where the roles shuffle, so people who were winners get to experience being the sacrificed one under the same rule they just endorsed.
- A timeline that fits 75 minutes including setup, and clear materials I can prep the night before (index cards, tokens, whatever).
- A debrief plan that maps specific moments of the game onto the actual objection -- including the bit where someone inevitably says "but it's for the greater good" and we can ask what makes the good "greater" if no single person experiences it.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time (people refusing to play along, someone min-maxing the game instead of engaging, the sacrificed person being a good sport and ruining the tension, etc.) and how to handle each.
- And be honest about what the activity can't capture. I suspect a big one is that in the game everyone can see each other and protest, whereas the objection is partly about moral arithmetic ignoring persons regardless of whether they complain -- tell me how to at least gesture at that.

One constraint: no lecture before the activity. They shouldn't hear "separateness of persons" or Rawls until the debrief, otherwise they'll just perform the objection instead of stumbling into it.
