I teach a mixed undergrad seminar on infinity (math and philosophy majors together) and we just finished the greatest hits: Hilbert's hotel, Cantor's diagonal argument, a bit on the finitist/constructivist backlash. My students came away finding the infinite kind of... cozy? The hotel always has room, the infinities stack up neatly into bigger and bigger sizes, it's all very tidy. This bugs me, because I remember infinity first hitting me as genuinely vertiginous, not charming. So for the last session I want to assign Borges's short story The Book of Sand -- the one where the narrator buys an infinite book from a door-to-door bookseller and it slowly ruins his life -- and I'd like you to write the reading I'll use as the backbone of the discussion.

A few things I specifically want the reading to engage. First, get into the weeds of the book's actual mathematical behavior: it has no first page, the page numbers appear in a jumble rather than a sequence, and the narrator can never find the same page twice, including the little illustrations. That's not how the natural numbers work at all -- omega has a first element and a fixed order. What structure does the book actually resemble? Something dense like the rationals? Something uncountable? Is the book even a consistent object, or did Borges describe something that's mathematically incoherent and the incoherence is part of the horror? Second, contrast it with Hilbert's hotel: the hotel is infinitely *accommodating*, the book is infinitely *withholding* -- you can always add a guest but you can never go back to a page. If these have (at least loosely) the same cardinality, then the dread can't come from size alone. What does the story suggest is actually disturbing about actual infinity -- unrepeatability? the impossibility of surveying it? Third, give the finitist their best shot: presumably they'd say any physical book is finite, the narrator is just bad at estimating, and the whole story is horror built on a misdescription, so the correct response to the story is a shrug. Is that a satisfying reply, or does the story dramatize something about our cognitive relationship to the infinite that survives even if every actual object is finite?

Also be honest about where the reading strains: Borges wrote a horror story, not a philosophy paper, and I don't want to tell my students the story "is really about" the rationals if that's an overfit. And say something about the ending -- the narrator finally hides the book among the hundreds of thousands of ordinary volumes in the national library where he works. Does dispersing the infinite book into a finite collection resolve anything, or is that ending doing something darker, like saying we've built our whole civilization of knowledge as a hiding place from the infinite? If the story gets something philosophically right that the textbook treatments miss, I want my students to leave feeling it.
