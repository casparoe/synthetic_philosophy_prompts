**Why Promises Bind: A First Attempt**

Nobody made me sign anything when I promised my sister I'd drive her to the airport. There was no contract, no enforcement, no consequences worth mentioning if I failed to show up. And yet it seems obvious to me that I would be doing something wrong if I simply didn't bother. This essay tries to explain where that wrongness comes from.

My theory is that a promise is a device for manufacturing certainty. The future is unpredictable, and other people are the least predictable part of it. When I promise, I voluntarily convert one small patch of the future from "uncertain" to "fixed," and I hand that patch to the promisee as a kind of gift. She can now build her plans on it. What I owe her is not really the drive to the airport; it's the fixedness itself. Breaking a promise is wrong because it destroys something I gave her, like smashing a gift I already handed over.

This has a consequence I find interesting: the wrongness of breaking a promise scales with how much the promisee built on it. If she never made any plans depending on my promise — say she forgot I ever made it — then breaking it is barely wrong, because the manufactured certainty was never used. The gift sat on the shelf.

A second consequence: promises made to people who cannot rely on them at all are nearly empty. Imagine promising a dying stranger that you'll scatter her ashes in the sea. She'll never know whether you did it; no plans depend on it. On my theory this promise binds only weakly, which feels wrong to me, and I can't yet say why.

Finally, I suspect there's a collective dimension. Every kept promise slightly raises everyone's confidence in promises generally; every broken one lowers it. So promise-breakers are free-riders on a shared resource they didn't produce.
