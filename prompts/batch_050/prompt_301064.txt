Our philosophy society records a long-form interview series, and our next guest is a moral philosopher whose recent book defends the thesis that lying is morally worse than misleading, other things equal. Her core claim is that asserting something you believe to be false solicits the hearer's trust in a way that merely inviting a false inference through true speech does not: the liar, on her account, abuses a relationship of good faith, while the misleader leaves the hearer to draw their own conclusion and therefore wrongs them less.

I want a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that goes well past having her restate the thesis. The questions should press where a well-prepared critic would press, and for each question I'd like the follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. In particular, please include:

1. At least one question built around a matched pair of cases -- a lie and a misleading utterance that produce the identical false belief, with the identical motive and harm -- asking where the extra residue of wrongness in the lie is supposed to come from, with a follow-up for the predictable appeal to what the speaker commits to.

2. At least one question on the agency reply: her view leans on the idea that the misled hearer retains interpretive freedom the lied-to hearer lacks. Press on whether ordinary hearers can actually help drawing standard conversational implicatures, and if they cannot, whether the residual agency is doing any moral work.

3. At least one question on the skill asymmetry: on her view, the articulate person who misleads and the inarticulate person who lies about the same thing come out morally unequal, which seems to make blameworthiness track rhetorical talent.

4. At least one question on cases where misleading looks worse than lying -- for instance, a misleading truth engineered so that the hearer feels they reached the conclusion themselves, making the false belief stickier -- and whether her ranking survives reversing cases.

5. A closing question about what would have to be true, empirically or conceptually, for her to concede that the lying/misleading distinction carries no moral weight of its own.

Avoid anything generic; every question should turn on the actual machinery of her position.
