I'm prepping a seminar on Bell's theorem and locality for our grad philosophy of physics course, and I keep coming back to a passage from Einstein's 1905 paper "On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies" (section 1, where he sets up clock synchronization). Here's the passage:

"If at the point A of space there is a clock, an observer at A can determine the time values of events in the immediate proximity of A by finding the positions of the hands which are simultaneous with these events. If there is at the point B of space another clock in all respects resembling the one at A, it is possible for an observer at B to determine the time values of events in the immediate neighbourhood of B. But it is not possible without further assumption to compare, in respect of time, an event at A with an event at B. We have so far defined only an "A time" and a "B time." We have not defined a common "time" for A and B, for the latter cannot be defined at all unless we establish by definition that the "time" required by light to travel from A to B equals the "time" it requires to travel from B to A."

Two things I want help with, and I'd like you to be rigorous about both. Act as an expert philosopher of physics who also actually knows the physics -- please think carefully, and start by brainstorming two or three candidate readings of the passage before committing to one, then show me why the reading you settle on wins.

First, the passage itself. What exactly is the "by definition" doing? My students always read this as Einstein being some kind of naive operationalist or verificationist, but that seems too quick: he doesn't say time is clock-readings, he says a common time for spatially separated events requires a stipulation about one-way light travel. So is this conventionalism about simultaneity, a claim about the epistemology of distant events, or a physical claim smuggled in as a definition? And what follows for the status of the one-way speed of light as opposed to the round-trip speed?

Second, the application, which is the real point of the seminar. Bell-locality is standardly glossed as "no superluminal influence": the outcome at one wing can't depend on the setting at the distant wing. But if this passage is right that cross-spatial temporal ordering requires a convention, then "the influence travelled from A to B faster than light" seems to inherit whatever conventionality simultaneity has -- for spacelike separated events, different frames even disagree about which event came first. So: does Einstein's point here mean the everyday gloss of Bell's theorem as ruling out "faster-than-light influence" is ill-formed unless we state locality in a Lorentz-invariant way? Does it undercut the Bohmian move of just accepting a preferred foliation (since the passage arguably shows nature gives us no empirical handle on one)? And conversely, is there a way the passage actually strengthens the nonlocality conclusion -- because if even our definition of distant simultaneity is stipulative, then the correlations' frame-independence is the only non-negotiable fact, and Bell shows that fact can't be explained locally?

I definately don't want the watered-down version where "Einstein showed time is relative and Bell showed quantum mechanics is weird." Push on where the argument actually bites and where it doesn't, and flag it if you think I'm misreading either the passage or the standard statements of Bell's theorem.
