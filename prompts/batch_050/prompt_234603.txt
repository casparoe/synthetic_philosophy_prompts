I am writing my dissertation on responsibility practices, and I am struck by how little ground the exchange between compatibilist defenders of blame in Dennett's orbit and free-will sceptics like Caruso has gained, given how much the two sides already agree on. Both reject libertarian free will; both want punishment and blame justified forward-lookingly, through deterrence, incapacitation, and rehabilitation. The residual fight is over basic desert: Caruso argues that absent ultimate responsibility nobody deserves blame or punishment, that the reactive attitudes are corrigible and largely unwarranted, and that our practices should be remodelled on something like a public-health approach to dangerous behaviour; Dennett replies that a suitably naturalised desert survives, that being held to account within a rule-governed practice is what deserving blame comes to, and that the sceptic is applying a standard of deservingness that nobody could meet and that nothing in our practices actually requires. My working hypothesis is that this ceased to be a metaphysical disagreement the moment both parties abandoned libertarianism, and that it is really a disguised normative dispute about how much of our ordinary moral psychology may legitimately survive naturalisation — that is, a question about the permissible scope of conceptual revision. Please diagnose the disagreement: is my hypothesis right, or is there a live substantive question underneath, perhaps about what a practice of blame would have to be like in order to count as one of desert, which neither side has managed to state cleanly? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — an agreed criterion for when a revised concept still counts as the same concept, empirical evidence about whether the reactive attitudes can be decoupled from desert attributions, or something else entirely?
