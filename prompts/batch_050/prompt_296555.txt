For the past year I have been part of an informal reading group that has been working through the socialist calculation debate, and we keep circling the same impasse. The outline as we understand it: Mises argued that without private ownership of the means of production there can be no genuine market prices for capital goods, and therefore no rational basis for allocating them; Lange replied that a Central Planning Board could announce prices parametrically, instruct managers to follow simple rules, and adjust prices by trial and error until excess demands clear, thereby replicating what a market achieves; Hayek then pressed a different objection, that the knowledge economic coordination actually runs on is dispersed, tacit, and time-sensitive, and cannot in principle be transmitted to a central board. The mid-century textbook verdict, encouraged by Schumpeter, was that Lange had answered Mises on the terms Mises had set. The later Austrian retellings insist that this verdict rested on conflating Mises's argument with Hayek's and mistaking a static equilibrium exercise for the real problem. Meanwhile the debate has gone live again: computational planning advocates now argue that modern processing power and detailed input-output data make central calculation tractable, and others point out that large firms like Walmart are internally planned economies bigger than entire historical planned states, which they take to show that scale alone cannot be the decisive issue.

My working hypothesis is that the two camps are answering different questions under one heading -- one side asking whether a planner could in principle reproduce a general equilibrium given the data, the other asking how the data come to exist and get discovered in the first place -- and that each side keeps winning its own question while the historical record (early Soviet industrial growth followed by stagnation) underdetermines the dispute because it conflates calculation with incentives and politics. Is that diagnosis right? Or is this instead a substantive disagreement about the epistemology of economic knowledge, an empirical disagreement about the informational and computational feasibility of planning that could actually be settled, or partly a verbal one about what rational calculation means? I want the diagnosis argued for, drawing on what each position actually claims rather than a survey of who said what, and I want it to say what would count as progress given the diagnosis: a conceptual distinction between kinds of knowledge, a formal result about the complexity of the planning problem, an attempted implementation of computational planning at firm or regional scale, or an argument that no such resolution should be expected because the sides' standards for what an economy must explain were never shared. Where part of the dispute is verbal, identify exactly which part and why.

Please lead with your bottom-line diagnosis in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
