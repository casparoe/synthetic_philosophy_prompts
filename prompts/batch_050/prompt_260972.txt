At our philosophy reading group last night, Nadia and Theo had a surprisingly heated arguement about laws of nature using this toy case: imagine a Newtonian universe containing fifty particles, all of whose actual interactions perfectly obey an inverse-square gravitational law. There is also one extra particle sealed forever in a causally isolated pocket, so it never attracts or is attracted by anything. The question was whether the actual mosaic of events settles what that hidden particle would do if the pocket were opened. Nadia defended a roughly Humean best-system view: laws are not extra ingredients in the world, but the axioms of the system that best balances simplicity, strength, and fit with the total pattern of particular facts. On her view, the inverse-square law is obviously part of the best system, and the isolated particle makes no difference.

Theo replied that this might describe the pattern but cannot explain it: the equation does not make the fifty particles move as they do, and a merely descriptive regularity cannot ground the further fact that the hidden particle would attract the others. He said a law has to govern, or involve some objective necessitation relation, so that the counterfactual is already settled rather than retrospectively read off a summary. Nadia answered that best-system laws can support counterfactuals: to ask what would happen if the pocket were opened, we vary the initial conditions while holding the best-system laws fixed, and the relevant nearby worlds are ones where the particle accelerates by inverse-square attraction. She also pressed Theo on whether saying that a law “governs” does anything more than attach an impressive label to the same regularity unless he can explain how this extra necessity produces or constrains events.

Theo then argued that the best-system account faces an underdetermination problem. A slightly uglier system could agree about all actual events but say that if the pocket were opened, the particle would obey an inverse-cube law instead. If both systems fit the entire mosaic, then the choice turns on simplicity; but if simplicity is partly dependent on our language, interests, or representational conventions, then what would happen seems disturbingly mind-dependent. Nadia said simplicity can instead track objective natural structure, and that Theo has the same problem anyway: two different alleged necessitation facts could be compatible with exactly the same mosaic, so his governing laws are also empirically undetectable. Theo replied that there is a difference between a fact being unknowable and there being no fact at all—the anti-Humean can say one necessitation relation really obtains even if nobody could discover which. Nadia’s final move was that this gives us metaphysical room for a difference without earning an explanation of it, and leaves mysterious how an abstract relation pushes concrete particles around. Can you adjudicate the exchange move by move, saying who is right on explanation, counterfactuals, simplicity, underdetermination, and the knowability of unmanifested laws? Please also say what distinction or consideration both of them may be missing, and give an overall verdict about whose position comes out stronger in this particular arguement rather than just describing generic Humean and anti-Humean views.
