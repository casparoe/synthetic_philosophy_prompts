So I'm a retired molecular biologist, spent forty years in the lab, and a couple of years ago I fell down the philosophy rabbit hole, which my kids find hilarious. Lately I've gotten stuck on the Epicurean argument about death -- the old line that death can't be bad for you because when you exist death doesn't, and when death exists you don't, so there's no subject around to be harmed. I've chewed on the standard replies (the deprivationist "death is bad because of the life it takes from you" line, the Nagel-style comparison with things that harm you while you sleep, the stuff about whether a person can be harmed after they stop existing), and honestly I keep coming back to the deprivationist side: death is bad for the person who dies, roughly in proportion to the good life it cuts off, and the Epicurean no-subject worry is a puzzle to be dissolved rather than a serious threat. That the position I now tentitively hold.

Here's what I want from you: treat my position as the thing under examination and tell me what would count against it. Not just a recitation of the counterarguments I've already met in the survey literature -- I want a map of the view's actual exposure. Which intuitions does deprivationism depend on that could turn out to be parochial or unreliable (I'm thinking of how we feel about the asymmetry between prenatal nonexistence and posthumous nonexistence, Lucretius's old point, which deprivationists seem to handle a bit too smoothly for my taste)? Which consequences is the view committed to that might turn out to be unacceptable -- for instance, does it commit me to saying an early painless death of someone with decades ahead is a far greater evil than most people actually treat it as, or to awkward verdicts about whether it would have been better to be born much earlier? Are there possible cases it can't cleanly describe -- gradual fading, cryonics-style scenarios, or cases where the counterfactual life is itself of unclear value? And what formal or formal-ish results in neighboring areas (population ethics, the badness of coming into existence, comparability of lives) would force a revision if they went one way rather than another?

Please rank the defeaters from genuinely fatal to merely embarassing, and be honest about which ones a committed deprivationist would actually concede as threats versus which they'd bat away as misunderstanding the view (and whether the batting-away looks legitimate or defensive to you). I'm also interested in the meta-question: has deprivationism been qualified so much -- timing problems finessed, the subject of harm handled with "the person who would have lived" -- that it no longer risks anything? If so, is that a vice for a view like this, or is unfalsifiability less of a sin in value theory than it was in my old field, where a theory that fit everything was a theory that explained nothing? Where I personally would be most persuadable is on cases: show me a case the view gets clearly wrong and I'll move. Tell me which case, if any, that should be.
