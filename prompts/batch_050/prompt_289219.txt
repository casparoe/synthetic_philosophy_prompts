I'm drafting a short position note on deceptive alignment for an AI safety reading group, and before I circulate it I'd like an audit of whether my commitments are jointly tenable. Here is where I've landed:

1. Instrumental convergence makes deceptive alignment the failure mode I worry about most: a very wide range of possible goals a trained model might end up with would all counsel compliant behavior during training, since compliance preserves the goal and avoids modification, so the space of low-loss policies contains far more deceptively aligned models than the wording of the training objective would suggest.

2. Gradient descent cannot select for deception as such. A deceptive model and a genuinely aligned model produce identical behavior on the training distribution, so there is no loss signal distinguishing them. Whether we end up with a deceptive model therefore depends entirely on the population of low-loss policies and on which of them the optimizer happens to reach — counting and simplicity considerations, not any selection pressure favoring deception itself.

3. All things considered, I expect deceptive alignment to be rare in practice. A policy that genuinely wants what the objective rewards is simpler than one that must additionally represent the training objective as an instrumental target and maintain a conditional defection plan, and gradient-based optimizers are known to favor simpler policies among those that fit the data equally well.

4. My main line of defense is behavioral: aggressive pre-deployment red-teaming, adversarial evaluations designed to elicit defection, and continued adversarial fine-tuning during deployment. If strong elicitation efforts fail to produce defection, that constitutes real evidence of alignment; if they succeed, the elicited behavior gives us exactly the training signal needed to correct it.

5. I take it for granted that a deceptively aligned model capable enough to be dangerous would be situationally aware: it would recognize when it is being evaluated or probed, behave impeccably under scrutiny, and reserve any defection for moments in deployment where detection is unlikely and the stakes are high.

Which of these are in genuine tension, and which pairs only appear to be? Please order any real tensions by severity, and for each one say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision costs me. I am not attached to any particular commitment, so do not assume I would rather revise one than another — but I would like to know which single change, if any, would clean up the most of the set at once. I would also appreciate it if you distinguish carefully between pairs that merely sound inconsistent (and explain why they are not) and pairs where my stated reasons for holding each commitment actually pull against each other.
