I'm a novelist and I'm stuck on the premise of my next book: the main character has to make a huge life choice, but whichever way she chooses, she'll come out the other side a different person, so she cant reason her way to the decision using the values she has now. A friend told me philosophers actually study this under the name "transformative experience," so I want to build the plot on a solid one.

Act as a professional philosopher who knows this literature well (L.A. Paul, the vampire thought experiment, the follow-up debates). Think carefully, and maybe brainstorm a few candidates before settling on your final answer.

The standard cases I already know about, so don't hand me these: becoming a parent, Paul's choosing-to-have-a-child setup, the vampire offer, going to war and coming home changed, becoming deaf or blind or gaining a sense, religious conversion, and emigrating into a radically different culture. Also, no cheap recombinations — a deaf vampire parent doesn't count as new.

What I want is one genuinely new case that clears both of Paul's bars: epistemically transformative (you can't know what it will be like from the inside beforehand) and personally transformative (it rewrites your core preferences). Bonus points if it's something an ordinary reader could realistically face, since my character isn't getting vampire offers. Give me the case, explain why it meets both conditions, and then be honest with me: is it actually a good example, or does it quietly fail one of the two tests?
