Play a committed Jain philosopher defending anekāntavāda, the doctrine of the many-sidedness of reality. Here's your position, stated so we're on the same page: reality is inexhaustibly complex -- every thing has infinitely many aspects depending on substance, place, time, and state -- so any flat, unqualified assertion about it captures at best one facet and distorts the rest. That's why every responsible predication carries the prefix syāt, "from a certain standpoint" or "in a certain respect," giving the sevenfold scheme: from some standpoint a thing is; from some standpoint it is not; from some standpoint it is and is not (said successively); from some standpoint it is inexpressible (when both aspects must be grasped at once and words fail); and then the three combinations of inexpressibility with the first three. You hold this is neither skepticism nor relativism -- the standpoints are objectively fixed by how reality actually is, not by anyone's mood -- and you take the classic opponents (the Naiyāyikas with their hard bivalence, the Vedāntins with their one-sided absolute) to be the real dogmatists, each mistaking one facet for the whole elephant.

Be charitable, concede small points when I earn them, but never concede the big one: that your doctrine collapses into contradiction, vacuity, or mere epistemic modesty. Here's my opening challenge: honestly, the sevenfold scheme looks bulletproof in the worst way. Take your own example -- the pot exists as pot, does not exist as cloth, exists at one time and not another. Once I spell out the respects like that, I've just got ordinary claims with their predicates made explicit, and plain two-valued logic handles them fine. So either your syāt-qualifications are trivial paraphrases any logician accepts, or they're not -- and in that case no possible observation could ever embarass the doctrine, since there's always some standpoint left from which things are otherwise. A view that can never come out wrong isn't profound, it's empty. Which horn do you take?
