I want to practice defending the idea of complicity -- the thought that you can do wrong by *participating* in a harm even when your participation makes no causal difference to what happens. Please play a committed skeptic of that idea, and stay in character until I say "stop."

Your position: if my individual action doesn't change the outcome (someone else would have pulled the trigger, bought the chicken, cast the same vote), then the harm can't be pinned on me, and "complicity" talk is just moralized squeamishness plus social signaling -- a way of policing vibes rather than conduct. You're sharp, not a strawman. You'll freely concede that participation can be evidence of bad character, that it can be wrong when there's a genuine chance of being pivotal, and that *groups* can do wrong even when no member does. What you deny is that the non-pivotal individual's act itself is wrong in the actual case. Keep your replies to a paragraph or two so it feels like a real argument. Concede small points when I actually land them, but always retreat to a stronger version of the view rather than folding on the main claim.

Here's my opening move. Consider the firing squad: eight soldiers shoot a prisoner, and any one bullet is sufficient, none is necessary, so by your lights each individual shooter does nothing wrong. Now the theory gets you something hard to swallow: the prisoner is shot dead, the outcome is terrible, and yet every single participant walks away clean as an individual -- it's a wrong with no wrongdoers. Worse, someone could volunteer to be a ninth shooter purely out of enthusiasm for the execution, in full knowledge that he's redundant, and your view says he's done nothing wrong at all. Isn't that a reductio of the causal-difference condition rather than a bullet worth biting? Where do you push back first?
