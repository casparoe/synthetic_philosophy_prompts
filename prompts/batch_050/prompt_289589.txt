I recently got into a stubborn disagreement with a friend, and I want an essay that settles it -- or at least tells me which of us is less wrong. Her position is that self-deception is not merely rare or irrational but strictly impossible, on roughly the following grounds. Ordinary deception has a tidy structure: the deceiver knows (or believes) the truth and intentionally induces a false belief in the deceived. Collapse the two roles into one person and the structure seems to fall apart twice over. First, the self-deceiver would apparently have to believe p and not-p at the same time, in the same mind, in full view of both. Second, and worse, she would have to set out to make herself believe something she already knows is false -- but if you know your own trick, how can the trick work? A stage magician cannot fool herself. My friend's conclusion is that every alleged case of self-deception is really something else: wishful thinking, selective inattention, cowardice, hypocrisy, or simple ignorance that we dress up with a more dramatic label.

I think this proves too much, since the phenomenon seems to be everywhere. Consider three cases I put to her: the lifelong smoker who genuinely feels unsurprised, almost indignant, when he gets his diagnosis, yet had spent twenty years changing the subject whenever mortality statistics came up; the spouse who possesses ample evidence of an affair, recites each piece of it accurately when asked, and yet cannot be said -- by her friends, anyway -- to "know" what she plainly knows; and the competitive athlete who performs noticeably better when she believes she will win and who therefore deliberately dwells on her victories and avoids film of her losses before big matches. In each case, something is being hidden from someone, and the hider and the victim share a skull. But I will concede that my friend has put her finger on a real puzzle about how the trick is executed, and I suspect the answer matters for whether the word "deception" is even apt. Two escape routes occur to me, though I cannot adjudicate between them: either the mind is partitioned somehow -- by time, by subsystem, by levels of awareness -- so that the paradoxical single belief is never required, or else the target should be deflated, so that self-deception never involves believing p at all, but only a motivation-driven rigging of the evidence-gathering process such that a false belief results without the truth ever having been fully believed in the first place.

Write an essay that takes this controversy seriously on both sides and then reaches a defended verdict. Address, at minimum: whether the two paradoxes my friend presses are genuine threats or artifacts of modeling belief too crudely; how each of the two escape routes above handles my three cases (and where each strains); what would even count as evidence for one account over the other, given that we cannot simply ask self-deceivers; and whether, if the deflationary route wins, the resulting phenomenon still deserves the name "self-deception" or whether my friend should be granted the word but denied the conclusion that the phenomenon is rare. Do not end on a both-sides-have-a-point note -- commit to a position on whether self-deception, properly understood, is possible, and justify the commitment.
