I am preparing a term paper for a graduate seminar on genocide and social theory, and I want to stress-test a reading before I commit to it. Zygmunt Bauman's argument in Modernity and the Holocaust, as I understand it, is that the Holocaust was not a regression into barbarism but an expression of distinctively modern capacities: bureaucratic division of labor that fragments moral responsibility, instrumental rationality that treats persons as variables in an administrative problem, physical and psychological distance between decision-makers and victims, and the displacement of moral evaluation by technical and procedural criteria. My question is what happens to this thesis when it is applied to the 1994 genocide in Rwanda, an episode Bauman does not analyze and which appears, on its face, to be nearly the opposite of his paradigm case: roughly 800,000 people killed in about a hundred days, much of the killing done face-to-face with machetes and clubs, frequently by neighbors and acquaintances, in a country with a relatively modest state apparatus.

Please treat the Rwandan case as common knowledge (roadblocks, identity cards, kill lists, the Interahamwe, incitement over RTLM radio, the collapse of distinctions between civilian and participant) and do the analysis in both directions. First, map Bauman's framework onto the episode with some discipline: where do his mechanisms genuinely apply — for instance, is the role of administrative lists, prefectural organization, and radio-mediated instruction enough to count as bureaucratic mediation, or does the intimacy of the killing defeat the distance thesis outright? Say clearly where the framework is silent rather than forcing it to speak. Second, run the inference the other way: if Bauman's theory of genocide-as-modernity cannot accommodate the most rapid mass killing of the twentieth century, carried out largely without the machinery he identifies as enabling, does that refute the theory, restrict its scope to industrialized states, or reveal that it was never a general theory of genocide at all but a reinterpretation of a single case?

Finally, connect this to the uniqueness and comparability debate. If a theory built to explain the Holocaust's distinctively modern character travels badly to Rwanda, does that vindicate the uniqueness thesis in an unexpected way — uniqueness not of moral horror but of causal mechanism? Or does it instead suggest that "modernity" in Bauman's hands is doing less explanatory work than advertised, and that some more basic variable (state sponsorship of killing, whatever its technological level) is what his case and Rwanda actually share? I want an honest verdict on which of these readings the comparison supports, and whether Bauman's defenders have a principled way to hold the theory's generality without diluting it into a claim about states in general.
