I need a thought experiment built to order, on causation by omission. Here's the spec.

Set up one realistic, everyday scenario (an office, a warehouse, an apartment building, whatever -- no sci-fi, and please not another gardener forgetting to water the flowers) where something bad happens and there are at least two salient absences that both look causally relevant. Engineer it so that three standard takes on omission causation each give a different verdict:

1. A straight counterfactual account (an absence is a cause if, had the missing thing happened, the outcome wouldn't have) should count BOTH absences as causes -- and ideally it should also, embarassingly, count a pile of other absences nobody would take seriously (the mayor's failure to personally patrol the area, etc.).
2. A norm-based account (the cause of an outcome is the absence of what was supposed to happen, where "supposed" gets fixed by duties, roles, or standing expectations) should count exactly ONE of the two absences as the cause.
3. A productive or physical-connection account (causation needs some actual process or transfer between events, so strictly speaking absences cause nothing) should count NEITHER -- on this view the outcome was caused entirely by what did happen, full stop.

The scenario itself should be a paragraph, max. Then do the check: for each of the three accounts, state its verdict on your case and show that the verdict falls out of the theory's own machinery rather than you just stipulating it. I'm using this to figure out whether norm-based accounts actually earn their keep against the counterfactual one, so no rigging -- don't just write "and obviously the norm was X"; the duty or expectation has to be the kind of thing that would plausibly exist in that setting anyway (a job description, a lease, standard practice), and the second absence has to genuinely lack that backing.

Then tell me which feature of the scenario is doing all the work in prying verdicts 1 and 2 apart, and what happens to each verdict if you remove that feature. Finally, the counterfactual account is presumably going to come out of this looking the most bloated, so finish with the best reply its defender has to your case: either why the extra "causes" aren't really a cost, or why your norm-based verdict is smuggling morality into what's supposed to be metaphysics.
