I work in a research lab that recently retired a long-established measurement technique in favor of a newer platform, and I now have to decide whether to report decades of our old results alongside the new ones in a paper we are drafting. The conceptual question underneath that practical one came to a head at our lab meeting last week, where two senior colleagues got into a genuine argument about Kuhnian paradigm shifts and incommensurability, and I would like an outside adjudication.

Mara's position was that our situation is a small-scale version of what Kuhn described. The new platform does not merely measure the same quantities faster, she said; it reclassifies phenomena, treats as artifacts some things the old technique counted as signal, and comes with its own standards for what a good result looks like. Because of that, the old and new datasets are incommensurable, and any conversion factor we might construct begs the question, since it presupposes that both techniques measure the same underlying quantity. When Daniel objected that this makes theory choice irrational, Mara said Kuhn accepted that consequence: paradigm choice is more like a gestalt switch than a proof, and the factors that settle it are not evidential in the way scientists like to think.

Daniel replied that Mara was inflating a meaning-theoretic point into a methodological one. Incommensurability, on his reading, concerns shifts in the meaning of theoretical terms, and failure of exact translation between vocabularies does not preclude rational comparison of theories or of measurements. He added that Kuhn himself denied being an irrationalist about theory choice, and that Mara was leaning on the early, most dramatic formulations while ignoring the later, milder view. Mara answered that even on the milder view the problem survives locally: predictive success is itself defined differently under the two techniques, since each has different criteria for distinguishing genuine effects from artifacts, so Daniel's appeal to comparison by predictive success is circular. Daniel's final move was to say that Mara's position is self-undermining, because if the two techniques really could not be compared, then our lab's evidence-based decision to switch would have been impossible — yet we made it, and we made it on reasons. Mara said that conflates having reasons for switching with the switch constituting rational progress, and that Kuhn's whole point is that these come apart.

I have tried to report this neutrally, because I genuinely do not know who is right, and my methods section depends on it. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who is right about each specific point: whether a change of measurement standards within one lab can count as a paradigm shift at all, whether incommensurability blocks the construction of principled conversion factors, whether predictive-success comparisons are circular in the way Mara alleges, and whether Daniel's self-undermining argument actually lands? I would also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing — for instance, whether there are distinctions (say, between kinds of incommensurability, or between comparability and a common measure) that would dissolve parts of their dispute without resolving all of it.
