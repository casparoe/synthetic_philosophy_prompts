I am a high school senior, and for the past year I have run a small philosophy club at my school. Next semester we have twelve weekly meetings, and I have decided to devote the entire semester to a single topic: the ethics of an infinite universe. My motivation is a passage I keep returning to from Nick Bostrom's paper "Infinite Ethics," where he defines the key case:

"More precisely, suppose that there is some finite value ε such that there exists an infinite number of local phenomena (this could be a subset of e.g. persons, experiences, characters, virtuous acts, lives, relationships, civilizations, or ecosystems) each of which has a value ≥ ε and also an infinite number of local phenomena each of which has a value ≤ (− ε). Call such a world canonically infinite."

Bostrom's worry is that in a canonically infinite world, anything we do changes the total amount of good and bad by only a finite amount, and a finite change leaves an infinite total untouched — so aggregative moral theories seem to imply that nothing we do matters. I cannot have my club spend the semester vaguely worrying about this. I need a defended agenda: which open questions we should actually spend our twelve meetings on, and in what order.

Here are the candidate questions I have in mind, though you may substitute better ones if you think I have framed things poorly:

(1) The localism question. Bostrom's definition assumes that value attaches to local phenomena and then aggregates. Could value instead attach to global features of the world — the overall pattern or distribution of joy and suffering across the cosmos — in a way that finite agents can genuinely change, thereby escaping paralysis without patching the arithmetic?

(2) The comparison question. When two worlds both contain infinite good and infinite bad, on what basis can we say one is better? Should we compare populations by density or limiting proportions rather than by totals, and does any such rule avoid collapsing into counterintuitive verdicts?

(3) The non-Archimedean question. If we let moral value take infinite and infinitesimal magnitudes (so that an infinite total can register a finite improvement), does the theory stay coherent, or does it generate worse absurdities than the paralysis it cures?

(4) The uncertainty question. We do not know that the cosmos is canonically infinite; we only have some probability that it is. Does decision-making under that uncertainty require a different rule than the certain case, and does the mere possibility of infinitude swamp finite considerations even at low probability?

(5) The empirical question. Whether modern cosmology actually supports a canonically infinite world.

Please rank these (or your improved versions) by how they should claim our twelve meetings, and defend the ranking. Specifically: tell me which criteria you are using (importance for resolving the paralysis, tractability for smart high schoolers without graduate-level math, and how many of the other questions depend on this one's answer); tell me which questions you would cut entirely and why — I suspect one of them is a time sink that feels productive but would not move the others; and tell me what I could learn in the first few meetings that should make me re-rank the rest. I am not asking for a summary of the debate; I am asking which parts of it deserve our scarce hours.

End your response with a final line of exactly the form \answer{...} containing a short phrase naming the single question you rank first.
