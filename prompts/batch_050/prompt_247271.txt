I'm a philosophy PhD student (I work on classical Chinese ethics) and I got roped into being the grad student rep on my university's academic integrity council. We have a case coming up next week and I volunteered to draft the descision memo, which I now regret, so please write it for me.

The case: a second-year undergrad, call him D., plagiarized about a page of a term paper — lifted from an essay floating around online and lightly reworded. Two things complicate it. First, he basically confessed before anyone accused him of anything. His TA noticed something off about a passage and emailed him a neutral clarifying question about it, and D. wrote back within the hour with a full unprompted admission, apparently pretty distraught. Second, his major has a genuine corner-cutting culture — three other cases from the same cohort this year alone, and upperclassmen openly pass around old essays. The Ox Mountain parallel practically writes itself, though I don't want the memo to be too cute about that.

The council has exactly two options on the table: (a) the standard sanction — failing grade in the course, one-semester suspension, notation on the transcript; or (b) a new "educative" track the provost's office floated this year — he stays enrolled, takes a one-credit academic ethics seminar, meets weekly with a writing mentor for a term, and writes a reflection essay that gets reviewed, with successful completion expunging the notation.

The vote is splitting roughly along a Mencius/Xunzi line, which is honestly my fault for bringing it up at the last meeting. The Mencius camp: D.'s instant unprompted remorse is exactly the spontaneous alarm response, like anyone who suddenly sees a kid about to fall into a well — evidence his moral sprouts are intact, and the real culprit is an environment that kept hacking away at them, so a suspension plus a permanent mark just does more environmental damage to something that was already recovering on its own. The Xunzi camp: spontaneous guilt is cheap and proves nothing about future behavior; people get reshaped through deliberate artifice and sustained external structure — crooked wood gets steamed straight, it doesn't straighten itself — and the educative track only works *because* it's coercive scaffolding, so don't flatter ourselves that we're nurturing sprouts. They also point out that with three prior cases in that cohort, whatever we do here functions as a signal to everyone else, and soft treatment has deterrent costs that good feelings about D.'s conscience don't cancel.

Write the memo I'll circulate: recommend (a), (b), or a specific hybrid with concrete conditions attached, bottom line up front, roughly 800–1200 words. It should read like a decision document the council can actually vote on, not a term paper — and it has to give the strongest argument on the losing side a fair shake, since half these people are voting the other way and I sit across from them every month.
