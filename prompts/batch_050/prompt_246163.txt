I'm a TA for a second-year philosophy of law course, and I've got one 50-minute discussion section to teach the puzzle of naked statistical evidence. The students have just finished a unit on standards of proof, so they know "preponderance of the evidence" and "beyond reasonable doubt" as seperate phrases, but nobody has made them sit with the weirdness: in the gatecrasher case (1000 people at the rodeo, only 499 paid, the organizer sues an attendee picked more or less at random) the plaintiff has better-than-even odds, and in the blue bus case the plaintiff's evidence can be made as strong as you like, and still almost nobody wants to find for the plaintiff.

Act as someone who has taught this paradox to undergrads a dozen times and knows exactly where it goes wrong. Think carefully -- maybe brainstorm the misconception map first -- and then give me a teaching plan, not the explanation itself. Specifically:

- The order of ideas: what has to be in place before the puzzle even bites. I'm guessing they first need to genuinely absorb that civil courts openly decide cases on the probabilities, since most of them quietly assume verdicts rest on something more solid. Tell me what else comes first, and what depends on what.
- The carrying example: I was planning to open with the blue bus and save the gatecrasher for later, but I'm second-guessing that. Argue for which one should carry the section, what that example has to have and has to lack to do its job, and what the other one is for once it shows up. I also have a case of my own: a festival where the scanners show 9,500 of 10,000 attendees jumped the fence, and the organizer sues one attendee -- first on the numbers alone, then, in a second version, after digging up a blurry photo of that very person climbing the fence. Tell me where in the sequence this earns its keep, if anywhere, and what it's good for that the classics aren't.
- The misconceptions, in the order they'll surface: I expect "80% just isn't enough, you'd need 95" and "this is really just the civil vs. criminal difference." What else should I expect, and exactly where in the sequence do I head each one off rather than letting it harden?
- One question to ask at the end that shows me whether they got the distinction rather than memorized a slogan.
- What to explicitly leave out at this level and say so. I assume the reference class problem is one; is the "but DNA database matches feel totally fine" issue another, or does it belong in the room?

One constraint: don't just hand me the received label "individualized evidence" as the answer. Part of the point is that the students should feel the pull of the puzzle before any fix gets named, so tell me how long to let them sit in it.
