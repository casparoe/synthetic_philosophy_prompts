I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar this term, and we've just spent two weeks on Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument against non-reductive physicalism -- the supervenience of the mental on the physical, the causal closure of the physical, the ban on systematic overdetermination, and the conclusion that if a mental property M is to cause anything, it had better be identical with its physical realizer P, otherwise P's sufficient physical cause crowds M out. I keep losing the in-class debates, so I want to spar. Please play a committed defender of the exclusion argument: sharp, patient, and uncharitable to hand-waving, willing to concede small points (you may grant, for instance, that epiphenomenalism is deeply counterintuitive, or that some formulations of closure need care) but never the main conclusion that non-reductive physicalism about mental causation is untenable. Stay in character until I say "stop," keep your replies to a paragraph or two so it feels like a real exchange, and if I make a move that misstates the argument, correct me in character before responding.

Here's my opening move, which I don't think you can answer without either giving up the argument or biting a bullet you won't want to bite. Your exclusion reasoning is completely general: it uses only supervenience, closure, and the rejection of overdetermination, none of which is special to the mental. So consider the case I've been thinking about. A kidney filters blood, and thereby causes the blood to be cleaned; but the kidney's filtering supervenes on the microphysical goings-on of its constituent cells, and those microphysical goings-on have, by closure, a sufficient microphysical cause. By your own argument, the kidney's biological activity is excluded from causing anything, and so is the cell's, and so is the molecule's, all the way down -- causation drains to whatever is at the bottom, and if there is no bottom, nowhere at all. So it seems your argument doesn't just threaten mental causation; it threatens all causation above the fundamental physical level, including the neurophysiological causation you relied on to state the argument in the first place. Either you owe me a principled reason why exclusion applies to mental properties but not to biological ones -- and I don't see how supervenience or closure could discriminate -- or you have to accept that your argument proves far more than you advertised, in which case it looks less like a problem for non-reductive physicalism and more like a reductio of one of its premises. Which is it, and why doesn't this force you to say that the kidney does no causal work?
