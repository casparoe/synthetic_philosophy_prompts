In my philosophy of perception seminar we've just finished a unit on the argument from illusion and disjunctivism, and I came away realizing I don't actually know what I believe — I can rehearse the moves (Hinton's disjunctive formulation of appearances, McDowell's resistance to the "highest common factor" conception, the causal argument from hallucination) but I couldn't tell you my own credence in the view or what it's hostage to. So here is a precise proposition: let D be the thesis that a subject's veridical perception of, say, a red cube, and a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination of a red cube, are experiences of fundamentally different mental kinds — that there is no single experiential factor present in both episodes, and that the appearance of a common factor is merely epistemic, a matter of the hallucinating subject being unable to know her experience is not a perception. What is your credence in D?

I want the reasoning decomposed rather than the number alone, and to force the issue I want you to route part of it through a scenario I haven't seen in the literature we read. Suppose that by 2080 neuroengineers have built a replay device that can induce in a blindfolded subject a brain state that is, by every measure available to a mature neuroscience, token-identical to the brain state that subject was in during an earlier veridical perception of a red cube — and the induced episode is subjectively indistinguishable from the original. Intuitively this seems lethal to D: a perfect internal duplicate of a veridical perception that is nonetheless a hallucination looks like exactly the common factor the disjunctivist denies. But presumably a sophisticated disjunctivist will deny that experiential kinds are individuated by neural states at all, and hold that what the device produces is a state that is internally identical and yet not the same kind of experience. Decompose your credence in D into, at minimum: the probability that the inference from subjective indistinguishability to a shared experiential kind is sound even before the neural scenario is introduced; the probability that experiential kinds supervene on internal neural states in the way that would make the replay device a genuine counterexample; and the probability that disjunctivism can adequately explain why hallucinations seem identical to perceptions without positing any common factor, plus whatever additional term you think the decomposition needs. Give a number and a one-line justification for each, state how the terms combine into your overall credence in D, and identify which term the final number is most sensitive to.

For calibration: my own credence in D is around 0.35, and I suspect the neural-duplication scenario is doing most of the work in pulling me below 0.5, though I can't tell whether that's insight or a supervenience intuition I've never examined. If your number differs substantially from mine, tell me which term in the decomposition the disagreement most plausibly lives in, and what argument or empirical finding would move your credence the most.
