ok here's my idiosyncratic set of gut verdicts on what makes something an 'a', and I want to know what principle (if any) unifies them. A lowercase 'a' in any font I've ever seen, double-story or single-story: definitely an 'a'. Uppercase 'A': same letter, sure, even though it shares basically no visual features with lowercase. A kid's wobbly handwritten 'a' where the circle doesn't close: still an 'a'. A Greek lowercase alpha, which looks nearly identical to a single-story 'a': NOT an 'a', feels obvious. A normal 'a' rotated 90 degrees: not an 'a' anymore, even though every stroke is intact. An 'a' rendered in dots or dashed lines or made of tiny other letters: still an 'a'. But here's where I confuse myself -- a cursive 'a' that I can only read as 'a' from context counts, and yet I hesitate to say an abstract logo shape that everyone in the office reads as 'a' (in the company name) counts, even though the context clue is doing the same work. Also a 'd' with the ascender broken off so it looks exactly like 'a': I want to say it's a broken 'd', not an 'a', which seems to contradict my kid-handwriting verdict. Is there one account of letterform identity that gets all of these -- shape, role in a writing system, authorial intent, recognizability -- or do I have to give one up? And tell me what the resulting account says about CAPTCHAs: is a distorted 'a' that only a human can read still the same 'a'?
