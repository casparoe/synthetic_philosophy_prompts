I'm a grad student TAing an undergrad seminar on money in politics (mostly poli sci majors, a few philosophy stragglers), about 16-18 students, one 75-minute slot. Here's the problem I want to break. Most of them come in with the cartoon picture: lobbyists hand politicians envelopes of cash, votes get bought. Then they skim some political science, see that studies keep finding roughly zero effect of contributions on roll-call votes, and swing too far the other way -- money doesn't matter, case closed. I want an in-class game or simulation that breaks both pictures at once and lands them on the subtler third view: money mostly doesn't buy votes, it buys *access*, it shapes which issues even make it onto the agenda, and it works by *helping* rather than pressuring -- lobbyists function like free extra staff for already-sympathetic legislators, donating research, bill-drafting, and whip counts, but only on the issues they care about.

So: design the activity such that nobody is allowed to offer anything in exchange for a vote. No bribes, no threats, nothing conditional, and the facilitator enforces that hard. Yet just from the structure of time, staff capacity, and who shows up offering free help, the legislator's agenda at the end should be visibly tilted toward the interests with money. Ideally the students only notice the tilt during the debrief, when someone lays the enacted agenda next to what the district actually wanted, and they're a bit disturbed that nothing illegal or even rude happened at any point.

Give me:

- Setup and roles. I'm imagining one legislator with tiny staff capacity, several lobbyists with distinct portfolios, and some constituents who show up with genuine needs but no resources to offer -- but if a different structure produces the effect better, go with that instead.
- The actual rules, including whatever tokens track time/attention/drafting labor, and exactly how the no-quid-pro-quo rule is worded so it blocks vote-buying without blocking the mechanism I want.
- A minute-by-minute run of show for the 75 minutes.
- The debrief: which specific moments map onto access vs. agenda-setting vs. the free-staff mechanism, plus the discussion questions that get them to see why "just ban bribery" or "just disclose donations" wouldn't have changed anything they just did.
- A second round where we flip one rule (public financing? a bigger legislative staff? a constituent group that gets real access?) so they can feel which reform actually bites and which is cosmetic. Tell me which rule change youd pick and why.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to recover mid-game.
- An honest note on which feature of the real thing the game structurally can't reproduce -- my guess is the long-game relational stuff (careers, the revolving door, lobbyists becoming genuine friends whose advice you trust), but push back if you think that's capturable or if there's a bigger gap.

No lecture beforehand and no jargon up front -- they shouldn't hear "agenda-setting" or "legislative subsidy" or anything like it until the debrief. And keep materials cheap: index cards and tokens, no app required.
