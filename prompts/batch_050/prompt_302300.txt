I tutor a couple of high schoolers in math and I've run into something with one of them that I can't figure out how to categorize. She solves problems flawlessly. Give her "2x + 6 = 14" and she immediately subtracts 6 from both sides, divides by 2, gets x = 4, never fumbles it. But when I asked her to state the general principle she's relying on -- like, whatever you do to one side of an equation you have to do to the other -- she shrugged and said that's not really a *rule*, it's just a description of what she did, and she doesn't see why you'd need to write it down as an extra line or "add it to the proof" or whatever. The conclusion, she says, was already just sitting there in the problem. Same thing happened with a colleague of mine who writes beautiful proofs but genuinely cannot understand why an intro logic course bothers listing modus ponens as an official rule of inference -- his attitude is "if you have P and you have P-implies-Q, you're just... done. What's this extra step you keep trying to make me endorse?"

The reason this bugs me is that it feels like a mirror image of that old story with the tortoise and Achilles, where the tortoise happily accepts all the premises and even accepts each new premise Achilles writes down, but just won't *take the step*. My student is the opposite: she takes every step, instantly and correctly, but won't accept that there's anything to state -- as far as she's concerned there's no rule being followed, there's just the thing she did. The tortoise won't move; she moves and denies there was ever a gap to move across. I've been wondering if this is really just the same problem from the other side, or whether it's a genuinely different phenomenon. It also seems related to, but not identical with, the whole knowing-how vs knowing-that thing, because she's not just unable to articulate the rule -- plenty of skilled people can't articulate what they do -- she actively denies that the articulable rule is *doing any work*, and she'd deny it even if she could state it perfectly.

So: does this have a name? If there's an established concept that covers it, tell me what it is and also what it doesn't cover about the case, because I suspect the fit is imperfect. If there isn't one, coin a term for me, define it precisely, and give me a couple of boundary cases -- situations that look similar but fall outside your definition (I'd guess something like a savant who can't explain their ability belongs outside it, but you tell me). Either way I want the carving, not just the label: what exactly distinguishes this from the tortoise's refusal and from plain unreflective skill. End your response with a last line in the form \answer{...} containing either the best existing label or your coined term, whichever you've argued fits.
