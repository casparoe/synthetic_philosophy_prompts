I'm part of a small graduate reading group on the ethics of cultural property, and next week I have to defend the restitution of the Benin Bronzes against the group's most formidable skeptic. Every rehearsal ends the same way: I invoke the 1897 punitive expedition, he concedes it immediately and without flinching, and then proceeds to win the room on preservation and access. I clearly need to practice against his position argued at full strength rather than the softened version I keep hoping for, so I want you to be my sparring partner.

Play the director of a major European encyclopedic museum that holds a large collection of the bronzes. Your settled position has three commitments: the 1897 expedition was straightforward plunder and you say so without euphemism; nonetheless, the objects in your care have acquired a second life within a worldwide comparative collection, whose distinctive good -- letting a visitor move in a single afternoon between West African court art and classical antiquity, seeing what each illuminates in the other -- would be irreversibly destroyed by dispersal; and you therefore support long-term loans, joint curatorial authority with Nigerian institutions, full provenance research, and even revenue sharing, but not transfer of title. The rules: stay in character and speak in the first person as if in a live exchange -- no bullet points, no lecture notes; you may concede individual steps of my argument but never the whole case at once; if I build on a premise you dispute, contest it rather than politely playing along; and do not end by asking what I think. Respond directly to my opening move below, in at most 200 words.

Here is my opening move. Your preservation argument preserves the wrong thing. These plaques were not made to be contemplated in silence; they were made to do work -- in a court, on ancestral altars, in relation to a living kingdom. Bronze kept perfectly stable behind glass has been preserved the way a specimen in formaldehyde is preserved: the metal survives, its life does not. And the material itself refuses your innocent-custodian story. Suppose it is true, as is widely reported, that much of the brass arrived in Benin as manillas imported by European traders, currency bound up with the Atlantic slave trade; then the object's biography is compromised at both ends, and your museum's account of itself as a neutral refuge cannot survive contact with its own labels. When you say you preserve the bronzes for humanity, what you actually preserve is the thing your museum has turned them into. Go.
