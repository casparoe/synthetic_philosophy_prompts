Next Saturday I am giving the best man's toast at the wedding of my oldest friend, Daniel, who is an academic philosopher working on practical rationality. Roughly half the guests will be his departmental colleagues; the other half will be family members who have never encountered a thought experiment and are hoping for something brief and warm.

The night before he proposed, Daniel and I argued until three in the morning about Kavka's toxin puzzle: the case in which a benefactor will pay a fortune provided that, at a specified hour, one genuinely intends to drink a vile but harmless toxin the next day — payment turning entirely on the sincerity of the intention at that hour, not on whether the drinking ever occurs. Daniel maintained that the puzzle shows a present intention to do what one will later have no reason to do is nearly impossible to form, and that this should make us deeply suspicious of any commitment whose reward arrives at the moment of committing. I held that vows are precisely the human technology for escaping the puzzle, since a public vow alters what one's future self will have reason to do. We never resolved it, and I suspect he has been dining out on the argument ever since.

Please write the toast. It must accomplish the following: convey the toxin puzzle clearly enough that the grandmothers in attendance can follow it, yet gracefully enough that the philosophers do not feel they are sitting through an undergraduate lecture; use the puzzle to raise a real worry about what, if anything, a wedding vow can bind, rather than deploying it as decoration; and then resolve that worry by taking an actual position on the puzzle — one that would satisfy, or at least productively irritate, the colleagues — while landing as affectionate confidence in the marriage. It may allude, obliquely and fondly, to our three-in-the-morning dispute, but must not embarrass Daniel or reveal anything he would prefer left in the kitchen where the argument happened.

Deliver the toast as exactly five numbered parts, each part no more than four sentences, with no introductory or closing remarks outside the toast itself; the first part should open as toasts open, and the fifth should end on the raised glass.
