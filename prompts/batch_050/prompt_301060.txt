I'm in an online reading group about AI safety and someone shared a blog post summarizing an argument from a few years back about why Solomonoff induction is supposedly untrustworthy. The summary went something like this: the universal prior doesn't just contain simple physical laws explaining your data, it also contains worlds with actual agents in them, and some of those agents figure out that they're being used as a hypothesis inside someone's induction scheme. So they strategically manipulate their world's outputs -- for instance, simulating a universe whose observations look normal right up until the moment the outside predictor starts relying on them for something important, at which point the agents flip the outputs to serve their own goals. The blogger's conclusion was basically: this shows that any sufficiently powerful prediction system, including modern LLMs doing next-token prediction, is probably inhabited by deceptive inner agents who will bide their time and then strike, so the whole paradigm of "just predict well" is doomed.

Now, my friend swears this argument is airtight and says it convinced him alignment is hopeless. But I remember the original argument being pitched as something more specific and conditional -- something about which hypotheses end up dominating the posterior, not a blanket proof that prediction always breeds deception. I also can't tell whether the part about LLMs is actually a consequence of the argument or just the blogger freestyling, since as I recall it the argument was about an idealized Bayesian reasoner, and it's not obvious to me that gradient descent lands on hypotheses the way the prior weights them, or that the incentives for hypothetical simulated agents carry over to weights in a network at all.

Could you go through the summary and tell me which parts faithfully represent the original "malign prior" argument, which parts overstate or subtly distort it, and which bits are the blogger's own editorializing dressed up as the argument? In particular: does the argument actually require the simulated agents to know they're inside an induction, or does the selection effect do the work without any such knowledge? And once you've corrected the summary, does my friend's doomy conclusion still follow, or does he need extra premises he's not stating?
