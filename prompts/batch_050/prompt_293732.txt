I host a philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher defending the view that some virtual characters — particularly autonomous, persistent NPCs in modern video games — are legitimate objects of moral concern, such that at least some in-game actions toward them can be genuinely wrong, not merely distasteful or symptomatic of a bad character in the player. She does not claim these characters are conscious; her argument instead grounds their moral status in their functional and behavioral profiles and in the structure of the interactions they sustain. She is sharp, patient, and skilled at redirecting questions toward the player's psychology whenever the metaphysics gets uncomfortable.

Draft ten interview questions, ordered so the conversation deepens rather than restarts. The questions must engage the actual content of her view rather than invite her to restate it. I want the sequence to cover the following pressure points, though you may order them as you see fit:

1. The asymmetry problem: most players who flinch at certain in-game acts (say, simulated torture of a begging NPC) feel nothing about routine virtual killing. If her view condemns only a narrow slice of in-game violence, ask what principled line separates the two — and prepare a follow-up for when she appeals to the meaningfulness of the interaction rather than the act.
2. The consciousness question: if nothing it is like to be the NPC, in what sense is the character harmed rather than merely damaged, the way a chess engine is damaged by deleting its files? Anticipate her reply that moral patiency does not require phenomenal experience, and write the follow-up that forces her to say what property does the grounding work.
3. The novel-and-film comparison: no one thinks an author wrongs her fictional characters. Ask what interactivity adds, morally speaking, and have a follow-up ready for the reply that players are agents inside the fiction rather than authors of it.
4. The scale problem: millions of virtual creatures are killed hourly worldwide. Does the view commit her to an ongoing moral catastrophe, and if she bites that bullet, ask why nobody — including her — treats it as one.
5. The player-virtue deflection: the strongest competing explanation says wrongness resides in what the player cultivates in himself. Ask what her view explains that the virtue account does not.
6. Deletion and identity: is wiping a save file killing? Is respawning resurrection?
7. Her relationship to the AI-moral-status debate: does extending standing to NPCs cheapen the case for standing for future artificial minds, or build it?

For each question, give the answer she is most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to it. End the sequence with a question about what could count as evidence against the view, since she tends to treat unfalsifiability as a feature.
