so there's a move i see constantly on LW and in adjacent spaces where someone defends bayesianism by saying your credences are basically like odds on a betting slip: if your degrees of belief don't satisfy the probability axioms, a clever bookie can construct a set of bets you'll accept that loses you money no matter how the world turns out. the betting picture ends up carrying a lot of weight -- it gets used to argue not just "be coherent" but stuff like probabilism is a theorem of rationality, conditionalization is mandatory, and treating beliefs any other way is a category error. i want an audit of the analogy itself, not another rehash of the proof. give me the explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the bettor, the bookie, the stake, the payout, the assumption that you'll buy and sell at the same odds. then the places it breaks: i can just refuse to bet, dollars aren't utils and the standard fix for that has its own problems, there's no actual adversarial bookie inside my head, a sure loss in a forced one-shot game is not obviously the same kind of bad as believing p and not-p. then sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually bite for each conclusion being drawn versus the ones that are just pedantry. finally: does "you must be a bayesian" survive once the disanalogies are priced in, or does it deflate to something weaker like "incoherence is exploitable in principle and that should at least worry you"? if there's a better analogy than the betting slip for what the dutch book argument genuinely shows, propose it and say what it does better. lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning -- no preamble, no restating the question.
