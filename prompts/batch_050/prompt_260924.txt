My roommate is a causal decision theory diehard and tried to corner me with this at like 1am. Some background I grant him for the sake of argument: if the universe is big enough (spatially, or via a multiverse, whatever), then somewhere in it there will be lots of agents whose psychology and circumstances closely resemble mine, so whatever I end up choosing is some evidence about what they choose too. Call that evidential linkage. His argument:

(1) In Newcomb's problem you should one-box, and the only decent justification is that your choice -- without causing anything about the box -- is evidence about what's in it.

(2) So rational choice sometimes licenses acting on purely evidential, non-causal links between your action and distant facts.

(3) If the universe is large enough, there are enormous numbers of agents evidentially linked to me in this way: near-copies deliberating about similar problems right now.

(4) So my choice is evidence about an enormous number of other choices, which multiplies the stakes of everything I do astronomically. Skipping some small kindness here "means" thousands of near-copies skipping theirs too.

(5) So expected-value reasoning says I should treat trivial everyday decisions as matters of huge importance, and never defect in any cooperation-like situation, even when I'm dead certain no one will ever find out or retaliate.

(6) But that's magical thinking. Sitting alone in my room I cannot influence what strangers light years away do, and any decision theory that says otherwise has gone off the rails.

(7) Since (6) is obviously true, either the universe conveniently isn't that large -- an empirical bet it seems insane to hang ethics on -- or (1) is false and we should two-box after all.

I'd like to keep one-boxing and deny the magic, so I very much want him to be wrong, but I can't find the joint. Is there actually a slip somewhere between (3) and (5), or do I have to bite a bullet -- and if so, which bullet is cheapest? Tell me which step you'd reject, why it looked fine at first, and what rejecting it costs. Don't just reassure me that the conclusion feels too strong, I already know that.
