I work in systems security -- remote attestation, secure boot, that whole world of "how does one machine convince another machine to trust it." I keep running into a wall that smells a lot like something the logic people worry about, and I want to figure out if its actually the same wall.

The wall on my side: every trust chain has to bottom out somewhere. The bootloader verifies the signature on the OS, the OS verifies the signature on the app, but whatever verifies the first verifier can't itself be checked by the same machinery. You end up with a hardware root of trust you just take on faith, or on the manufacturer's word plus some side-channel arguments. And if a component vouches for itself using only its own attestation mechanism, that vouching is worthless -- an attestation of my own integrity, generated by me, only means something if you already trusted me.

Now the logic version, as I understand it (correct me if I'm mangling this): there's a theorem of Löb to the effect that a sufficiently strong consistent formal system can't prove, as a general schema about itself, "if my system proves φ, then φ." And this is supposed to cause real trouble for a hypothetical AI agent that wants to build a successor agent it fully trusts: it can't prove its successor is sound using only its own reasoning, so every generation of self-modification has to weaken its standard of trust somehow. The so-called Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents.

Here's what I want. Restate the Löbian obstacle in my vocabulary -- attestation chains, roots of trust, trusted computing bases, signed firmware, the works -- as honestly as you can. Then give me a residue report: which parts of the logicians' problem map cleanly onto things I already have names for, which parts you had to fudge, and which parts have no counterpart at all in systems security (fair warning, my world has adversaries and physics in it, and pure proof theory doesn't).

Then commit: is the Löbian obstacle genuinely the same wall I keep hitting -- is my root-of-trust problem just one instance of it? Or is one of the two fields mistaking a practical engineering constraint for a theorem, or a theorem for an engineering constraint? Pick a side and actually defend it. Please don't give me a tasteful both-sides summary where everyone is partially right.
