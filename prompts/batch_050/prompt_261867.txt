I run a small ethics reading group at my local library, mostly retirees and a couple of curious teenagers, none of them with any philosophy background. Next month we're doing lying vs. misleading, and I want to plan how to actually teach the distinction rather than just lecture at them.

Here's the case I want to build the whole session around, which I made up so they can't have opinions imported from elsewhere: Mara runs a pharmacy. A customer asks whether the store brand painkiller is the same as the name brand. It is, chemically identical, Mara knows this. But Mara also knows the customer is choosing for his elderly mother who has a weird sensitivity to one of the inactive fillers, and the store brand has that filler while the name brand doesn't. Mara says: "Yes, it's the same drug." Technically true. The customer buys the store brand and the mother has a bad reaction. Meanwhile Mara's coworker, asked the same question by a different customer, just flat says "no, they're different" because the store makes more margin on the name brand.

I want them to walk out genuinely grasping (a) that both employees wronged their customers but possibly in morally different ways, and (b) WHY anyone would think lying is worse than misleading, since to most normal people the whole distinction smells like a loophole for weasels. Honestly I'm half on the weasel side myself but I want to give the other view its best shot.

Plan the session for me. Specifically:

- What do they need to already have on the table before the lying/misleading question even makes sense as a question? I'm guessing something about what we owe each other in conversation at all, but you tell me, and tell me in what order to build it.
- Is my pharmacy case actually a good carrying example for this? What does a carrying case for this distinction need to have and need to NOT have? I worry mine loads the dice because the lie is motivated by greed and the misleading by... well, arguably also negligence. Should the motives be symmetric? Should one customer come out fine? Tweak it or tell me why it's fine as is.
- The misconceptions and pushbacks, in the order they'll actually arrive from this crowd. The retirees WILL say "she knew exactly what he meant, so she lied, end of story." One of the teenagers WILL say "so if I answer a question with a technically true statement I can never lie." Where in the sequence do I head each of these off, and which ones should I let breathe for a bit before addressing?
- How do I check at the end that they've gotten the distinction and the moral question, rather than just learned to apply the labels? Give me a question or mini-case I could throw at them cold where the right answer requires actually using the idea, not pattern-matching on my pharmacy story.
- What should I deliberately leave out at this level? e.g. implicature, Kant on the murderer at the door, the whole debate about whether lying requires saying something false -- tell me which of these would derail a lay audience and which is secretly load-bearing and has to be smuggled in without the jargon.

Two hours, one evening, coffee and cookies. Go.
