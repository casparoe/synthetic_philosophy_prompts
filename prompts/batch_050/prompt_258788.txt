I am a doctoral student in political theory, and I am trying to write a chapter comparing quota regimes across countries. What keeps stalling me is that the three cases I know best seem to rest on quite different pictures of what a quota is for, and I cannot tell whether each practice quietly presupposes a particular theory of justice or whether any of several theories would license it. Let me describe the practices as I understand them. Norway requires that boards of publicly listed companies contain at least roughly two-fifths of each sex, and the requirement has teeth: companies that fail to comply can ultimately be dissolved. Yet the law targets only board membership, not ordinary hiring or executive promotion, and it singles out sex while saying nothing about class background, ethnicity, or disability. In the public debate around it, two justifications were mixed together: that women were owed these positions as a matter of equality, and that mixed boards would actually govern companies better. India runs a very different system: constitutionally sanctioned reservations for enumerated caste categories in public employment, university admissions, and even seats in legislatures, justified openly as a remedy for historical exclusion and as a guarantee that marginalized communities will be present in the institutions that govern them. The United States, until the recent curtailment of race-conscious admissions, occupied a third position: explicit quotas were impermissible, race could figure only as one factor among many in a holistic review, and the officially accepted rationale was not compensation to the admitted students but the educational benefit that a diverse student body confers on everyone, including the majority.

What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit theory behind each practice, with a sharp distinction between what it strictly presupposes and what it merely fits well with. For instance, the Norwegian arrangement seems to treat a board seat simultaneously as a role to be filled on competence and as a distributable good that may be allocated by group membership, and I cannot see whether those two descriptions are compatible or whether the practice is committed to a view on which merit was never doing the allocative work in the first place. I am also puzzled by the selectivity of the axes: does any defensible theory single out exactly the dimensions a given law happens to pick, or is the choice of sex in Norway and caste in India an admission that quotas track political salience rather than principle? And the American rationale seems strangest of all to me: if the point of admitting a student is the benefit she confers on her classmates, the practice appears to presuppose an aggregative, almost instrumental picture of the admitted student, which sits awkwardly with the compensatory framing that motivates affirmative action in the first place.

So my questions are these. Which theory of justice, if any, does each practice actually need in order to make sense, and which parts of each practice are philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories would leave them untouched? Where the parts of a single practice presuppose inconsistent things, please say so rather than forcing coherence on it. And finally, as a test of the reconstructions: what would have to change in each practice if we swapped its implicit theory for one of the others -- would Norway's two-fifths target, its confinement to boards, and its exemption of smaller firms still be defensible if the quota were recast as compensatory rather than instrumental, and would India's reservations remain intelligible if justified by the educational-benefit rationale rather than by historical remedy?
