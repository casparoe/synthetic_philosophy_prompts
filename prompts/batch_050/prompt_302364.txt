I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of biology, and my advisor has spent the semester running a seminar on the units of selection in an infuriating way: she won't state her own view, she just rules on cases. Next week I have to present a case of my own and field her questions, so I've been keeping a ledger of her verdicts in hopes of reconstructing the principle she's actually running. Her rulings so far: a driving sex chromosome in a rodent that biases gametogenesis so it reaches ninety percent of offspring while reducing the organism's fertility is, she said, a straightforward unit of selection at the gene level. A tumor cell lineage that evolves to ignore growth arrest and shrug off chemotherapy, even though the lineage dies with the patient, counts as a unit of selection in its own right; she explicitly said there's no requirement that a unit's descendants found new organisms. In the classic haystack scenario, where altruists sacrifice their own fitness and groups containing more altruists outproduce other groups and seed more empty haystacks, she insisted the groups were units of selection, not merely the altruism genes, and that redescribing it as gene selection misses where the causal work happens. On the honeybee worker's barbed sting, which kills the worker defending the hive, she said the adaptation sits at the colony level: the colony is the unit and the beneficiary, the worker something closer to an organ of it. And on a clade where broadly distributed species go extinct and speciate less often, so that broadly distributed species accumulate over time, but where a daughter species' range is set by the accidents of where it originates rather than inherited from its parent, she refused to call the species units of selection, saying that differential persistence with nothing passed down is mere sorting, not selection among units.

The case I want to present involves a reef coral population living through repeated marine heatwaves. Take a colony to be the host animal plus its community of algal symbionts and bacteria, and stipulate the following: larvae acquire their symbionts fresh from the water column each generation; colonies whose symbiont communities happen to be heat-tolerant survive bleaching events and produce the great majority of the next generation's larvae; host genotype explains a small share of the variance in survival while symbiont community composition explains most of it; a colony's larval offspring reassemble their communities from the environment, with essentially no correlation between a parent's community and its offspring's; and the host does possess traits for regulating the uptake, expulsion, and maintenance of its symbionts. My question is whether she will call the holobiont -- the colony as a whole -- a unit of selection.

I'd like your best prediction of her verdict, and I'd like the reasoning done openly: lay out the candidate principles that are consistent with all five rulings in the ledger (I can see at least two, and I suspect a third), state what each predicts about the coral case, and identify which earlier ruling is the closest analog and doing the most argumentative work. Where her track record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so plainly rather than forcing a prediction, and tell me what further case -- either one of hers sharpened or a new one -- would most efficiently discriminate among the surviving principles. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the prediction you settle on, and single out the specific point in your reconstruction of her view where you are least sure of yourself.
