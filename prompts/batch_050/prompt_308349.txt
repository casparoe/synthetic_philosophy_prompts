A friend of mine dismisses all longtermist philanthropy with one move: giving money to reduce some tiny probability of an astronomically bad outcome is just paying Pascal's mugger. The mugger says hand me your wallet and I'll give you a thousand times its value in utility tomorrow; the x-risk pitch says fund us and you get a minuscule chance of saving trillions of future lives. Same shape, same con, he's says.

Audit the analogy for me. What maps onto what (mugger/grantee, the promise/the expected value calculation, the wallet/the donor's budget), and where does the mapping break? One disanalogy I keep circling: the mugger gets to set the stakes after seeing your wallet, and the bigger his promise gets the less you should believe him -- his credibility is a function of the payoff he's offering. Nature, presumably, doesn't lower the probability of an asteroid strike just because the stakes happened to get big. Does that asymmetry actually do the work of saving longtermist EV reasoning, or can you construct a mugging that survives it (say, a mugger who seems genuinely honest and whose offer is big for reasons independent of you)? And if the analogy fails there, what's the *legitimate* mugging-shaped worry about this kind of philanthropy -- maybe something about how unverifiable huge numbers are trivially easy to cook up and impossible to audit? Tell me which conclusion, if any, the analogy can actually carry.
