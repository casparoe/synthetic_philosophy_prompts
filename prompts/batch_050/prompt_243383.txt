I am a PhD student in political theory working on the epistemic defense of democracy, and I am stress-testing the standard reading of the Condorcet jury theorem for a chapter. Please construct a thought experiment in which the theorem appears to apply — every voter is individually better than a coin flip at identifying the correct outcome, and every voter votes sincerely for what they take to be correct — yet enlarging the electorate reliably makes the majority verdict worse, not better.

Constraints: (1) the setting must be realistic — a hiring committee, a parole board, a tumor board, a criminal jury — not an abstract model of balls and urns; (2) every voter's individual accuracy must exceed 0.5, and to foreclose the easy solution, all voters must have exactly the same individual accuracy, so the failure cannot be attributed to dilution by weak voters; (3) no strategic voting, no laziness, no motivated reasoning — each voter forms an honest judgment and reports it; (4) the failure must be generated by a structural feature of the epistemic situation that you specify, not merely stipulated in the setup.

After presenting the scenario in a single paragraph, please do the following: identify precisely which assumption of the classical theorem your case violates, and walk through the reasoning by which majority accuracy declines (or stays depressed) as the group grows, including enough of the arithmetic that the direction of the effect is visible; state what happens to your case if that assumption is restored — does it collapse back into a standard jury-theorem setup, or does some residue of the problem survive?; and finally, articulate the strongest objection a defender of the theorem would raise — namely, that the scenario has smuggled the conclusion in by redefining what counts as an independent voter — and give your best reply to that objection. Close by saying in one or two sentences what, if anything, your case shows about using the theorem to justify majority rule in real institutions.
