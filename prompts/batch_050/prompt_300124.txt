Write a dialogue between two epistemologists — a contextualist about "knows" and a defender of pragmatic encroachment (subject-sensitive invariantism) — who are stranded together in an airport lounge and end up arguing over the following case, which you should introduce in a brief stage-setting paragraph before the dialogue begins:

Anaya glances at the departure board, sees her flight listed at Gate 14, and settles in to read; the stakes are trivial, since a wrong gate costs her nothing but a short walk. Her companion Bruno is on the same flight but will be immediately deported if he misses it — a visa technicality makes this his last permitted departure for a decade. Both see the same board from the same distance at the same time. An hour later, with the gate possibly changed and no chance to re-check (the boards have gone dark in a power cut), each is asked whether they knew, when they sat down, that the flight left from Gate 14. Have the contextualist and the encroacher each give a verdict for both Anaya and Bruno, and then press each other with the strongest available objections: the encroacher should push the contextualist on why Anaya's true low-stakes assertion seems retractible ("I didn't know — not for someone in Bruno's position") even though nothing about Anaya changed, and the contextualist should push the encroacher on the implausibility of Bruno's knowledge evaporating without any new evidence, purely because his visa situation worsened, and on whether knowledge that can be destroyed by a phone call from an immigration lawyer can still do the theoretical work knowledge is supposed to do (settling inquiry, licensing assertion, rationalizing action). Both characters should be equally sharp: each must answer the other's actual challenge at least once before raising a new one, neither may retreat to a caricatured version of their view, and each should concede at least one genuine cost of their own position rather than defending it at every point. The dialogue need not end in agreement, but it should end with each character stating, in one sentence, the specific further case or phenomenon that would persuade them to abandon their view. No more than 1,100 words total, stage-setting paragraph included.
