In a probability seminar I'm taking, the instructor justified putting a uniform prior over a coin's unknown bias by saying, as if it were obvious, that when you have no reason to favor one possibility over another, the possibilities are equally likely. Later in the same course we hit Bertrand's chord problem, where "no reason to favor" seems to generate contradictory answers depending on how you describe the random mechanism, and we also read Chapter IV of Keynes's A Treatise on Probability, where Keynes constructs cases in the same spirit -- for instance, he argues that knowing only that some physical magnitude lies within an interval leads you to one uniform distribution if you carve up the scale of that magnitude directly, but to a different, incompatible one if you carve up the scale of a simple function of it instead, even though your ignorance is, intuitively, the very same ignorance. So now I suspect the slogan I was given is doing several different jobs at once, and that the Bertrand-style and Keynes-style examples only bite some of them.

Could you disambiguate the slogan into the distinct precise theses it could be expressing? I'm imagining a menu that might include, for example: a trivial definitional reading (where "no reason to favor" is just defined by equal probability assignments, making the slogan empty); a logical symmetry or invariance reading (probabilities must be unchanged under permutations or transformations that leave the evidence unchanged); a substantive epistemic-norm reading (rationality *requires* equal credence under symmetric ignorance); and a methodological-convention reading (uniform distributions are a legitimate default or starting point without being rationally compulsory). For each reading, I want to know: is it trivial, is it false, or is it a substantive position someone could actually defend? In particular, which readings are genuinely refuted by the reparametrization problem that Bertrand's and Keynes's examples pose, and which survive it -- because it seems to me that the invariance reading might survive precisely by *denying* that the two descriptions express the same state of ignorance, whereas the epistemic-norm reading is what actually gets destroyed.

Tell me which single reading you think is the strongest version of the slogan, whether that reading is one my instructor's justification of the uniform prior actually needed or whether the classroom argument was trading on a stronger, indefensible reading, and give me your bottom-line verdict: should the slogan be retired outright, or retained in a restricted form (and if restricted, restricted to what)? I'd rather have a defended position I can argue with in office hours than an even-handed survey.
