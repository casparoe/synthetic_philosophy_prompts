I am helping organize a workshop on transitional justice, and two of the invited scholars have been arguing past each other for years in print and on panels. The first defends what she calls the conditional-amnesty view: when a society is emerging from atrocity and prosecution of every perpetrator is politically impossible or would reignite conflict, amnesty traded for full, individual, public disclosure of one's crimes — along the lines of South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission — can itself be a form of justice, and in some transitions the best available one. The second holds what he calls the prosecution floor: for atrocity crimes, criminal trial and punishment are a non-negotiable requirement of justice, such that an amnesty, however conditional, is at best a prudential compromise that must be honestly labeled as a sacrifice of justice, never as an instance of it; he points to Argentina's eventual prosecution of junta members as evidence that deferred justice is possible and to Rwanda's gacaca courts as evidence that mass participation in accountability is feasible even at enormous scale. Their exchanges have grown unproductive, so the organizers have asked them to attempt an adversarial collaboration instead, and I have volunteered to draft the document they would produce if they took the exercise seriously.

Draft that joint statement. It should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both scholars can sign without reservation — about the empirical facts of the South African, Argentinian, and Rwandan processes, about what victims are owed in the abstract, about the constraints new governments actually face, and about what each of the two views does and does not commit its holder to; (2) a statement of each remaining disagreement, phrased neutrally enough that each scholar would accept the wording of their own position and of their opponent's; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would genuinely move at least one side. I expect the cruxes to include at least: whether disclosure-plus-amnesty is best understood as a lesser punishment, as a non-punitive form of accountability, or as no accountability at all; what the empirical record supports about whether prosecutions deter future atrocities or destabilize fragile transitions; and whether a victim's claim is specifically to punishment of the perpetrator or to acknowledgment, truth, and repair, with punishment as one instrument among others. For each crux, specify a concrete case, comparison, or piece of evidence that both scholars agree bears on it, and record in advance what each predicts that evidence would show — so that the disagreement becomes testable in principle rather than merely rehearsed. Where a disagreement dissolves on inspection into a difference over words (for instance, over what may count as 'justice' or 'impunity'), say so explicitly, identify the verbal residue, and state what, if anything, remains once it is removed. Close with a short section both can sign describing what kind of case — historical or hypothetical — would persuade each of them that their own position had been shown to be wrong.
