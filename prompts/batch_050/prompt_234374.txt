Essay draft:
In incident response we say we triage, but really we aggregate: 500 users failing checkout beats 5 users seeing a weird tooltip. I used to think the ethical version was obvious: if you can save five strangers or one stranger, save the five. Then I read about Taurek's challenge to "the numbers count" and got less sure. His point, as I understand it, is not that five deaths are fine, but that each person only gets to lose their own one life; there is no sixth person who collects the extra four lives like reward points. So adding lives may be a bookkeeping trick we perform after the moral fact has already been fixed.

Systems make this tempting because dashboards genuinely do sum. Error budgets, blast radius, customer minutes lost: these are real aggregates. But a load balancer can treat ten requests as one metric because requests are interchangeable. People are not requests. My proposal is a moral load balancer with three modes. Utilitarian mode: route rescue to the bigger group. Lottery mode: when claims are equal and indivisible, give each person an equal chance, even if that risks the many. Snapshot mode, my maybe-naff invention: before acting, write the incident postmortem from the perspective of the loser; if the reason only sounds good because you assumed you'd be in the bigger group, reject it.

The worry: snapshot mode is just publicity dressed up, and lottery mode feels monstrous when numbers get huge. If a meteor will hit either London or a village, equal-chance-per-person seems like a category error, not courage. So maybe numbers count only when harms are the same kind and the difference is vast? But "vast" is a slider, and sliders are where principles go to become vibes. My tentative ending is that engineers should stop asking whether to count heads and start asking what we are allowed to treat as fungible. That defintely sounds profound, but I can't tell if it's true or just well-formatted.
