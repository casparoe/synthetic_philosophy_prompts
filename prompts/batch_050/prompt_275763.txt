A colleague of mine in municipal government is overseeing the procurement of a recidivism-risk scoring tool, and she keeps reaching for an analogy I want audited before it hardens into policy. The orchestras, she says, solved their discrimination problem by putting applicants behind a screen: once the committee could no longer see who was playing, the playing alone decided, and the composition of orchestras changed accordingly. Her proposal is to do the same with the algorithm — strip the protected attributes from the training data, require the vendor to remove obvious stand-ins for them, and then the score will reflect risk alone, the way the screened audition reflects musicianship alone. On this basis she wants to strike the fairness-audit line item from the contract entirely: if the model never sees the attribute, what would there be left to audit?

Please treat the analogy as an argument and audit it accordingly. First, give the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the screen, of the committee's ears, of the music, of the candidate, of the decision the committee reaches at the end of the round, and of the attribute that must not influence the outcome. Then set out where the mapping breaks, sorting the disanalogies by whether they matter for her conclusion, since some presumably do not. At minimum, please address the following. (a) The difference between blocking a signal at decision time in a live performance and deleting a variable from a dataset whose remaining variables may carry the same information redundantly — I gather the orchestras themselves had to lay carpet because footsteps carried through the screen, which suggests the screen was leakier than the story admits. (b) The fact that the audition committee evaluates the very property it cares about, performed on the spot, whereas the score predicts a stand-in for the property the city cares about, recorded years later under conditions the city itself helped create. (c) Whether blindness can even in principle deliver what she wants, given the impossibility results in this literature: as I understand them, when base rates differ across groups, no score can be simultaneously calibrated within each group and equalized in its error rates across groups, except in degenerate cases — and this looks like a conflict among properties of the output that no amount of input-side screening could resolve. (d) The fact that the orchestras could count who advanced past the screen and so verify that the screen worked, whereas her proposal would discard the very data needed to run that check on the model.

Finally: does some version of her conclusion survive the audit — perhaps the weaker claim that blinding is good hygiene even though it is no substitute for auditing, or that it blocks one identifiable failure mode while leaving others untouched? And if the orchestra analogy cannot bear the weight she is putting on it, is there a better analogy in common use for what fairness-conscious procurement actually requires? I am not after a bare verdict on her policy; I want to know exactly how much weight the analogy can carry, and at which joint it gives way.
