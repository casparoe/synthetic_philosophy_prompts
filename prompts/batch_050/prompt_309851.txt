My partner went through her citizenship ceremony last month (oath of allegiance, certificates, the lot) and I've been chewing on something since. In plenty of countries, immigrants have to formally swear loyalty to their new home, sometimes renouncing old allegiences, while people born there are never asked to affirm anything in their entire lives. And then separately, some places have schoolkids reciting a daily pledge of allegiance, which would seem faintly creepy if a workplace tried it on adults. Meanwhile citizenship tests quiz newcomers on history that half the native-born population would flunk.

What I want to know is what all this presupposes about what patriotism actually is and what it's for. Give me exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no introduction and no conclusion. Roughly, I want you to cover: what view of allegiance or political obligation the oath-for-immigrants-only setup presupposes (consent? gratitude? something murkier?), whether the school pledge fits that same view or secretly a different one, where the package as a whole is coherent versus where its parts presuppose contradictory things, which rival takes on patriotism (virtue, vice, morally neutral taste, and so on) the practices quietly rule out versus merely sit comfortably with, and what citizenship would have to look like if you swapped the presupposed view for a rival. If you reckon the question itself rests on a confused premise — say, that these practices are just historical hangovers and don't presuppose anything philosophically interesting — then tell me that straight rather than politely playing along. Oh, and write it in British English please.
