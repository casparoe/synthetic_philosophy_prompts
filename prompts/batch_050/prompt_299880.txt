I've been thinking about Fitch's paradox of knowability -- the proof that if every truth is knowable, then every truth is actually known, derived from the fact that "p and nobody knows p" can't be consistently known. The responses I already know, so don't bother with these:

(1) Tennant-style restriction: limit the knowability principle to "Cartesian" truths, i.e. propositions that remain true when known, so conjunctive truths like the Fitch conjunction get excluded by stipulation.

(2) Edgington's situation-relative fix: what's knowable is knowledge-of-actual-facts in other possible situations, and "p is true and unknown" is perfectly knowable in some *other* situation where p is false, so the paradoxical premise never gets off the ground.

(3) Bullet-biting: only conjunctions of a truth with the claim that it's unknown are unknowable, these are a weird and tiny class, and the anti-realist can happily concede them while keeping the spirit of knowability.

(4) The intuitionist/verificationist response that the argument isn't a reductio at all -- on their semantics every truth really is knowable (even known, eventually), and Fitch just confirms what they already thought.

(5) The idealization objection: "knowable" quantifies over worlds with arbitrarily idealized knowers, and failures at that idealized limit tell us nothing about whether actual truth outruns actual knowledge.

Can you give me a response to Fitch that is genuinely different from all of these -- not a rewording, special case, or combination? And then tell me honestly whether you think it actually works or whether it secretly collapses into one of the five.
