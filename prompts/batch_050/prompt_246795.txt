I'm the token "community member" on the ethics commitee of a mid-sized fertility clinic (my actual day job is backend engineering, I have zero philosophy training, so please keep the jargon to a minimum). We've got a case the committee has to rule on and I'm apparently the swing vote, lucky me, so I need to draft the decision memo and I want your help thinking it through.

The case: a couple wants to start IVF now. The wrinkle is that the woman just started a medication she needs for about the next 8 months (an autoimmune thing, not life-threatening, but her doctor wants her on it), and the drug has a well-documented risk -- call it 30 to 40 percent -- of causing a specific congenital condition: significant hearing loss plus some skeletal abnormalities. Serious but manageable. Think hearing aids or cochlear implants, a couple of surgeries during childhood, normal lifespan, normal cognition. If they wait until she's off the drug plus a 3-month washout, the risk drops back to the general-population baseline. Waiting isn't free though: because of her age their per-cycle success odds drop a bit, and they'd likely need an extra cycle paid out of pocket, which they can afford but it's real money.

Here's the part that's been breaking my brain. Nobody -- not the couple, not our consulting genetic counselor, not the doctor on the committee -- thinks a child born with this condition would have a life not worth living. Everyone agrees that kid, asked at age 20, would almost certainly be glad to exist. And there's no possible world where *this* kid, the one conceived next month, exists without the condition, because a kid conceived 11 months from now is genetically a different person entirely. So the couple's argument is basically: name the victim. You can't. Any child we have now will be glad we didn't wait.

The physician on the committee says this reasoning proves way too much -- by that logic the clinic could never be faulted for anything so long as the resulting kid is happy to be alive, and our whole "foreseeable harm" policy becomes dead letter. She also keeps pointing out that the low-risk option is *right there* and costs the couple maybe a year and some money. The couple counters that "a year and some money" is easy to say when it's not your last good shot at a kid.

Our written policy says the clinic may decline services that create "foreseeable, avoidable harm to a future child." It was drafted with obvious stuff in mind and nobody at the time thought about the no-victim angle.

So: draft the memo. It needs to (1) actually come down on one side -- proceed, decline, or proceed with conditions -- and not just survey the options, (2) give reasoning in plain language that the lawyers and doctors on the committee could poke holes in, (3) take the strongest version of the losing side seriously and explain why it doesn't win, and (4) recommend whether the policy wording should be amended going forward, since this exact loophole is going to come up again.

American English please.
