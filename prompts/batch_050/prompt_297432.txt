For a graduate seminar I teach on the foundations of rational agency and artificial intelligence, I want my students to study what a disciplined adversarial collaboration actually produces, rather than another round of position papers. I would like you to draft the joint statement that two of my (fictional but philosophically serious) colleagues could both sign after a year of structured disagreement about how an artificial agent ought to be oriented.

The first colleague defends goal-direction: a well-functioning agent is one that pursues outcomes, and the central design problem is specifying the right objective -- some function over states of the world -- and ensuring the agent optimizes it competently. The second defends approval-direction: an agent should be oriented not toward outcomes but toward what an overseer would endorse, choosing on each occasion the action the overseer would most approve of, where the overseer may be an idealized construction (say, what a panel of careful human judges, assisted by the agent's own earlier outputs and given unlimited time to deliberate, would converge on). The second colleague holds that this construction avoids the need to write down any objective at all; the first replies that maximizing expected approval is itself just another objective, and a worse one.

Draft the joint statement, structured as follows. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: claims both colleagues will sign about what each position minimally commits one to, about the failure modes of naive versions of each (including what happens when a goal-directed agent's objective is slightly misspecified, and what happens when an approval-directed agent can influence what the overseer observes or believes), and about which common objections each side concedes are legitimate rather than caricatures. Second, a table of residual disagreements, each stated in one or two sentences of neutral wording that the side whose view it describes would accept as a fair formulation of its own position. Third, a cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one colleague. I expect candidates to include whether approval-direction collapses into goal-direction once the overseer is idealized; whether a finite overseer's fallibility makes approval-direction exploitably manipulable in ways goal-direction is not; whether the overseer's dependence on the agent's own outputs creates a bootstrap problem; and whether corrigibility -- willingness to be corrected, redirected, or shut down -- comes more naturally to one architecture than the other. You may add cruxes or reject one of mine, but justify the choice. For each crux, specify a concrete thought experiment, formal consideration, or observable behavior of deployed systems that both colleagues agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about that case and what result would count against its view. Fourth, a short closing section identifying which part of the overall dispute, if any, both colleagues now regard as verbal -- a disagreement about the extension of the word goal rather than about agents -- and why.

The statement should read as a document two people with real reputations at stake would sign: no strawmen in either direction, no agreed claims so weak that both sides sign them without cost, and no crux that is merely the original disagreement restated. Aim for the length and register of a working paper's framing section.
