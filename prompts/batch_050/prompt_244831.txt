I'm a retired physicist, spent my career on instrumentation and signal processing, and only got around to reading philosophy in the last few years, so forgive me if this is naive. Lately I've been reading about autonomous weapons and this idea of "meaningful human control" -- the claim that a human has to be genuinely in the loop, understanding and able to override, for the use of lethal force to be legitimate, not just nominally pressing a button.

The case I want analyzed is the USS Vincennes shooting down Iran Air Flight 655 in 1988. As I understand the basics: a US cruiser with the Aegis combat system, in the middle of a tense engagement in the Gulf, misidentified a climbing civilian airliner as a descending Iranian F-14 and killed everyone on board. The Aegis system apparently tracked it correctly as climbing; the human operators read it as descending. No full autonomy involved, just a human-machine team that failed badly.

Here's what I want: run the meaningful-human-control framework over this case properly. Who actually had control at each stage -- the crew, the Aegis software, the captain -- and in what sense? Where exactly did the "loop" break: was it automation bias, bad interface design, time pressure, training, or something else, and does the framework even have the vocabulary to distinguish those? Then the other direction: what does the case show about the framework itself? It seems to me the awkward part is that Vincennes had humans firmly "in the loop" and it didn't help -- arguably the humans made it worse by overriding what the machine got right. Does that mean meaningful human control is the wrong demand (maybe the problem is control at all, not who has it), or just that the framework needs to say more about what makes control meaningful rather than nominal? And does the case transfer to today's debates about fully autonomous systems, or is the disanalogy -- no one claims Aegis was autonomous -- fatal to using it that way?

Keep it under 800 words. I'd rather have a tight analysis than a survey, and don't pad it with a bibliography.
