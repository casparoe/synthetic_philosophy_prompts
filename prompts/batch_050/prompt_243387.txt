I'm taking a political philosophy seminar this term, and we've just finished the unit on rights and welfarism, culminating in Sen's result about the impossibility of a Paretian liberal. Our professor has a habit of refusing to state her own view outright; instead she gives verdicts on cases and lets us reverse-engineer the principle. A few of us have started a running game of predicting what she'll say about new examples before she says it. Her consistent framing is this: a liberal society must sometimes protect an individual's choice even when everyone's preferences -- possibly including the chooser's own, once you account for what they'd prefer others to do -- would be better satisfied by interference. The question is always which choices get that protection. Here are the verdicts she's given so far. Two people, one of whom is prudish and one of whom is prurient, disagree about who should read a scandalous novel: she said each person's decision whether to read it is protected, full stop. A diarist's refusal to make their private correspondence public: protected, even though a curious public would be gratified. A person's decision to pray alone at home: protected, even if most citizens find the practice distasteful and would sincerely prefer it cease. Whether to smoke inside one's own detached house: protected. Whether to smoke in one's apartment when the smoke seeps into shared hallways and neighbors' flats: not protected, society may regulate. Wearing a T-shirt with a slogan many passersby find genuinely offensive: protected, on the grounds that being offended by a stranger's self-presentation is not the kind of stake that defeats the protection. And a shopkeeper's pricing decisions, where there is no monopoly or scarcity but customers broadly resent the prices: not protected, on the grounds that market transactions sit in the public domain.

Next week she's promised us a harder case, and I want to make my prediction before she reveals the answer. During an epidemic, a resident declines vaccination. Their refusal is grounded in a considered conviction about bodily integrity, which they describe as the very core of their private sphere. The refusal modestly but genuinely elevates transmission risk to others in the community -- not mere offense or disapproval, but a probabilistic physical harm -- and a large majority prefers that vaccination be compelled. Assume compulsion would take the form of a fine rather than physical force. My request is threefold. First, reconstruct at least two distinct rules that each account for all seven of her prior verdicts -- I can see that something like a "protect the conventionally recognized private sphere" rule fits most of them, but so does a "protect self-regarding choices, where offense to others doesn't count as an effect" rule, and I suspect there are more. Second, tell me which rule she is most plausibly running, what it predicts about the vaccination case, and give me your best guess at her actual verdict with reasons -- noting that the candidate rules I can construct seem to diverge precisely on this case. Third, if the track record genuinely underdetermines the answer, describe the single additional verdict -- on a case of your choosing -- that would most efficiently distinguish the competing rules, and explain why it discriminates between them.
