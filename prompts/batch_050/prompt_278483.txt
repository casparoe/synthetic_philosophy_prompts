My philosophy reading group has two people who've been going at it all semester about the reasons for love, and I've decided to force them into an adversarial collaboration. One defends the appraisal or quality view: love is a response to the beloved's valuable qualities, so there really are reasons for love, and loving the wrong sort of person can be a rational mistake (Velleman-flavored, roughly). The other defends a bestowal view in the spirit of Frankfurt: love isn't grounded in reasons at all, we don't love people because they're valuable, if anything they become valuable to us because we love them, and demanding reasons for love already misunderstands what love is.

Write the joint statement they could both actually sign. Specifically I want:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These shouldn't be vacuous -- I want real common ground, e.g. stuff both sides accept about how love differs from liking someone or judging them to be a good person, about the phenomenology (neither side thinks lovers walk around consulting a checklist of their partner's merits), about the fact that "trading up" for a slightly better partner strikes everyone as awful and any adequate theory must explain or explain away that reaction. Flag any apparent agreement that turns out to be just both sides using the same words for different things.

2. A disagreements table with exactly these columns: # | Crux (the subquestion such that settling it would move at least one side) | Quality-view position, phrased so its defender would sign it | Bestowal-view position, phrased so its defender would sign it | A case or argument both sides accept as bearing on this crux | What the quality view says/predicts about that case | What the bestowal view says/predicts about that case

Give me at least 4 cruxes. Some candidates you can use, replace, or extend: the trading-up / replaceability objection (if love tracks qualities, why not switch to someone who instantiates them better?); the duplicate case (an exact qualitative copy of your partner appears -- do you have the very same reason to love the copy, and does it matter either way?); whether grief makes sense as mourning this person rather than mourning a bundle of qualities that could recur; whether it ever makes sense to call someone's love irrational; and the Kolodny-style thought that relationships themselves generate reasons independent of the beloved's qualities, which both camps have to react to somehow.

3. After the joint statement (clearly marked as not part of the signed document), a short note saying how confident you are that each side's wording is one its real-world representatives would actually accept -- including where you had to guess a bit at what Frankfurt-type or Velleman-type thinkers would say -- and name the single point in the whole document you're least sure about.

Keep the statement's framing strictly neutral; the whole point of the excercise is that neither camp should be able to complain the thing was rigged in the other's favor.
