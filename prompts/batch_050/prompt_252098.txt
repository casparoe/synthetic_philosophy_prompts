I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on normative ethics, about 14 students, and I have one 75-minute session to devote to moral uncertainty. I want an activity that makes the students run into the problem of inter-theoretic value comparison themselves, rather than hearing me lecture about it. Most of them will arrive assuming that being uncertain between moral theories is like being uncertain between weather forecasts: you just weight each theory's verdict by your credence and average. I want them to discover, in the course of actually having to decide things, exactly where that assumption breaks down.

Design a structured game, provisionally titled "The Allocators," in which students sit as the board of a small charitable foundation and must distribute a fixed budget across candidate projects over several rounds. The catch: before each round, each student draws (or the table is assigned) a credence profile distributing confidence across a handful of stipulated moral outlooks — for instance, some blend of a total-utilitarian view, a constraint-based deontological view, and a view on which only certain kinds of goods count at all — and they must justify their allocation under their own credences, not under the theory they personally hold.

The design should escalate deliberately. The early rounds should be ones where the averaging strategy works smoothly, so that the students become committed to it; a later round should introduce a theory in the mix whose verdicts don't naturally come in units comparable to the others' (it forbids some options and permits the rest, full stop), so that the averaging procedure stalls in a way the students have to feel, not just be told about. A final round should engineer the fanaticism problem: a small assigned credence in a theory that treats the stakes as astronomically large, so that the students watch their own procedure drag them toward an allocation they find absurd, and have to decide whether to follow it or abandon it on the spot.

Please give me: the full rules and materials (including how the credence profiles and project cards should be constructed, with enough texture that I could write the cards myself without needing further decisions from you); a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes; instructions for how much the facilitator should and shouldn't explain up front; and a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the game onto the theoretical distinctions — the difference between uncertainty about facts and uncertainty about values, why the merely ordinal or constraint-issuing theory jams the procedure, and what it would take to patch the procedure at each failure point and what each patch costs.

Also tell me, from your knowledge of how such activities actually run, what will predictably go wrong: where students will game the rules, where a confident student will short-circuit the stall that the design depends on, and how to handle a student who simply refuses to take an assigned credence profile seriously. Finally, be honest about which feature of real moral uncertainty the game cannot reproduce — the obvious candidate being that assigned credences aren't believed credences, but say if you think the deeper gap is something else — and what the facilitator has to supply in the debrief to compensate.
