So I'm giving a twenty-minute paper at our department's work-in-progress seminar in a fortnight on Parfit's hitchhiker case — the one where you're stranded in the desert, the famously selfish driver will only bother rescuing you if he's confident you'll pay him once you get to town, and once you're safely there you seemingly have no reason to pay. My paper defends a slightly unfashionable line: I argue that Parfit's talk of it being *rational to make yourself irrational* is a muddle, and that the real lesson is that rationality attaches to deliberative policies rather than individual acts, so the person who pays in town isn't being irrational at all — they're just executing a policy it was rational to adopt, full stop. I got here after arguing with a friend who does game theory, who kept insisting that if the driver can't observe your mental state at the moment of the rescue then no story about dispositions does any work, and that the case only has bite if you assume some kind of transparency about character. I concede something like that in the paper but claim it doesn't rescue the standard reading, because drivers are precisely the sort of people good at reading faces, wich is why the case feels realistic.

The audience is a mixed bag and that's what worries me. There are two proper formal decision theorists (one a committed causal decision theorist who thinks all these Newcomb-adjacent cases are pseudo-puzzles), a couple of Parfit scholars who know Reasons and Persons backwards and will pounce on any misreading, a philosopher of economics who'll want to translate everything into cheap talk and signalling equilibria, and the usual grad students. Prep me for question time: give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually arrive, phrased the way those specific people would phrase them (the causalist will be snarky, the Parfit scholars will be textual), and for each one tell me whether to concede, deflect, or stand my ground, with a sketch of what standing my ground would require. Also flag the weakest joint in my policy-vs-act move — the place where I should have a rehearsed answer rather than improvise — and tell me the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks, so I can at least decide in advance whether to bluff or fold if it comes.
