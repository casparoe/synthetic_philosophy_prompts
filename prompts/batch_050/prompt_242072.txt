I'm working on a short essay on s-risks and I want pushback before I write the thing properly. Below are my notes — basically the argument in skeleton form. Quick background so we're on the same page: by s-risk I mean risks of astronomical suffering, futures where the sheer amount of suffering is enormous by any scale, not just a bad century or two. The essay is aimed at people who already take longtermism seriously but treat s-risk work as a niche concern for negative utilitarians.

The argument, as it stands:

1. Standard longtermist prioritization runs on expected value: extinction risk dominates because the future might contain a huge amount of value and extinction forfeits all of it. S-risk reduction gets folded in as one consideration among many, no special status.

2. My claim: even a pure expected-value maximizer, with a perfectly symmetric axiology where happiness counts exactly as much as suffering, should still overweight s-risks relative to what the naive EV calculation suggests. Not because suffering matters more, but because of structural asymmetries between creating value and preventing disvalue:

(a) Fragility of the good. Getting a good future requires many things to go right at once — aligned values, coordination, stable institutions, whatever. Preventing a catastrophically bad future only requires blocking fewer pathways. So on the margin, effort aimed at the worst outcomes buys more certainty per unit of effort.

(b) Compromise stability. Actors with wildly different values — utilitarians, deontologists, religious traditions, egoists, whatever coalition ends up governing the future — can converge on avoiding astronomical suffering the way opposed states converge on arms control. Building a specific good future requires agreeing on what the good is, which nobody manages. So s-risk reduction is far more robust to moral and political disagreement, and that matters a lot if you think the future gets shaped by bargaining between agents who don't share your values.

(c) Irreversibility asymmetry. Missing out on value is bad, but the option to create value stays open — the future can always get good later. Some s-risks lock in: a stable totalitarian system optimized purely for control, value lock-in of an indifferent superintelligence, spreading ecosystems full of intense suffering to other planets. Once locked, they persist for astronomically long stretches.

3. The objections I take seriously. First: this whole argument might secretly be negative utilitarianism wearing a trenchcoat. If preventing disvalue systematically beats creating value on tractability, robustness, AND irreversibility grounds, maybe the structure of the world just vindicates the negative utilitarians, and I should either admit that or find where the asymmetric axiology got smuggled in. Second: the extinction tension. If some s-risks are worse than extinction, then taken to the limit my view prefers extinction over certain futures, which most people (me included) find hard to swallow, and which sets up nasty game theory if powerful actors disagree about which futures count as worse-than-death.

What I want from you:

- Comments on accuracy first. Am I describing the standard longtermist position and the s-risk side fairly, or am I fighting a strawman somewhere? No need for citations, just flag it if I'm mischaracterizing something.
- Which of (a), (b), (c) is doing the most work, and which is weakest? I suspect (a) might be empirically backwards in some domains — sometimes preventing a bad outcome means blocking one pathway out of ten thousand, which is harder than "block fewer pathways" makes it sound.
- Does (c) actually survive the obvious reply that extinction is the most irreversible event there is? I've gone back and forth on this and can't settle it.
- Is the compromise-stability point (b) as strong as I think, or does it cut both ways? Specifically: isn't the most robust compromise position of all "let's just not think about the far future at all"?
- Finally, be honest about whether the essay is interesting or whether I'm just repackaging three well-known considerations with a new label on the jar.

Be blunt. I'd rather scrap the whole thing than publish a warmed-over version of something everyone already beleives.
