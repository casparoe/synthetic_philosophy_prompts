Hi so for my media studies class I'm doing a project on infographics and I ended up down a rabbit hole on Otto Neurath and his 1936 book International Picture Language: The First Rules of Isotype. Quick warning, I don't have the book in front of me so this is a paraphrase not an exact quote: in it he basically lays out rules for how a picture language should work. Stuff like -- if you want to show that one town has twice the population of another, you draw two little person-symbols next to one, not one person-symbol that's twice as tall. A bigger symbol isn't a bigger number, more symbols are. He also says signs should be as simple and standardized as possible, that details which don't carry information should be cut, and that the same kind of thing should always get the same kind of sign, so anyone anywhere can learn the system once and read every chart.

What I want is a decision tree or sorting quiz called something like "which philosophy of infographics do you actually believe in?" that sorts people based on how they'd react to Neurath's rules, without ever asking "do you agree with Neurath?" directly. Here's what I need:

- 6 to 8 questions, each one a concrete design choice, like: you're making a poster about falling vaccination rates for a bus shelter and you have room for either a plain repeated-syringe chart or a dramatic drawing of a crying kid, which do you pick? Or: to show oil production doubling, do you draw two barrels or one barrel twice the size? Answer options should be what you'd actually do, not labels.
- A scoring key that maps answer patterns onto at least these four end positions: Neurath's own Isotype position (standardized signs, quantity by repetition, cut everything that doesn't inform), Tufte-style data-ink minimalism (also anti-decoration but totally fine with bars and lines and no little pictograms), Nigel Holmes-style embellished charts (decoration and humor are features because they make people actually look and remember), and a cognitive-psychology position (whatever the perceptual research says is easiest to decode accurately, even if that breaks Neurath's rules or Tufte's).
- The key thing: the tree should be built so Neurath and Tufte come apart on the pictogram question (both hate decoration but for different reasons!), and Holmes and the psychology position come apart on what to do when decoration is memorable but inaccurate.
- If two of the four positions can't actually be separated by my questions, say so instead of faking it, and if there's an answer combination nobody sensible would hold, mention that too.

Keep it casual enough that I could actually hand it out to classmates. Oh and one of the questions should test whether people think a picture language can really be international/neutral like Neurath hoped, or whether signs always smuggle in culture -- that's the part I'm most unsure about tbh.
