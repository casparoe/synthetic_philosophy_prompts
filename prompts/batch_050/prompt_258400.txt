I am drafting the chapter of my dissertation that surveys responses to the nonidentity problem, and I want my taxonomy to be exhaustive before I defend my own view. The problem, for reference: in choices that determine both whether and which people come to exist — the woman who conceives now rather than waiting two months, knowing that a child conceived now will have a painful but livable condition, or a legislature that adopts a resource-depletion policy that leaves future generations worse off but changes who those future people are — the seemingly wrong act appears to make no one worse off, since the affected individuals would not have existed at all had the better choice been made.

The responses I have already catalogued are these:

1. Deny that anything wrong was done. Nonexistence is not a state anyone occupies, so a life worth living cannot be worse for its bearer than never existing; the intuition that the act was wrong rests on a confusion.

2. Impersonal revision. Abandon the person-affecting principle in favor of an impersonal one: outcomes can be better or worse simpliciter, and bringing about the less good outcome is wrong even though it wrongs no one in particular.

3. Non-comparative harm. Harm does not require making someone worse off than they otherwise would have been; being brought into existence with serious and avoidable suffering can itself constitute a harm, so the child is harmed despite not being made worse off.

4. De dicto person-affecting. Retain the requirement that a wrong act must be bad for someone, but read it de dicto: the act is bad for whoever occupies the relevant role, even if it is not bad for the particular individual who actually comes to occupy it.

5. Agent-focused diagnosis. The wrongness lies not in any effect on persons but in the character of the agent's reasons: the mother who conceives now acts on deficient reasons, and the legislators display culpable indifference toward the future, and this is where the wrongness resides rather than in any victim's complaint.

What I need is a sixth response that is not a variant, special case, or combination of these five. Ideally it should preserve both of the intuitions the problem puts under pressure — that these acts really are wrong, and that wrongness is somehow a matter of what befalls persons — rather than sacrificing one to save the other. If no such position is available, then give me the strongest remaining response of a genuinely different structure and explain which intuition it gives up and why that sacrifice is smaller than it looks. In either case, state the view precisely, show how it handles the two cases above, and assess honestly whether it stands on its own or collapses into one of the five already on my list.
