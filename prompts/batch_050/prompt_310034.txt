I'm a senior philosophy major taking a seminar called Free Speech in Two Legal Cultures, which spends the term setting the American First Amendment tradition against the European dignity-based approach to hate speech. My study group has a standing arrangement for exam prep: each of us drafts one practice final question, complete with a model answer and marking scheme, and we grade each other's attempts under timed conditions. I've been assigned the unit where the two traditions seem to converge and then, on inspection, sharply diverge — Mill's exception clause. Everyone in my group has read On Liberty, Chapter II, and come away thinking of Mill as a near-absolutist about expression, but in Chapter III he writes:

No one pretends that actions should be as free as opinions. On the contrary, even opinions lose their immunity, when the circumstances in which they are expressed are such as to constitute their expression a positive instigation to some mischievous act. An opinion that corn-dealers are starvers of the poor, or that private property is robbery, ought to be unmolested when simply circulated through the press, but may justly incur punishment when delivered orally to an excited mob assembled before the house of a corn-dealer, or when handed about among the same mob in the form of a placard.

Here is what I want the practice question to do. It should be unanswerable by reciting "Mill permits restricting incitement but not mere offense," because that's exactly the recitation everyone in my group has prepared. The crux I want it to force is this: does Mill's criterion of a positive instigation to some mischievous act map cleanly onto the American imminent-lawless-action standard (making Mill an honorary American, hostile to laws like Germany's Volksverhetzung statute or the ICCPR's Article 20, which punish advocacy of hatred and group degradation without requiring that the audience be moved to do anything), or can the passage be read to license European-style prohibitions? A genuinely strong answer would have to specify what the candidate "mischievous act" even is in a hate speech case — hatred is an attitude, not an act, so instigation to hatred may fail Mill's test by its own terms; confront the objection that punishing a speaker because a mob happens to be excited builds a heckler's veto into the doctrine; and grapple with whether vilifying speech might constitute an injury itself (say, by destroying its targets' standing as equals in public life) rather than merely instigate one downstream. I'd also like the question to include a hypothetical modeled on Mill's placard case but updated, so students can't just paraphrase the corn-dealers, and to invite students to notice that Mill's own examples of punishable-when-instigated speech are seditious political opinions, which raises the question of whether his criterion is content-neutral in the way American doctrine demands.

So, the deliverables: write the exam question itself (one essay question, calibrated for an upper-level seminar, answerable in 75 minutes from course materials); a model answer of about 600 words; and a marking scheme out of 20 marks built around five must-hit points, stating what each is worth and adding a one-line note on the specific way students typically fumble that point — for instance, treating Mill as an absolutist and ignoring the passage altogether, or sliding from "offense" to "harm" without argument. Please make sure the model answer actually takes a position on whether European-style bans survive Mill's test rather than surveying both sides and stopping.
