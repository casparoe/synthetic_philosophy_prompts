I am taking a university seminar on predictive processing and the free energy principle, and our class keeps treating active inference as if it were simply a more active-sounding version of ordinary Bayesian updating. I want an activity that makes the distinction bodily and intuitive: sometimes an agent reduces prediction error by revising its model, but sometimes it does so by intervening on the world so that the evidence better matches its prior expectations. The setting is a 75-minute undergraduate seminar with 24 students who can be divided into six groups. I do not want an introductory lecture explaining the theory. Instead, the rules and incentives should lead participants to enact both strategies before they are named. Ideally, the activity should also create a genuine tension between improving one’s model and maintaining control, so that students can see why a system that reliably makes the world predictable is not necessarily a system that learns what the world is independently like.

Please design a complete, runnable classroom game with materials, roles, hidden information, turn structure, and a payoff scheme that does not involve money. It should include some controllable feature of the environment and some feature that cannot be controlled, so groups must decide when prediction errors call for belief revision and when they call for intervention. I would especially like the game to incorporate something analogous to precision weighting: for example, different sources of sensory evidence might vary in reliability, but participants should initially have to infer rather than simply be told which source is trustworthy. The design should make at least one confirmation-seeking strategy locally rational without making it obviously foolish, and it should allow a group to fall into a simplified dark-room-style equilibrium in which minimizing surprise replaces exploration. Include a debrief that maps specific events in the game onto prediction, prediction error, precision, belief updating, action, and policy selection, and that asks participants whether their group was discovering the environment or sculpting it. Also describe what will predictably go wrong when the game is first run, how the facilitator should respond without prematurely revealing the theory, and which aspects of the full free energy principle the activity inevitably fails to capture.
