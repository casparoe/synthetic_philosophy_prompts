A friend of mine — bright, philosophically curious, but not acquainted with the academic literature on aggregation — has been indulging me over a series of pub evenings while I put pairwise choice cases to her. Each time I ask which of two outcomes she would bring about if forced to pick, and her answers have been consistent enough that I suspect she is running some implicit rule, though I cannot pin down which. Her verdicts so far: (1) between one stranger being tortured for an hour and one stranger getting a mild tension headache, she chooses the headache without hesitation; (2) she would divert a runaway tram to kill one person rather than let it continue and kill five, and says it is simply because five is more than one; (3) between one person being tortured for fifty years and a dust speck landing in the eye of each of 10^18 people — momentarily irritating, no lasting effects — she would inflict the dust specks, insisting that no number of specks adds up to that; (4) between one person breaking a leg and a million people each getting a paper cut, she would again inflict the paper cuts; yet (5) between one person losing £5,000 and ten thousand people each losing £1 (a total loss of £10,000), she would let the single person bear the £5,000, on the grounds that the larger total loss is worse and that £1 is nothing to anyone.

I now want to predict how she will answer three further cases before I put them to her. Case A: a billion people each break a wrist — six genuinely painful weeks in a cast, then full recovery — versus one person being tortured for a month. Case B: the ladder — would she trade one person's fifty years of torture for two people's forty-nine years, and that for four people's forty-eight years, and so on, step by small step, all the way down, and what does she make of the fact that accepting every step commits her to an endpoint she has already rejected? Case C: 10^10 people each suffer a moderately painful headache for one hour versus one person suffers that same kind of headache continuously for ten years.

Please do the following. First, set out the plausible rules that jointly fit verdicts (1)–(5) — I can see at least three myself (a simple total-aggregation view with some patch, a lexical severity-tier view, and a view on which small claims count only when sufficiently close in severity to the largest claim at stake), but I may be missing candidates, and I want the rules stated precisely enough to yield predictions rather than just redescribed verdicts. Second, for each rule, say what it predicts about cases A, B and C, noting where the rules diverge and where they awkwardly agree. Third, give your best guess as to her actual verdict on each case, with the reasons for preferring that guess. Fourth, describe the single further pairwise case whose outcome would best discriminate between the two rules you find most plausible. Finally, tell me how confident you are in your best guesses overall, and single out the one claim in your answer — a reconstruction, a prediction, or a rule's implication — that you are least sure of.
