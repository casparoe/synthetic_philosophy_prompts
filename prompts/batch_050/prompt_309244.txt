I'm preparing the midterm for an undergraduate course on strategic thinking and behavioral decision-making (students have seen basic game theory and the standard heuristics-and-biases catalogue). I need one exam question, answerable in 75 minutes and worth 20 marks, on escalation of commitment in auctions.

The question must not be answerable by reciting the standard dollar auction story. To force genuine analysis, I want the exam to present the following variant, which I made up, and ask students to reason about it from scratch: two bidders compete for a prize of $100. Bids must rise in whole-dollar increments. As in the classic setup, the highest bidder wins the prize but both bidders pay their final bids. The twist is this: after every bid, the auctioneer publicly announces a running "exit price" equal to half of the bidder's accumulated payments so far, and at any point a bidder may drop out, paying only that exit price instead of their full accumulated total (the other bidder then wins at their current bid, paying it in full).

The question should ask students to do three things: (1) explain whether, and when, entering this auction can be rational even though no individual bid in the middle of it need be irrational -- i.e., identify where the irrationality, if any, actually lives in the sequence of decisions; (2) state precisely how the exit-price modification changes the strategic situation compared with the classic version, including whether it eliminates escalation, merely softens it, or perversely worsens it, with a reasoned verdict; and (3) judge whether a bidder who keeps bidding in this variant is committing the sunk cost fallacy, being careful to distinguish falling for sunk costs from rationally responding to a trap one has already entered -- students must say what observable or structural feature of the situation, not the size of past losses, determines which description applies.

Please provide: the exam question as I would print it (worded for students, with the variant described cleanly); a model answer of roughly 500-600 words written at the level of a strong student; and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points totaling 20 marks, stating what each point is worth and giving, for each point, a one-line description of the most common way a mediocre answer fumbles it (for example, treating every escalation as ipso facto irrational, or assuming the exit rebate straightforwardly fixes the problem without checking what it does to incentives to enter).
