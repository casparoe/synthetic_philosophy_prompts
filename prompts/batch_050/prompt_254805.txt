I sit on the biosafety review board of an institute that builds synthetic minimal cells, protocells, and self-replicating chemical systems, and several times a month I have to classify a proposed construct as "living" or "not living" — the label determines which containment rules apply, whether the construct gets any welfare-style protections, and who has to sign off. Our current rulebook just says "use your best judgment," which produces inconsistent rulings. Please act as a philosopher of biology with practical laboratory experience and write a decision procedure my board can actually run on any candidate system in under an hour: which candidate criteria from the definitional literature (metabolism and self-maintenance, reproduction with heredity, capacity for Darwinian evolution, autopoiesis, information-based accounts) deserve a place in the procedure and in what order of application; how to handle the familiar trouble cases built into each criterion (viruses and prions that fail metabolism, dormant spores and lyophilized cells that only have dispositional capacities, mules and sterile constructs that cannot reproduce); and a principled way to resolve conflicts when a system satisfies some criteria but not others, rather than just counting yeses. Think carefully and begin by briefly listing the main definitional proposals and their known failure modes before turning them into steps. Then apply the finished procedure to three cases: a protocell that metabolizes and self-divides but has no heritable variation; a digital "organism" that mutates and evolves inside a simulation; and a designer virus engineered to carry a minimal synthetic metabolism. One of these should be a case where the procedure's verdict is one a board member would find counterintuitive. Finally, mark explicitly the points where the procedure bottoms out in a value judgment no checklist can eliminate, and say what the procedure cannot decide at all.
