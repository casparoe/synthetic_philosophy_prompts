After thirty years in experimental physics, I retired and promptly fell down the rabbit hole of metaphysics, and I now help run an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of the infinite. I am drafting this term's final examination and want one essay question on Thomson's lamp that students cannot answer by retelling the setup, calling it "paradoxical," and gesturing at infinity. Please write everything in American English.

The question should be answerable in a 90-minute sitting and should force students to take a position on where, exactly, the argument goes wrong (if it does). Specifically, it should push them to distinguish three diagnoses that are constantly run together: (i) that the switching schedule is outright incoherent, so no final state is possible; (ii) that the schedule is perfectly coherent but simply silent about the lamp's state at the two-minute mark, so the "contradiction" is an equivocation between "the description determines no final state" and "no final state is possible"; and (iii) that the real pressure comes from smuggling in an extra premise -- say, that the lamp's final state must be causally explicable by, or continuous with, its earlier history -- in which case the paradox is not a contradiction but a constraint on what physical realizations of the schedule would have to be like. The question should also require students to explain why Zeno's runner completing infinitely many ever-shorter strides seems benign while the lamp does not, and to state a principled criterion separating the benign supertask from the pathological one. As a former physicist, I want one limb of the question to ask whether empirical facts about switching mechanisms (finite switching speeds, material fatigue, and so on) bear at all on the metaphysical question of coherence, or whether they are simply beside the point -- students should have to argue for their answer, not assume it.

Along with the question itself, please supply: a model answer of roughly 600 words that takes a definite stand among the three diagnoses while doing justice to the strongest one it rejects; a marking scheme totaling 25 marks, built around five specific moves a top answer must make (state the mark value of each, and append one line on the characteristic way a mediocre candidate fumbles that move); and a short closing note on what separates an excellent answer from a merely competent one -- for instance, whether the candidate notices that "the description is incomplete" can be a dissolution of the paradox or merely a relabeling of it, depending on whether one thinks a full physical story must fix the final state.
