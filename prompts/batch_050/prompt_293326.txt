I teach an upper-level undergrad metaethics course and I'm writing the final exam. I want one essay question on evolutionary debunking arguments that students can answer in about 80 minutes. The catch: I want to anchor it in a passage they will NOT have seen in the readings, so they can't just regurgitate the standard "Street's Darwinian dilemma" summary they've memorized. Here's the passage, from Chapter IV of Darwin's The Descent of Man:

"It may be well first to premise that I do not wish to maintain that any strictly social animal, if its intellectual faculties were to become as active and as highly developed as in man, would acquire exactly the same moral sense as ours. In the same manner as various animals have some sense of beauty, though they admire widely different objects, so they might have a sense of right and wrong, though led by it to follow widely different lines of conduct. If, for instance, to take an extreme case, men were reared under precisely the same conditions as hive-bees, there can hardly be a doubt that our unmarried females would, like the worker-bees, think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers, and mothers would strive to kill their fertile daughters; and no one would think of interfering. Nevertheless, the bee, or any other social animal, would gain in our supposed case, as it appears to me, some feeling of right or wrong, or a conscience."

I want the question to force students to do something with the tension inside this passage: Darwin thinks the contents of conscience are contingent on the social conditions we were reared under (the bee case), yet he insists any social animal with our intellect would still end up with a conscience, and he clearly doesn't take himself to have shown morality is bunk. A good question should make them decide whether this passage is better read as the seed of a modern debunking argument or as a genealogical vindication of moral judgment -- and what extra premises you'd have to bolt on to get from what Darwin actually says to the skeptical conclusion that our moral beliefs are unjustified. I especially want them to have to explain what the bee example is even supposed to show: that our beliefs don't track moral truth, or just that different creatures would feel differently, and why the first claim doesn't follow from the second without more work.

Please write the question itself (one essay question, answerable in 80 min), then a model answer of roughly 600 words, then a marking scheme out of 20 marks built around five must-hit points, with a one-line note for each on how students typically screw that point up. The marking scheme should make clear what seperates an answer that has actually thought about the passage from one that just name-checks Street and Joyce.
