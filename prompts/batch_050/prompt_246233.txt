I am a novelist at work on a book about a woman who belongs to an online community I call the Correspondence: a private forum devoted to the conviction that a well-known novelist's fiction encodes a suppressed historical scandal, and that credentialed scholars conspire to keep this from the public. In plotting her eventual departure from the group, I have run up against a distinction I only half understand, and I would like your help thinking it through rather than a reading list.

As I have pieced it together, an epistemic bubble is a structure of omission: contrary voices simply never reach the person inside, so their worldview is impoverished but not necessarily fortified. An echo chamber, by contrast, is a structure of distrust: outsiders are not absent but discredited in advance, so that contrary testimony, when it does arrive, is dismissed precisely because of who it comes from. Please begin by telling me whether this way of drawing the line is sound.

The reason it matters to me is that I have built both mechanisms into the Correspondence without knowing which does the real work. The forum's recommendation software filters out mainstream scholarship so thoroughly that my protagonist sincerely believes the critics have fallen silent; yet new members also receive what the group calls an orientation briefing, rehearsing the standard objections and explaining why each objection, coming from an invested institution, only confirms the cover-up. I need to know which mechanism bears the explanatory weight in her captivity, because I suspect they imply different exits: a bubble might be popped by a chance exposure, whereas a chamber might yield only to something like repaired trust in a particular outsider, and a novel can plausibly dramatize one of these but not both at once.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

1. The Distinction, Stated Precisely (roughly 150 words): confirm or correct my formulation of the difference between the two structures.
2. Where the Mechanisms Pull Apart (roughly 200 words): the cases in which the two concepts make different predictions about how a person reasons, since these are the cases a novelist can dramatize.
3. Diagnosis of the Correspondence (roughly 150 words): which mechanism is doing the load-bearing work in the group as I have described it, and whether the two can genuinely coexist in one community or whether one tends to supplant the other.
4. Consequences for a Credible Exit (roughly 200 words): given your diagnosis, what kind of event or relationship could plausibly loosen the group's grip on my protagonist, and what kind of exit would ring false.
5. One Complication Worth Dramatizing (roughly 80 words): a single wrinkle in the distinction that you think would make for the most interesting scene.

I am not looking for citations or scholarly apparatus; I want the reasoning itself, stated carefully enough that I can build character psychology on it.
