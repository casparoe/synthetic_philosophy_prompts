You're an expert on Indian epistemology, especially Mīmāṃsā theories of testimony. Think carefully here — maybe brainstorm the possible weak points before committing to one.

A friend in my reading group put together this argument, which he claims is just the Mīmāṃsā doctrine of intrinsic validity (svataḥ prāmāṇya) spelled out, and it seems to prove way too much:

(1) A cognition's validity is intrinsic: it comes from the very conditions that produce the cognition itself, not from any later confirmation by another cognition.
(2) This applies to testimony just as much as to perception — a statement's validity doesn't wait on being cross-checked against what we see or infer.
(3) If validity did require confirmation by another cognition, that confirming cognition would itself need confirmation, and we're off on a regress where nothing ever gets validated.
(4) So every piece of testimony is valid simply in virtue of being heard and understood.
(5) But then no one could ever lie, misremember, or be a unreliable source — or at least we'd never be justified in treating any testimony as false.
(6) That's absurd; courts, historians, and ordinary people reject testimony all the time.
(7) So (1) is false, and validity must come from external verification after all.

Which step should I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I suspect the genuine Mīmāṃsā view has more moving parts than my friend lets on — something about how falsity gets discovered after the fact, and a puzzle: if all testimony were automatically valid, why did the Mīmāṃsakas bother insisting the Veda is authorless, as if having a human author would be a problem? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep the anti-regress intuition in (3) than the conclusion in (4), but I want to know what I'm paying. Also tell me whether (2) quietly smuggles in an assumption about who the speaker is.
