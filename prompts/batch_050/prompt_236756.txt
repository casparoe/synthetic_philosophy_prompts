I'm a PhD student working on well-being measurement in public policy, and I'm compiling a catalogue of objections to the idea that a government should try to climb the international happiness rankings (the ones based on average life-evaluation scores from large cross-country surveys, where respondents rate their lives on a 0–10 ladder). My list so far: (1) the ladder measures cognitive life evaluation, not experienced or affective happiness, and the two can come apart, so topping the rankings may not mean citizens feel good day to day; (2) scale use differs across cultures, so cross-country averages partly reflect reporting norms rather than differences in actual welfare; (3) the statistical models behind the rankings lean heavily on income, so chasing the ranking largely reduces to chasing GDP under another name; (4) evaluations are made relative to reference groups and adapt over time, so national gains may evaporate as everyone else's scores rise too. Is there a further objection I'm missing — one aimed specifically at the policy goal of maximizing a country's rank rather than at happiness measurement in general? It should be genuinely distinct from the four above, not a special case or combination of them, and I'd like a brief assessment of whether it survives the obvious replies.
