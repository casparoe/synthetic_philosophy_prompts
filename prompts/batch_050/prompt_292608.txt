Hey, so I'm a PhD student working on philosophy of mind/cog sci, and I'm prepping the handout for a reading group I'm running on Dennett's idea of "free-floating rationales" -- the claim that there are reasons in nature (why the cheetah's legs are shaped that way, why the cuckoo chick pushes eggs out of the nest) that no agent ever thought of or represented, and that evolution by natural selection can track these reasons without any comprehender in the loop. Competence without comprehension, basically.

The group is a mixed crowd -- some phil mind people, a couple of biologists, one person from an AI safety lab who keeps insisting this matters for how we attribute goals to trained models -- and I want a decision tree that sorts a reader into a position on whether there can be reasons without reasoners. Not a taxonomy of what Dennett said when, but of the live options.

Here's what I'm after:

- Start the tree from something everyone in the room accepts, like "natural selection genuinely explains the functional design of organisms, and this isn't just loose talk." Each branch node should be a question phrased so someone can answer it without knowing which camp it lands them in -- e.g. questions about whether a reason needs to be represented by anyone to exist, whether "tracking" a reason requires anything mind-like at all, whether selection itself counts as a minimal reasoner or just a sieve, whether a thermostat or a trained neural net "has" reasons or only instantiates them.
- Leaves I'd like to end up at (feel free to add or merge if the logic doesn't cooperate): the full Dennettian view (rationales float free, minds are late arrivals that started representing them), the view that natural selection itself is a proto-reasoner so reasons never really float free, the deflationary/instrumentalist view (reason-talk about organisms is just a useful stance, nothing in nature literally has reasons), the eliminativist (only representing minds have reasons, and talk of evolutionary "reasons" is a metaphor we should retire), and a quietist position that the whole dispute is verbal because "reason" is ambiguous between cause, function, and justification.
- Build it from the logical space first: if there's a combination of answers that no one occupies, leave an unoccupied leaf and say in a sentence whether it's incoherent or just unattractive/unexplored.
- Flag anywhere the tree can't cleanly separate two of the positions -- I suspect the instrumentalist and the quietist might collapse into each other, but tell me if you think otherwise.
- At the end, add three concrete test cases the group can run through the tree: a cuckoo chick's egg-ejection behavior, a thermostat, and a large language model that produces correct reasoning-like output. The point is to see whether the tree sorts the AI case the same way as the biological cases, since that's the argument I actually want to have.

Keep it to maybe 6-9 decision nodes so it fits on one page. And phrase the node questions carefully -- the biologists in the group will revolt if the tree begs the question by using "design" or "purpose" in the question text itself.
