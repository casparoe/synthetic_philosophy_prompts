I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about official apologies. Here's the pattern I keep running into, stated abstractly but accurately enough: a national government issues a formal apology for a historical injustice -- say, a policy of forced assimilation carried out a century ago -- and the ceremony is elaborately sincere. The head of government speaks of the state's shame; a day of remembrance is instituted. Meanwhile, the same government's lawyers are in court arguing that descendants of the victims have no claim to compensation: the wrongs were committed by officials long dead, under laws valid at the time, and current taxpayers cannot be held liable for them. Everyone involved treats this combination -- full apology, no liability -- as perfectly coherent, and no journalist I know has found it strange.

I want to understand what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes, and I'd like you to reconstruct its implicit theory rather than just list objections. Some questions that seem to me to be in the background, though you should feel free to reframe them:

Ordinary apologies are addressed by a wrongdoer to a victim. Here both are dead. Does the practice presuppose that a state is an agent that persists across generations and can be guilty in its own right -- and if so, why doesn't that same persistence ground liability for compensation? Or does the apology instead presuppose that the current officeholders are merely speaking for a continuing institution they did not create, in which case it's unclear who, exactly, is sorry? An apology also seems to be a speech act that can succeed or fail: if the victims cannot receive it, and the descendants refuse it, has anything been apologized for, or has the state merely performed a feeling? And note the asymmetry the government relies on: continuity of the state for purposes of taking responsibility in words, discontinuity of the state for purposes of avoiding responsibility in money. Is there a theory of institutional identity on which that asymmetry is principled rather than convenient?

Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with -- I suspect several theories of collective responsibility could license the apology itself, and I'd like to know where the practice is genuinely underdetermined. Then tell me what would have to change if we dropped the assumption of transgenerational state identity altogether: could anything recognizable as an official apology survive, or would the honest options reduce to either compensation or silence?
