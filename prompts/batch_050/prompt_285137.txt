I recently took over as managing editor of a quarterly magazine that publishes long-form narrative history for a general educated audience, and my first month has produced a crisis of identity. A prominent historian published an open letter about our latest cover story — a gripping, meticulously sourced account of the weeks preceding a famous revolution — arguing that the piece never explains why the revolution happened; it only recounts that it did. Half our editorial board agrees with the letter; the other half thinks demanding anything beyond a well-told story is a scientistic mistake. Meanwhile our contributors submit both kinds of piece: pure narratives, and social-science-inflected arguments built on general claims. We commission roughly a dozen features a year, and I need a standard my two staff editors — both history graduates, neither with any philosophy training — can apply during a first read of a manuscript, within a few hours, and can defend in a rejection or revision letter.

Please design that standard as an explicit decision procedure: the questions an editor asks, in order; what a manuscript must actually contain to pass each one; where the procedure terminates in a clear accept, a clear reject, or a revise-and-resubmit; and where it deliberately stops and hands the decision to editorial judgment because what remains is a question of taste or values rather than of explanatory adequacy. Ground the procedure in what philosophy of history has worked out about the covering-law model and about narrative as explanation, but translate it so that an editor who has never encountered that literature can run it. Wherever you import a philosophical distinction, state which parts of it the procedure preserves and which it discards, and why the discarded parts are safe to drop at our level of stakes. Also say plainly what the procedure cannot do — which disputes among submissions it will leave unresolved no matter how carefully it is followed.

Then run the procedure on three submissions we actually hold. First, our flagship contributor's account of the weeks before the revolution: vivid and impeccably sourced, containing almost no general claims, with causal language confined to what participants themselves said they intended. Second, a piece arguing that the peasant revolts of a particular century follow a pattern — revolt follows sharp taxation increases after poor harvests — supported by three cases, two of which fit the pattern and one of which the author explains away. Third, a biography-driven piece attributing a port city's long decline to the personality of one entrenched mayor. For each, show the procedure's verdict step by step, and include at least one case where it returns an answer I will not enjoy defending to the author — flag that one explicitly and tell me what to say in the letter.

Finally, be candid about the epistemic status of what you are building on. My current understanding is that the strict covering-law model is broadly regarded as having failed as a general account of historical explanation, but that whether narratives themselves explain — or merely assemble the materials for an explanation, or quietly rely on tacit generalizations — remains genuinely disputed. Correct me where that is wrong. At each step of the procedure, mark whether it rests on ground that specialists treat as settled or on a live position I would be committing the magazine to, and do not present either camp's view as the consensus.
