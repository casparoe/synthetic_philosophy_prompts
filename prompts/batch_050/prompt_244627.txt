I write novels for a living and somehow agreed to contribute an essay to a small anthology about "language in fiction," and now I have to actually write the thing. Below are my rough notes. What I want from you: exactly five numbered comments, each a sentence or two, no intro, no conclusion. Tell me where I've gone wrong or said something confused, what's missing that a reader would notice, and what I should cut.

Notes:

1. My starting observation: readers hold fictional characters to their promises. If a character in chapter two says "I swear I'll come back for you" and doesn't, readers feel a real betrayal, not just a plot event. Somehow the promise binds inside the story even though nobody actual promised anybody anything.

2. Speech acts in dialogue seem to work *within* the fiction exactly as they work in life — an apology between characters is felicitous or not depending on the same stuff (sincerity, standing, etc.), and part of writing good dialogue is getting this right, often by deliberately breaking the rules so the reader notices.

3. But speech acts aimed at the reader are dead on arrival. A character turning to the reader and saying "I forgive you" or "I challenge you to a duel" does nothing, and the weird flat feeling when a book tries it is evidence.

4. The narrator is the hard case. When a narrator opens with something like "everything I'm about to tell you is true," I can't decide if that's a real promise from the author, a fictional promise by a made-up narrator, or a third thing. My hunch: it works as a promise even when the book then lies, and unreliable narrators are basically people who made a promise and broke it.

5. Half-formed thought I'll probably drop: an insincere promise still counts as a promise in real life, and maybe that's *why* fiction runs on deception plots — the performative succeeds and only the morality fails. Not sure this earns its place though, it might just be trivail.
