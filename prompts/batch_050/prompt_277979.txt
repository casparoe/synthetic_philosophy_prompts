I’m a software engineer with no philosophy background, and I’ve been stuck in a surprisingly heated argument about whether you can assign a probability to your own future choice. Say I’m deciding between spending tomorrow fixing a flaky deploy pipeline or building a new feature. One friend says I can sensibly be 70% confident I’ll choose the pipeline, just as I can predict a coworker or a coin flip. Another says deliberation doesn’t work like that: while I’m genuinely choosing, my action is something I settle by weighing reasons, not an event I observe from outside. If I’m already 70% committed, maybe I’m not really deliberating; if I’m not, the 70% is just a guess about how my deliberation will end.

The weirdest case is when the prediction affects the choice. A forecasting system trained on my calendar, repo history, messages, etc. predicts “feature work.” If it tells me that before I decide, I might choose the pipeline out of annoyance. If the prediction stays sealed, apparently it can be tested normally. So does showing me the prediction create a self-reference problem, or merely change the input data?

We’ve gone in circles because both sides call the same thing “uncertainty.” My hypothesis is that this is partly an indexical confusion between my uncertainty about what I’ll do and my practical uncertainty about what to do. Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is—verbal, psychological, epistemic, or something about agency—and say what would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a formal fixed-point argument, an experiment, or proof that the original question is ill-posed.
