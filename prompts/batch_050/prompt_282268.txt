I help run a housing cooperative with about 120 members, and every two years we elect a three-person steering committee. We've never been happy with the voting methods we've tried, so this year I designed my own, and I'd like an honest assessment of whether it's sound -- and whether any method could be.

Here's the rule I came up with. Each member ranks all candidates from first to last. In the first round we award points the obvious way: a first-place ranking is worth the most, a second-place ranking slightly less, and so on. But before we total anything, we eliminate whichever candidate received the most last-place rankings -- my thinking was that a candidate many members actively dislike shouldn't win even if they have a devoted bloc of supporters -- and then we recompute the points on the shortened ballot, shifting everyone's remaining candidates up. We repeat this until three candidates remain, and they form the committee.

At last week's meeting, one member told me, quite cheerfully, that she was planning to rank the strongest challenger to her favorite candidate dead last, even though she actually thinks he's the second-best person running, because under my system a few strategic last-place rankings can knock out a front-runner early. I realized I had no good answer for her. Worse, I started wondering whether the problem is my specific design or something deeper. I can imagine tweaks -- different point values, eliminating two candidates at a time, only counting the top portion of each ranking -- but I can't shake the suspicion that for any deterministic rule of this general shape, someone, in some configuration of honest opinions, will do better for themselves by ranking insincerely than by ranking honestly. I have no training in this area, so that suspicion is just a gut feeling formed by playing with examples.

My questions: First, does my method in fact reward insincere ranking in the way my fellow member described, and can you show me concretely how, with a small worked example? Second, is there any fix -- either to my rule or to the overall approach -- that would let us choose three committee members in a way where every voter is always best off reporting their genuine ranking, or is my gut feeling correct that this is impossible in principle given more than two candidates? Third, if it is impossible in principle, does that actually matter at the scale of a 120-person cooperative, or is it a theoretical possibility that rarely bites in practice?

One thing I care about: please distinguish carefully between what is genuinely established here and what is disputed or uncertain among people who study this. If some result rules out honest-voting-proof systems, tell me exactly what its assumptions are -- for instance, whether it covers multi-winner elections like ours, whether it assumes voters can rank all candidates rather than just name favorites, whether randomization or weaker definitions of fairness would let a system escape -- rather than stating it more broadly than it holds. And if the practical significance of strategic voting is contested among experts, I'd rather hear that it's contested, with both sides sketched, than receive one confident verdict dressed up as the consensus view.
