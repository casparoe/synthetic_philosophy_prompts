So I work in climate policy, and the discount rate wars have been going on my whole career and seemingly for decades before that. On one side you've got the philosophers and the Ramsey/Stern types saying that discounting someone's wellbeing just because they live later is indefensible -- Ramsey basically called it an ethical failure and Parfit has the stuff about how a time isn't a morally relevant difference the way a place isn't. On the other side you've got most working economists saying of course we discount the future, people demonstrably prefer stuff now, capital has an opportunity cost, and if you set the pure rate to zero you get absurd implications for how much the present generation has to sacrifice.

My working theory is that this debate is mostly fake, in a specific way: when economists defend "discounting the future" they mean a bundle of things -- expected consumption growth, diminishing marginal utility, uncertainty about whether future people will even be around to enjoy the benefit, returns to capital -- and almost none of them are actually defending pure time preference, which is the only thing the philosophers are attacking. So the two sides are just sliding between different components of the same rate and never actually colliding.

Is that diagnosis right? Or is there a genuine residual disagreement underneath once you strip it down to pure time preference -- like a real clash over whether revealed preferences have any normative authority, or over whether impartiality across time is just a brute intuition you either have or you don't, in which case the debate can't be settled by anything?

I want the diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of positions. Please be concrete about which arguments each side is actually making and where (if anywhere) they genuinely touch. And tell me what could count as progress given your diagnosis -- a distinction everyone should adopt, a piece of evidence, an experiment in revealed preference, whatever -- or argue that nothing would help and say why. Also: if I've set this up wrong, e.g. mischaracterized what the economists or the philosophers actually claim, or my "it's all verbal" hypothesis rests on a confused premise, say so plainly rather than politely going along with the framing. I'd rather be corrected than agreed with.
