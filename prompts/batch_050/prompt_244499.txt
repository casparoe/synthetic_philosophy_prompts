I spent my career as an experimental condensed matter physicist, and since retiring I have been working my way through topics I never had time for, most recently the economics literature on decision-making under uncertainty. I came across the recent work on so-called ergodicity economics, associated with Ole Peters and colleagues, which argues that for multiplicative processes like repeated gambles, the time average growth rate of an individual's wealth can differ from the ensemble average across parallel outcomes, and that the time average is the quantity a rational agent should care about. I found this persuasive enough as far as it goes, but it has left me genuinely puzzled about my own field.

The puzzle is this. If taking an ensemble average is an error whenever the process is non-ergodic, as this work seems to claim, then it appears to me that the entire edifice of statistical mechanics is in trouble, since ensemble averages are its bread and butter. Should we conclude that Gibbs's method only ever worked by accident, and that physicists ought to replace ensemble averages with time averages wherever the two diverge, the way the ergodicity economists say economists should? I find it hard to believe that a mistake of this magnitude could have gone unnoticed in physics for a century and a half, which makes me suspect I am misunderstanding the scope of the critique, but I cannot see where my reasoning goes wrong. Perhaps the resolution has something to do with how quickly physical systems mix compared to economic ones, but I would like this spelled out properly rather than hand-waved.

So my question is: does the ergodicity critique of expected value imply that ensemble averaging in statistical mechanics rests on a mistake, and if not, what exactly distinguishes the two cases? Please answer in American English, and feel free to correct my premises where they need correcting.
