Take the Classic Maya "collapse" -- the Terminal Classic thing where the southern lowland cities (Tikal, Palenque, Copan, etc.) stopped erecting dated monuments, got abandoned, and the region's population cratered sometime around 750-900 CE -- and analyze it as a test case for the concept of collapse itself. Not the details of why it happened, though you can use them: the question is what "collapse" even picks out and whether this episode fits it.

Here's the tension I want worked through. On one side you've got the collapse-as-failure tradition: Tainter's diminishing-returns-on-complexity story, Diamond's ecocide framing, the general picture of a society that overbuilds, degrades its environment, and then falls apart when drought or warfare pushes it over the edge. The Maya case looks like Exhibit A -- monumental construction stops, dynasties end, cities empty out, population drops by something like 90% in the southern lowlands. On the other side you've got the resilience/transformation critique (the Questioning Collapse crowd, McAnany, Yoffee, etc.): the Maya didn't go anywhere. Millions of Maya people and dozens of Maya languages exist today, the northern lowlands carried on urban life for centuries after the south emptied, and calling it a "collapse" imports a value judgment that equates the end of a particular political order -- divine kingship, basically -- with the end of a society. By that light the Classic period didn't collapse, it was abandoned, and arguably good riddance.

So: who is right, and more interestingly, what does each side get right about the other side's blind spots? Does Tainter's model even apply -- was Classic Maya society "complex" in his sense, and did people actually walk away because the returns stopped being worth it, which would be a weird kind of confirmation of the theory? Does the transformation framing secretly concede that *something* collapsed (you can say the people persisted and the polities still failed), and if so is the whole debate just about which unit of analysis gets to count as "the society"? And does it matter for the verdict that the archaeological record mostly preserves the monumental stuff -- kings, stelae, temples -- so our picture of what "ended" might be badly skewed toward what elites cared about?

I want an actual verdict, not "it's complicated." Pick which framing handles the Maya case better, and then tell me what would change your mind: what archaeological finding, or what conceptual argument, would flip you to the other side, and where your reasoning here is most likely to be leaning on a contested reading of the evidence rather than the evidence itself.
