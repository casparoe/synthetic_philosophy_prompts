In public communication about genetics, and in a good deal of theoretical biology, DNA is described as a code, a language, a blueprint, or a program: the genome is said to be written in a four-letter alphabet, read by the cell, and to encode the instructions for building an organism. The analogy does real argumentative work. It licenses talk of 'reading' and 'editing' genomes, it frames gene therapy as the correction of typos, it supports the idea that sequencing a genome is a step towards understanding the organism, and it occasionally surfaces in debates about whether life is fundamentally information, with everything else as hardware. Audit the analogy properly. Begin with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the alphabet, the words, the syntax, the semantics, the reader, the author, and the text's meaning, and which of these roles has no occupant in the biological case. Then separate the weak version of the claim -- that there is a systematic, largely arbitrary mapping between codons and amino acids, which deserves the name 'code' as much as Morse does -- from the strong version, which attributes to the genome something like linguistic meaning or programmatic content. Which features of real languages and real codes does the strong version quietly import that DNA lacks: an encoder, a convention shared between sender and receiver, context-free reading, a distinction between message and medium? Consider the standard complications -- overlapping reading frames, alternative splicing, RNA editing, regulatory and epigenetic marks, the fact that the 'same' sequence does different work in different cells -- and say which of these are mere footnotes to the analogy and which undermine it at the level of the correspondence table. Then assess what the analogy smuggles in for its popular uses: does calling DNA a blueprint presuppose a direction of control from sequence to organism that developmental biology does not support, and does calling it a program smuggle in a programmer, or at least a teleology that has to be earned rather than assumed? Finally, say which conclusions survive the audit: whether 'the cell reads the genome' can be literalised without remainder, whether the 'editing' framing of gene editing is misleading in ways that matter, and whether 'life is information' says anything beyond the weak cipher claim. If a better analogy exists for some of these purposes, give it and say what it gets right that the code analogy gets wrong.
