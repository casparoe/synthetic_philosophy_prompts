So here's the situation. I'm a retired chemist (thirty-odd years in industrial polymers, if anyone cares) and about two years ago I fell head-first into philosophy, mostly through podcasts at first and now actual books. Lately I've been chewing on this debate about the "norm of assertion" — i.e. what you need to have in place before you're properly entitled to just flat-out SAY something, as opposed to hedging it with "I think" or "probably". Some people say you need knowledge, some say truth is enough, some say rational or justified belief is enough even if you're wrong, and I gather there's a camp that says there's no single norm at all and it depends on the stakes or the conversation or whatever.

Anyway, I run a little reading group at the library — mostly other retired scientists plus one very patient high school english teacher — and I want to make a quiz for them called "which norm of assertion do you actually hold?" Here's what I need:

Eight to ten questions. Each one should be a concrete little scenario, not a question about theory. So NOT "do you think you should only assert what you know?" — the whole point is to sort people by their verdicts on cases, the way you'd sort somebody's politics by asking about specific policies instead of just asking them their party. Two to four answer options per question, each option a gut-verdict like "fine to say it" / "she shouldn't have said that, even though it turned out true" etc.

The scenarios need to actually pull the positions apart, so the quiz needs at least these ingredients (you can dress them up however you like):

- a lottery-type case: someone asserts their ticket will lose based purely on the odds — justified but, as I understand it, not knowledge even if it wins... loses. whichever
- a Gettier-ish case: belief that's true and well-supported but true only by fluke
- a case where someone asserts something true on a pure hunch or gut feeling, no good reasons at all
- a case where someone asserts something false but entirely reasonable to believe (good evidence, just unlucky)
- one of those weird Moore-paradox ones, like "it's raining but I don't believe it is" — that one broke my brain a little when I first read it
- a case involving how people naturally RETRACT, like someone saying "ok, I take it back, I didn't really know that" when their evidence gets challenged — my understanding is the knowledge-norm people lean on this a lot
- a case where someone asserts something they themselves believe is false for decent pedagogical or professional reasons — like a teacher presenting the textbook line she privately disagrees with

Then a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the positions: the knowledge norm, the truth norm, a justified-or-rational-belief norm, a plain belief norm (just say what you believe, that's it), and a no-single-norm / it-depends-on-context option. The key should handle mixed patterns gracefully — if somebody answers like a knowledge-norm person on six questions and a justification person on two, tell them what that probably means rather than just calling it an error.

Two more things. First, flag any pair of positions your quiz genuinely can't separate — I'd rather know that upfront than have my friend Dave find the hole in it over coffee, he lives for that. Second, keep all jargon OUT of the questions themselves; the words "knowledge," "justification," "norm" etc. shouldn't appear, just people saying stuff in situations. My group are smart people but they glaze over fast.

Casual but precise tone, like a good pub-quiz. Thanks!
