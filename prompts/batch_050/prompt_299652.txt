Thomas Metzinger's self-model theory holds that what we call the self is the content of a phenomenal self-model: an internal model of the organism's own states that is "transparent" in the sense that the system cannot represent the model as a model, so its contents are experienced simply as the way things are. A subject has an experience of selfhood, on this view, insofar as a unified, transparent self-model is in place, including a model of the subject as an agent directed at objects. Higher-order theories of consciousness, by contrast, hold that a mental state becomes conscious in virtue of being represented by a suitable higher-order state, and proponents often treat the sense of self as arising from this higher-order monitoring of one's own states.

Construct a thought experiment in which these two theories deliver different verdicts about whether a subject has an experience of selfhood, while agreeing that the subject has conscious experience at all. Constraints: (1) the scenario must be physiologically and psychologically plausible — draw on documented phenomena such as the rubber hand illusion, out-of-body experiences, depersonalization, or asomatognosia, and do not appeal to speculative technology, brain lesions invented for the occasion, or science-fictional devices; (2) it should be describable in one or two paragraphs; (3) the divergence in verdicts must fall out of the theories' commitments as sketched above rather than being stipulated — a case that simply announces what each theory says has failed.

After presenting the scenario, do the following: (i) state precisely what the self-model theory predicts and why, identifying which functional property of the self-model — its transparency, its integration into a single global model, or its representation of the subject's directedness at objects — is carrying the weight; (ii) state what the higher-order theory predicts and why, saying which higher-order representations the subject plausibly has and what they represent; (iii) identify the single structural feature of the case on which the divergence turns, and say whether removing that feature would collapse the case into one where the two theories agree; (iv) give the strongest response available to a defender of each theory who wants to deny that the case establishes the claimed divergence — in particular, how each might redescribe the scenario so that their own theory predicts exactly what the subject experiences. Conclude with your assessment of which redescription is more plausible and what, if anything, would decide between them empirically.
