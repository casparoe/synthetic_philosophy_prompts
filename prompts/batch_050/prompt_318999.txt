I'm a PhD student in philosophy, writing a dissertation on the historiography of fringe claims, and the position I find myself defending -- in the dissertation and, increasingly, in departmental reading groups -- is that pseudohistory can be demarcated from legitimate history by its methods rather than its conclusions. On my view, what makes ancient-astronaut speculation, literalist readings of Atlantis, or Fomenko-style chronological revisionism pseudohistorical is not that their conclusions are false, outlandish, or held by amateurs, but that they exhibit a characteristic cluster of epistemic vices: insulation from counterevidence, arguments from ignorance treated as positive proof, hyperdiffusionism as a default explanation, asymmetric standards of evidence for and against the favored claim, and a tendency to treat anomalies in the mainstream record as vindications of the alternative. I hold that heterodox and even badly mistaken history -- amateur genealogy, romantic nationalist historiography, speculative revisionism -- can avoid these vices and thereby remain history, however wrong it turns out to be. At a recent workshop, a colleague whose judgment I respect pressed me hard on whether this view is doing real work or merely relabeling a list of things historians already dislike, and I realized I could not say with confidence what would actually falsify it.

So: what would count against the methodological-demarcation view? I want a map of its exposure, not a polemic. Some candidate defeaters I have been turning over, which you should feel free to use, refine, or replace: first, the possibility that a claim arrived at by paradigmatically vice-ridden methods gets vindicated -- the long-dismissed fringe intuitions about pre-Columbian contact look different after L'Anse aux Meadows, even if the confirmation came from conventional excavation rather than from the fringe itself; second, the possibility that episodes of respectable mainstream consensus display the full cluster of vices -- the decades-long resistance to pre-Clovis sites in American archaeology strikes me as the strongest candidate, and Piltdown as a case of vice within the citadel -- which would suggest my criteria track professional standing rather than epistemic practice; third, the Laudan-style worry that demarcation projects generally fail because no condition is both necessary and sufficient, so the cluster either catches respectable research or lets obvious pseudohistory slip through; fourth, the circularity risk that I have defined the vices by generalizing from the canonical targets, making the view unfalsifiable in the way my colleague suspected.

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether a committed methodological demarcationist would concede it as counting against the view or dismiss it -- and whether that dismissal would be principled or defensive. If the view can be qualified to survive the serious cases (say, by retreating to a family-resemblance account, or by conceding that demarcation is a matter of degree), tell me what that qualification costs and whether the surviving position still does the work I need it to do in the dissertation. Where a defeater turns on an empirical or historical premise you are not certain of, say so rather than papering over it. I would find most persuasive a concrete case where my own criteria deliver a verdict I cannot accept. Finally, indicate how confident you are in your overall assessment, and single out the one point in your answer where your confidence is lowest and why.
