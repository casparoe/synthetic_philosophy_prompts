I keep thinking about the setup in Donald Davidson’s Radical Interpretation: the field linguist has to move from patterns of assent, dissent, and “holding true” under observable conditions to a whole theory of meaning for a speaker’s language, with charity doing real methodological work because belief and meaning get fixed together rather than one after the other.

Could you write a satirical in-universe field manual, about 600-700 words, for newly hired interpreters at the imaginary Office of Radical Interpretation? Make it deadpan bureaucratic prose, with sections like “Standard Equipment,” “Do Not Confuse the Speaker with a Cipher Machine,” and “When to Blame the Environment.” The manual should treat radical interpretation as if it were a dangerous civil-service job: forms for registering a T-sentence, procedures for deciding whether someone sincerely believes something bizarre or your translation has gone wrong, penalties for assuming every native who says something false is either irrational or talking about something invisible, etc.

The jokes need to come from actually understanding Davidson: the evidence has to be non-question-begging, interpretation is holistic, charity isn’t just “be nice,” truth and meaning are bound together, and more than one theory may fit the same behavioral evidence. Ideally include one running joke about a rabbit-like animal that keeps making the trainees overconfident, and one moment where the manual briefly admits that massive error about the world is easier to attribute to the interpreter than to the interpreted. Don’t wink at the reader or explain the satire; the absurdity should come from applying Davidson’s method with total institutional seriousness. Also don’t let it drift into a generic parody of anthropology -- the philosophy of language has to be doing the comedic heavy lifting. Definately no citations or bibliography needed.
