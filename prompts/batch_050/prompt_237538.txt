I am a PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on Rorty's antirepresentationalism, and I keep running into the slogan he made famous -- that truth is what your peers will let you get away with saying. It gets quoted at me in two very different settings: by critics who treat it as proof that Rorty licensed saying anything you can socially pull off, and by sympathetic readers who treat it as a harmless redescription of ordinary justificatory practice. Please separate the distinct precise theses this one-liner could be expressing: at minimum, a sociological observation about how justification is actually conferred, a substantive identification of truth with idealized communal assent, a recommendation that we stop using 'true' as anything more than a compliment within our practices, and the crude claim that whatever you can get away with is true. For each reading, take a definite stand on whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false, and give your reasons rather than hedging. Then commit to an answer on two further points: which reading Rorty's broader case against the mind-as-mirror picture actually requires in order to go through, and which reading is operative when the slogan is deployed to end an argument about whether truth can outrun warranted assertibility. If you think the slogan is useless precisely because it trades on the ambiguity between these readings, say so outright instead of rescuing it.
