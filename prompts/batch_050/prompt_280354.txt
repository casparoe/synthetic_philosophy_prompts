Next month I'm presenting at a small interdisciplinary workshop on perception and design, defending a thesis about letterforms: that what makes a lowercase 'a' an 'a' is not any shape property or family of shape properties -- not closure, not the number of strokes, not resemblance to a prototype -- but rather its role within a complete stylistic system of twenty-six letters, such that the same abstract 'a' can be realized by radically different geometries so long as it plays the 'a'-role relative to the style's other letters. On my view, trying to define 'a'-ness by listing visual features is like trying to define "knight" by describing a carved piece of wood: the category is constituted functionally, by the system's internal contrasts, and the human ability to recognize a wildly stylized letter in a new typeface -- one we've never seen before -- is evidence that readers grasp the role, not a stored template. I'm aware this echoes some ideas circulating in cognitive science about fluid concepts, but I'm presenting it as my own argument and want it to stand on its own.

The audience is genuinely mixed, which is why I'm nervous. There will be several working type designers, people who spend their days making deliberate choices about terminals, counters, and stress, and who I suspect will resist any account that treats letterforms as mere occupants of abstract roles rather than crafted objects with histories. There will be two or three empirical psychologists of vision who work on object recognition and will want to know how my view differs from well-known recognition theories -- prototype theory, exemplar models, recognition-by-components -- and what it predicts that those don't. And there will be at least one philosopher who works on rule-following and family resemblance, who will be alert to whether I've actually said anything beyond "it's a cluster concept" in fancier dress. Nobody in the room will be hostile to the idea that categories are interesting; they'll be hostile to vagueness and to claims that can't be tested.

Prepare me for the Q&A. Give me roughly the eight objections I should expect, ordered by how much damage each would do to the talk, each phrased the way that particular audience member would actually phrase it -- a type designer does not talk like a vision scientist. For each, tell me what I should concede outright, what I should hold, and give me a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, flagging where that reply is weakest. In particular, help me think through the hard cases my role-based view has to handle: letters that are illegible out of context but fine within a word (does the role story explain that, or is it just a restatement?), ambiguities like the numeral-letter and uppercase-lowercase overlaps that seem to depend on the reader's prior parsing rather than on any system of contrasts, and invented scripts, where there is no shared system yet at all and my account seems to get no grip. Finally, tell me the single question I'm hoping nobody asks, and how I should answer it if they do.
