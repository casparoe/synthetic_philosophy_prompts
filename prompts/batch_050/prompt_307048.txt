I coordinate peer review for a mid-sized journal covering real and functional analysis, measure theory, and operator algebras. Last year, after a reader's complaint, we adopted a policy that every published paper must carry a "choice declaration" stating the strongest choice principle its results depend on: full axiom of choice (AC), the ultrafilter lemma / Boolean prime ideal theorem, dependent choice (DC), countable choice (CC), or "none, provable in ZF alone." The problem is that our referees are analysts, not set theorists, and twice now a referee has signed off on a "no choice used" declaration for a paper that plainly needed one. I need to turn our policy into something a competent analyst-referee can actually execute.

Write a review procedure — a checklist of ordered tests — that a referee can run on a 20-page paper in about 45 minutes, without any set theory beyond a standard graduate education, and that ends in a defensible choice declaration. For each test, state exactly what counts as a positive result and which declaration level it assigns. The procedure must at minimum cover: transfinite enumerations and "index the elements of X by ordinals" constructions; Zorn's lemma and maximal-principle arguments, including how to tell when a Zorn application could have been discharged with something weaker; "choose one element from each set in this family" steps, and why the answer depends on whether the family is finite, countable, or arbitrary, and on whether a definable selector exists; proofs that pass silently through intermediate results (e.g., Hahn–Banach, Tychonoff) whose own choice content the referee must look up rather than re-derive; and arguments that use countable additivity or "take a sequence" reasoning where the choice is easy to miss.

The procedure must also handle three classification subtleties correctly: (i) the difference between "this proof uses AC" and "this theorem requires AC" — a Zorn's lemma proof does not by itself mean the theorem needs full choice; (ii) the existence of principles strictly between ZF and full AC, so that "needs some choice" is not "needs AC"; (iii) the fact that deciding whether a flagged use is essential to the theorem, rather than an artifact of the proof, is often a hard research question — so the procedure should declare what the proofs use, and escalate "essentiality" disputes to our consulting set theorist rather than adjudicate them.

Then apply the procedure, step by step, to three worked cases: (1) a paper proving the Hahn–Banach theorem via Zorn's lemma, where the correct declaration is weaker than the referee's first instinct; (2) a general-topology paper invoking Tychonoff's theorem for arbitrary products of compact spaces; and (3) a measure-theory paper that makes free use of the countable additivity of Lebesgue measure and of the equivalence between continuity and sequential continuity — a case where your procedure must produce a declaration the authors will dislike, because results they treat as choice-free are not. State explicitly where in case (3) the procedure bites and what the referee should write to the authors.

Conclude with a short section on what the procedure cannot settle: classes of papers or questions where the honest output is "consult the set theorist," and at least two named failure modes you expect when analysts run it.
