Question: Higher-order theories of consciousness hold that a mental state is conscious in virtue of being represented by a suitable higher-order state. (a) State the theory and its motivation. (b) Explain the misrepresentation problem for higher-order thought (HOT) theory. (c) How might a defender of HOT theory respond?

Student's answer:

(a) On higher-order theories, a mental state becomes conscious when it is the object of another, higher-order mental state. On Rosenthal's version, the higher-order state is a thought: a noninferential, assertoric thought that one is in the target state. Importantly, the higher-order thought must itself be conscious, since a nonconscious thought couldn't confer consciousness on anything — this is what distinguishes genuine HOT theory from mere dispositionalist versions. The motivation comes from "transitive consciousness": being conscious of something is what makes us conscious of it. Just as we become conscious of a tree by seeing it, we become conscious of our pain by having a thought about it. This also explains why conscious states feel like they're mine: the HOT represents the state as one's own.

(b) The misrepresentation problem (sometimes called the rock problem) is that a HOT might represent its target inaccurately — e.g., representing a red-experience when the first-order state is actually a green-experience, or even when there is no first-order state at all (a "targetless" HOT). If consciousness requires the HOT to match its target, then these cases wouldn't be conscious, which seems wrong; but if it doesn't require a match, the theory apparently allows conscious experiences with no first-order basis at all.

(c) Rosenthal bites the bullet: what it's like for the subject is fixed by the HOT, so in the red/green case the subject consciously experiences red, and in the targetless case the subject still has a conscious experience since the HOT alone suffices. Critics reply that this renders first-order states explanatorily idle and makes it unclear what the HOT is even representing in targetless cases.
