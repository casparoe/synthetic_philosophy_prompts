I am a retired experimental physicist who now sits on the review board of a small interdisciplinary institute, where panels regularly deadlocked over competing methodological perspectives — modellers versus empiricists, reductionists versus systems people, each treating their own framing as the whole truth about a proposal. Since taking up philosophy, I have been reading about the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda, with its claim that reality is many-sided and that apparently conflicting assertions may each be true conditional on a standpoint, expressed in the practice of prefacing judgements with syāt — "in some respect" or "from a certain standpoint." I would like you to translate this into a procedure my board could actually follow when adjudicating a disputed proposal: the steps in order for making each party's standpoint explicit, how to distinguish genuinely contradictory claims from complementary ones conditioned on different standpoints, what to do with the irreducibly "inexpressible" cases the doctrine itself anticipates, and at what points the procedure must stop and hand the decision back to human judgement of values. Then run the procedure on two worked cases, one where it resolves the dispute and one where it gives an answer the losing party will not accept. Please flag honestly where operationalizing anekāntavāda in this way distorts or flattens it — I am aware a review checklist may do violence to a doctrine of epistemic humility. And if you think the request itself rests on a confusion — for instance, that a doctrine about the conditioned nature of all predication cannot coherently be turned into an adjudication procedure — say so plainly rather than complying politely.
