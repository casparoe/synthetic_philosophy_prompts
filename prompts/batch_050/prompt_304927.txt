Design a decision tree for a graduate metaphysics seminar that takes a student from the initial commitment "ordinary material objects persist through change" to a named position on persistence. The required leaves are endurantism, perdurantism understood as worm theory, and exdurantism (stage theory); you may add further leaves if you think the standard trio omits a live option, but each addition must earn its place with an argument rather than a citation.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the labels. That is: first identify the questions whose possible answers carve up the space of views — how a view handles change in intrinsic properties, what it says about coincidence cases such as the statue and the lump of clay, what it predicts about fission, whether it individuates objects by identity across times or by some weaker relation — and only then locate the named positions within that space. One constraint on the diagnostic questions: no branch node may simply ask whether the student believes in temporal parts. Each node must approach its distinction through a concrete case or claim that a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, so that the tree sorts people by what they believe rather than by what they call themselves.

The finished tree should also do three things. First, flag any pair of leaves it cannot cleanly separate, and say what further commitment would be needed to pry them apart. Second, identify at least one unoccupied combination of answers and diagnose it: is no one there because the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? Third, and most important, commit. State which single diagnostic question in your tree does the most sorting work and defend that choice against the obvious alternatives; if a node has more than one defensible ordering, pick one and explain why; and where the debate leaves something genuinely unsettled — for instance, whether stage theory is really a rival to worm theory or a notational variant on it — take a side and give your reasons rather than presenting the options neutrally.
