I teach a seminar on the philosophy of history, and every year the same stalemate recurs. One faction of students arrives having absorbed the slogan that history is written by the victors: they take the selection of sources, the survival of archives, the canonization of some narratives and the burial of others, and the alignment of national historiographies with state interests to show that historical knowledge is, at bottom, an expression of power, and that claims to historical objectivity are naive or self-serving. The opposing faction -- usually students who have taken actual history courses -- responds that this proves far too much: that the evidentiary methods of the discipline (source criticism, triangulation, archaeology, the ability of later scholars to overturn entrenched narratives using the very archives the victors left behind) discipline power rather than merely reflecting it, and that the relativist position cannot explain why we are able to *know* that certain victor-narratives were false. Both sides talk past each other, and I have decided that instead of another round of essays I will require them to draft, collectively, the joint statement that a serious version of each camp could sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration.

Please draft that document for them. It should contain: a numbered list of agreed claims -- things both camps can honestly sign, such as (I would guess) that source survival and access are non-random and correlated with power, that specific victor-narratives have been conclusively refuted, that historians' questions as well as their answers are shaped by their situation, and that some historical propositions are simply true regardless of who won; a precise statement of each remaining disagreement, phrased so that the side whose view it describes would accept the wording -- I suspect the real fault lines concern whether power distorts *which truths get told* versus *what counts as truth*, and whether "distortion everywhere" entails "nothing but distortion"; and a set of cruxes, meaning sub-questions such that settling them would oblige at least one camp to move. For each crux, specify a concrete case, body of evidence, or argumentative result that both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each side predicts -- for example, how each would expect historiography of a defeated power's own atrocities to develop, or what each would say about cases where scholars from the victorious society did the most to expose their own side's founding myths.

Two further requirements. First, if part of the dispute is merely verbal -- if, say, the relativist is making a claim about the sociology of archives while the objectivist is making a claim about the epistemic status of well-attested propositions -- identify that part explicitly, explain why it is verbal, and state what each camp should concede once the ambiguity is removed; but do not dissolve the entire disagreement this way unless you genuinely think nothing substantive remains, and if you do think that, argue for it rather than assuming it. Second, end the document with a short section each camp could sign describing what it would count as evidence against its own position -- the relativist's falsification conditions tend to be conspicuously absent from the classroom debate, and I want that asymmetry examined rather than assumed. The intended audience is advanced undergraduates, so the statement should be rigorous without presupposing familiarity with particular theorists; name figures such as Thucydides, Ranke, or Foucault only where doing so genuinely sharpens a formulation.
