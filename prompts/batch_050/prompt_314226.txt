I'm preparing the syllabus for a graduate seminar on vagueness, and before I assign papers on any particular theory -- supervaluationism, epistemicism, degree-theoretic semantics, contextualism, or anything else -- I want to be able to tell my students what question those theories are answers to, and what would count as a good answer. So: write the desiderata that any adequate theory of vagueness must satisfy. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and indicate whether you take it to be non-negotiable or something a theory could reasonably trade away.

I have some candidate requirements in mind, but I want you to improve on, reorganize, and where necessary reject this list rather than simply endorse it: a theory should dissolve or block the sorites paradox rather than merely refusing to state its conclusion; it should explain why borderline cases feel genuinely undecidable even to a speaker with complete non-linguistic information, and not just assert that they are; it should say something principled about higher-order vagueness, rather than relocating the sharp boundary to the border between the clear cases and the borderline ones and calling that progress; it should account for penumbral connections, the way 'tall' and 'short' constrain each other even when both are vague; it should explain why the sorites reasoning feels compulsive for predicates like 'heap' or 'rich' but never tempts us with predicates that wear their cutoffs openly; and it should explain our practice -- why we get along fine using vague language in law, science, and ordinary life if the semantics is as troubled as the paradox suggests. One thing I particularly want adjudicated: whether preserving classical logic wholesale is a genuine desideratum or just a prejudice of one camp, and the same question about preserving the intuition that vague predicates are tolerant to small changes.

The deliverable I care about is the criteria list itself, not a verdict on the literature, so please keep the weight there. That said, two things would make it much more useful. First, flag any pair or triple of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which you'd sacrifice and why -- if the desiderata list is itself inconsistent, that is a result, not a failure of the exercise. Second, after the list, give a brief and openly provisional scoring of the main contenders against it, with the caveat that the scoring leans on contested readings of those views while the criteria are where the real thinking is. Finally, please be explicit about the epistemic status of your list: which desideratum are you least confident belongs there, what argument or case would convince you to strike one of your non-negotiable requirements, and where is your reasoning most likely to have smuggled in the assumptions of one theoretical camp?
