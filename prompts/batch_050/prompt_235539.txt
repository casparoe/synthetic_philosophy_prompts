As a retired biochemist who has taken up philosophy in earnest only in the last few years, I keep running up against a pattern in everyday life that I suspect the philosophical literature has already dissected, but I lack the vocabulary to find it. I would like your help identifying it, and then pressing on a moral question attached to it. Please answer as a professional philosopher working at the intersection of philosophy of language and normative ethics. Think carefully and slowly. Before settling on an answer, brainstorm the candidate concepts that come near the phenomenon, and only then select, distinguish, and assess.

Here is the pattern, in two instances I have personally witnessed.

First instance. A colleague was asked directly, in a departmental meeting, whether anything in his group's published dataset had been manipulated. He replied that there was not a single fabricated number in the table. This was, as it later turned out, strictly accurate: the table was clean, but a figure based on it had been smoothed in a way he knew about. Every word he uttered was true, and everyone in the room walked away believing something false, which was exactly what he intended.

Second instance. Asked by a patient's family whether the treatment was working, a physician said that every patient who completed the trial protocol showed improvement. Also strictly accurate. She omitted that a third of the enrolled patients had dropped out, mostly because the treatment was failing them, and she chose her sentence precisely so that the family would not think to ask about the dropouts.

What strikes me is the aftermath. In both cases, when the full facts surfaced, the speakers defended themselves with some version of: everything I said was true. And here is the part I find philosophically interesting rather than merely irritating: their colleagues, myself included, judged them noticeably less harshly than we would have judged someone who had simply asserted a falsehood outright — even though the audience in each case ended up with the same false belief, and even though the intent to produce that false belief was equally deliberate and equally calculated. The method differed; the deception did not.

My questions, then, are these. Does this phenomenon — deliberate deception accomplished entirely through true utterances, where the false conclusion is drawn by the listener rather than stated by the speaker — have an established name in the philosophical literature? If there are several neighboring notions (I gather there is a general category of deception-by-truth, and possibly more specific terms for particular varieties), please lay out the nearest candidates, state precisely how the pattern I have described differs from each, and say which fits best. In particular, I want to know where the edges are: does it still count if the speaker's true statement is merely irrelevant rather than carefully chosen, if the listener's mistaken inference is one a reasonable person would not have made, or if the speaker stays silent and lets a false impression he did not create persist? If no existing term carves things at quite these joints, say so plainly and coin one, with a definition and some boundary cases.

Then, the moral question. Is the widely shared intuition — that deceiving someone by means of true statements is less blameworthy than deceiving them by means of a false statement, holding intent and outcome fixed — defensible? What are the strongest arguments on each side, and where do you come down? Please do not merely name positions; give me the best version of each argument, including the argument that the distinction is a mere convention of language with no moral weight, and the strongest reply available to it.
