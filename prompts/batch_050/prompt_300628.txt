I'm a literary novelist at work on a linked collection about machines that mean things, and I want one story to dramatize the dispute between Searle and Dennett over derived intentionality, using Dennett's two-bitser: a vending machine built to detect US quarters is shipped to Panama, where quarter-balboas match the quarter in size and weight, and it begins accepting them. Its designers insist it still detects quarters; its new users insist it detects balboas. Searle would say the machine has no aboutness of its own, only what makers and users lend it; Dennett's deeper point is that nothing settles what its states are about — and that our own minds may be in the same predicament.

Write this story for me, roughly 1,500–2,000 words, under four constraints:

1. Narrate the machine partly from inside, giving it a point of view, but never settle — even implicitly — what its internal states are about. Any attribution of content to it must be contestable by some character in the scene.
2. Stage the conflict through two figures with rival claims on the machine's meaning (its original designer and the vendor who now profits from it, say). Their argument must be genuinely philosophical, driven by real stakes; neither may be a straw man.
3. At some point, turn Searle's move against itself: show that if the machine's aboutness is merely derived from its designers, then a human character's aboutness is derived in the same way — from parents, teachers, employers, evolution — without anyone using the words intentionality, original, or derived.
4. End with the machine doing something that neither interpreter can describe in their preferred terms.

The reader should feel the indeterminacy of content rather than be told it; no character may state the moral outright.
