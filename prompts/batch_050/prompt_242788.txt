I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and a recurring piece of advice there — design your environment rather than relying on willpower; you are a bundle of situation-specific routines, not a coherent character — keeps colliding with my older Aristotelian intuition that the point of a life is becoming a certain kind of person. I've realized I don't actually know which position I hold in the debate between virtue ethics and the situationist critique, and I suspect many people in my orbit share the same unexamined muddle. So: construct a decision tree that sorts holders of positions in this debate.

Fix the root commitment explicitly: anyone entering the tree accepts that the experimental record — the obedience-to-authority experiments, the study of seminarians hurrying past someone in distress, the mood-manipulation helping studies, the early large-scale work on honesty in schoolchildren — is sound enough to count as prima facie data, and that together these results suggest small situational variables predict behavior at least as well as commonsense trait attributions do. The tree's job is to sort what one should make of that fact.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Each branch node should be a question someone could answer without having heard of any of the positions — questions like whether the experiments threaten the frequency of virtue or its very possibility; whether a character trait must manifest as cross-situationally consistent behavior or can instead live in an agent's patterns of reasons and deliberation; whether fragmented, situation-relative traits still deserve the name of character; whether virtue is an empirical hypothesis about human psychology or a normative standard no experiment could disconfirm; and — the node I care most about — what follows practically for self-improvement: habituation toward stable traits, engineering one's situations and incentives, or retiring character-talk in favor of outcome-talk.

To force the positions apart, run one invented case through several nodes. Call it the Audit: imagine a perfect log of your last five years of choices reveals that whether you acted generously, honestly, or courageously on any given occasion is predicted better by time pressure, ambient mood, and who was watching than by anything stable in you. At each relevant node, ask: what, if anything, does the Audit show that you lack?

The leaves should number about eight and must include: Aristotelian globalism that meets the challenge on its own terms; the rarity or degree response (virtue exists but is uncommon and fragile); local-trait or fragmentarian virtue ethics in Doris's style; character eliminativism in Harman's style; reasons-responsiveness accounts of the Kamtekar or Merritt type; social-cognitive reconciliations treating personality as a system of if–then signatures rather than traits (Mischel's route); and the practicalist exit — keeping virtue language as self-fulfilling social technology regardless of its descriptive accuracy (Alfano-adjacent). Aim for six to nine branch nodes. Note any combinations of answers that no named position occupies (for instance, "global traits exist but make no behavioral difference") and diagnose each briefly: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say what further question would be needed to split them.

On epistemic discipline: keep sharply separate what is genuinely settled — the basic experimental findings and their broad replication status — from what remains contested among philosophers and psychologists, such as whether the experiments measure character at all and whether aggregation across occasions rescues trait talk. Do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus.

Present the deliverable beginning with the tree itself, with no preface or framing commentary: render it as an indented text flowchart, then follow it with the scoring key mapping answer-sequences to named positions, the notes on unoccupied combinations, and the inseparability flags.
