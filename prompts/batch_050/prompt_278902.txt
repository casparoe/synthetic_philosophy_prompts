You are a philosopher specializing in population ethics and wild animal welfare; think carefully, and begin by briefly brainstorming the relevant considerations before settling on a structure. Take as your case the ongoing conversion of Borneo's lowland rainforests into oil palm plantations, one of the largest deliberate habitat destruction episodes of the last half-century, and analyze it through the lens of the thesis, defended in parts of the wild animal suffering literature, that habitat destruction may reduce total suffering by shrinking populations of wild animals whose lives are on balance net negative. Map the case carefully: distinguish the animals killed outright during clearing, those displaced into forest fragments or plantations, those who would have been born but never are, and the species that recolonize the new landscape -- often generalists and fast-breeding invertebrates with very different population dynamics from the displaced vertebrates. For each group, say what the thesis predicts about the net welfare effect and what empirical fact would actually settle the question. Then run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode show about the thesis? Does it suggest that "habitat destruction" is too coarse a category, so that what matters is what replaces the habitat rather than its mere removal? Does the net-negative-lives premise even have determinate application to animals with extreme fecundity and near-universal juvenile mortality? And where is the thesis genuinely silent rather than confirmed or contradicted? Be disciplined about flagging where you are reasoning from general principles versus relying on empirical claims about the case that a careful reader should treat as uncertain.
