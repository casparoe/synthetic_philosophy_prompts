Analyze the placebo-controlled trials of short-course zidovudine to prevent mother-to-child HIV transmission in lower-income countries in the 1990s through the lens of clinical equipoise. For the purposes of the analysis, take the following facts as given: a longer and more burdensome zidovudine regimen had already been shown in high-income countries substantially to reduce perinatal transmission, but its cost and infrastructure requirements made it impractical in many of the trial settings; the shorter regimen was plausible but had not yet been shown effective; and investigators randomized some pregnant women to receive the shorter regimen and others to receive placebo. Critics objected that participants were denied a known-effective intervention available elsewhere, while defenders argued that the relevant local uncertainty remained and that a placebo control was the fastest and most scientifically reliable way to identify a regimen that could actually be implemented.

Begin by specifying what clinical equipoise requires and what state of uncertainty it concerns: uncertainty within the relevant expert community, uncertainty in an individual clinician, or a principled evidential judgment about comparative therapeutic merits. Map the possible comparisons carefully: short-course zidovudine versus placebo, short-course versus the longer regimen, treatment versus the locally available standard, and treatment versus the best treatment available anywhere. Does equipoise fail as soon as any effective intervention has been demonstrated somewhere, or can it persist with respect to a materially different regimen and population?

Then assess the central ethical dispute. Should the relevant standard of care be determined by what was locally feasible, by the best proven treatment globally, or by some intermediate requirement? Could the trial be in equipoise and still be exploitative? Conversely, could an active-control trial have avoided the placebo objection while producing a result too inconclusive to benefit the host population? Explain whether methodological need can ever contribute to ethical justification, rather than merely coinciding with it.

Finally, consider what the episode reveals about clinical equipoise itself. Is the doctrine too centered on physicians’ epistemic states, insufficiently attentive to global injustice and post-trial obligations, or ambiguous between a test of evidential uncertainty and a standard for permissible risk to participants? Reach a defended verdict on whether the placebo design was ethically permissible under an adequately formulated equipoise requirement, specifying any conditions necessary to your conclusion and identifying a preferable trial design if you think it was not.
