In my philosophy of mathematics seminar this term, the organizing question has been when mathematics genuinely explains a physical phenomenon, as opposed to merely representing or redescribing one, and we opened the course with a passage from the preface to Book V of Pappus of Alexandria's Mathematical Collection. In that preface, Pappus praises the honeybee and attributes to it a kind of geometrical foresight: having been allotted, as he puts it, the task of storing honey in vessels, the bees settle on the hexagon because, of the three regular figures that can tile the plane, the hexagon encloses the most honey for the same expenditure of wax, and Pappus presents this as though the bees themselves grasp the isoperimetric comparison. Our professor is openly skeptical of that reading, and over the semester she has given verdicts on a series of candidate cases of distinctively mathematical explanation in science. Her verdicts so far: first, the periodical cicada case — the fact that North American magicicadas have life cycles of 13 or 17 years, prime numbers, which minimizes the frequency with which their emergences coincide with the cycles of hypothetical periodic predators — she calls a genuine mathematical explanation, since what explains the advantage of 13 and 17 over, say, 12 or 15 is a theorem-level fact about divisibility and least common multiples, not anything further about wasp physiology. Second, the Königsberg bridges case — Euler's proof that no walk crosses each of the city's seven bridges exactly once — she says is not a mathematical explanation of anything physical: once the city is redescribed as a graph, what remains is a pure theorem about graphs, and the only physical facts in sight are the contingent geography, which does the explaining if anything does. Third, the honeycomb case — the modern theorem (proved by Thomas Hales around 1999) that the regular hexagonal tiling minimizes perimeter per unit area, cited as explaining why bees build hexagonal cells — she flatly denies is mathematical explanation: natural selection explains why wax-efficient bees prevailed, surface physics explains why even soap bubbles approximate hexagons, and the theorem merely identifies the optimum that those processes converge on; the mathematics, she says, is doing bookkeeping, not explaining, and she takes this verdict to refute Pappus's suggestion that geometry itself is in the driver's seat.

Next week she has promised us a new case, and I want to anticipate her. Any closed cage built entirely from hexagonal faces — a soccer-ball-type structure — must contain exactly twelve pentagons, no more and no fewer; this follows from Euler's formula for polyhedra, and it is why carbon fullerene molecules such as C60, geodesic domes, and certain virus capsids, which are hexagonal cages closed around a sphere, invariably incorporate exactly twelve pentagonal defects. The question she will pose is why every such structure, from the molecule to the dome, exhibits exactly twelve pentagons. Predict her verdict on whether this is a genuine mathematical explanation of a physical phenomenon, given her track record. In doing so, please reconstruct at least two rules that are each consistent with all three of her prior verdicts but that diverge on the fullerene case — I can already see that one natural rule turns on whether the mathematical fact concerns the physical magnitudes themselves rather than an abstract surrogate, while another turns on whether the distinguishing work in the explanation is done by mathematics rather than by physics or selection — state what each rule predicts about the fullerenes, say which rule you take her actually to be running and why, and identify the closest analog among her three earlier cases. Finally, name a further hypothetical case that would discriminate between the two rules you attribute to her, and say whether, on the verdict you predict, she owes Pappus a partial apology: if combinatorial necessity can explain why carbon cages have twelve pentagons, is it so clear that geometry explains nothing about why bees build hexagons?
