I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation chapter that applies Lewis's account of convention to regularities that remain stable despite being strictly worse than an available alternative. My test case is the file-naming scheme in my department's shared data repository: everyone finds it cumbersome, everyone would prefer a particular cleaner scheme if the others adopted it too, yet the old scheme persists because nobody wants to deviate unilaterally. I worry that this case falsifies Lewis's analysis. A convention, on his view, is supposed to be an arbitrary solution to a coordination problem with multiple equally acceptable equilibria; but here one equilibrium is plainly inferior, so the regularity seems not to qualify as a convention at all. Moreover, rational agents who unanimously prefer the alternative should simply switch — so the scheme's persistence looks like sheer irrationality that a rational-choice analysis cannot capture. Am I right that such cases fall outside Lewis's account and require a non-rational explanation of their stability?
