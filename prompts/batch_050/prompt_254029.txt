I chair the benefits committee of a large regional health insurer, and our charter requires us to cover medically necessary treatment while excluding elective enhancement. Over the past three years we have built up a small body of precedent, and I would like help thinking through how it constrains our next decision. Our rulings to date: we covered growth hormone for a child with a diagnosed pituitary deficiency, but denied it for a child with idiopathic short stature whose projected adult height would be unusually low despite there being no underlying disorder; we covered cochlear implants for a child who lost her hearing to meningitis; we covered cataract surgery for elderly patients with age-related lens clouding; we covered breast reconstruction following mastectomy; and we denied a group of healthy hospital residents who wanted a wakefulness drug to extend their alertness across long shifts. I should note that we never articulated a general principle in any of these decisions — we simply voted, and the votes were never close.

The pending application concerns a new, apparently safe neuroprotective drug that has been shown to modestly slow, and in some patients partially reverse, ordinary age-related memory decline. The applicants are healthy 78-year-olds. None of them meets the diagnostic criteria for mild cognitive impairment or dementia; on standardized tests their memory sits comfortably within the normal range for people their age. Their argument rests instead on occupational and educational records from their forties and fifties demonstrating that each of them now performs well below the level they themselves once reliably achieved. They are, in effect, asking us to fund restoration of their own prior functioning — not improvement beyond it, and not treatment of any diagnosed condition.

My questions are as follows. First, does our track record actually settle this case, or is it consistent with more than one rule? If the latter, lay out the two or three most plausible principles we might have been running all along — for instance, rules keyed to diagnosed pathology, to age-adjusted normal human functioning, or to restoration of a person's own prior level — and state what each would deliver here. Second, which of our earlier rulings is the closest analog: the cataract case, where we treated an age-related loss, or the idiopathic short-stature case, where we denied coverage for a statistical shortfall in the absence of pathology? Give the strongest argument for each pairing. Third, whatever we decide, state plainly what principle we would thereby be adopting, including what we would implicitly be committed to saying about whether ordinary aging is itself a medical condition. Finally, describe a hypothetical future application — an invented one, not a case from the real literature — whose resolution would reveal which principle we had in fact settled on, and explain why that case discriminates where our existing precedents do not.
