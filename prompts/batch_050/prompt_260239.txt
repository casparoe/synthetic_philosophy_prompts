I am a doctoral student working on the metaphysics of biological individuality, and my dissertation keeps returning to an analogy that the social-insect literature has leaned on for over a century: an ant colony is itself an organism, a 'superorganism'. The mapping that gets offered is familiar: the workers are like somatic cells, the queen and males are like the germline, the nest architecture plays the role of a body plan, the foraging trail network and pheromone signals function as a nervous and circulatory system, and colony-level division of labour is taken to mirror differentiation among tissues. Defenders of the analogy draw real conclusions from it: that natural selection can act on colonies as units, that we should expect colonies to evolve internal integration the way multicellular bodies did, and more recently (in holobiont debates) that hosts together with their microbes might constitute organisms in the same extended sense. My supervisor is sceptical and keeps pressing me on whether this is doing genuine explanatory work or is just an evocative redescription of things kin selection already explains without it.

I would like you to audit the analogy properly rather than simply endorse or dismiss it. Give the correspondence explicitly, as a table if that helps: which features of the multicellular organism are supposed to map onto which features of the colony. Then identify where the mapping breaks. I am particularly interested in whether the standard disanalogies actually damage the intended conclusions: workers are not genetically identical clones of one another the way a body's cells (roughly) are, colonies harbour reproductive conflicts that ordinary bodies have largely suppressed, worker reproduction and policing mean that colony 'soma' members retain their own reproductive interests, and there is no germ-soma sequestration comparable to a Weismann barrier. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the superorganism's theoretical uses from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Note also the direction-of-explanation worry: if we individuate 'organism-like' colonies by their degree of internal cooperation and suppressed conflict, does the analogy become circular, since it then only applies to cases already organised enough to look organismal?

Finally, say what the analogy can and cannot carry. Does the conclusion that selection operates at the colony level really need the organism comparison, or does it stand on its own via multi-level selection theory? Does the analogy earn its keep in the holobiont case, or does it mislead there (given that microbiota lack even the relatedness structure of an ant colony)? If there is a better comparison than 'organism' for what colonies are, propose it. Please write in British English.
