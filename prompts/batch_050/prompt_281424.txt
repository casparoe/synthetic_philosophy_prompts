I've been trying to write down my considered view on pleasure and welfare, and before I commit to it I want to know whether the pieces actually fit together. Here is where I've landed, with the motivation for each commitment.

First, hedonism about well-being: how well a person's life goes for them is entirely a matter of the pleasure it contains and the pain it avoids. Nothing else — achievement, knowledge, virtue — contributes except by producing pleasure or pain.

Second, phenomenal heterogeneity: there is no single felt quality common to all pleasures. The taste of ripe fruit, the relief of bad news turning out false, the absorption in a good proof, the warmth of an old friendship's company — these share no phenomenal character, no common sensation or "tone." Whatever unifies the pleasures as pleasures is not something they feel like.

Third, an attitudinal account of pleasure: given the heterogeneity point, I hold that an experience is a pleasure in virtue of the subject's intrinsic positive attitude toward having it — taking pleasure in it, in something like Feldman's sense. The pro-attitude, not a sensation, is what does the unifying work the second commitment says no sensation can do.

Fourth, experientialism about prudential value: how good an episode is for a person at a time is fixed entirely by what the episode is like for them from the inside. Two subjects undergoing phenomenally identical episodes are, at that moment, doing equally well, whatever they happen to want, approve of, or care about.

Fifth, hedonic aggregation is meaningful: there are facts of the form "this pleasure is twice as intense as that one" and "her week contained more pleasure than his," robust enough to support both intrapersonal weighing and interpersonal comparisons of welfare. Without this, I don't think the first commitment can do the work I want it to in distributive questions.

Sixth, the sadist verdict: the cruel person's pleasure in another's suffering still counts positively for his own welfare. Discounting pleasures by the moral quality of their objects conflates the question of how well a life goes for the one living it with the question of whether it is a good life, full stop.

My question: is this package jointly tenable? Some of these look like they might pull against each other, but I can't tell which frictions are real and which are only verbal. Please structure your answer as follows: Verdict (one short paragraph); Genuine tensions, ranked by severity (for each, name the exact commitments involved, explain why they conflict rather than merely sounding like they do, and state what would have to give — roughly two paragraphs each); Apparent tensions that dissolve on inspection (one paragraph each, explaining the confusion that generates them); and finally The cheapest consistent revision (one or two paragraphs identifying which single commitment, if dropped or weakened, restores consistency at the lowest cost, and being candid about what that cost is).
