I've read rationalist blogs and forums long enough to have internalized the standard litany: preferences must be transitive, because cyclic preferences get you money-pumped; expected utility maximization isn't one decision theory among many but the shape of coherence itself; the consistency axioms are constraints any agent must satisfy on pain of exploitation. A friend recently assembled an argument that seems to show this framework proves too much — that coherence is impossible for any agent that could actually exist, human or machine. The conclusion can't be right, but I can't locate the flaw. Here is the argument.

Setup: Quinn's self-torturer. A device with settings 0 through 1000 is implanted in someone; each click upward pays $10,000 and raises the pain level by one increment. No single increment is perceptible, but the difference between setting 0 and setting 1000 is the difference between comfort and agony. At every setting, one more click looks like a strictly good trade. The argument generalizes:

(1) An agent is instrumentally irrational if its preferences license choices that leave it predictably worse off by its own lights — for instance, being cycled through trades and ending where it began, minus money.
(2) Strict preferences must therefore be acyclic: a cycle A≻B≻C≻A makes the agent pumpable.
(3) Indifference must be transitive for the same reason. If A~B and B~C but C≻A, a trader can sell the agent C in exchange for A plus a small fee (the agent pays, since it strictly prefers C), then swap C for B and B for A at no charge (the agent is indifferent, so accepts), leaving the agent back at A and poorer. Repeat indefinitely.
(4) Any physically realized agent has finite discriminatory resolution: for any fine-grained good there is a sorites sequence x0,...,xn such that the agent cannot discriminate xi from xi+1, while xn is clearly better than x0.
(5) An agent cannot rationally hold a strict preference between options it cannot discriminate: preference requires some detectable difference, and preferring one of two subjectively identical states is arbitrary. So xi ~ xi+1 for all i.
(6) By (3) and induction, x0 ~ xn. But by (4), xn ≻ x0. Contradiction.
(7) Hence no physically realized agent can have fully coherent preferences. The coherence theorems describe a standard nothing can meet; every actual agent, including any AI we could build, is exploitable in principle, and 'irrational' applies to everything and therefore distinguishes nothing.

The conclusion proves too much: a norm that is categorically unsatisfiable isn't a norm, and a diagnosis under which all possible agents are incoherent has lost its bite. So something above has to give. What I'd most like to keep: transitivity of strict preference as a genuine requirement, and the idea that bounded agents can count as approximately rational rather than categorically broken.

Tell me which step or premise to reject — or, if you think the argument is valid, which premise is cheapest to abandon. Explain why the failing step looked compelling. And price the obvious escape routes: (a) deny that indiscriminability implies indifference — but then what grounds preferences over imperceptible differences; (b) accept intransitive indifference and block the pump in (3) — but what exactly is wrong with the construction, and does blocking it require giving up the assumption that indifferent agents may swap freely; (c) reject money-pump arguments as a foundation for transitivity altogether — but then what becomes of the standard justification for expected utility theory that the rationalist canon leans on? Finally: does your diagnosis dissolve the original self-torturer puzzle — locally rational steps, globally disastrous trajectory — or does that puzzle survive intact?
