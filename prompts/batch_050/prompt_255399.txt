My epistemology professor argues that Moore's proof of an external world and the skeptic's brain-in-a-vat argument are the same inference run in opposite directions: one person's modus ponens is another's modus tollens. Reconstructed schematically, Moore reasons: I know that this is a hand; if I know that this is a hand, then an external world exists; therefore an external world exists. The skeptic reasons: if I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat, then I do not know that this is a hand; I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat; therefore I do not know that this is a hand. From this symmetry she concludes that Moore's proof settles nothing: it persuades only those already more certain of their hands than of the skeptic's premises, and the skeptic's argument persuades only the opposite camp.

Audit the analogy. Set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the shared conditional, the contested premise, and the punchline in each argument. Then identify where the symmetry framing breaks. Does it presuppose that a proof's force consists entirely in how certain its audience already is of the premises, and is that an accurate picture of what Moore took his proof to accomplish? Does it matter that one contested premise is a deliverance of ordinary perception while the other is a piece of epistemological theory, such as a closure principle or a claim about what skeptical hypotheses rule out? Does the framing quietly assume that the two arguments' premises are on a par as starting points, so that the choice between them can only be a matter of prior confidence? Finally, say whether my professor's conclusion -- that the proof settles nothing -- survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion from the start.
