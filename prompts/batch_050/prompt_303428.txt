I'm a journalist who covers science, technology, and ideas, and I was recently appointed as the public member of the data-protection advisory board of a mid-sized German city (a partly honorary role the city gives to someone from outside the administration; I took it because it overlaps with my reporting beat). Next month the board must issue a recommendation on the city's new transparency portal, and the proposal on the table bundles three very different visions, which map almost embarrassingly well onto three national traditions I've written about. Option A is the American-style version: maximum openness, with machine-readable publication of everything that is technically public record — property and company registries, court decisions, officials' meetings with lobbyists, even individual-level administrative data wherever the law permits — on the theory that sunlight is the best disinfectant and that citizens have nothing to fear from a state they can fully inspect. Option B is the classically German one, shaped by the postwar doctrine of informational self-determination and a historical memory of what registers can be used for: publish only aggregated statistics, minimize personal data by default, and treat any linking of datasets across agencies as presumptively illegitimate. Option C is a vendor's add-on that several council members find attractive: a "civic reliability" module that would combine parking fines, tax arrears, utility payment histories, and welfare compliance into a per-resident score, used to fast-track trustworthy residents for permits and flag others for audits — pitched to us as smart administration, though my colleagues will not be surprised that it resembles, in scaled-down municipal form, the logic of Chinese social credit pilots.

The board is split roughly three ways, and the debate keeps circling a question none of us can quite formulate: is there a general right of citizens to opacity vis-à-vis the state, and does it cut both ways — that is, does the justification for making the state transparent to citizens also justify making citizens transparent to the state, or is the asymmetry the whole point? Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote: roughly 700 words, addressed to the board, recommending that we adopt one of the three options (or a specified combination with conditions), with a concrete bottom line the council could implement. The opinion should not just invoke privacy as a trump card or openness as a slogan; it should articulate the principle doing the work — who owes visibility to whom, and why — and it must give the strongest serious argument for the option(s) I recommend rejecting, stated fairly enough that a supporter of that option would recognize their own position in it. No citations or legal case law are needed; argue from principle.

After the memo itself, append a JSON object summarizing the recommendation with exactly these fields: "recommendation" (a short string naming the chosen option or combination), "conditions" (an array of strings listing any safeguards or qualifications attached to the adoption; empty array if none), "core_principle" (one sentence stating the underlying norm about citizen-state visibility), "strongest_objection" (one sentence giving the best argument against the recommendation), and "reply_to_objection" (one sentence giving the memo's answer to it).
