I've been turning over an argument I built for myself after moving from a country where people cross an empty street on a red light to one where everyone waits, and it seems to prove something I can't accept. (1) A rule a society does not actually enforce is not really a rule; the whole point of having one is that compliance is expected and defections are discouraged. (2) The benefit of nearly every social rule -- queues, traffic lights, contracts, noise limits -- comes from reliability: each defection costs the rule's followers more than it gains the defector, and the more reliable compliance is, the more the rule is worth. (3) So for any rule worth keeping, pressing compliance ever closer to 100% always makes the society better off. (4) But a society that demands perfect compliance with every rule would be unlivable: no crossing on red at 3 a.m. on an empty street, no exceptions for emergencies, no discretion for cases the rule's authors never imagined. (5) Since the conclusion in (4) is absurd, something earlier must be false -- yet (1) and (2) each strike me as obviously true. Where does this argument actually go wrong? If I have to give something up, I would rather surrender part of (3) than either premise, but I want to understand precisely what mistake licensed that step, not merely be told the conclusion is false. And if the mistake is in (1) or (2) after all, I want to know what it costs me to admit that, since those two claims are doing all my thinking about why my new country works as well as it does.
