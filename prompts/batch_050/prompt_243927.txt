Last month at a family dinner my brother-in-law said Congress should be chosen by lot like a jury, and to my own surprise I didn't hate the idea -- which sent me down a rabbit hole, because I've noticed I have very strong gut feelings about when random selection and forced rotation are legitimate, and they don't seem to line up with any single rule. Here are my verdicts, take them as data: Picking jurors by lot is obviously right, and I'd be disturbed if litigants could request the "best" jurors. The Vietnam-era draft lottery by birthday struck me as deeply fair, actually fairer than the deferment system it partly replaced, even though it was pure chance deciding who bore a terrible burden. When a festival has more fans than tickets, a lottery among everyone who applied is completely fine. But when there are more patients than donor kidneys, allocating purely by lot among equally sick people feels monstrous to me, even though it's the exact same scarcity structure as the festival. Hiring a plumber by drawing names from the licensed registry is absurd; I want the one with good references, and I don't think the unchosen plumbers have been wronged. Ireland's citizens' assembly picked by lot seemed to work well and I approved. My HOA rotates the chair position every year and that's healthy. But if my chess club imposed term limits on its secretary I'd call it bureaucratic overkill. Term limits for presidents: good, obviously. Career civil servants who spend forty years in an agency: fine, even valuable. Career politicians who spend forty years in office: vaguely disgusting, and yet both are long-tenured experts in the machinery of government, so what exactly is the difference?

What I want is the principle, or small set of principles, that best captures these verdicts. I've tried some candidates myself and they all fail somewhere. "Lotteries are fine for burdens but not benefits" fails on the festival tickets. "Lotteries are fine when judgment can't rank the candidates" fails on the kidneys, where arguably the patients really can't be ranked and yet I still balk. "Rotation is good when power corrupts" seems to explain the president and the HOA but then can't explain why the chess club feels different, and it definately can't explain why career civil servants get a pass but career politicians don't. Maybe my kidney verdict is just squeamishness I should drop, or maybe it's tracking something real about who is vulnerable to whom. State your best candidate principle precisely, tell me which clause is doing which work against which case, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save -- I'd rather keep a clean principle and lose a verdict than adopt a patchwork. And say what it predicts for cases I haven't considered: term limits for supreme court justices, random assignment of cases to judges, lotteries for oversubscribed PhD programs among applicants above some threshold, and randomly rotating who moderates an online forum.
