My discrete math prof explained the axiom of choice with Russell's shoes-and-socks picture: given infinitely many pairs of shoes you can pick one from each pair by a rule (always take the left), but with infinitely many pairs of identical socks there's no rule, so you need a special axiom saying a selection exists anyway. He used this to argue the axiom is obviously true -- of course the selection exists, the socks are all right there! A friend who does set theory says the analogy actually undersells how strange choice is and also misleads people about what the axiom even claims. Can you audit the analogy for me: what exactly maps onto what, where does the mapping break down (my friend mentioned something about choosing being an activity vs. a set existing, which I didn't follow), and does my prof's "obviously true" verdict survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Also curious whether the socks picture explains why some choice-requiring results, like every vector space having a basis, feel so much weirder than the sock case suggests.
