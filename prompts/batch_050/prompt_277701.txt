So my younger brother came out to the family at Thanksgiving, which went fine except my uncle (who is a cop and cannot help himself) made some crack about how this stuff used to be a hanging offense. Anyway that sent me down a rabbit hole and I found out Jeremy Bentham wrote a whole essay around 1785 arguing against sodomy laws, called "Offences Against One's Self" (sometimes "Paederasty"), which he never dared publish. I read it and I can't stop thinking about this bit where he's tearing into Blackstone for classifying the act as an offense against "security":

"He does not say whose security, for the law makes no distinction in its ordinances, so neither does this lawyer or any other English lawyer in his comments make any distinction between this kind of filthiness when committed with the consent of the patient and the same kind of filthiness when committed against his consent and by violence. It is just as if a man were to make no distinction between concubinage and rape."

I've been trying to nail down what I actually believe about criminal law, partly because of family dinners and partly because it turns out I was coasting on vibes. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. The only thing that can justify punishment is that it prevents more suffering than it causes. Mere public antipathy toward a practice is not a reason to punish it, full stop.
2. Bentham is exactly right in that passage: consent is the thing that makes the moral difference, and a law that lumps the consensual act in with the violent one has made a category mistake, not just a sentencing error.
3. That said, I'd still keep some fully consensual private acts criminal. A consensual duel to the death, or that German case where a man killed and ate a willing volunteer — I want those to be crimes even with documented, sober, enthusiastic consent.
4. I'm with Bentham that pleasures and pains all count the same no matter whose they are or how base we find them. Pushpin is as good as poetry; the law doesn't get to discount a satisfaction because it's prejudiced or nasty.
5. Self-regarding imprudence isn't the law's business, which is why I think drug prohibition is wrong — but I'm fine with mandatory seatbelt laws and don't feel bad about it.

So: does this hang together? Go through it pair by pair if that's useful, tell me which tensions are real and which only look real, and rank the real ones by severity. For each real one, what's the cheapest revision and what does it cost me? And on the passage specifically — I take it to support (2), but tell me if I'm overreading it: is Bentham saying consent is *sufficient* to get the law out, or only that the law has to mark the distinction? Also if Bentham's own essay contains a resource for fixing one of my tensions (I have a suspicion it does, re: (3)), point me to the move rather than doing it for me. Don't tell me which commitment *you'd* drop first — actually no, do tell me, but only after the audit.
