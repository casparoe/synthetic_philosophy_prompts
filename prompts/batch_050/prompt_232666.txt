After forty years as a practicing biochemist, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy of science, and I keep returning to Daniel Dennett's distinction between cranes and skyhooks -- explanations of complexity that build upward from comprehensible, ground-level mechanisms, versus explanations that invoke some miraculous cause hanging unsupported from above -- and to his related complaint that some reductionism is greedy, skipping levels of organization that do genuine explanatory work. The distinction strikes me as exactly right in spirit, and exactly vague in application. I raised this at my reading group last month and we foundered on a simple question: when a neuroscientist proposes to explain moral judgment entirely in terms of amygdala activation, or when a sociobiologist proposes to explain altruism entirely in terms of gene fitness, what principled test tells us whether we are looking at a perfectly good crane or at greedy reductionism? Intuitions at the table ran in opposite directions, and each side accused the other of smuggling in a skyhook.

What I would like, then, is a specification before any verdicts: the desiderata that an adequate criterion demarcating legitimate reductive explanation from greedy reductionism would have to satisfy. Please give me the requirements list, with the case or consideration motivating each. It should say which historical episodes the criterion must classify correctly (for instance, the replacement of vitalism by molecular mechanisms presumably has to come out as a success rather than an overreach), which distinctions it may not collapse (I am thinking of ontological versus explanatory reduction, of explaining a phenomenon versus explaining it away, and of whether denying that a level is fundamental is the same as denying that it is real), and which established results from the sciences it cannot contradict without disqualifying itself. For each requirement, indicate whether you take it to be genuinely binding or merely the prejudice of one camp -- my physicist friends, I notice, treat every skipped level as a crime, while my old laboratory colleagues treat every complaint about reductionism as nostalgia for skyhooks, and I suspect both are guilty of loading the desiderata in advance.

Two further requests. First, if any subset of the desiderata you list is jointly unsatisfiable -- if, say, the requirement that the criterion deliver firm verdicts on contested cases like consciousness or free will conflicts with the requirement that it rest only on considerations both camps accept -- please say so explicitly, since a list that cannot be jointly met would itself be an interesting result. Second, once the list is fixed, assess briefly against it the few candidate criteria one might actually propose (for example: a reduction is greedy when the skipped level has predictive power the lower-level account lacks; or when the lower-level story is promissory rather than delivered; or when the explanandum at the higher level simply disappears rather than being recovered). Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring, and identify which single requirement, in your judgment, excludes the most plausible candidates.
