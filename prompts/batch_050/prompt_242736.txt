I work for a county child welfare office and our board keeps getting pulled into fights about whether some parenting decision crosses the line from "weird but legal" into "the state should step in." Not abuse cases -- those are covered. The gray zone stuff: parents refusing a cochlear implant because they're Deaf and want a Deaf kid, a family raising the kid in a separatist religious community with no schooling past 14, vegan diets, refusing psychiatric meds, one family that wants to raise their kid speaking only a constructed language so he'll be "unbound by convention" (this one is real and it broke our last meeting).

The framework we keep circling is Feinberg's thing about the child's right to an open future -- the idea that some of the kid's rights are held in trust until they're an adult, so parents can shape a life but can't close off the option of leaving it. I want that turned into an actual procedure my caseworkers can run in maybe 20 minutes per case, because they have like 40 of these a quarter and no philosophy background.

So: give me the steps in order -- what questions to ask about the decision, what evidence to gather, what thresholds to apply. The part I care most about is which distinctions have to survive the simplification. Like I assume "closes an option now" vs "makes an option harder to choose later" has to survive, and "irreversible" vs "very expensive to reverse" probably matters, but tell me which ones you think are load-bearing and which we can drop without the procedure falling apart. A flowchart in words or a numbered test sequence is fine.

Requirements: it has to be symmetric across majority and minority cultures (our lawyers insist, and honestly I do too), it has to handle the cochlear implant case and the constructed-language case as worked examples, and include at least one case where the procedure spits out an answer I won't like -- either letting something through that feels wrong or blocking something that feels fine -- and say which.

Also be honest about where the procedure just can't decide and a caseworker has to use judgment, and what the right to an open future was never going to settle in the first place (I've heard people stretch it to cover private school choice and that seems like a reach).

Don't give me a menu of positions where I pick my favorite -- commit. Take a definite stand on where the line is, what the tests are, and defend it. If you think the whole open-future framing is the wrong tool for some of my cases, say that too and say what you'd use instead. American English please, and keep the jargon low -- caseworkers, not grad students.
