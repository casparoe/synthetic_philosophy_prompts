Over coffee, two colleagues of mine — Daniel, a philosopher of science, and Mara, an evolutionary biologist — fell into an argument about whether natural selection is a tautology, and I have been asked to referee.

(1) Daniel opened with the classic objection: 'the fittest' are simply those that survive and reproduce, so 'survival of the fittest' reduces to 'the survival of the survivors' — true by definition, hence unfalsifiable, hence not science. He noted that Popper himself once concluded that Darwinism was not a testable scientific theory but something closer to a metaphysical framework for generating research.

(2) Mara replied on two fronts. First, Popper retracted that verdict late in his career, conceding that natural selection was testable after all. Second, the circularity charge confuses a slogan with the theory: fitness is defined as expected reproductive success, a propensity an organism possesses before it survives or reproduces, so the claim that fitter variants increase in frequency is empirical rather than definitional.

(3) Daniel answered that propensities change nothing: expected reproductive success is only ever estimated from actual survival and actual reproduction, so the allegedly independent definition collapses back into the outcome it was meant to explain. Worse, any result whatever can be accommodated after the fact by a suitably inventive story about selection pressures — which is precisely what unfalsifiable theories do.

(4) Mara conceded that post hoc storytelling is a real vice in her field but insisted the theory makes genuinely risky predictions: the direction of trait change under known selection pressures (resistance evolving under antibiotics, never against them), Fisher-style arguments for near-equal sex ratios, and the absence of adaptations that benefit the group at a net cost to their bearers. A theory that can turn out to be wrong about such things is no tautology.

(5) Daniel's final move: those predictions issue from auxiliary models grafted onto the core principle, and a tautology plus sufficiently flexible auxiliary assumptions will 'predict' anything one likes. The core principle remains empty even if the models built around it are not.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — say who is right at each step, and why — and then say what, if anything, both parties are missing about what kind of claim the principle of natural selection actually is.
