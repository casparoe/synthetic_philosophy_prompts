So, quick background: I'm not an academic at all, I fix boilers for a living, but a few years ago I realised basically every old philosophy book is free online and I've been slowly working through Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding on my phone ever since. In Book II, chapter 9, he brings up this question his friend Molyneux sent him in a letter — whether a man born blind, who's learned to tell a cube from a sphere by touch, could tell which is which the very first time he sees them. That question bit is apparently famous, but its what Locke says right after, in his own voice, that I can't stop thinking about:

"I agree with this thinking gentleman, whom I am proud to call my friend, in his answer to this problem; and am of opinion that the blind man, at first sight, would not be able with certainty to say which was the globe, which the cube, whilst he only saw them; though he could unerringly name them by his touch, and certainly distinguish them by the difference of their figures felt."

Two things nag me here. One, he sounds extremely sure of himself for someone answering from an armchair with zero actual cases to go on. Two, look at that little phrase "at first sight." He's quietly conceding the man WILL get it eventually — the whole question is just the first instant. But how long is "at first"? Ten seconds? A fortnight? And what would the figuring-out even feel like from the inside?

So here's what I want: a short story that stress-tests Locke's answer instead of just illustrating it. Rules:

- Don't use the literal setup (blind man, eye surgery, cube and sphere on a table). Transpose it to some other pairing of two channels for knowing the same shapes, where a character is a genuine expert through one channel and then suddenly gets the other — a sonar operator seeing camera footage for the first time, a gamer who's only ever known a world through a haptic suit, whatever you like. But it has to recognisably be the SAME problem: mastery in one channel, sudden access to the other, then a cold identification test.
- There must be an actual test scene where the character has to commit and say which is which. The outcome has to stay genuinely arguable — I want to finish the story and still be able to argue either that Locke was right or that he was wrong, using details you planted. No winking at the reader about which side you're on.
- Somewhere, the story has to cash out what "at first" means in your setup: how much time passes, how many tries it takes, and what specifically changes for the character. Don't let it stay vague the way Locke leaves it.
- Nobody in the story may say "Locke" or "Molyneux," and no lecture-y dialogue about knowledge or the senses. It has to work as a story first.

Format, in this order:
1. The story — roughly 900–1,200 words.
2. The choices I made — about 200–300 words explaining how your transposition maps onto the touch/sight pairing, which words in the passage you leaned hardest on (I'd especially like to know what you did with "at first sight" and "with certainty"), and where you had to simplify or cheat the problem to make it fiction.
