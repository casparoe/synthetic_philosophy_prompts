After forty years in physical chemistry, I have spent my retirement working through metaphysics with a reading circle of ten adults — mixed backgrounds, no formal training in philosophy, meeting monthly for ninety minutes. I am to lead our next session, on the unity of the proposition and the regress F. H. Bradley presses in Appearance and Reality: if a proposition consists of a relation joining its terms, then something further must join the relation to the terms, and so on without end; a mere list of constituents, however complete, is not a unity that says anything.

I will confess my stake in the session. My instinct, carried over from the laboratory, is that propositional constituents have something like valencies: a predicate is unsaturated, with an open site, and a name slots into it the way a ligand binds a metal centre, unity being achieved once every site is occupied. I suspect this picture merely relabels the difficulty — the binding site is one more constituent that itself needs attaching — but I have failed to make the circle feel that suspicion rather than simply hear me assert it. I want an activity that does the feeling for me.

Please design the session accordingly, with these requirements:

1. The regress must happen to the participants before anyone names it. The rules and materials (cards, tokens, string, whatever you judge best) should allow the group to converge spontaneously on some connector-and-slot solution, then corner them into needing a connector for the connector, until someone proposes higher-order connectors and the room must decide whether an infinite tower of attachments is a solution or a restatement of the problem.

2. Give me concrete roles or rules, a timeline for the ninety minutes, and at least one forced choice the group must make collectively — not a poll of opinions but a decision the activity's own logic compels.

3. For the debrief, map specific moments of the activity onto the standard moves: Bradley's regress itself, Frege's doctrine of the unsaturatedness of concepts, the option of taking facts rather than constituents as the true primitives, and the quietist reply that the demand for a binder was malformed from the start. Tell me which exit the activity's likely resting place most resembles, since I should not let the group mistake an artefact of the rules for a philosophical result.

4. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong when I run this: the slide from propositions to sentences, with grammar offered as the answer; the member with set theory who declares the ordered tuple settles it; the one who is perfectly content with an actual infinity of bindings. For each, give me a way to respond that does not collapse into lecturing.

5. Close with a candid note on what the activity cannot reproduce. I expect the gap between arranging physical tokens and unifying senses is precisely where my valency picture will look better than it deserves, and I would rather know in advance what I must supply in discussion than discover it on the night.

No reading list or citations are needed; the circle works from first principles, and I would not be able to verify references in any case.
