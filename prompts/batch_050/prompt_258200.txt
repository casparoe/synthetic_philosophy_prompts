Daniel Dennett has proposed that the self is best understood on the model of a center of gravity. A center of gravity is not a part of an object, not made of anything, and not detectable by any instrument that probes the object's interior; yet it is indispensable for predicting and explaining how the object moves. Likewise, Dennett suggests, a self is not a part of a person, not located anywhere in the brain, and not the sort of thing one could find by introspection or neuroscience; it is an abstractum that earns its keep by organizing our predictions and explanations of what a person will do and say. He adds a narrative twist: the self is the fictional protagonist of the autobiographical story each human being ceaselessly spins, so that asking which of a person's competing self-descriptions picks out the real self is like asking which character in a novel is the real one. Dennett deploys this analogy to license a deflationary conclusion: puzzles about where the self really resides, whether it survives various transformations, or which of several candidate selves is genuine, are to be dissolved rather than solved, just as it would be confused to ask what a center of gravity is made of or to search for it with a microscope.

Please audit this analogy rigorously. First, give the correspondence table: what plays the role of the mass distribution, of the center itself, of the object's trajectory, of the physicist who calculates it? Second, identify the disanalogies, and separate those that matter for Dennett's intended use from those that do not. Consider at least the following: a center of gravity is uniquely and precisely determined by the mass distribution, whereas selfhood seems interpretively indeterminate; a center of gravity is computed by an external observer, whereas a self seems to be narrated by the very system it characterizes, raising the question of who or what the narrator is; and centers of gravity figure in strict laws, whereas self-attributions figure in loose, revisable folk-psychological generalizations. Third, say what the analogy quietly imports: does it presuppose an instrumentalist or pattern-realist view of theoretical posits, and would someone with a different philosophy of science be entitled to reject the conclusion while accepting the physics? Finally, adjudicate the upshot: does the deflationary conclusion survive the audit, and do the disanalogies weaken it or, perversely, strengthen it, on the ground that interpretive indeterminacy is itself an argument against a robust self? If the center-of-gravity comparison ultimately misleads, propose a better analogy for whatever is right in Dennett's picture, and specify exactly which philosophical work your replacement can and cannot do.
