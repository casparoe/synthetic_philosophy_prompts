Two colleagues and I have spent three meetings circling Newcomb's problem, and I want an outside verdict in quantitative form rather than another restatement of the standard positions.

The classic setup, as we understand it: a highly reliable predictor has placed either nothing or a large sum in an opaque box, depending on whether it predicted you would take only that box or also a transparent box containing a smaller, guaranteed sum. The boxes are already filled; you now choose. One colleague says rationality requires taking only the opaque box; the other says that whatever the predictor did is fixed, so taking both dominates, and no rational agent leaves guaranteed money on the table.

To keep this from being the stock debate, we added a wrinkle. In our variant, the predictor does not simulate you or scan your psychology in any fine-grained way. It bases its prediction on a coarse dispositional proxy: whether, in an unrelated situation weeks earlier, you kept a costly promise to a stranger when no one would have known otherwise. The correlation between that behavior and one-boxing is real but modest. Our question: does the case for one-boxing survive, weaken, or collapse when the prediction rests on a noisy proxy for a general disposition rather than on something like a near-perfect replica of the deliberation you are now conducting?

Please answer with the following sections, in this order:

1. **Your numbers** (roughly 50 words): your credence that one-boxing is the rational choice (a) in the classic problem and (b) in our proxy variant.
2. **Decomposition** (roughly 300 words): break each credence into the sub-questions it depends on, with a rough probability and one or two lines of justification for each. At minimum, address: whether causal decision theory is the correct norm for an ideally rational agent; whether, granting that norm, two-boxing follows in the classic problem; whether the dispute is partly verbal, trading on an ambiguity in "rational" between evaluating the act and evaluating the disposition that produces it; and, for the variant, how much predictive accuracy the one-boxer's case actually needs.
3. **Combination and sensitivity** (roughly 120 words): show how the sub-probabilities combine into your headline numbers, and identify the single term each number is most sensitive to.
4. **Locating our disagreement** (roughly 120 words): my credence for one-boxing in the classic problem is about 0.7; my colleague's is about 0.15. Using your decomposition, say which sub-question(s) our disagreement most plausibly lives in, and whether one of us is making an identifiable error rather than merely weighting things differently.
5. **What would move you** (roughly 80 words): for your least confident term, state what argument or evidence would shift it most.

One request throughout: clearly separate what is broadly settled among people who work on decision theory (for example, what causal decision theory actually recommends) from what is genuinely contested (for example, whether it is the right theory), and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
