Last night I spent four hours arguing with a friend about the paradox of hedonism and walked away convinced his argument proves far too much, though I cannot say exactly where. I would like a careful diagnosis. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher with expertise in ethics and practical rationality. Think carefully and slowly; before committing to an answer, spend a short paragraph brainstorming which steps look suspicious and why, and only then give your final verdict.

His argument runs as follows:

(1) If directly aiming at some end X reliably frustrates the achievement of X, then rationality requires achieving X indirectly, by pursuing other ends instead.

(2) Directly aiming at one's own happiness reliably frustrates it: the person who monitors how happy they are, and chooses activities by asking which will make them happiest, ends up less happy than the person absorbed in something else. This is the paradox of hedonism, and we both accept it as roughly true.

(3) Therefore rationality requires achieving happiness indirectly: one should pursue things like friendship, meaningful work, and craft for their own sake, letting happiness arrive as a byproduct.

(4) But one cannot pursue an end "for its own sake" while knowing that one pursues it as an instrument to something else. Wholehearted absorption in friendship or work requires regarding it as a final end, not as a means. The moment you see the friendship as your strategy for becoming happy, the absorption that was supposed to produce the happiness is gone.

(5) Any reflective person who accepts (2) and (3) knows that their pursuit of these other ends is instrumental. That is what it is to act on a reason you are aware of.

(6) So a reflective person cannot do the very thing (3) requires of them.

(7) So a reflective person cannot be happy: reflection itself is disqualifying.

His conclusion strikes me as absurd, and it seems to prove too much in a specific way: if he is right, then the standard advice the paradox of hedonism is taken to support -- stop chasing pleasure, lose yourself in projects and people -- is advice no one who understands it can follow. The cure would be exactly as self-defeating as the disease. Worse, the argument would seem to generalize to any good that requires unselfconscious engagement, which is most of them, so the lesson would be that self-knowledge is a catastrophe. I refuse to believe that.

For the record, I would prefer to keep (2), since the psychological observation seems plainly right, and I would strongly prefer not to conclude that reflection ruins happiness. But I want the honest answer, not the comfortable one.

Second, explain why that step looked compelling, since my friend is not stupid and the argument persuaded several people in the room. Third, price the alternatives: for each other premise one might reject instead, state what rejecting it would cost me, given my stated preferences. Finally, tell me what position your diagnosis leaves me in -- can a fully reflective person, aware of everything in this paragraph, still rationally pursue happiness, and if so, what does their psychology have to look like?
