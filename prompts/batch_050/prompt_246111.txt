I'm on the research ethics committee at a teaching hospital and we're stuck on what sounds like a daft little question but has split us right down the middle. There's a proposed trial of a new sedative that reliably produces complete short-term amnesia -- patients given it form no memories for a stretch of about twelve hours and then recover fine (it's a close relative of drugs already used routinely, the safety profile isn't the issue). The researchers want to study how people re-orient after total amnesia, so here's the protocol: on Sunday evening each participant goes to sleep in the ward, and the researchers flip a fair coin. If it lands heads, the participant is woken once, on Monday morning, interviewed for an hour, and the study ends for them. If it lands tails, they're woken on Monday morning, interviewed, given the sedative again so they remember nothing of it, and woken again on Tuesday morning for the same interview. The participant knows all of this in advance. The key feature is that whenever a participant is woken, they genuinely cannot tell whether it's Monday or Tuesday -- a Tuesday waking feels exactly like a first waking.

The fight is over one sentence in the consent form. The draft currently says: "If you are woken during the study, the probability that you are in the group who will be woken twice is one in two, since that's the probability of the coin landing tails." Half the committee thinks this is obviously right -- it's a fair coin, 50/50, done, and anything else would be misleading. The other half (led by a statistician who's been insufferable about it all month) says the honest answer is two thirds: put yourself in the woken participant's shoes, there are three equally plausible situations you might be in (heads-Monday, tails-Monday, tails-Tuesday), and in two of them you're in the twice-woken group, so from where you actually sit the probability is 2/3, and consenting on the basis of 1/2 means consenting on the basis of a number that's wrong relative to your own epistemic position. Someone on the committee mentioned this is basically a dressed-up version of some philosophy problem about a sleeping princess or something, which is not helping the statistician's case with the clinicians.

Draft the written opinion I said I'd circulate before Friday's meeting: recommend which figure (or what alternative phrasing) should go in the consent form, and give the committee the actual reasoning, not just a verdict. Please take the losing side's strongest point seriously rather than waving it away -- whichever way you come down, one of these two groups has a real argument and I don't want a memo that pretends otherwise. If you think the whole dispute is a confusion about what "probability" means here and neither number is straightforwardly right or wrong, that's also a legitimate bottom line, but say so plainly and tell us what the form should say instead. Keep it to the length of something a busy committee would actually read, and write it in British English.
