I’m a Year 12 student giving a twelve-minute presentation at my school’s politics and history symposium on whether constitutional monarchy can be justified in a democracy. My thesis is not simply that monarchy is harmless because modern monarchs rarely intervene. I’m defending a more specific position: a monarchy is democratically legitimate only when the public understands the Crown as an expendable constitutional office rather than as the political property of a particular family. On my view, hereditary succession cannot itself create legitimacy; at most, it can be justified as a device that makes succession automatic, keeps the office separate from electoral competition, and prevents the head of state from claiming a personal political mandate. I use the United Kingdom, the Scandinavian monarchies, and Japan as contrasting models, but the talk is mainly about the underlying principles rather than their detailed histories.

My argument has three tests. First, democratic ownership: citizens must be able in principle to redefine or abolish the institution through ordinary democratic procedures. Second, office before person: the monarch’s status, funding, and privileges must attach to a publicly defined role rather than to any supposed superior worth of the family. Third, constrained usefulness: any remaining constitutional functions must either be governed by clear rules or be so limited by convention that the monarch cannot plausibly use them to pursue an independent political agenda. I call this position the expendable Crown: the institution is justified only if it remains useful to democracy while being fully subordinate to it.

The audience will include our politics teacher, who cares about constitutional design and democratic equality; our history teacher, who is likely to challenge tidy principles with awkward historical examples; an economics teacher who approaches institutions through incentives and rent-seeking; and about forty students. Several students are committed republicans, several think monarchy is an essentially harmless tradition, and a few will object that my position is too abstract because it ignores national identity and social continuity.

Prepare a Q&A sheet for me. Give me the nine objections I am most likely to receive from this particular audience, ordered by both likelihood and potential damage. Phrase each objection the way that person would actually ask it, rather than as a generic philosophy question. For each one, identify who is most likely to raise it, explain the strongest version of the concern, give me a reply of about ninety seconds, state what I should concede immediately, identify what I must not concede, and flag the weakest part of my answer.

Please make sure the objections cover more than the familiar points about cost, tourism, and whether monarchs still have formal powers. I expect harder challenges about inherited status and equal citizenship, the difference between symbolic power and political power, whether an institution can be legitimate merely because the public could abolish it, whether automatic succession is really an advantage rather than an arbitrary exclusion, and whether a monarchy can remain politically neutral when its ceremonial role still expresses a judgment about national identity.

Also include the question I should hope nobody asks, explain why it is especially dangerous for my three tests, and give me the best available answer anyway. Finish with one sentence that captures the reply which must land if my overall argument is to survive. I do not need a bibliography or a list of further reading.
