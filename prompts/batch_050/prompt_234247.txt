Exam question: The passage below is from the Introduction to Francis Fukuyama's The End of History and the Last Man (1992), where he explains the argument of his famous 1989 article. In about 250 words: (a) explain the distinction Fukuyama draws between "history" and "History" and what he means by saying History can "end"; (b) state how his end state differs from Marx's; and (c) explain what role economics plays in his argument.

"And yet what I suggested had come to an end was not the occurrence of events, even large and grave events, but History: that is, history understood as a single, coherent, evolutionary process, when taking into account the experience of all peoples in all times. [...] For both of these thinkers, there was a coherent development of human societies from simple tribal ones based on slavery and subsistence agriculture, through various theocracies, monarchies, and feudal aristocracies, up through modern liberal democracy and technologically driven capitalism. This evolutionary process was neither random nor unintelligible, even if it did not proceed in a straight line, and even if it was possible to question whether man was happier or better off as a result of historical "progress."

Both Hegel and Marx believed that the evolution of human societies was not open-ended, but would end when mankind had achieved a form of society that satisfied its deepest and most fundamental longings. Both thinkers thus posited an "end of history": for Hegel this was the liberal state, while for Marx it was a communist society. This did not mean that the natural cycle of birth, life, and death would end, that important events would no longer happen, or that newspapers reporting them would cease to be published. It meant, rather, that there would be no further progress in the development of underlying principles and institutions, because all of the really big questions had been settled.

[...] its subject returns to a very old question: Whether, at the end of the twentieth century, it makes sense for us once again to speak of a coherent and directional History of mankind that will eventually lead the greater part of humanity to liberal democracy? The answer I arrive at is yes, for two separate reasons. One has to do with economics, and the other has to do with what is termed the "struggle for recognition.""

Student's answer:

Fukuyama's "history" with a small h is just events happening one after another, while "History" with a capital H is the Hegelian idea that human societies develop in a single coherent evolutionary process, from tribal slave-holding societies through theocracies and monarchies up to liberal democracy and technologically driven capitalism. When he says History can "end," he doesn't mean events stop happening — he explicitly says important events would continue and newspapers would keep publishing. He means there would be no further progress in underlying principles and institutions, because "all of the really big questions had been settled": no rival system offers better basic principles than liberty and equality, so whatever political change remains is really just implementation. On the difference from Marx: Marx thought the end state was a communist society, while for Hegel and Fukuyama it is the liberal state, so Fukuyama is essentially siding with Hegel against Marx. On economics: since Fukuyama lists economics as his first reason, the engine of History for him is basically material — capitalist development outcompetes rival systems and pushes societies toward the liberal end state as they modernize, wich is what makes the process "coherent and directional." And because History is a coherent evolutionary process, Fukuyama is committed to seeing historical progress as a smooth, linear ascent through the stages he lists, even if he admits people may not be happier at the end of it.
