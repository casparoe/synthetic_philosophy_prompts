I'm a novelist, mid-drafted on a book where one of my main characters goes through something properly horrific about two thirds of the way in, and I've been arguing with a mate of mine (who did a philosophy degree years ago and never lets you forget it) about whether an ending can "earn" the darkness you put a character through. I said some things you write can't be balanced by any amount of joy later, however lovely the final chapter. He said that's sentimental nonsense and gave me this argument, which I've been chewing on for a week because every step looks fine and the conclusion seems daft:

(1) A bad toothache can be made up for. Nobody doubts this. Enough genuine happiness afterwards -- a brilliant decade, say -- and you'd take the package, toothache included.

(2) Take any two ordeals that differ by only the tiniest notch: one extra minute of it, one degree more intense, whatever. If some quantity of happiness can balance the milder one, then some larger quantity can balance the one that's a notch worse. Happiness isn't rationed; you can always pile on more -- more years, more depth, more people enjoying it.

(3) The worst torment I could put on the page is connected to a toothache by a chain of these tiny notches. Make it a minute longer, a shade sharper, one small step at a time, and eventually you've gone from the dentist's chair to the torture chamber without ever making a jump.

(4) So working along the chain, step by step, there's some amount of happiness that balances even the worst thing. Which means, in principle, a life -- or a book, or a whole world -- containing any amount of agony whatsoever could still be a net good, provided there's enough joy piled on top. Nothing is unredeemable, it's all just quantities.

I can't accept (4), or anyway I don't want to. If it's right, then there's no difference in kind between a stubbed toe and being broken on the wheel, only a difference in how much bliss you'd need on the other side of the ledger, and it would even follow that some vast hell could be worth it if the heaven attached to it were big enough. That strikes me as the argument proving too much. But I honestly can't see which step to attack. (1) seems obvious, (3) is just a fact about how you can make things slightly worse incrementally, and (2)... (2) is where I keep staring, because it feels like the "slightly worse" phrasing is doing something sneaky, like those puzzles where you remove grains of sand one at a time -- but saying "it feels sneaky" isn't an objection.

So: which step actually goes wrong, why does it look so innocent, and what does it cost to reject it? If the honest answer is that I have to accept a hard cut-off somewhere on the chain -- a point where no amount of happiness tips the scale back -- I'd like to know how people who hold that view deal with the arbitrariness of wherever they plant the flag. And if the honest answer is that the argument is sound and I'm just squeamish, tell me that too, but I'd want the bullet properly described before I bite it.
