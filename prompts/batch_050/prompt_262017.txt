I've been auditing a graduate seminar on consciousness science, and every time Integrated Information Theory comes up, the discussion collapses into the same two camps: people who think the counterintuitive implications (conscious photodiodes, consciousness in inactive grids of logic gates, high-phi structures that seem plainly non-conscious) are fatal, and people who think any theory bold enough to be wrong in testable ways deserves credit. Both sides seem to me to be arguing at the wrong level. Before I let the seminar score IIT or its rivals, I want the standard of evaluation itself made explicit. So: write the desiderata that any mathematical, identity-style theory of consciousness must meet to count as a serious candidate theory — not just IIT, but any theory that proposes some quantity or structure that is, or constitutes, conscious experience.

The requirements list is the deliverable, so put the weight there. I want the cases and phenomena the theory has to get right (the difference between wakefulness and dreamless sleep, split-brain cases, why the cerebellum seems irrelevant to experience despite its neuron count, the binding of features into a unified scene, why experience has a definite grain and content at all), the distinctions it cannot collapse (between the neural correlates of consciousness and consciousness itself, between access to information and phenomenal character, between a measure of consciousness and a theory of it), and the constraints from neuroscience and physics it cannot contradict. For each desideratum, give the consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference, and mark whether it's non-negotiable or camp-specific — I suspect some demands people make of theories of consciousness (e.g., that it must not attribute any experience to simple systems) are intuitions dressed up as constraints, and I'd like that suspicion tested. Most importantly, flag any subset of the desiderata you think is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, whether a theory can simultaneously be derived from the phenomenological axioms of experience, remain empirically testable, and avoid panpsychism-adjacent consequences, or whether something has to give. That joint-unsatisfiability analysis is the part I most want.

Then, briefly and only at the end, score IIT itself against your list: where it clearly satisfies a desideratum (e.g., handling the cerebellum and sleep cases better than global-workspace-style accounts), where it clearly fails one (e.g., the way phi can be high in structures with no plausible experience, or the difficulty of measuring it in real brains), and where the verdict depends on a desideratum you've classified as negotiable. Structure the response in four sections, in this order: "The requirements list" (the bulk, roughly two-thirds of the response, each desideratum named, motivated, and marked non-negotiable or camp-specific), "Joint satisfiability" (roughly 200 words on which requirements pull against each other and what would have to give), "IIT against the list" (roughly 300 words, desideratum by desideratum, no hedging where the verdict is clear), and "Which requirement does the most excluding" (roughly 100 words on the single desideratum that knocks out the most contenders, IIT included).
