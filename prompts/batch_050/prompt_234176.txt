Here's where I've landed on naming and necessity, and I'd like an audit of whether the package hangs together: (1) Proper names and natural-kind terms are rigid designators, so true identity statements like "Hesperus is Phosphorus" and "water is H2O" are metaphysically necessary even though we know them only a posteriori. (2) The appearance that such identities could have been otherwise is fully explained by reference-fixing: we fix reference via contingent descriptions (the body seen in the evening sky; the potable liquid in rivers and rain), and what could have turned out otherwise is only which object or stuff satisfied the description, never the identity itself. (3) Mind-brain identities admit exactly the same treatment: "pain is C-fiber firing" would be necessary a posteriori, and its seeming contingent is explained the same way, via a reference-fixer like "the state that feels like this." (4) The contingent a priori is genuine: someone who stipulates that "one meter" names the length of a particular stick at a particular time knows a priori that the stick is one meter long then, though this is contingent. (5) De re modal knowledge about ordinary objects is generally a posteriori, which is why astronomy had to discover that Hesperus is Phosphorus. Is this set jointly tenable? I suspect (4) sits oddly with (1), and I'm not sure whether (3) really inherits the explanation I offer in (2), but I can't locate the real fault line. Give me the tensions ordered by severity, say which ones dissolve on inspection and which are real, and for each real one state the cheapest revision and what it costs -- without presuming which commitment I'd rather drop.
