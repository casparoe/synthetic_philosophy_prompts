The debate over the Knobe effect — the asymmetry whereby most people say a chairman intentionally harmed the environment as a foreseen side effect of a profit decision, yet deny that an otherwise identical chairman intentionally helped it — has now been running for over two decades, and despite hundreds of follow-up studies I cannot see that the central question has moved. The original reading was that the folk concept of intentional action is partly normative: moral evaluation enters into the very criteria by which people apply the word 'intentionally.' The main rivals relocate the asymmetry downstream: a desire to blame that colors the judgment people report; pragmatic features of the word 'intentionally' (saying the helper acted intentionally falsely suggests he aimed at the outcome); differences in the knowledge or skill attributed to the two agents; or the influence of a violated norm on how the causal role of the agent's mental states is judged. Each camp has its own positive experimental results, and each dismisses the other's studies as measuring the wrong thing.

My hypothesis is that this is no longer one disagreement but at least three, stacked under the single label of 'explaining the Knobe effect': an empirical dispute about which psychological mechanism produces the asymmetry; a conceptual dispute about what counts as part of a concept rather than a performance factor or a bias on its expression; and a methodological dispute about what lay responses to vignettes can establish about concepts in the first place — and that no experiment currently on offer could settle all three at once. I want this hypothesis tested or refuted, not merely assumed. Concretely: (i) take the strongest version you know of each major explanation and state what it actually predicts and what it is silent about; (ii) identify which pairs of explanations are genuine rivals, making different predictions about the same data, and which merely classify the same mechanism differently, for instance as constitutive of the concept versus as a distorting influence on its application; (iii) diagnose the stalemate itself — a factual disagreement awaiting better experiments, a disagreement about standards of evidence for claims about concepts, or a verbal disagreement about where to draw the line between concept and performance; and (iv) given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress — a specific experimental design, a principled criterion for concept-hood, a reframing of the question — or argue that the question as standardly posed is ill-formed and should be abandoned rather than answered.

One constraint on the form of the answer: if my three-layer hypothesis is itself confused, or if the debate has in fact advanced more than I claim, say so plainly rather than fitting your analysis to my framing. I would rather have the premise corrected than the task politely completed.
