In Grice's 1957 essay "Meaning," he argues, roughly, that a speaker non-naturally means something only when she intends her audience to form a belief partly *through recognizing that very intention*; and in "Logic and Conversation" he builds his account of conversational implicature on the assumption that the hearer can openly work out what the speaker meant from what was said plus the presumption of cooperation. I have been collecting cases that seem to fall just outside both frameworks, and I want to know whether there is an established name for them.

The pattern: a speaker gets a hearer to believe something true or false, not by saying it, and not by openly implying it, but by manufacturing what looks like involuntary evidence, so that the hearer draws the inference while believing he has caught the speaker out rather than been addressed. Three instances:

1. An employee leaves a printed draft resignation letter visible on her desk where her manager will see it, hoping he concludes she is restless and offers her a raise. If confronted, she can honestly say she told him nothing; and crucially, the manager's confidence in his conclusion depends on his *not* thinking she meant him to draw it.

2. A spokesperson, asked whether a minister will resign, gives a technically true denial but with a carefully performed hesitation, intending journalists to read the hesitation as a leak and report the opposite of what was said.

3. A guest at dinner "accidentally" mentions a prestigious award and then feigns embarrassment at having let it slip, so that the others take the achievement to be more creditable precisely because it seemed dragged out of him.

In each case the inference succeeds only because the hearer mistakes a staged signal for a symptom. This looks close to implicature but the mechanism seems inverted: Gricean implicature is transparent (the hearer is meant to see that he is meant to see it), whereas here the communicative intention must stay hidden to work. It also resembles Grice's own handkerchief-at-the-crime-scene case, which he excluded from genuine meaning, except that my cases involve speech-adjacent behaviour and are ubiquitous in ordinary social life, not just detective fiction.

Is there an established term for this configuration — deliberately engineering apparent evidence so that a hearer reaches a conclusion while not recognizing any communicative intention? If it has been treated in the pragmatics or philosophy-of-language literature, where does it sit relative to conversational implicature, to Grice's showing-versus-telling distinction, and to the literature on misleading? I am not looking for a label alone: I want to know exactly where this phenomenon's edges are — for instance, whether deliberately suppressing a visible reaction, or genuinely having a reaction one knows will be observed, fall inside or outside it — and whether the right verdict is that these speakers mean something in Grice's sense after all, or something else entirely.
