I teach a final-year jurisprudence seminar (sixteen students, one ninety-minute session) and we have just spent a fortnight on the paradoxes of legal self-reference: whether a sovereign parliament can bind its successors, whether an entrenched clause can protect itself from amendment, and what happens when a change enacted through the correct procedure collides with the rules that define that procedure. My students can state the positions fluently, but I suspect none of them feels why any of it matters — the cases remain bloodless. I would like you to design an in-class game built on the idea of Nomic (a game in which the players' moves consist largely of proposing changes to the rules themselves, so that to play is to legislate) such that the students run into these problems in play before I ever name them.

The design needs a few specific properties. Start with an initial ruleset of no more than a dozen rules, covering how turns work, how proposals are put and adopted (and by what majority), a win condition scored in points rather than anything of value, at least one entrenched rule, and a rule governing the amendment process itself. Crucially, the ruleset should be seeded so that three flashpoints arise organically by mid-session even if nobody is trying to be clever: a proposal to amend the amendment rule; two validly adopted rules that turn out to contradict one another; and a proposal that complies with the letter of every rule yet makes continued play impossible. Give me a referee role with a protocol for ruling on whether a proposal is legal — and, since that protocol should itself be amendable, tell me how to stop disputes over my rulings from consuming the session. The incentives should make self-amendment tempting rather than merely permitted, so that conservative play loses. Then give me a debrief that maps what happened onto the seminar material (parliamentary sovereignty and the binding of successors, eternity clauses, the difference between a rule's validity and its content), a candid account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and the one thing the game cannot reproduce — that real legal systems have interpreters and conventions rather than bare text — which I will have to supply in discussion myself.

Please present the initial ruleset as a table with the columns Rule no. | Text | Entrenchment status | Flashpoint it is designed to produce, and the debrief as a second table with the columns Moment in play | Rules implicated | Legal-theoretic analogue | Question to put to the room. The running order, materials, referee protocol, and likely failure modes can be given in prose before the tables.
