I am a judge, and next month I must deliver my sentencing remarks in an unusual case. The defendant, now seventy-eight, has been convicted beyond reasonable doubt of an armed robbery committed at twenty-one; the evidence is conclusive that he is the man who did it. But he now suffers from a condition that has erased his memory of the crime itself, while leaving intact his memories of being that same young man in every other respect — he remembers his twenty-first year vividly, the city, the friends, everything except that night. The courtroom will hold the victim's family, a cohort of law students, and the press. Write my remarks, roughly five minutes when spoken. They must explain, in language a grieving family and a first-year student can both follow, why I hold that sentencing this man is (or is not) sentencing the person who committed the robbery. The explanation must genuinely work through the difficulty rather than gesture at it: if continuity of memory is what makes a person the same over time, as Locke's criterion suggests, then Reid's brave-officer problem — the general who remembers the ensign's deeds but not the boy's flogging, so that identity seems to fail the transitivity it obviously has — bears directly on whether this forgetful defendant is my prisoner at all; and if memory is not the criterion, I owe the courtroom an account of what grounds identity instead, and why that account does not collapse the distinction between punishing a man and punishing his biography. You may decide the sentence as you see fit, but the remarks must commit to a position. After the speech, append a short note, not to be read aloud, stating where the remarks take a contested stand on personal identity, how confident you are in that stand, and the single point in your own reasoning you are least sure of. Close the whole response with a final line beginning "Summary:" that states the speech's core claim about the defendant's identity in one sentence.
