You are a philosopher of physics with particular expertise in the metaphysics of time and the foundations of special relativity. I would like you to adjudicate a dispute between two colleagues of mine, which took place after a departmental colloquium and which I witnessed without taking a side. Please work carefully: reconstruct the central argument before evaluating anything, canvass the strongest version of each party's position, and only then deliver verdicts. Resist the temptation to split the difference; it is entirely acceptable to find one party clearly more in the right.

Background: Elena is a physicist, Ravi a philosopher. Both accept special relativity as our best current account of spacetime structure. Their dispute concerns what, if anything, follows from it. Elena is pressing a version of the Rietdijk–Putnam argument.

Move 1 — Elena. Consider two people passing one another in a corridor at walking speed. Because they are in relative motion, their planes of simultaneity are tilted with respect to one another; at the distance of the Andromeda galaxy, the tilt between the two walkers amounts to a difference of days. So for one walker, some distant event — say, the launch of an invasion fleet from Andromeda — lies in her present, whilst for the other it lies days in his future. Now suppose the present is what is real. Then the launch is both real and not yet real. But what exists cannot turn on whether a person on Earth is strolling or standing still. So the launch is already real, settled amongst the furniture of the universe. The same reasoning, applied through chains of observers, reaches every event whatever: past, present and future are equally real, spacetime is a four-dimensional block, and the apparent passage of time is a perspectival artefact.

Move 2 — Ravi. The argument equivocates on "real". What relativity actually establishes is that simultaneity at a distance is a three-place relation: the launch is present relative to one frame and future relative to another. From "present-for-the-stroller" and "future-for-the-stander", nothing follows about being real simpliciter — just as nothing follows about left and right simpliciter from the fact that a tree is to your left and my right. Frame-relative verdicts may differ without contradiction; that is what frames are for.

Move 3 — Elena. The left-right analogy backfires. "Left of" is avowedly a relation to a perspective; existence is not. Relativise reality itself to frames and you end up with as many worlds as there are states of motion, which is not a metaphysics but an abdication of one. A single shared reality must be frame-invariant, since relativity permits no privileged frame, and the only frame-invariant totality containing every observer's present is the whole four-dimensional spacetime. Ravi's alternative also leaves him unable to say anything whatsoever about distant events from here — an odd result, given that nobody disputes that the launch occurs.

Move 4 — Ravi. Two replies. First, the argument requires a transitivity premise: that if one event is real relative to a second, and the second relative to a third, then the first is real relative to the third. Anyone who believes in becoming will simply deny that premise, and Elena has asserted rather than defended it. Secondly, even if the argument were entirely sound, it would deliver less than she advertised: it would establish that all events are equally real, but a four-dimensional block is consistent with an objective, moving present, so the conclusion that passage is merely perspectival requires a further argument she has not given.

Move 5 — Elena. A moving present is precisely what relativity rules out. An objective "now" sweeping the block would be an extended hypersurface, a privileged foliation of spacetime, and hence a privileged frame — the very thing Ravi invoked relativity to defend. So the further argument he demands is already in the room.

Please provide: (1) a premise–conclusion reconstruction of the argument as Elena intends it, flagging any suppressed premises (a brief brainstorm of candidate reconstructions before settling on one is welcome); (2) a rating of each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification apiece; (3) an overall verdict on who is more nearly right, and about what specifically; and (4) an account of what, if anything, both parties are missing or leaving unexamined.
