A friend of mine is a nurse, and her hospital recently rolled out one of those ML sepsis-prediction tools. The official setup is that the model flags patients, but a human clinician has to review and sign off before anything happens, so "a human is always in the loop," and if something goes wrong the responsibility lands on whoever signed off, or maybe on the hospital. Once I started noticing this pattern I saw it everywhere: loan officers who can technically override the algorithm but rarely do, safety drivers in self-driving cars who are expected to grab the wheel in the half-second after the system has already messed up, pilots told the autopilot is merely "advisory". Meanwhile everyone who studies this stuff knows the human in these arrangements is often basically decorative -- they can't understand the model, they've been implicitly trained to trust it, and they defer to it almost every single time (there's a whole literature on automation bias). And yet the liability keeps landing on them, occasionally on the org, and almost never on the people who actually designed and trained the system.

So here's what I want to know: what theory of moral and legal responsibility does this whole "meaningful human control" regime actually presuppose? Please genuinely reconstruct it rather than just gesture at it. Does it assume responsibility tracks control, and then just pretend the nominal control is real? Does it assume responsibility can be voluntarily assumed by signing a form, like assumption of risk? Or is it really a role-based view where responsibility attaches to a social position regardless of what the person filling it could actually have done? I'm especially interested in whether the practice is even internally consistent, since the same legal culture that makes the safety driver the responsible party will happily prosecute an individual engineer (think dieselgate) when the figurehead defense looks too cynical, and it would never accept a CEO absorbing all responsibility for technical decisions she couldn't possibly have understood. If the practice presupposes some specific view, tell me which and whether that view is defensible on its own terms; if it's compatible with several rival views, tell me what further commitments would pin it down. And finally: what would concretely have to change -- in deployment rules, liability rules, clinician/driver training -- if we swapped the implicit view for a strict capacity-and-control view, where you can only be responsible for what you genuinely understood and could realistically have influenced? Would anything worth keeping about human-in-the-loop requirements survive that swap?
