Two colleagues of mine, a deontologist and a consequentialist, have spent years arguing about constraints against killing and have agreed to settle their differences by co-authoring a single document, in the spirit of an adversarial collaboration. They have asked me to commission a first draft, and I am turning to you. Please write the joint statement they should produce.

Some background on the standoff. The deontologist holds that there is a constraint against killing an innocent person, and that this constraint binds me even in a case where my killing one innocent person is the only way to prevent five other agents from each killing an innocent person. The consequentialist replies that this is paradoxical: if five killings are worse than one, and the deontologist's own constraint is what makes killings bad, then the constraint appears to forbid me from minimizing violations of the constraint itself. The deontologist counters that constraints are not goals to be promoted but limits on what an agent may do, so there is no incoherence. The consequentialist answers that calling the constraint agent-relative relabels the puzzle rather than solving it, since it remains obscure why the victim's moral status should generate reasons that differ depending on who is positioned to act. They have also tangled over a temporal variant: whether I may kill one innocent person now to prevent my own future self from killing five, where the agent-relativity story seems strained. Each accuses the other of building the conclusion into the description of the case.

The document should contain the following, in this order.

First, a numbered list of claims both parties can sign without reservation: about the data of moral experience (for instance, whether the prohibition on killing feels different in kind from a reason to minimize deaths), about the logical structure of the paradox itself (that it arises only given certain premises, and which ones), and about what each position does and does not claim (for instance, that the consequentialist need not deny that killing feels different from failing to save, and that the deontologist need not deny that outcomes matter at all). Every claim must be one both would genuinely accept, not a vacuous formulation papering over the dispute; if a proposed agreement turns out to conceal a disagreement, move it to the next section.

Second, a statement of the remaining disagreements, each phrased neutrally enough that each party would accept the wording of its own position as fair. Where a disagreement proves on inspection to be verbal -- two descriptions of the same commitment rather than rival commitments -- say so explicitly rather than listing it as live.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one party. I suspect these include whether the constraint is best understood as protecting victims or as governing agents, whether agent-relativity is an intelligible feature of reasons or a confession of theoretical defeat, whether intuitions about the five-cases track anything other than the doing/allowing distinction the parties may share, and whether the future-self variant genuinely breaks the deontologist's standard response. Do not feel bound by my guesses; identify the cruxes you judge load-bearing.

Fourth, for each crux, a concrete case, argument, or result that both parties agree in advance would bear on it, with each party's prediction recorded beforehand: what the deontologist expects the case or argument to show, what the consequentialist expects, and what each would concede if the other turned out to be right about it. Where no imaginable result would move a party, note this, since it bears on whether the crux is genuinely empirical or conceptual.

Keep the tone of the document measured and professional throughout, and close with a brief section stating what, if anything, the drafting process itself revealed about why the two had been arguing past each other.
