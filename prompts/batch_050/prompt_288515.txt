I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm writing a piece on why people seek out art that makes them miserable. One of my colleagues is my informal case study, and I want to predict her reaction to a forthcoming film from her track record. Her verdicts so far: she loved an austere, formally masterful war film with invented characters and no redemptive arc; she praised the craft of a true-crime documentary about real murder victims but switched it off halfway, saying she had no right to be entertained by it; she was devastated, in a way she clearly valued, by a deliberately unbeautiful stage production about fictional parents losing a child; and she walked out of a glossy fictional melodrama, calling it emotionally manipulative despite admiring the performances. The new case: a restrained, critically acclaimed dramatisation of a real and recent atrocity, with invented dialogue and composite characters, which reviewers have singled out for its refusal to manipulate the audience.

Give me your best prediction of her verdict, but reach it by doing the following: identify the two or three rules that are each consistent with all four of her past verdicts; show where those rules diverge on the new film; say which rule you judge most likely to be hers and why; and name one further artwork whose expected verdict would discriminate between the rules that survive. Present this as a table with four columns: Rule (a one-sentence statement of the principle), Fit (how it accommodates each of the four verdicts), Prediction (her verdict on the new film, and with what confidence), and Test case (the artwork that would tell this rule apart from its rivals).

If you think her track record already fixes the rule uniquely, or conversely that the new film is underdescribed in some respect that makes any prediction arbitrary, say so plainly rather than forcing a tidy answer.
