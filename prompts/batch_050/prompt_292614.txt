I'm a retired organic chemist and over the last year or so I've been making my way through the classics of philosophy the way I used to work through a new area of the literature, and I've landed on Pascal's Pensées, in the old Trotter translation. The bit I can't stop thinking about is in §233, the wager fragment. Everyone knows the famous part about gaining all and losing nothing, but what got me is the little exchange just before, where Pascal's imagined objector says "I blame them for having made, not this choice, but a choice; for again both he who chooses heads and he who chooses tails are equally at fault, they are both in the wrong. The true course is not to wager at all." And Pascal basically replies: "You must wager; it is not optional." As a scientist my whole training was that when the evidence isn't in, you suspend judgment and go home -- withholding assent is the honest default position, not a sin. Pascal seems to be saying that for certain questions there is no bench to stand on, that living as if the coin came up tails is itself a bet on tails.

Here's why this is nagging me. An old friend of mine from my department days has been in Alcoholics Anonymous for about fifteen years, credits it with saving his life, and I've sat in on a few open meetings over the years. AA strikes me as a huge, decades-long, real-world running of something like Pascal's machine. Newcomers -- plenty of them atheists or agnostics -- are told to pick a "higher power" of their own understanding, to act as if it exists even before they believe anything, on the theory that the practice will carry them until belief or something belief-like shows up. And there's a well-known attitude in the rooms that refusing to commit to anything beyond yourself is not a neutral stance but its own position ("self-will run riot" is their phrase, roughly), which sounds awfully like "you must wager; it is not optional." So: analyze AA as a worked example of Pascal's wager, or as a stress test of it. Do the mapping carefully -- what are the actions, what are the states of the world, what is actually being staked -- and notice where it gets tricky, because the stakes here are enormous but finite (your life, your family, your liver), not infinite, so Pascal's trump card about an infinity of happy life never gets played. And the "higher power as you understand him" move seems to dissolve the many-gods objection by design, but maybe it does so by giving up on the belief part entirely and keeping only the practice.

Then run it the other direction: what does the episode say about Pascal? My friend would say the acting-as-if part genuinely worked on him, that the practice produced something he'd now call faith, which sounds like a point for Pascal's psychology. But I want to know whether AA vindicates the "not optional" move or actually refutes it -- the agnostic member who never picks a higher power and just keeps showing up and staying sober seems to be living proof that you can decline to wager and do fine, contra Pascal. Or is that person, on a stricter reading, still wagering on their own self-sufficiency? Tell me where the mapping breaks, what Pascal would have to say about sobriety-without-belief, and whether the whole episode shows that the wager was really about practice all along and the theology was scaffolding. Don't feel obliged to make AA come out as a clean confirmation or a clean counterexample; I mostly want to know which parts of Pascal the case loads with weight and which parts it quietly leaves untouched.
