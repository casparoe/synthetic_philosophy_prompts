I am a novelist researching a book set between 1968 and 1975 in the overlapping worlds of an American psychiatric hospital and the gay-liberation movement. One character has been hospitalized under a diagnosis of homosexuality; another is a junior psychiatrist observing the professional dispute that led the American Psychiatric Association in 1973 to remove homosexuality itself from the DSM while retaining a diagnosis for people distressed by their orientation. I do not need a history of the episode or a bibliography. I need a disciplined analysis of it through Ian Hacking’s idea of looping effects: the claim that classifying people can change how the classified understand and conduct themselves, and that those changes can in turn alter the classification and the evidence supporting it.

Map the episode carefully. Identify who is classifying whom, what the diagnosis does clinically and socially, and where awareness, conformity, resistance, collective organization, and professional revision might form a feedback loop. I have been distinguishing three possible loops: a downward loop in which patients internalize or enact the diagnosis; an upward loop in which classified people organize and contest the experts’ description; and a rebound loop in which the revised diagnosis creates new identities and new borderline cases. Are these genuinely distinct mechanisms, or have I divided one process arbitrarily? Also address what the episode may show against a strong looping account: the removal was driven partly by empirical criticism, changing views of distress and impairment, and moral-political argument, not simply by patients behaving differently. Where does Hacking illuminate the episode, where is his framework too vague to discriminate among causes, and would this case confirm, complicate, or restrict the theory? End by drawing out one consequence I can use in characterization without reducing either psychiatrists or activists to symptoms of a concept.
