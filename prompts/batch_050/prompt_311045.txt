I'm in the second year of a politics degree and something from a seminar last week is still bugging me. We were discussing AI, automation and whether current tech change is going to reshape class relations, and someone shut the whole discussion down by bringing up Marx — that famous line of his about how the hand-mill gives you society with the feudal lord and the steam-mill society with the industrial capitalist (I believe it's from The Poverty of Philosophy, 1847?). Their point was basically: Marx thought technology mechanically determines the form of society, he was a crude technological determinist, that's been discredited, so there's nothing in Marx worth bothering with on AI. Case closed, apparantly.

The annoying thing is I've also seen the exact same line do the oposite work. Tech-optimist types quote it approvingly to mean that new technology remakes society whether anyone likes it or not, so resistance is futile, regulation is a waste of time, and we should all just adapt. Same sentence — one side uses it to dismiss Marx, the other uses it to dismiss politics.

So I'd like you to do a proper job of disambiguating it. What are the distinct precise claims this one-liner could actually be making? Lay them out as a list, roughly weakest to strongest — everything from "technology and social arrangements tend to go together" up to full-blown "the current state of technology fixes the relations of production, which fix everything else about society". For each reading, tell me: is it a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or just false? Which readings are plausible things Marx himself held, given that he also wrote endlessly about class struggle, workers resisting the introduction of machinery, political organisation and so on — none of which sits obviously well with the strongest reading? And then the payoff: which reading does the "Marx was a crude determinist, nothing to learn here" dismissal actually need in order to work, and does his wider writing support that reading or undermine it? Same question for the "resistance is futile" tech-optimist use.

Also, where does the line itself sit in the argument of the book? I have a vague sense it was aimed at Proudhon and was making a methodological point rather than issuing a prophecy, but I'm not sure.

One more thing: please be careful to separate what's basically settled among people who actually study this stuff (e.g. whether serious scholars still read Marx as a simple technological determinist) from what's genuinely contested (e.g. how much causal primacy the productive forces are meant to have, whether the functional interpretation of his theory of history holds up). Don't present a contested interpretation as if it were the scholarly consensus.

British English please.
