I've been keeping a record of when I actually find things funny, and I'd like to know whether there is a single principle behind my verdicts — or, failing that, which of my verdicts I should give up. Here are the cases, with my honest reactions:

1. A friend deliberately trips over an obviously placed banana peel in a sketch we're filming. Funny.
2. A friend genuinely trips, is unhurt, and bursts out laughing at herself. Funny.
3. A friend genuinely trips, is unhurt, but is visibly mortified and on the verge of tears. Not funny at all, even though the physical event was identical to case 2.
4. An online video of a total stranger tripping, unhurt; I know nothing about how she felt. Funny.
5. I'm later told the video in case 4 was staged with an actor. Still funny, perhaps slightly less.
6. A joke mocking a brutal dictator's vanity. Funny.
7. A structurally identical joke mocking an unpopular classmate's stutter. Not funny.
8. A close friend tickles me and I laugh helplessly; I try to reproduce the exact sensation on myself and feel nothing comic at all.
9. A pun exploiting an ambiguous word. I groan, yet I concede it is funny — "funny-bad," but funny.
10. A pure nonsense punchline with no discernible connection to the setup. Not funny. But an anti-joke ("Why did the chicken cross the road? To get to the other side.") is funny.
11. A joke about a recent local tragedy. Not funny. A joke about the Black Death, similar in structure. Funny.
12. My own most embarrassing adolescent story: funny when I tell it at a dinner party, excruciating when my sibling tells it at the same dinner party.

Here is what I want. First, state precisely the best candidate principle — one that delivers as many of these verdicts as possible. I'm familiar with the standard families (superiority, incongruity, relief, and benign violation theories), so please say which family your principle belongs to or how it modifies its nearest relative; in particular, cases 2 through 5 seem to put pressure on any version of "benign" that is fixed by the victim's welfare or by objective harmlessness rather than by something else, and I'd like that "something else" spelled out. Second, be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate — I suspect 3 and 4 are in tension with each other, and that 9 sits awkwardly next to 10 — and tell me which verdict you'd counsel me to revise if I want the simplest defensible theory. Third, tell me what your principle predicts about two cases I haven't listed: (a) the pratfall from case 3 shown again at a reunion twenty years later, with the friend now laughing about it herself; and (b) the joke from case 6 told word-for-word by one of the dictator's cronies rather than by one of his critics. Finally, name one further case that would best distinguish your favored principle from its closest rival.
