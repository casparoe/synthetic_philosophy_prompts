So I work at a company that makes a budgeting app, and we're building a feature where users can lock money away from themselves — like you set a rule "don't let me spend more than $X on takeout" and then the app refuses to let you change it for 30 days even if you beg. Classic Ulysses contract stuff, tying yourself to the mast. My PM keeps saying this obviously makes people better off because their "sober self" is making the real decision and their "craving self" would just mess things up. But I'm not so sure. It got me thinking about the bigger version of the same question: does binding your future self actually work, or does the future self just find the loophole?

So here's my actual question. Give me your credence on this claim: precommitment mechanisms — stuff like our lockbox feature, or constitutions that are deliberately hard to amend — genuinely constrain the future chooser when it counts, rather than just getting reinterpreted, evaded, or overridden the moment the future self really wants something. Say the claim is "true" if binding rules measurably change outcomes in the cases where temptation or political pressure is strongest, not just in easy cases where nobody wanted to defect anyway.

Give me a number, but more importantly decompose it. Some sub-questions I can think of: is there even a coherent "true self" doing the binding, or is that just a story we tell? Does the binding actually hold under pressure, or do people (and courts, and legislatures) always retain an escape hatch they use exactly when it matters? And does the thing you bound yourself to turn out to be what you'd actually want later — like, is the whole enterprise assuming the binder knows the future better than they do? Assign a rough probability to each of those plus whatever I'm missing, show how they combine into your final credence, and tell me which sub-question the number is most sensitive to. Also, if you had to bet on where my PM's optimism and my skepticism actually diverge, which sub-question is it probably living in?

One more thing: what evidence or argument would move your number the most? Like if you learned that supermajority amendment rules in some country got steamrolled in a crisis, or that commitment-device users save way more than control groups, which direction does that push you and by how much roughly.
