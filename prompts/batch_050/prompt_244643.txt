I'm part of a small philosophy reading group that meets in a pub, and we've developed a running game: one member, Mira, has very stable intuitions about when you can and can't come to know something on someone else's say-so, and we try to predict her verdict on new cases before she gives it. Over the past year we've collected these data points. A stranger tells her the library closes at nine: knowledge, no problem. Her doctor tells her she has high cholesterol: knowledge. A mathematician tells her a theorem has been proved, though she couldn't follow the proof if she tried: knowledge — she said that's simply how mathematical knowledge gets around. A chef tells her the sauce she is eating contains cardamom, which she cannot taste: knowledge, and she was impatient with us for even asking. But then: a film critic she trusts and has agreed with for years tells her a film she hasn't seen is a masterpiece, and Mira flatly refuses to say she now knows it's a masterpiece — she'll only say she knows the critic thinks so. A friend tells her a joke she didn't get is hilarious: same refusal. And when a moral philosopher she respects told her, without argument, that it is permissible to lie to the murderer at the door, she said she couldn't know that on his say-so either — she'd have to see for herself why it's true. Notably, she doesn't think the critic, the friend, or the philosopher is unreliable; she'd bet on all three being right. She just won't call it knowledge.

Here is the new case, which we're putting to her next week. The same moral philosopher now sits down with her and lays out his full argument for lying to the murderer: the premises, the steps, the responses to her objections. She follows every step and finds it compelling, but — and this is the stipulation that matters — her grip on the conclusion runs entirely through his presentation of it; left alone with the question beforehand, she had no view, and if you asked her to reconstruct the argument a week later she isn't confident she could. Does she now know it's permissible, and does her answer differ from the bare-testimony case?

Please give me a committed prediction of Mira's verdict, with your reasons, rather than a menu of options she might endorse. Then tell me which single principle best explains her full pattern of verdicts — whether it's something like an acquaintance principle, a fact/value split in what testimony can transmit, a view about which domains have genuine experts, or something else — and identify at least two rival principles that fit all seven data points just as well, since I suspect her pattern underdetermines the rule. Say what each rival predicts about the new case and where your preferred principle parts company with them. Finally, name the one further case — a verdict we could elicit from her — that would do the most to discriminate between your preferred principle and its strongest rival, and say briefly where you think your prediction is least secure.
