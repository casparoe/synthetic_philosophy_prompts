I'm putting together discussion materials for a reading group on archaeological method (we're a mix of grad students and enthusiastic amateurs, so the level is uneven), and I want to compile a list of the classic pitfalls in inferring past beliefs and institutions from material remains. Here's what I have so far:

(1) Hawkes's ladder of inference -- reconstructing technology and subsistence from objects is relatively tractable, social and political organization is harder, and religious belief hardest of all, because each rung requires more assumptions that the artifacts alone can't support.
(2) Formation processes -- the record isn't a snapshot of life (the so-called Pompeii premise) but the outcome of deposition, decay, looting, and later disturbance, so patterns may say more about what survived than about what happened.
(3) Burial as performance -- grave goods don't transparently encode the deceased's status, since the funeral reflects the choices, grief, and ideology of the living as much as the dead person's standing.
(4) Equifinality -- very different processes can leave identical traces, so a burned layer or clustered hearths can't on their own decide between accident, violence, ritual, or abandonment.
(5) Ritual as a residual category -- anything without an obvious practical function gets labeled religious, which tells us about the archaeologist's ignorance rather than the object's meaning.

I'd like one more pitfall to add, and it has to be genuinely different from these -- not a special case of any of them, not two of them combined, and not a rewording. For example, "sometimes we misidentify artifacts" is just (2) again, and "analogy with living peoples can mislead" is arguably a version of (5) or (1), so neither would count unless you can convince me otherwise. Ideally it should be something a field archaeologist would actually worry about rather than a purely theoretical worry, but if the best candidate is more theoretical that's fine, just say so.

Please give the response as a numbered list of exactly four items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) name the pitfall in a phrase, (2) explain it with one concrete example of the sort of evidence it applies to, (3) state in one line why it isn't covered by any of my five, and (4) give your honest verdict on whether it's a serious practical problem or a niche one.
