I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized payments company, writing with no formal philosophy background, so please forgive any imprecision in what follows. Last sprint, two senior colleagues of mine, Mara and Jonas, got into a long argument — first about a system-design decision, then about decision theory itself — and eventually asked me to referee. I found I couldn't, and I'd like a careful move-by-move adjudication.

Background: we had to choose a migration strategy for our transaction database ahead of the holiday season. Three options were on the table: (A) stay on the current system, which degrades under heavy load but has never failed catastrophically; (B) a full cutover to a new system, which is much faster and passed all our tests, but whose failure mode under conditions we can't fully simulate is a total outage; (C) a staged rollout, middling on every axis. The key unknown was peak-season traffic, which depends on a new client deal whose size we genuinely cannot estimate. Mara and Jonas agreed on the rough outcome table and agreed we had no defensible probability distribution over traffic scenarios. The argument then went:

1. Mara: precisely because we have no information about the probabilities, we should treat the scenarios as equally likely and pick the option with the best expected outcome — that's B. Absent any basis for differential weighting, uniform weighting is the only non-arbitrary choice.

2. Jonas: uniform weighting is not the absence of an assumption; it is an assumption, and a load-bearing one. Under genuine ignorance the rational rule is maximin: compare only each option's worst case and pick the option whose worst case is least bad — that's A. A total payments outage in December could end the company, and a strategy whose justification evaporates if your made-up probabilities are wrong is not a justification.

3. Mara: maximin throws away every column of the table except the worst one. It would prefer a guaranteed loss of $1,000 over a gamble with a 99% chance of a fortune and a 1% chance of losing $1,001. Taken seriously, it paralyzes: the worst case of shipping anything is catastrophic if you let imagination define the scenario space. And note that Jonas happily asserts A's worst case is "known" to be mild — exactly the kind of unjustified certainty he accuses her of.

4. Jonas: he conceded that unrestricted maximin is too strong, and restated: maximin is warranted specifically when (i) probabilities are unavailable, (ii) some option's worst case is ruinous while another's is tolerable, and (iii) the upside being forgone is merely nice to have. All three conditions hold here. He also offered a compromise rule, minimax regret: compute, for each scenario, how far each option falls short of the best option available in that scenario, and choose the option whose maximum shortfall is smallest. On our table that recommends C, and he said he'd gladly accept C.

5. Mara: minimax regret has a structural defect — the regret table depends on which options happen to be on the menu. Add a fourth option nobody would ever pick (a from-scratch rewrite by December) and the "best achievable" benchmark shifts in some scenarios, which can flip the recommendation among A, B, and C even though nothing about those three options has changed. A rule whose verdict on three options is hostage to the presence of an irrelevant fourth can't be the rational compromise. She also remarked that Jonas moving from "A is mandatory" to "I'd gladly accept C" in a single step looked less like principled retreat and more like anchoring.

6. Jonas: every decision rule needs some input; the real question is whether the recommendation is robust. B is recommended only under Mara's particular guessed distribution, whereas A and C come out ahead under wide ranges of distributions. A recommendation that survives any assignment of probabilities is the right kind of thing to bring to the CTO. Mara replied that "robust under every distribution" just is maximin again, so Jonas hadn't identified a third position — he'd re-described his own. She also mentioned this is apparently a famous dispute in political philosophy, something about Rawls and designing society from behind a veil of ignorance, but neither of them could reconstruct the details.

Please go through moves 1–6 and say who is right at each step, and why. In particular: is treating unknown probabilities as equal a genuine principle of reason or a dressed-up assumption? Does Mara's paralysis objection in 3 land against maximin as Jonas actually stated it in 4? Is her menu-dependence objection in 5 fatal to minimax regret, and is her anchoring jab fair? Is the robustness argument in 6 really distinct from maximin? And finally: who is more right overall about what we should deploy, and is there something important that both of them are missing?
