In two weeks I'm giving the ethics grand rounds at the regional transplant center in my city — an invitation I accepted before fully appreciating the room. I'll be defending a proposal from a working paper of mine: that the state should permit and itself operate a regulated market in kidneys from living vendors, with a single public purchaser offering a fixed, generous price paid partly as a lump sum and partly as an annuity; allocation of purchased kidneys through the existing waitlist under unchanged priority rules; mandatory lifetime health coverage and scheduled long-term follow-up for vendors; an independent vendor advocate and a six-month cooling-off period before nephrectomy; and one feature I added after a long argument with my sister-in-law, a dialysis nurse — a vendor's first-degree relatives receive a one-time waitlist priority credit should they ever need a transplant themselves. My argument is that this design severs the objections that sink cruder market proposals: the rich cannot buy priority because allocation is untouched, there is no race to the bottom because the monopsony fixes a high floor, and the annuity-plus-cooling-off structure is meant to defuse the charge that a desperate person will trade an organ for quick cash. I am aware the design may be doing more work than I admit.

The audience: transplant nephrologists and surgeons who follow the living-donor outcome literature and will know it better than I do; a clinical ethicist of broadly Kantian-principlist formation who sits on the advisory board of the organ procurement organization; the social worker who counsels prospective living donors and has personally screened out financially motivated offers; a patient representative who spent years on dialysis before receiving a deceased-donor graft; and a hospital administrator whose nightmares involve local television. Build me the prep sheet for the question period: the objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones I'd get at a philosophy colloquium, ordered by how likely each is to come up and how badly it hurts if it lands, phrased in the idiom the person raising it would use — the nephrologists will talk about long-term donor outcomes and vendors concealing risk factors, the social worker will talk about consent that is formally valid and substantively coerced — each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front before anyone asks, which of my design features I should be ready to sacrifice as amendments, and give me the single question I'm hoping nobody asks, with the best answer available to it.

One constraint on how you build the sheet: this topic mixes empirical claims of very different standing with genuinely normative disagreement, and I don't want to be caught presenting the former as more settled than they are. For each objection, mark whether its force depends on (i) an empirical claim that is reasonably settled among people who study donation and transplantation, (ii) an empirical claim that remains genuinely contested — for instance, whether compensation would crowd out altruistic and directed donation — or (iii) a normative commitment that no evidence will settle, and phrase my replies accordingly rather than letting me overclaim.
