I'm a graduate student in machine learning, and a remark from my philosophy-of-science seminar has been bothering me for weeks. In my methods training, a set of practices was simply drilled into me as good craft: prefer simpler models over complex ones when they fit comparably well; use cross-validation and holdout sets rather than trusting in-sample fit; penalize complexity explicitly with criteria like AIC or BIC, or with regularization terms; distrust a model that merely interpolates its training data. None of my instructors ever argued for these practices -- they were presented as obviously rational, the way one learns to control for confounders. Meanwhile, in the seminar I learned two things that seem to pull the floor out from under all of this: Hume's problem of induction (no non-circular justification exists for expecting unobserved cases to resemble observed ones), and the no-free-lunch theorems, which as I understand them show that, averaged over all possible data-generating processes, every learning algorithm performs identically -- a model-preference for simplicity does exactly as well as a preference for complexity, or for matching Tuesday's data and inverting Wednesday's.

What I want to understand is what my field's standard practices actually presuppose, philosophically. Does the near-universal preference for parsimony commit working statisticians to the substantive metaphysical claim that nature is simple -- and if so, is that claim an empirical hypothesis that could fail, an a priori principle, or something else? Or can the practices be defended on weaker grounds: for instance, that simplicity preferences don't need to track truth but only need to match the actual distribution of problems our corner of the universe throws at us, which would make the no-free-lunch result irrelevant because the average it takes is over a measure we have no reason to care about? I'm also unsure the practices are even internally consistent: cross-validation seems to presuppose that future draws resemble past draws (straight inductivism, vulnerable to Hume), while penalizing complexity seems to presuppose something about the world itself, not just about sampling -- are these the same commitment wearing two hats, or two different ones? And where a practice turns out to be compatible with several rival philosophical accounts, I'd like to know that too: I want to distinguish what my training strictly committed me to from what it merely sits comfortably with, and what would have to change in day-to-day statistical practice if, say, one fully accepted that simplicity is just a bias with no cosmic endorsement.
