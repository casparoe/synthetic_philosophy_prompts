So my roommate and I got into it last night over the blackmail paradox and he hit me with an argument that I'm pretty sure is broken but I couldn't find the crack in it, and it was still bugging me at work today. His thing goes like this:

(1) If I find out my coworker is cheating on his wife, it's perfectly legal (and maybe even morally fine) for me to just never tell anyone.
(2) It would also be legal for me to tell his wife, or to tell literally everyone, since it's true and I obtained it legally.
(3) If each of two options is permissible on its own, then it's permissible to present someone with a choice between those two options.
(4) It's permissible to charge money for doing something you're permitted to do -- that's just selling a service, and people sign NDAs and get paid for silence all the time.
(5) So offering to stay quiet about the affair in exchange for $5,000 is just selling a service I'm permitted to provide.
(6) Therefore blackmail is at worst tacky, and making it a crime is indefensible.

The reason this freaks me out is that the same machinery seems to legalize basically every conditional threat built out of legal parts. "Pay me or I'll show these photos to your boss" -- showing them is legal, staying silent is legal, so apparently that's a service now. It feels like the argument proves way too much, but when I pushed on it I kept flailing between like four different steps.

Tell me which premise or step to reject, and then do the honest accounting: why does that step look totally fine when you first read it, and what does it cost to reject it? Like, if the problem is with (3), what's the general principle about combining permissible acts that survives, and does it wreck anything we want to keep? If it's (4), what separates an NDA from blackmail without just pointing at the law and going in circles? And if you think the argument is actually valid and one of (1)-(2) has to go, I'd love to keep those, so talk me out of that gently.
