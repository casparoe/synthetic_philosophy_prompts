So here's something that's been bugging me for a while. Context: longtime LW/ACX reader, and the claim that nuclear deterrence basically worked is all over the rationalist-adjacent water supply — the Long Peace wasn't luck, MAD is why no two nuclear powers have fought a full-scale war, the risk of deliberate exchange is small enough that we should expect deterrence to keep holding. I've sort of absorbed this as a load-bearing background belief without ever actually stress-testing it, wich is exactly the kind of thing this community keeps telling me not to do.

So: act as an expert on nuclear strategy AND on epistemology/forecasting (weird combo, I know, but that's the point — I want someone who knows the Schelling stuff cold but also thinks in base rates). Think carefully. First do a messy brainstorm of every candidate defeater you can think of, THEN give me the organized version. I actually want to see the brainstorm, don't skip it.

The position, stated as strongly as I can: deterrence between roughly rational great powers is highly reliable; the absence of great-power war since 1945 is substantially *caused* by nuclear weapons rather than being a coincidence or caused by something else; and the failure probability per decade is low enough that "just keep the deterrent and don't do anything crazy" is a fine long-run policy.

What I want from you:

1. What would have to be true for this to be false? Which observations, arguments, or historical episodes actually count against it — and be honest about distinguishing decisive defeaters from merely awkward ones.

2. The near-miss argument, because this is the one I keep going back and forth on. Petrov in '83, Arkhipov during the Cuban crisis, Able Archer, the '95 Norwegian rocket thing. The survivorship-bias line says: a long peace that contained this many near-misses is evidence that the system fails sometimes and we've just been lucky, so "zero wars" is weaker evidence for reliability than it looks. Is that right? And steelman the response too — something like "the system absorbed multiple shocks without failing, that's evidence of robustness, like a plane that's had engine trouble and kept flying." Which framing is actually correct here, and what does the answer depend on?

3. Rival explanations: the nuclear taboo (norms doing the work, not threat logic), economic interdependence, conquest just not paying anymore. If one of those did the causal work, the deterrence position survives but gets demoted. Is the position even falsifiable in practice, or can its defenders always absorb any rival explanation? If it's become unfalsifiable-by-qualification, say so.

4. What would the position's own defenders concede as counting against them, vs. what would they dismiss? Don't just give me the critics' list.

5. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me where you'd actually bet if you had to put a probability on "deterrence fails somewhere in the next 50 years."

Also, where does the stability-instability paradox fit? If nukes make conventional conflict *more* likely at the lower rungs (Ukraine being the obvious case), is that a defeater for "deterrence works" or just a cost the position always admitted upfront?

One thing I'd personally find most persuasive: anything that quantifies. If you can turn the near-miss record into even a hand-wavy failure-probability-per-decade estimate, do it. Fermi estimates welcome, false precision not.
