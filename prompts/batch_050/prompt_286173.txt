I am a philosopher working on conceptual engineering, and I am preparing a paper for a workshop on ameliorative analysis. Below is a draft of the paper's core section, followed by the points on which I would like your comments. Please read the draft as an examiner would: I am less interested in general praise or summary than in places where the argument is confused, overstated, or quietly assuming what it needs to prove.

DRAFT SECTION

Carnap's explication and Haslanger's ameliorative analysis are usually presented as cousins: both replace a concept with a successor better suited to our purposes, and both are accused of "changing the subject." I think the cousin picture conceals a difference that matters, and I propose to mark it with a distinction between what I call *replacement engineering* and *reweighting engineering*.

In replacement engineering, the engineer discards the old concept's criteria of application and supplies new ones. Carnap's explication of the everyday notion of the probable by the formal notion of degree of confirmation is the paradigm: nothing in the explicatum needs to resemble the explicandum's old inferential role, provided the new concept does the relevant scientific work. In reweighting engineering, by contrast, the engineer keeps the old criteria in place but redistributes their authority. When Haslanger proposes that we understand "woman" in terms of systematic subordination on the basis of perceived sex, she is not discarding the everyday criteria — perceived anatomy, self-presentation, social treatment — as Carnap discarded the everyday criteria of probability. She is demoting some of them from constitutive to evidential status: they become signs of the phenomenon rather than parts of its definition.

This distinction, if sound, dissolves the standard "changing the subject" objection asymmetrically. The objection has real bite against replacement engineering: if the explicatum shares no criteria with the explicandum, the skeptic is entitled to ask why we should regard the inquiry as continuous, and Carnap's only honest answer is pragmatic — the continuity lies in the purposes served, not the topic discussed. But reweighting engineering keeps every criterion on the table; it only rearranges them. So the reweighting engineer can claim genuine topic continuity: the same facts remain relevant, and the dispute is only over which of them are definitive. The ameliorative project, on my reading, is conservative in its materials and radical only in its architecture.

The distinction also generates a testable prediction about failure modes. Replacement engineering fails when the explicatum, however elegant, cannot be connected back to the purposes that motivated the inquiry — Carnap's own demand of fruitfulness. Reweighting engineering fails differently: it fails when the demoted criteria refuse to stay demoted, because too much of the surrounding inferential network is load-bearing on them. Call such a criterion *structural*: a criterion is structural to the degree that revising its status forces revisions elsewhere. My conjecture is that ameliorative projects stall exactly when they underestimate which criteria are structural. The perceived-sex criterion in the concept "woman" may be an example: a reweighting that treats it as merely evidential will nonetheless have to negotiate with every institution — medicine, sport, census-taking, etiquette — that has built its practices on treating it as constitutive.

END OF DRAFT

My requests:

1. Is the replacement/reweighting distinction actually stable, or does it collapse under pressure? Specifically, I worry that any demotion of a criterion from constitutive to evidential status can be redescribed as a wholesale replacement in which the new concept happens to license many of the old inferences. If the distinction is merely a difference of degree, is the asymmetry I claim against the changing-the-subject objection still defensible, and on what grounds?

2. In the third paragraph I attribute to Carnap the view that continuity of inquiry is continuity of purpose rather than of topic. Is this attribution fair, and does it undermine my claim that reweighting engineering enjoys a kind of continuity unavailable to the Carnapian — or does Haslanger's project ultimately rest on continuity of purpose in exactly the same way, making my asymmetry illusory?

3. The notion of a "structural" criterion in the final paragraph is doing a great deal of work. As defined, structurality is measured by propagation of forced revisions, which seems to make it a sociological fact about institutions and inferential habits rather than a fact about the concept itself. Does this matter for the conjecture? If structurality is contingent and historically variable, does the conjecture amount to more than the truism that conceptual revision is hard when many people rely on the unrevised concept?

4. Finally: the draft contains no case where reweighting engineering clearly succeeded. Can you think of a historical example of a concept whose criteria were redistributed in authority without being replaced — in science, law, or ordinary language — and would including one strengthen the paper or expose it to objections I am not seeing?
