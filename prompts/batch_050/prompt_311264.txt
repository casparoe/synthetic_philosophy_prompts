I'm one of the TAs on a third-year module on rational choice and its discontents, and I've been landed with writing this year's exam question on the St Petersburg paradox. The trouble is that last year's question basically begged students to recite the standard story — infinite expected value, yet nobody sensible pays more than a tenner to play, enter Bernoulli and diminishing marginal utility, job done — and I had to mark forty near-identical essays. The course convenor has told me the new question has to punish recitation, so I want something the students can't answer out of their notes.

To that end I've invented a variant game (they won't have seen it, and the paper will state the rules in full, so the question needs to be doable from first principles). I'm calling it the Petersburg Tower: the casino flips a fair coin until the first heads appears; if the first heads lands on flip n, the player wins 2^(2^n) ducats — so 4 ducats if heads comes on flip 1, 16 on flip 2, 256 on flip 3, 65,536 on flip 4, and so on. The crux I want the question to probe is whether candidates actually understand what Bernoulli's fix does and doesn't establish: concave utility (log utility and friends) does NOT tame the Tower, since expected log-utility is still infinite, so what the original resolution secretly relied on was utility being bounded, which is a much stronger and much costlier assumption. I'd like the question to include a short computational sub-part that forces them to verify the divergence themselves (this is where the reciters will fall over), then discursive parts asking what bounded utility costs you elsewhere — e.g. it commits you to turning down arbitrarily attractive long-shot gambles — and whether "no real casino could bankroll the Tower" dissolves the paradox or merely changes the subject.

Deliverables, please: the question text itself, answerable in about an hour and worth 25 marks in total; a model answer of around 600 words written the way a genuinely good student would write it under time pressure rather than a textbook; and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, saying how many marks each carries, plus for each point one line on the specific way weaker candidates typically bungle it. Finish with a short note on what seperates a first-class answer from a solid 2:1. British English throughout, please.
