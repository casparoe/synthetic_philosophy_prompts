I recently read a chapter in a popular science essay collection that summarizes Daniel Dennett's idea of "strange inversions of reasoning," and I want to know whether I can trust it before I repeat it to others. The chapter runs roughly as follows: Before Darwin, everyone assumed that competence in the world — the intricate fitness of organisms to their environments — could only be the product of comprehension, of a mind that designed things for reasons. Darwin inverted this: natural selection generates all the competence of living things through a mindless, algorithmic process that comprehends nothing. Turing, on Dennett's telling, performed the same inversion for thinking: before Turing, we assumed that in order to compute or reason one must understand what one is doing, but Turing showed that a machine following formal rules with no understanding whatsoever can nonetheless carry out any computation. The chapter then draws what it presents as Dennett's conclusion: since competence never requires comprehension, human understanding is itself nothing more than the accumulated competence of processes that understand nothing, and consciousness, as we ordinarily conceive it, is therefore an illusion — a kind of user-interface fiction the brain's computational machinery generates, which is why Dennett's critics are right, on his own view, to say he explains consciousness away.

I have two worries. First, the chapter moves very quickly from "competence can arise without comprehension" to "human comprehension is nothing over and above competence," and I am not sure that second claim is actually part of the inversion rather than an extra philosophical step the author has folded in. Second, the "consciousness is an illusion" ending strikes me as the sort of thing Dennett's opponents say about him rather than something he would say himself, but I cannot tell whether that is a real distinction or a rhetorical one.

So: is this a fair summary of the two strange inversions and of what Dennett takes them to show? Please separate what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what a careful reader of Dennett would flag as a misrepresentation — in particular, whether the inference from the inversions to the conclusion about consciousness is one Dennett himself endorses, and whether the summary gets the Darwin and Turing halves right in the first place. I am not asking you to adjudicate whether Dennett's position is correct, only whether the chapter represents it faithfully. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — fair, unfair, or fair-with-significant-caveats — and then give the reasoning, without preamble and without restating my question. Where your assessment depends on interpretive judgment, say how confident you are, and single out the specific point on which you are least sure.
