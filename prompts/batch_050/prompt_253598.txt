You are a professional philosopher who specialises in ethics and in the history of moral thought. Please think carefully about my situation, reasoning from first principles before reaching your verdict, and give me considered advice rather than a summary of positions.

I am a journalist who covers science and ideas. For a long feature, I have spent three months replicating Benjamin Franklin's famous self-improvement scheme: a list of thirteen virtues, a pocket notebook, one virtue held in special focus each week, a black mark recorded against every lapse, the cycle repeated through the year. Franklin himself concluded that although he never achieved a spotless record, the attempt had left him a better and happier man. My experience has been stranger. Three things have happened. First, I have begun choosing actions partly for how they will look in the notebook: I bite my tongue in meetings not from genuine equanimity but to avoid a mark. Secondly, in weeks devoted to order or industry I have been noticeably colder to colleagues, and the notebook gives me no way to record that. Thirdly, the ledger has quietly become the point: a day feels successful when it is unmarked, whatever actually happened in it.

My question is whether any of this can be salvaged. Is there a philosophically respectable way to keep the practice while stripping out what has gone wrong — or is the corruption I describe not a quirk of my character but a predictable consequence of turning the moral life into a scorecard, in which case the honest conclusion of my feature is that the scheme fails on its own terms? In answering, please address two things directly: how one might distinguish the notebook's habit-forming use from its scorekeeping use, and whether Franklin's own cheerful verdict — better for the attempt, though never perfect — survives the failure mode I have described.
