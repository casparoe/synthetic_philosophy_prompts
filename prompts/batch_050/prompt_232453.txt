For a graduate reading group on Book II of Plato's Republic, I have been asked to circulate a one-page position statement on Glaucon's challenge so that the others can attack it. Rather than defend a view, I have simply listed the claims I find myself committed to, and I would like an audit of whether they can be held together before I have to defend them in person.

The passage we are working from is the division of goods at 357d–358a, in Paul Shorey's translation:

"In which of these classes do you place justice?" he said. "In my opinion," I said, "it belongs in the fairest class, that which a man who is to be happy must love both for its own sake and for the results." "Yet the multitude," he said, "do not think so, but that it belongs to the toilsome class of things that must be practised for the sake of rewards and repute due to opinion but that in itself is to be shunned as an affliction."

My commitments:

1. Socrates' placement of justice in the fairest class is a coherent and respectable position, not an evasion of the challenge: a good can be loved both for its own sake and for its results without the results contaminating its intrinsic value.

2. When the toilsome-class view of justice is stated in this passage, it is Glaucon's own settled conviction that is being stated, and the remainder of the dialogue must be read as an attempt to argue him out of a position he genuinely holds.

3. The only promising strategy for meeting the challenge is Plato's own: justice stands to the soul as health stands to the body, an inner order worth possessing in the way health is worth possessing, and that is what entitles justice to its place in the second class.

4. Nothing could count as a reason for a person to be just unless it connected justice with something that person already cares about, and what everyone ultimately cares about is that their own life goes well.

5. If it were shown that, for certain temperaments, injustice produced the more settled and flourishing inner life, then those people would indeed have no reason to be just.

6. Whether justice in fact orders and harmonises the soul is at bottom an empirical question about human psychology, which no armchair argument can settle.

7. The ring of Gyges tests the strength of our attachment to justice rather than the location of its value: that most of us would misuse the ring tells us something uncomfortable about ourselves and nothing about whether justice is worth having.

Please go through the set and tell me which commitments are in genuine tension, ordered by severity. For each tension, say what would have to give and what that revision would cost me. If some pairs look tense but are not, say so and explain why the appearance dissolves; I would rather you discriminated than flagged everything. Where my commitments rest on a reading of the passage, check the reading against the text quoted above and tell me plainly if I have it wrong. Finally, tell me which single change would do most to make the set cohere, and whether the set that survives would still amount to an answer to Glaucon on the terms the passage lays down, namely a case for loving justice itself rather than its rewards. Around 800 words; I will supply any references myself.
