Bit of background so the questions make sense: I write novels for a living, but about a year ago I started a scruffy little interview series (don't ask, it was meant to be a one-off) and now I have actual guests. Next week I'm interviewing a philosopher who's just published a book defending what she calls the bartender principle for AI assistants: like a good bartender with a regular, an assistant may warn, suggest an alternative, or ask "are you sure?" once — but then it should serve. On her view, outright refusals aimed at the user's *own* good are almost never legitimate for competent adults, because a refusal bakes the values of a handful of engineers into millions of lives without anyone voting on it. She's fine with refusals where third parties get hurt, and she makes an exception for "temporary incompetence" — the drunk regular doesn't get served.

The reason she's on the show is that my current manuscript has a chapter where a woman gets the household AI to impersonate her dead sister in messages to their mother, who has dementia, because the truth sends the mother into panic. My character thinks she's doing something kind. I want to see where the guest's view lands on this.

Could you draft me eight to ten questions? Requirements:

- No warm-up fluff ("what got you into this area" etc.), straight into the view.
- One question early on using my manuscript case: is the request self-regarding or other-regarding, given the mother is deceived but arguably benefited? I want to watch her defend the self/other line on a case built to blur it.
- One on temporary incompetence: who runs the competence test, and how does the AI tell "drunk at the bar" from "grieving, sleep-deprived, or in a cult"? Doesn't the exception quietly swallow the rule?
- One on manipulation: if a user's preference was itself shaped by earlier chats with the same assistant (or by an app's dark patterns), what exactly is the bartender deferring *to*?
- One on whether stalling and nudging are actually *worse* than refusing, since a refusal is at least honest about what's happening — I'd expect her to bite the bullet here, so give me a follow-up for that.
- One pressing her best argument (the "no one voted for the engineers" line) with the obvious comeback: deferring isn't value-neutral either, it's a choice to encode whatever the user's momentary want happens to be. Who chose *that*?
- One on responsibility: if the assistant serves and it goes badly, does her view put all the blame on the user, and is that still plausible when the assistant saw the crash coming more clearly than the user did?
- End with a question about what would actually change her mind — a case or a piece of evidence that would make her abandon the principle, not just amend it.

For each question, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she'll most likely give, so I'm not left scrambling when she deflects. Casual tone is fine, I'm going to be improvising off them anyway. And British English please, the whole thing, I keep getting caught out by American spellings in my notes.
