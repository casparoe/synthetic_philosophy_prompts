I am revising a paper on Leibniz's modal metaphysics, and in an old draft I formalized a worry that goes back to his correspondence with Arnauld: that Leibniz's doctrine of complete individual concepts collapses contingency into necessity. The formalized version came out tighter than I intended, and now it seems to prove something no Leibnizian can accept. I would like your diagnosis before I decide what to cut.

You are a specialist in early modern metaphysics with deep knowledge of Leibniz's logic and philosophy of modality. Work through this carefully: first reconstruct the argument on its strongest reading, and only then diagnose it. Do not simply recite standard Leibnizian doctrines at me; I want to know exactly where this particular chain of reasoning breaks, if it does.

The argument:

(1) Leibniz holds that in every true proposition, the notion of the predicate is contained in the notion of the subject, so that every truth about an individual is grounded in that individual's complete concept.

(2) If the predicate-notion is contained in the subject-notion, then the truth of the proposition can be demonstrated a priori by analysis of the subject-notion alone.

(3) A truth that can be demonstrated a priori by conceptual analysis is a necessary truth, since demonstration reduces the proposition to definitions and identities, and the negation of anything so reducible implies a contradiction.

(4) Therefore every truth is necessary, including truths about created individuals such as Judas's betrayal or Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon.

(5) If every such truth is necessary, then there is no genuine contingency, and neither divine freedom in creation nor human freedom in action is real.

(6) So Leibniz's own logic commits him to a necessitarianism indistinguishable from Spinoza's.

My problem: (4) and (5) are conclusions I cannot accept, and I do not think Leibniz accepted them either, yet each step looks like a fair statement of something he says. I would strongly prefer to keep (1), since concept-containment is the heart of his account of truth, and I do not want to abandon it just to escape the argument. I also have some investment in (2), because if containment does not ground a priori demonstrability, I lose my explanation of why God knows contingent truths without experiencing the world.

So: which premise or inference should I reject? Explain why that step looked fine — it should be a reading of Leibniz that a competent interpreter could plausibly defend — and then state precisely what rejecting it costs me, given my stated preferences. If you think the flaw lies in a slide between two senses of a term like demonstration, analysis, or necessity, identify the slide and say which sense each premise needs in order to be true. Finally, price the main alternative to your diagnosis: if I rejected a different premise instead of the one you chose, what would I gain and what would I lose?
