I have no formal training in ethics or sociology, but I've lived on both sides of a divide I keep thinking about. I grew up in a community where reputation was everything: an insult left unanswered marked you as fair game, disputes were settled by families and public standing rather than by authorities, and people policed their own honor because nobody else would. As an adult I've lived somewhere the opposite holds: people let slights pass, treat involving courts or employers or the police as the normal response to conflict, and regard my childhood norms as thuggish. Lately I've also noticed a third pattern online, where people seem to gain standing by publicly cataloguing the harms done to them -- something neither of the worlds I know quite predicted.

Two friends of mine have been arguing about this for years, mostly past each other. One defends honor-based norms: she thinks a society where respect must be personally claimed and defended produces candor, courage, and real accountability, and that outsourcing conflict to institutions makes people passive and lets aggressors hide behind procedure. The other defends dignity-based norms: he thinks every person should be treated as having standing that doesn't depend on their capacity or willingness to fight for it, and that honor systems are just protection rackets where the strong set the terms. Both of them are uneasy about the third pattern and disagree about whether it's a corruption of dignity culture or its logical endpoint.

I've asked them to stop debating and instead produce a joint statement they can both sign, and I've promised to draft it for them. Please write that draft. It should contain:

1. A numbered list of claims both would accept. I suspect these include: that every enforcement system for respect has characteristic failure modes, not just characteristic virtues; that institutions which handle conflict for people change those people's capacities over time; and that each of the three patterns secretly depends on mechanisms it officially disavows (dignity systems lean on something like honor when procedure fails the powerless; honor systems lean on something like dignity when protecting those who can't defend themselves).

2. The remaining disagreements, each stated in one or two sentences worded so that each friend would accept the formulation of their own position -- not a parody of it, and not so hedged it says nothing.

3. The cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of them. For each crux, give one concrete case or observation both agree is genuinely diagnostic, and record in advance what each of them predicts about it. One crux I want included: whether external enforcement of respect produces actual respect or merely suppressed resentment that resurfaces in uglier forms -- including, as the diagnostic case, what each friend predicts happens to conflict inside workplaces with very strict formal complaint procedures.

4. A closing note identifying any part of their dispute that is verbal rather than real, and explaining why.

Keep the statement under a length two stubborn friends could plausibly negotiate, and don't have it smuggle in a verdict on who is right.
