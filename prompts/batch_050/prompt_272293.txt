I teach an undergraduate seminar on historical periodization, and this term a dispute broke out among my students that I could not settle to my own satisfaction. We were discussing two rival proposals for when the Anthropocene -- the geological epoch defined by human dominance over Earth systems -- should be taken to begin. The first, associated with the geographers Simon Lewis and Mark Maslin, fixes on roughly 1610: the so-called Orbis spike, a dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide visible in Antarctic ice cores, plausibly caused by forest regrowth across the Americas after European colonization and the epidemics that killed most of the Indigenous population. The second, favored by the stratigraphic working group that spent years preparing a formal proposal, fixes on roughly 1950: the Great Acceleration, marked by plutonium fallout from nuclear weapons testing, fly ash, plastics, and a sharp upturn in nearly every measurable human impact. (As it happens, the stratigraphic community voted in 2024 not to ratify the 1950 proposal as an official epoch boundary, but the term marches on regardless.)

Compare these two proposals, not merely as candidates for a date but as answers to two different questions. The 1610 proposal seems to answer the historian's question -- when did human action first become a force legible in the whole Earth system, and what kind of action was it? -- while the 1950 proposal answers the stratigrapher's question -- where is the sharpest, most globally synchronous marker that a future geologist could actually find in the rock record? Which question should govern? Consider that the choice is not innocent: dating the Anthropocene to 1610 ties the epoch to colonialism and genocide in a way that distributes responsibility very differently than dating it to postwar industrialization, and some critics argue the 1950 date conveniently exonerates earlier centuries of extraction. At the same time, a defender of 1950 might reply that a geological epoch is a technical unit of stratigraphy, not a verdict in a moral trial, and that importing distributive blame into a boundary definition corrupts the science.

So: which proposal is better, and better for what? If you conclude that the two dates serve genuinely different purposes, say whether that is a stable pluralism (historians get their Anthropocene, geologists get theirs) or an evasion -- since the word is now doing political, moral, and scientific work simultaneously, and someone, in effect, has to decide what it means. Finally, address the meta-question my brightest student asked: who should have the authority to settle this? Stratigraphers vote on geological boundaries by a procedure designed for the Ordovician; is that procedure legitimate, or even coherent, when the boundary in question is one the voters themselves are living through and partly causing?
