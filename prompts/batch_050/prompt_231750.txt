Okay so next month I'm presenting a paper at a workshop on philosophy of history and historical methodology, and I want to be overprepared for the Q&A. Act as a veteran discussant who's torn apart a hundred talks like mine — think carefully, brainstorm a long list of objections first, then prune.

My thesis in a nutshell: historians keep arguing about contingency ("for want of a nail," butterflies, etc.) purely qualitatively, and I propose a measurable proxy I call fork density — roughly, for a given historical episode, the number of independently attested small perturbations (documented near-misses, failed coups, storms, deaths averted by chance) that occurred within some window before the outcome, weighted by how tightly historians can trace each one to the actual outcome. My claim is that episodes with high fork density are the ones where contingency talk is doing real explanatory work, and low fork density episodes are where structural explanations should win, so this could settle some classic debates (e.g., WWI inevitability vs. accident) instead of just re-litigating them.

The audience: about 60/40 split between analytic philosophers of history (mechanism-realist types, skeptical of counterfactuals as evidence, several of whom think "contingency" is just an artifact of incomplete causal knowledge) and empirical historians/cliometricians who hate untestable metaphysics and will bristle at the weighting scheme.

What I want from you: the objections they'll actually raise, phrased how they'd phrase them (philosophers will say "closest possible world," the cliometricians will say "selection effect" or "post hoc"), ranked by likelyhood and by how badly they hurt; for each, a two-minute reply plus where that reply is weakest; what I should just concede gracefully; and please be honest about which objection I'm secretly hoping nobody asks. Also flag any way the whole fork-density framing smuggles in what it's supposed to measure.
