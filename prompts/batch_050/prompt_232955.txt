I'm putting together a seminar on the ethics of the welfare state, and I keep getting stuck on something that came up in a heated office-hours argument with a colleague last week. Most welfare systems I know of — universal credit in the UK, US-style workfare, and so on — make benefits conditional: claimants have to log a certain number of job applications each week, attend mandatory meetings with caseworkers, sometimes accept any job offered, and they can be sanctioned, i.e. have benefits cut for weeks, for missing an appointment, sometimes even when they had a decent excuse like a sick kid or a bus that never showed. Fraud is policed aggressively, with tip lines and investigations, and the surrounding political rhetoric is alot about making sure nobody gets something for nothing and sorting the deserving from the undeserving poor.

Here's what puzzles me. First, none of this applies to other large transfers from the state: pensioners don't have to prove they're spending their pension wisely, farmers receiving subsidies face no weekly effort tests, homeowners benefiting from tax breaks have no caseworker, and disaster relief gets handed out without anyone asking whether the flood victim bothered to prepare. So the practice seems to presuppose that some transfers need desert-scrutiny and others don't — but under what theory would that line fall exactly there? Second, there's a tension I can't resolve: sanctions only make sense if claimants are rational enough to respond to incentives (miss the meeting, lose your rent money, so don't miss the meeting), but the undeserving-poor framing appears to presuppose roughly the opposite — that these are feckless, short-sighted people who can't manage their own lives. If claimants were the kind of agents sanctions are built to work on, they presumably wouldn't need sanctioning, and if they genuinely need it, the sanctions shouldn't work. Third, the system can't distinguish genuine effort from box-ticking: someone can hit their application quota by spraying out applications for jobs they're obviously unqualified for, while someone else spends the week doing a training course and gets sanctioned for skipping a check-in. What does that reveal about whether the practice tracks desert at all, as opposed to mere outward compliance?

So: what theory (or cluster of theories) of desert, responsibility, and agency does conditionality actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory for me — is it a reciprocity picture (benefits as your share of a cooperative scheme you must contribute to), a character picture (poverty as evidence of a vice to be corrected), plain deterrence, or something else? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out — could someone who takes benefits to be an unconditional right of citizenship, or a luck egalitarian, coherently endorse sanctions? Please also separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with (perhaps several theories would license it), and flag where it's just philosophically underdetermined. And finally: if we threw out the presupposed theory and rebuilt the system around benefits-as-unconditional-entitlement, what would concretely have to change — everything, or surprisingly little?
