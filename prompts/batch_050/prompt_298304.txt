I am preparing for a seminar discussion on the philosophy of international criminal law, and I keep running into a puzzle about the postwar trials that I cannot resolve to my satisfaction. The basic facts are familiar enough. The London Charter of 1945 defined the charges under which the major war criminals were tried at Nuremberg: war crimes, crimes against humanity, and crimes against peace, the last covering the planning and waging of aggressive war. Defense counsel objected that no individual had ever been criminally punished for starting a war, so the charge violated the principle that there can be no crime and no punishment without a pre-existing law. The tribunal answered, in part, by pointing to the Kellogg-Briand Pact of 1928, in which the powers including Germany had renounced war as an instrument of national policy. At the Tokyo trial the same structure was repeated, and the Indian judge, Radhabinod Pal, dissented at length, arguing among other things that the proceedings amounted to retroactive legislation dressed up as adjudication. Meanwhile the defense of tu quoque — the claim that the Allied powers had themselves done comparable things, from area bombing of cities to the Soviet conduct in Poland — was ruled inadmissible, and no Allied soldier, airman, or statesman faced trial for anything.

What puzzles me is what this whole practice presupposes about the nature of law. The tribunal's reply on retroactivity seems to presuppose that conduct can be criminal for an individual even where no sovereign enacted a statute against it and no penalty was fixed in advance, which sounds like a natural-law view and sits badly with the legal positivism that was orthodox in the very legal cultures doing the prosecuting. Yet the tribunal also insisted it was applying law and not merely punishing wickedness, which suggests it did not see itself as legislating. And the exclusion of tu quoque seems to presuppose that the validity of a norm, and the authority of a court, do not depend on the norm being applied symmetrically to the accusers — a claim that is in obvious tension with ordinary rule-of-law ideals, where equal application is close to the whole point. A friend I debated this with thinks the trials are simply incoherent unless you accept a strong natural-law picture; I suspect they are underdetermined, and that several different jurisprudential packages could license them, but I cannot say which commitments are strictly required and which merely fit comfortably.

So here is my request. Reconstruct the implicit jurisprudence of the postwar trials: what theory of law, and of legitimate punishment, does this cluster of practices — retroactive charges answered by appeal to a treaty, individual liability without prior statute, refusal of tu quoque — actually need in order to be coherent, as opposed to merely being compatible with? Where the practice is underdetermined, say which rival theories would each license it and what they would disagree about. Then answer two further questions. First, if one insists on a positivist view of law, what would the trials have to be redescribed as — political acts of the victors, exercises of morality rather than law, something else — and would that redescription be more honest, or would it lose something the legal framing captures? Second, does the refusal of tu quoque presuppose that justice does not require consistency, or can the asymmetry be defended on grounds that a rule-of-law theorist could in principle accept? I would rather you tell me plainly where the practice has no coherent implicit theory, if that is your verdict, than force a charitable reconstruction.
