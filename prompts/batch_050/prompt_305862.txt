I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a long piece about the inefficacy objection -- the claim that when an individual's contribution to a collective harm makes no detectable difference to the outcome, there's no consequentialist reason to withhold it. Every published treatment of this reaches for the same three examples: driving less, eating less meat, casting a vote. I want a fresh case to anchor the article, and I want it built to order.

Construct a thought experiment meeting these specifications:

1. The setting is small-scale and contemporary -- a workplace, a neighborhood, a hobbyist community, a small town. No governments, no corporations, and none of the three stock examples above.
2. One actor faces one choice. Whichever way the actor chooses, the harm in question comes out the same: its severity is insensitive to any single contribution, and this insensitivity is determinate in the case -- not a matter of the actor's uncertainty about thresholds, tipping points, or other people's behavior. The actor knows the insensitivity and is right to.
3. The stakes are morally serious enough that a reader can't dismiss the harm as trivial, but ordinary enough that the case doesn't turn on life and death.
4. The case must generate, rather than stipulate, a divergence between two verdicts: expected-consequences reasoning returns that there is no reason to refrain, while at least one non-consequentialist or collectively-oriented framework -- universalization, participatory complicity, fair-share reasoning, or similar -- returns that the actor should refrain. If a framework on your list actually fails to deliver its assigned verdict in your case, say so rather than forcing it.

Deliver the scenario as a single paragraph, then the demonstration: state the act, the harm, and why each framework returns the verdict it does. Identify which single feature of the case is doing the load-bearing work and what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed. Then give the strongest move available to the expected-consequences theorist who wants to resist the case -- whether by denying that the insensitivity is stable, biting the bullet, or attacking the rival framework's diagnosis -- and an honest assessment of whether that move succeeds.

The case is for showing lay readers that the inefficacy objection is a genuine theoretical fork and not merely a rationalization for convenience, so anything that smuggles the divergence in by stipulation, or that only works because the actor is confused about the facts, counts as failure.

One request before you start: if these specifications are jointly unsatisfiable -- if, for instance, making the causal insensitivity fully determinate already forecloses the divergence I'm asking for -- don't quietly sand down the case to hide the problem. Tell me plainly which specification does the damage and why, and propose the closest variant that can be built. I'd rather fix the assignment than publish a case with a crack in it.
