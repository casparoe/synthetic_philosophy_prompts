I'm designing the syllabus for an undergraduate seminar I'm calling From Myth to History, which spends the semester on three cases: the Trojan War (Hisarlik really was excavated, and one of its destruction layers really does sit roughly where the tradition puts the war), King Arthur (a figure the early sources treat as a war-leader at Badon, if they treat him as anyone at all, and who accretes a sword, a round table, and a Avalon over the centuries), and the Exodus (an event central to a people's self-understanding with no clear footprint in the Egyptian record, though the Merneptah stele shows Israel existed in Canaan not long after the period in question). What I keep telling my students is that the three cases aren't three points on one spectrum from "pure legend" to "real history" — they're three different relationships between story and event: legend draped over a real place, history inflated into legend, and legend that may be carrying a memory it no longer resembles.

I'd like to open the course with a poem printed on the first page of the syllabus, and I'd like you to write it. Specifically: a sestina whose six end-words are Troy, Arthur, Exodus, ash, seed, and sieve — or a comparable set of your own choosing, provided it includes one word tied to each of the three cases and three words that name the mechanisms by which events pass into story (destruction, germination, sifting/selection). The reason I want a sestina is structural, not decorative: the retrogradatio cruciata, in which the same six words return in every stanza in a new order, should do the argumentative work — the same events keep coming back, rearranged, each retelling a permutation of the last, so that by the final stanza the reader should feel that legend isn't the opposite of history but its recurrence pattern. The three case-words should migrate across the poem: the stanza where "Exodus" lands on a line about archaeology, or "Troy" lands on a line about scripture, should be the stanza that makes my students notice the migration.

In the three-line envoi, where all six end-words must appear, take an actual position on the seminar's title question — when does a legend become history? — and make it a position a historian could disagree with rather than a shrug (something like: never, because history is what a legend can't stop being about; or: at the moment the spade finds what the story no longer needed). The poem should be accurate enough that a classicist, a medievalist, and an Egyptologist could each read their stanza without wincing, and it should work as a poem for a first-year who has never heard of the Merneptah stele. No archaisms for their own sake, and please don't let any of the three cases collapse into a wink about whether it "really happened."
