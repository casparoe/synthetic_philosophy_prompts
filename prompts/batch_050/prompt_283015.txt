I'm applying for a three-month summer research grant in practical ethics and need a proposal of roughly two pages. My topic is griefbots — chatbots trained on a dead person's messages, emails, and other digital traces to simulate conversation with them. My half-formed idea is this. The existing discussion seems to fall into two frames: either the griefbot is treated as a kind of continuation of the dead person, in which case the central questions are about the deceased's consent, dignity, and posthumous interests; or it is treated as a coping tool for the bereaved, in which case the central questions are psychological — does it prolong grief or ease it. I suspect both frames miss what may be the most distinctive ethical feature of these systems: a griefbot does not merely preserve the dead person's words the way letters or recordings do, but generates novel utterances in their voice, and those utterances can circulate beyond the private grieving relationship — to other family members, to colleagues, eventually to the public. My hunch is that the griefbot is better analogized to a literary executor who doesn't just publish an author's papers but composes new work under their name, and that thinking through this analogy would reorient the debate away from consent and grief-management and toward questions about who gets to speak for the dead, what obligations of fidelity bind whoever controls the simulation, and whether the wrong done by a badly behaved griefbot is best understood as a wrong against the deceased, against the bereaved, or against third parties who come to hold false beliefs about what the dead person thought and said.

Please turn this into a grant proposal. It should: state the research question precisely, in a form that admits of a determinate answer; explain why the two dominant frames, even granting their best versions, leave this question open rather than merely underemphasize it; lay out the approach, including how I would actually use the executor analogy — where it is likely to illuminate and where it is likely to break down — and why this method is preferable to the obvious alternatives (an empirical study of grief outcomes, or a general theory of posthumous harm); sketch what a finished result would look like, including which conclusions the project could reach and what considerations would decide between them; and give an honest account of what could make the project fail or prove too large for three months, with a plan for narrowing it if so. Do not include citations or a bibliography; I will add references to the literature myself. Begin with the proposal itself, with no preface or commentary.
