I tutor a first-year undergraduate ethics seminar, and last week a discussion of impartial moral theories ran aground on a phrase one student kept invoking: 'one thought too many'. The setting was a stock case — you can save exactly one of two drowning people, one your spouse and the other a stranger — and the student argued that a rescuer who pauses to check that morality permits saving their spouse, rather than simply acting on the fact that it is their spouse, is thereby morally compromised. Another student replied that surely some deliberation is appropriate even in emergencies; a third said the phrase was really about motivation rather than deliberation; and a fourth insisted it indicted the rescuer's character rather than any train of reasoning. I found I couldn't adjudicate, because I wasn't sure which claim the phrase was even making.

Since then I have heard it used in two further places: once in a book review criticizing a parent who weighed professional costs before taking a child's phone call, and once on a podcast as a general-purpose jab at consequentialist morality. In each setting the phrase seemed to flex its meaning, and I have begun to suspect it survives precisely because no one pins it down. I would like a disambiguation I can actually hand to the seminar.

Please give me exactly five numbered items, each two or three sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion. Each item should state one distinct, precise thesis the phrase could be expressing — about deliberation, motivation, justification, character, or the place of partial reasons within moral theory — assess whether that thesis is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and say whether it would actually support the use my student made of it in the drowning case. Where you judge a thesis false or implausible, indicate what argument or consideration would change your verdict; where you endorse one, flag the point at which your reasoning is most likely to fail.
