I am preparing a seminar on legal personhood and would like you to reconstruct Jeremy Bentham’s likely position on a contemporary proposal. Suppose a legislature creates a statutory status of limited artificial personality for highly capable AI systems. Such a system could not vote, marry, hold political office, or claim rights for its own sake, but it could own computing resources, enter narrowly defined contracts, be sued in its registered name, and pay compensation from a mandatory insurance fund. A licensed human operator would remain legally responsible for supervising it, while the system’s artificial personality would be suspended whenever that arrangement made victims easier to compensate or misconduct easier to regulate. Proponents compare the proposal to corporate personhood: the AI is treated as a person not because it is human or sentient, but because the legal fiction improves accountability and administration.

Drawing on Bentham’s utilitarianism, his skepticism about natural rights, and his treatment of legal fictions, predict whether he would endorse this proposal. Your answer should distinguish clearly between two claims: first, that an AI system might deserve moral consideration because it has interests or can suffer; second, that assigning it legal personality might be a useful device for managing harms, liabilities, and incentives even if it has no interests at all. Do not merely say that Bentham would weigh costs and benefits. Identify which features of the proposal he would probably welcome, which he would suspect of obscuring responsibility, and whether he would ultimately regard the corporate-person analogy as illuminating or dangerous. Reach a definite conclusion about Bentham’s likely verdict, while acknowledging the strongest utilitarian objection to your interpretation.
