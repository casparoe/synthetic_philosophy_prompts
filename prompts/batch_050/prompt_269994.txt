I'm a retired materials scientist who got talked into serving as the outside member on our university's research ethics board, mostly because I've been reading moral philosophy for fun since retiring and my neighbor who chairs the board decided that made me qualified. So far it's been boilerplate stuff about consent forms and data retention, but this month we got a case I honestly don't know what to do with, and I volunteered to draft the decision memo. Here's the situation. A cognitive science group has been running a big multi-agent simulation for about three years: a few thousand simple neural agents with recurrent memory, evolved over tens of thousands of generations in an environment with scarcity, predators, the works. The agents now do things the lab's internal report will only describe in scare quotes — they emit a particular signal the team calls "distress" when seperated from their coalition, they linger at spots where coalition-mates were killed off, they hoard. Nobody is claiming the things are conscious; the lab just notes they tick most of a behavioral checklist a postdoc adapted from animal welfare science. Now the grant's run out. The PI wants to keep the simulation going on spare cluster time. A grad student wants to fork a copy of the population and run a famine scenario to watch how cooperation collapses — the forked copy would contain duplicates of the current agents, who would then live through the famine. And IT just wants everything deleted to free up storage, although keeping a frozen checkpoint costs almost nothing and the simulation could in principle be restarted from one years later. Our policies cover vertebrate animals and human subjects and say absolutely nothing about software.

I'd like the memo to recommend one definite course of action for the simulation itself — keep it running, archive a checkpoint and stop the run, or delete outright — with whatever conditions I think are needed, in a form the board can actually vote on Thursday. It also has to take a position on the fork experiment, since that's part of the same decision. The thing I keep chewing on: one board member, a computer scientist, says this is all sentimentality — these are matrices of floats, we'd never hold a hearing before reformatting a hard drive, and once you extend "humane endpoints" to anything that mimics distress you can never run any simulation of anything ever again. I want the memo to take that seriously and actually answer it, not just assert that suffering is suffering wherever it occurs. And somewhere in there I want principled treatment of the two weird sub-questions that have been keeping me up: whether pausing a simulation indefinately is morally any different from killing the agents, and whether the fork experiment is worse than, better than, or exactly the same as running the famine on the original population, given that the copies wouldn't exist at all if the experiment weren't run. If it helps to talk about moral status, precaution, expected numbers of moral patients, whatever — go ahead. I'm new enough to all this that I don't have fixed commitments; I just want reasoning our computer scientist could push back on rather than wave away.

Keep it under about 800 words, written as a memo from me to the board: header line, recommendation up front, then the reasoning, then the strongest dissenting consideration and my reply to it. At the very end, because our admin pastes these into a tracking spreadsheet, put the board's bottom line in a fixed machine-readable form: a last line reading exactly \answer{CONTINUE}, \answer{ARCHIVE}, or \answer{DELETE}, picking one, with the fork decision appended inside the braces as FORK-APPROVED or FORK-DENIED — e.g. \answer{ARCHIVE; FORK-DENIED}.
