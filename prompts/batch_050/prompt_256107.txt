I overheard two of my colleagues going at it in the lounge after our logic seminar last week, and I've been turning it over since. Neither is a slouch -- one does set theory, the other works on proof theory -- so I'd like a careful referee. The topic was: what would actually happen if a contradiction were discovered in a standard foundational system, say PA or ZFC? (1) The set theorist opened by saying it would be a genuine catastrophe: in classical logic a contradiction entails everything, so the system becomes trivial, and since virtually all of mainstream mathematics can be encoded in ZFC, the whole edifice loses its warranty overnight. (2) The proof theorist pushed back hard: mathematics doesn't actually rest on ZFC's consistency, she said -- mathematicians believed their theorems long before anyone axiomatized anything, and when Russell's paradox blew up Frege's system we didn't lose arithmetic, we just rebuilt. An inconsistency would trivialize the formal system, sure, but not the informal proofs, which were never really *in* the system in the first place. (3) He replied that the rebuilding move only works if the contradiction is up in the fancy infinitary machinery, where you can retreat to something safer -- but if the rot is down in the arithmetic, the very finitistic reasoning you'd use to do the rebuilding, including reasoning about syntax and proofs themselves, is exactly what's in question. There's no floor to retreat to. (4) She said that conflates a system being inconsistent with our basic arithmetical competence being incoherent, that any actual contradiction would almost certainly be localizable -- some specific large cardinal or comprehension instance -- and that mathematicians would do damage assessment the way they always have, keeping the thousands of proofs that never go near the problematic principle.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? Rate each of the four moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification each, and tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing. One thing I care about: please be upfront about which parts of this are settled among people who actually work in logic -- e.g., what Gödel's results do and don't show, what explosion does in classical systems, what happened historically -- versus which parts are genuinely contested philosophical terrain (like whether informal proof really floats free of foundations, or whether paraconsistent approaches are a live option here). I dont want a contested view presented as the consensus position.
