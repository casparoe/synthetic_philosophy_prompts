Two friends of mine, both of whom also read the same decision-theory threads I do, got into an argument after a philosophy reading group, and it ended unresolved. The setup: a trusted lab offers to run a one-shot prisoner's dilemma in which your opponent is a high-fidelity emulation of your own brain, made from a scan taken that morning. Payouts are real money: 10/10 for mutual cooperation, 0/0 for mutual defection, and 15/1 if exactly one of you defects. You choose once, simultaneously, with no communication.

Mara said she'd cooperate, and that this is straightforwardly the rational move. Her reasoning: the emulation runs the same decision procedure she does, on the same inputs, so whatever she ends up choosing, the emulation chooses too. Her real choice is therefore between mutual cooperation and mutual defection, and between those she prefers mutual cooperation. She said she's not claiming her decision causes the emulation's decision — she's claiming that her deliberation is the deliberation, happening twice, so when she settles on an answer she settles it for both instances at once.

Daniel said this is a dressed-up version of a classic mistake. Round one of his objection: the emulation's choice is fixed by its own computation, which is physically sealed off from hers, so her actual options are still "cooperate while it does whatever it does" and "defect while it does whatever it does," and defecting dominates in each column. Her "real choice" framing smuggles in control over an outcome she has no causal grip on. Round two: he pressed her with a test — suppose, one second before the buttons lock, a screen revealed that the emulation had already committed to defection. Would she still cooperate? If she switches to defecting in that case, then her cooperation never actually depended on the emulation's choice matching hers, which he said proves the symmetry was doing no real work in her reasoning, and if she doesn't switch, she's just paying 9 dollars to affirm a slogan.

Mara's reply: the test is rigged, because the scenario in which the emulation defects while she doesn't is exactly the scenario her reasoning says can't arise given a faithful copy — Daniel is evaluating her policy in a world where its premise is false. More importantly, she said, judge decision procedures by what they get the agents who run them: two copies of her walk away with 10 each, two copies of Daniel walk away with 0 each, and a theory of rational choice on which the rational agents reliably end up poorer than the irrational ones, in a problem built entirely from rational agents, has gone wrong somewhere even if each local step of dominance reasoning looks airtight.

Daniel's last move: that standard proves too much, because it lets you justify anything by comparing joint outcomes of joint policies — the question rationality has to answer is what *this* agent should do, here, with the other side's move already settled, and the fact that everyone following his rule does worse than everyone following hers only shows the problem was designed to punish his rule, not that the rule is wrong. Then they turned to me, and I found I couldn't cleanly adjudicate it.
