While reading about strong longtermism—the thesis that positively influencing the very long-run future is the key moral priority of our time—I came across what looks to me like a decisive refutation, and I'd like your help checking it. The view, as I understand it, rests on the claim that the far future may contain astronomically many people whose well-being counts as much as ours, and that this sheer scale makes far-future effects the dominant term in our moral calculations. But we plainly cannot assign well-founded probabilities to the consequences of our actions thousands of years from now; any expected-value calculation over such horizons is guesswork dressed up in numbers. And if the probability terms are unknowable, multiplying them by an enormous number of future lives yields an unknowable product—so the far future cannot rationally swamp present-day concerns, and strong longtermism collapses back into ordinary short-term reasoning. Answer as a professional moral philosopher would: before responding, lay out the structure of this argument step by step, identify every premise and inference it depends on, and then determine whether it succeeds. If it fails, pinpoint exactly which step is defective and why, rather than simply asserting the standard longtermist position.
