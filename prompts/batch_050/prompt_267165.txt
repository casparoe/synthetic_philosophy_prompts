So I'm in my school's philosophy club and last week we got into this big argument about whether consciousness is just brain stuff, and our teacher mentioned this distinction (I think it comes from a book by Dennett?) between cranes and skyhooks in explanation. A crane is an explanation built up from below — complex things get explained by slower, simpler, mindless processes piling up layer by layer, like evolution, where each level rests on the one under it and you can trace the whole tower down. A skyhook is a magic explanation that just hangs from nowhere, like saying mind was there from the start and everything else descends from it — it looks like it explains things but nothing is holding it up. And then there's greedy reductionism, which is the opposite failure: skipping all the middle layers and trying to explain eveything directly at the bottom, like explaining why people fall in love purely in terms of atoms.

I want to write a pantoum about this for the club's end-of-year zine, like 5 or 6 stanzas. If you don't know the form: every stanza's 2nd and 4th lines come back as the 1st and 3rd lines of the next stanza, so the poem literally constructs itself out of material from the level below it, which should be the poem doing the crane thing, not just talking about it. But here's the part I care about: somewhere in the middle I want one stanza that cheats — one that doesn't take its lines from the stanza before and just hangs there, so the repetition pattern breaks and the reader feels what a skyhook is like before anyone names it. And the ending should do something about greedy reductionism, maybe by trying to jump straight back to the opening lines too fast instead of arriving at them through the proper chain. Someone who knows the cranes/skyhooks thing should be able to point at the skyhook stanza and say exactly why it's the skyhook, but it should also just work as a poem for people who've never heard any of this — the broken repetition has to be felt, not footnoted.
