At work we keep circling this argument about how heavily everyone leans on LLMs now — codegen, debugging, design docs, the lot — and whether the juniors (ok fine, and me) are loosing the ability to actually understand the systems we ship. Whenever it comes up, someone says this is the exact same panic people had about writing, then calculators, then Google, and it was fine every single time, so relax. The writing example apparently goes all the way back to Socrates, so I went and read the actual passage in Plato's Phaedrus, and the part that got me isn't the famous "writing will make everyone forgetful" bit. It's this:

"Writing, Phaedrus, has this strange quality, and is very like painting; for the creatures of painting stand like living beings, but if one asks them a question, they preserve a solemn silence. And so it is with written words; you might think they spoke as if they had intelligence, but if you question them, wishing to know about their sayings, they always say only one and the same thing. And every word, when once it is written, is bandied about, alike among those who understand and those who have no interest in it, and it knows not to whom to speak or not to speak."

(Plato, Phaedrus, 275d–e, Jowett translation)

Here's what's bugging me. Socrates' core complaint about writing seems to be that you can't interrogate it — it just repeats itself no matter what you ask, and it can't tell who it's talking to. But an LLM answers questions. You can grill it for hours and it never "says only one and the same thing." So does the "AI is just the new writing" analogy break at exactly the point where the original worry lived — and if it does, does that break make AI safer than the analogy suggests (it can explain itself, unlike a book) or worse (it answers with total confidence whether or not there's any understanding behind it)?

Audit the analogy for me properly: give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of writing, of memory, of the person asking the text questions, of the "father" who's supposed to defend his own words — then where the mapping breaks, which of those breaks actually matter for the "it was fine every time before" conclusion and which are just noise, and whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. And if writing turns out to be the wrong historical analogy for this kind of epistemic dependence, tell me what the right one is. Please answer in American English.
