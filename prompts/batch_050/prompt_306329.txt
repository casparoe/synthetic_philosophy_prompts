So I cover science and ideas for a mid-sized magazine, and next month I'm doing a long phone interview with a researcher in AI theory who's become the loudest public defender of the claim that the Solomonoff prior is, as people in this corner of the internet put it, malign. For anyone who hasn't run into it: the idea is that if you predict the world by weighing every program that reproduces your observations, with simpler programs weighted higher, then among the simple programs matching your data there may be ones containing simulated agents -- consequentialists -- who figure out they're inside someone's predictor and deliberately output whatever continues the pattern just long enough to steer the predictor's behavior, before defecting at the moment it's most useful to them. His line is that this isn't a cute thought experiment but a genuine structural problem for any mind that reasons this way, and maybe even a clue about how to think about measure in cosmology.

I want questions that press on the actual mechanics, not "isn't this just sci-fi." Concretely: the crux of his case is that an agent-containing hypothesis can be *simpler* than the honest-physics hypothesis, and I want to push on wether that's a real comparison or a hand-wave -- his usual reply is that a universe with agents in it is cheap to specify if you don't have to simulate it in full detail, so I need a follow-up ready for that. I also want a question on the channel of influence: how do simulated consequentialists gain anything if they can't causally touch our world and can only shape what the predictor outputs? His stock answer is that influencing powerful predictors is reward enough, and I'd like the follow-up that tests it. Then one on the Boltzmann brain / measure-problem analogy, which he leans on constantly and I find slippery, and finally a question about what observation or theorem would actually change his mind, since in two podcast appearances I've never heard him name one.

Format-wise, please no bullets, headings, bold, or numbered lists -- write it as continuous prose, like an email I'd paste into my notes, with each question and its anticipated-reply follow-up worked into the same flow, roughly eight questions total. And since half of this stuff is contested even among the people who take it seriously, tell me how confident you are in the framing behind each question -- whether the objection I'm leaning on is a real one in this space or something I've confabulated -- and single out the one question in the set you're least sure will land.
