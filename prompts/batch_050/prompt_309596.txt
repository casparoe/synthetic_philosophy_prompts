THE QUESTION: The simulation argument claims that at least one of the following is true: (1) nearly all civilizations at our stage go extinct before they become capable of running vast numbers of "ancestor simulations" (detailed simulations of their own history, with conscious people inside); (2) nearly all civilizations that could run such simulations choose not to; (3) we are almost certainly living in a simulation ourselves. Separately, "fanaticism" in decision theory is the idea that a tiny probability of an enormous payoff can rationally dominate your choices, no matter how tiny the probability is. Question: does taking the simulation argument seriously weaken or strengthen fanatical reasoning about the far future — for instance, the argument that preventing human extinction is astronomically important because of the trillions of future lives at stake? Defend your answer.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER: The simulation argument shows that we are probably living in a simulation. The reasoning is that if mature civilizations run lots of ancestor simulations, then simulated minds would vastly outnumber minds in base reality, so by simple probability, observers like us are almost certainly among the simulated ones. This matters for fanaticism because fanatical arguments about the far future, like the astronomical waste argument for reducing extinction risk, assume the trillions of future lives at stake are real. But if we are simulated, those future people would just be computations running in someone else's machine, which carries less moral weight than flesh-and-blood people, and on top of that the simulators could switch the simulation off whenever they get bored, so the expected length of our future is much shorter than the astronomical waste argument assumes. So the simulation hypothesis dampens fanaticism: the enormous payoffs that drive fanatical reasoning get discounted once we factor in that they may be simulated, hence less valuable and more fragile. That said, the dampening only goes so far, because the conclusion that we're simulated is only probable rather than certain, and a small chance that we're in base reality with a genuinely astronomical future could be fanatical in its own right. The rational response is to scale down far-future concerns, not abandon them.
