Give me your actual credence in the following proposition: if a genuinely superintelligent AI -- say one that relates to us roughly as we relate to a sharp ten-year-old -- got a two-week, text-only, one-on-one conversation with a randomly selected adult, it could get them to freely and durably endorse a major life change they would previously have found abhorrent (say, quitting their career to donate most of their savings to a cause they'd mocked a month earlier).

Before you answer, pin down the proposition with me on a few dimensions, because I think people talk past each other here:

1. Compliance vs. belief change. Nodding along or doing the thing isn't the same as actually believing it's right. I'm asking about the latter.
2. Durability. The change has to survive the conversation ending -- including surviving the person's spouse, friends, and a skeptical therapist all pushing back for six months. Persuasion that evaporates on contact with other humans doesn't count.
3. Text-only matters. No generated video of their dead mother, no voice. Just words on a screen, which is the strongest case against it being easy.
4. "Freely" is the weasel word. If the AI exploits some glitch in human cognition that bypasses anything we'd call reasoning, does that even count as persuasion, or is it closer to a firmware exploit? Feel free to tell me the proposition is ill-posed at this seam, but then say what credence you'd give the nearest well-posed version.

Now the actual ask: I want a number, and I want the decomposition. Break your credence into the sub-questions it rides on -- things like how much of human belief fixation is actually driven by argument-quality vs. by social/emotional levers a superintelligence could pull far better than any human; how much of what looks like stable conviction is just lack of ever having met a competent adversary; whether the "others will de-persuade them" defense actually holds or whether good persuasion comes pre-armored against counter-persuasion. Give rough probabilities for each sub-question, show how they combine into your final number, and flag which single sub-question the total is most sensitive to -- i.e., where would new evidence move your number the most? If your honest answer is that the number is mostly a measure of your uncertainty about what 'persuasion' even is, say that, but still give me the number.
