I write a column on science and big ideas for a general audience, and I'm working up a piece on why some places industrialized early and others lagged. I keep coming back to the old Weber chestnut about the Protestant ethic and capitalism, but I don't want to just rehash the textbook version -- what I actually want to understand is the fight underneath it, i.e. whether a value system can even count as a *cause* of something like economic growth, or whether that's always just storytelling after the fact.

So: write me a dialogue between two people who both know this terrain cold and who disagree about it. One is an economic historian who thinks culture and values really do explanatory work -- that something like a Protestant orientation to work, saving, and worldly calling helped push parts of Europe toward capitalism, and that saying so isn't mush. The other is a cliometrician who thinks "culture did it" is basically unfalsifiable and that whenever you actually measure things, the values story evaporates.

A few moves I specifically want to see, because these are the ones I'm stuck on:

- The skeptic should hit the obvious hard problems: reverse causation (maybe getting rich makes you act "Protestant"), selection (maybe the kind of people who'd thrive in a market economy converted or migrated in the first place), and the awkward fact that Weber himself mostly talked about elective affinity rather than a one-way causal arrow -- so is the strong causal thesis even his?
- The culture defender should hit back with the within-country comparisons -- the studies of 19th century Prussian counties where more-Protestant regions were more prosperous even with shared institutions, currency, legal system etc. If institutions are identical, what's left to explain the gap?
- Then the twist I really want them to fight over: the finding, in that same literature, that the Protestant advantage seems to run through literacy and schooling (everyone had to read the Bible), not through any measurable "work ethic." The skeptic should treat this as a kill shot -- the values were never doing the work, it was human capital all along. The defender should argue this is a *vindication* of the deeper claim: a religious norm (you must read scripture) causally produced the human capital. So the dialogue should get into whether that rescue is legitimate or just moving the goalposts -- what would even distinguish "culture matters" from "culture is just the delivery mechanism for skills"?
- They should respond to each other's actual arguments, not give parallel speeches. Roughly equal eloquence; I don't want a designated loser. It's fine -- good, actually -- if they end up narrowing their disagreement to something small and sharp rather than one converting the other.

One more thing that matters for the column: at the end, give me a short note separating what's genuinely settled in this literature from what's still contested -- e.g., I have a feeling the naive strong version of Weber's thesis is broadly rejected by people who work on this, but "culture is irrelevant to growth" is NOT the consensus either, and I don't want the dialogue (or my article) to accidentally present one contested reading as the agreed view. Flag anywhere the dialogue leaned on a disputed interpretation of what Weber himself claimed.
