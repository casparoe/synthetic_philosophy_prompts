I am preparing a final examination for an upper-level undergraduate course in philosophy of mind and would like one essay question on the cognitive penetration of perception. The question should be answerable in 90 minutes and should resist responses that merely define cognitive penetration or summarise familiar disputes about whether perception is theory-laden.

Base the question on the following fictional study:

Radiologists examine identical lung scans under two conditions. In one, they are told that the patient belongs to a very high-risk group; in the other, they are told that the patient belongs to a very low-risk group. Radiologists in the high-risk condition report substantially more ambiguous shadows as tumours. The same result occurs even when participants are paid for accuracy and insist that they are reporting what they see rather than what they infer. Researchers disagree about four explanations: current expectations alter the content of visual experience; expectations direct attention towards different parts of the scan; visual experience remains unchanged while judgement and reporting change; or repeated diagnostic training has permanently recalibrated perception, so that no present expectation need affect the visual system.

The essay question should require students to explain what evidence would distinguish these four explanations, rather than treating any influence of knowledge on perception as automatically proving cognitive penetration. It should also require them to consider whether the distinction between early and late visual processing is essential to the issue, and why appealing to predictive processing does not by itself settle it.

Please provide:

1. the examination question, stated in no more than 180 words;
2. a model answer of approximately 750 words, written in British English;
3. a marking scheme totalling 30 marks, identifying the specific moves a first-class answer must make and the marks allocated to each;
4. a concise explanation of the difference between a first-class answer and a competent upper-second answer;
5. a list of five mistakes the scheme should penalise, including confusing cognitive penetration with attention, judgement, memory, perceptual learning, or the generic claim that observation is theory-laden.

Do not require citations, bibliographical references, or discussion of particular named experiments; the assessment should test conceptual and methodological understanding rather than recall of the literature.
