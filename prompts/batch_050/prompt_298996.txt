Every time the 2019 college admissions scandal comes up in my political theory seminar we end up in the same fight, so I want a referee. Quick recap of the facts as I understand them, and correct me if I've mangled something: Rick Singer ran a scheme where wealthy parents paid him, often routed through his fake charity so the payments looked like donations, and he bribed college coaches to flag the kids as athletic recruits (sometimes with photoshopped sports profiles for sports the kids never played) or had a complicit proctor fix their SAT/ACT answers. Dozens of parents charged, a couple of celebrities among them, convictions all around. Meanwhile everyone knows there's a perfectly legal route where a family donates a building and the kid gets a very close look from admissions, plus legacy preferences that do something similar for free. Singer apparently pitched his version to parents as a cheaper, more guarenteed alternative to the donation route, which is part of what makes the case philosophically juicy.

So: analyze the scandal as a case study in what counts as corruption. Take three candidate accounts and run each one against the case -- the legalistic account (corruption is the violation of law or formal rules), the standard textbook account (roughly, abuse of entrusted power for private gain), and an institutional-purpose account along Lessig's lines (a practice is corrupted when money steers it away from its proper purpose, whether or not anyone breaks a rule). Be disciplined about the mapping: for each actor -- Singer, the coaches, the parents, the universities themselves -- say who the entrusted party is, what their office or role actually demands, and what counts as the private gain, since for the parents it's not obvious the gain is even theirs. Then the interesting part. On the legalistic account the donation route is clean and Singer's route is dirty, but the outcomes look identical, so does the case show that account is hopelessley underinclusive, or is the appearance of identity doing the real work and there actually is a morally relevant difference between the two doors? On the institutional account, is the admissions office corrupt every time the development office gets a say, which seems to prove too much? And finally run it back the other direction: is there anything in the episode that all three accounts handle badly -- the fact that several parents seemed genuinely unsure they'd done anything wrong, or that the money was deliberately dressed up as charitable giving, sitting right on the gift/bribe borderline? Does the scandal end up confirming one of these theories, or does it reveal that they were all built with public officials in mind and fit awkwardly onto a private university that was never pretending to be a meritocracy in the first place?
