Last night a friend and I argued until two in the morning about whether anything God might command would thereby be good. His position was blunt: goodness is whatever God wills, and if God commanded cruelty for its own sake then cruelty would be good, since to say otherwise is to set a standard above Him. I pressed the Euthyphro horn; he replied that I was limiting omnipotence; we went in circles. On Thursday I lead a fifty-minute discussion section on the Mu'tazila–Ash'arite dispute for undergraduates in a medieval philosophy survey who have read one short secondary summary and nothing else, and I want the section to go better than the argument did.

Do not teach it to me; plan how I should teach it. Give the order in which the pieces must come, with reasons: I suspect students first need to see that both schools affirm God's justice and differ over what justice consists in, but I am genuinely unsure whether the Euthyphro dilemma should open the session as a familiar doorway or be withheld as a distorting frame. Choose the single example to build around — the Mu'tazilite claim that reason unaided knows we owe gratitude to a benefactor, or the starker case of God commanding gratuitous cruelty — and say which carries the dispute better and what the chosen example must contain and avoid. Map the misconceptions to expect, three of which my friend kindly supplied: that the Ash'arites make God capricious, that the Mu'tazila subordinate God to a law He did not make, and that the dispute concerns how we know right from wrong rather than what makes right right — naming the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. Close with one question that tests whether the students grasp what the two sides actually disagree about, and say what I should openly decline to cover at this level.
