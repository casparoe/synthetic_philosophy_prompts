I'm in an informal reading group on formal epistemology / decision theory and last week we got into the Pasadena paradox (I think it comes from a paper by Nover and Hájek, but don't quote me on that). One of the mathematicians in the group presented an argument from it that he claims shows decision theory is in much deeper trouble than philosophers admit. I couldn't refute him on the spot, but the conclusion seems crazy, so I want a proper diagnosis of where it goes wrong.

Quick setup so we're on the same page. The Pasadena game: flip a fair coin until it lands heads. If the first heads comes on toss n, you receive (-1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n dollars — so +$2 if heads comes up right away, -$2 if on toss 2, +$8/3 on toss 3, -$4 on toss 4, and so on. Computed in the natural order, the expected payoff works out to 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ... = ln 2 ≈ 0.69, so naively you should pay up to about 69 cents to play.

His argument, numbered roughly as he gave it. (1) A rational agent evaluates a gamble by its expected value when it has one; and even when the expectation diverges to +∞ or -∞ we can still say so and act accordingly. (2) The expectation of the Pasadena game is the alternating harmonic series. (3) That series converges only conditionally, and by the Riemann rearrangement theorem its terms can be reordered to sum to any real number you like, or to diverge. (4) Reordering the terms is just listing the same outcomes in a different order — it doesn't change the gamble at all — so no ordering is privileged, and the game's expectation isn't ln 2 or anything else; it's simply undefined. (5) Where expectation is undefined, expected value reasoning delivers no verdict whatsoever: the gamble cannot be rationally compared to anything. (6) Now consider Pasadena-Plus: the same game, except you get one extra dollar no matter what. Pasadena-Plus strictly dominates the original — it's better in every possible outcome — but its expectation is just as undefined. (7) So by (5), there is no rational basis for preferring Pasadena-Plus to plain Pasadena. (8) Hence rationality permits you to pay 50 cents to trade Pasadena-Plus for Pasadena. (9) But any theory that permits paying to make yourself strictly worse off in every outcome is bankrupt — and since similar constructions exist for any unbounded utility function, expected utility theory fails not just for contrived casino games but in principle everywhere.

He says the only escapes are (a) denying that dominance is a requirement of rationality, or (b) banning unbounded utility functions wholesale, which he calls ad hoc. I feel like the sleight of hand is somewhere between (4) and (7) but I cant pin it down. Which step should I actually reject, why does it look so convincing, and what does rejecting it cost? For instance: if you say the game genuinely has no expectation, how do you keep dominance reasoning alive without smuggling expected value back in; and if instead you insist it does have a value, namely ln 2, what do you say about the rearrangements? And does whatever fix you offer generalize to all games of this family, or does it just relocate the weirdness somewhere else?
