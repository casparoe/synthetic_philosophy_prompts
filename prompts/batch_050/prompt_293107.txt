You are a philosopher with expertise in axiology, phenomenology, and population ethics. Think carefully and work through this in stages: first brainstorm the full space of candidate readings before evaluating any of them, then assess each one, and only at the end return to the practical question of which readings do the argumentative work my interlocutor needed.

Here is the situation. In a reading group on population ethics, we were debating whether bringing additional happy people into existence makes the world better, or whether morality only gives us reason to prevent and relieve suffering. I had defended the common-sense view that creating lives full of positive goods -- joy, interest, love, achievement -- adds something of value, even though failing to create them wrongs no one. A fellow participant shut me down with the slogan: "happiness is just the absence of suffering." His argument went roughly: if happiness really is nothing over and above the removal of pain, distress, and frustrated desire, then there is no positive good for a merely possible person to miss out on; a world without them lacks nothing of value; and so the only reason to create or preserve lives is to prevent the suffering those lives would otherwise contain. I later learned that he subscribes to a position sometimes called tranquilism, roughly the view that what ultimately matters is only the absence of craving and suffering -- that there is no positive experiential good beyond the relief or absence of these states, and that a state of untroubled contentment is, if anything, the ceiling of well-being rather than a floor on which real goods are built.

I am sure the slogan is doing more work in his mouth than it can bear, but I cannot yet say precisely where. What I want from you is a disambiguation, laid out as a menu rather than a single exegesis. Distinguish the different precise theses "happiness is just the absence of suffering" might be expressing, along dimensions such as: whether it is a phenomenological claim about what pleasant experience actually feels like (e.g., that every pleasure is in fact the relief of some discomfort or wanting), a conceptual or analytic claim about what the words "happiness" or "pleasure" mean, a claim about the metaphysics of value (that only the absence of bad states has value, and nothing has positive value), a claim about measurement or comparison (that well-being scales bottom out at zero and never go above it), and a psychological claim about motivation (that all desire is essentially aversion). For each candidate reading: state it precisely enough that it could be true or false; say whether it is trivial or merely verbal, substantive and defensible, or false; and identify what would count as evidence for or against it (for instance, whether there seem to be pleasures that are not reliefs of any prior discomfort, and what the tranquilist should say about such cases). Then, for each reading, assess whether it actually delivers my opponent's conclusion in the population-ethics debate -- that there is no value lost in never creating happy lives -- or whether it is silent on that question and leaves him needing further premises. Conclude with your judgment about which reading he most plausibly meant, whether the slogan equivocates between a defensible reading and the reading his argument requires, and what I should have asked him to clarify.
