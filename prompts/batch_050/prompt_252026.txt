I've been reading LessWrong and the surrounding blogs for well over a decade, and one theme I've never seen treated rigorously there is the status of heuristic arguments in mathematics -- the places where mathematicians reason "as if" a deterministic object were random and get away with it. This year I'm helping run a self-organized advanced seminar (roughly advanced-undergraduate level) on analytic number theory and the epistemology of mathematical evidence, and I'm writing the final assessment myself.

I'd like you to draft one problem, answerable in 90 minutes, on the presumption of independence in probabilistic heuristics for the primes. The intended content: the Cramer random model treats each integer n as "prime" independently with probability 1/log n, and on that basis one can guess the Hardy-Littlewood asymptotics for twin primes and Goldbach representations; the naive independence assumption is visibly false (consecutive integers share divisibility by small primes, which is exactly what the singular series corrects for), and even the corrected model is known to fail for primes in short intervals, where Maier showed the fluctuations exceed what independence permits. Meanwhile, the whole framing sits oddly with the fact that the primes are a fixed deterministic sequence, so the "probabilities" can't be frequencies of anything.

The question should force candidates to do three things rather than recite: first, state the presumption of independence as an explicit, checkable conjecture schema rather than a vibe (i.e., pin down what precisely is being asserted to be independent, and over what ensemble); second, diagnose at least one concrete failure of the naive schema, explain the standard repair, and say whether the repair is a patch or a principled correction; third, take a position on what the heuristic's track record actually evidences -- if the corrected model is known to fail somewhere, what exactly is confirmed when its predictions hold elsewhere, and can this kind of support be formalized at all given that the underlying object is deterministic? The difficulty should come from the candidates having to choose a stance on that last point and defend it, not from obscurity of setup.

Please provide:

1. The exam question as it would appear on the paper, with any definitions or reminders the candidates need stated inline (assume they've seen the Cramer model and the singular series before, but state the relevant facts so the question is self-contained).

2. A model answer of about 600-700 words written to the standard of a strong candidate.

3. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, the marks each is worth (total 20), and for each point a one-line note on the specific way candidates typically fumble it (for instance: stating independence in a way that's trivially false or trivially vacuous, treating the singular series as an ad hoc fudge factor, or sliding between "the primes are random" and "we are uncertain about the primes" without noticing).

4. A short note on what separates an A answer from a B answer, naming the specific moves the A answer must contain.
