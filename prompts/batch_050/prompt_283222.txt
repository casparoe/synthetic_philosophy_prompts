i'm writing the requirements section of a working-group paper on lethal autonomous weapons, and i've been told to nail down what any proposed standard of "meaningful human control" would have to satisfy before we're allowed to argue about whose proposal wins. so don't pitch me a definition of MHC and don't survey the existing proposals -- write the spec first.

fix the purpose like this: the standard is for judging treaty language and military doctrine, not an engineering spec and not pure moral theory. something only counts as a requirement if dropping it would let through something obviously unacceptable or make the standard useless for that purpose. for each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and label it non-negotiable or one-camp's-preference.

here's what i think belongs on the list -- push back where i'm wrong:

- it has to handle automatic close-in defensive systems (shipboard CIWS and the like) that have been firing on their own for decades. a standard that bans them is dead on arrival; a standard that waves them through as "defensive" so loosely that anything can be called defensive is worse.
- it has to take human-factors evidence seriously -- automation bias, mode confusion, the out-of-the-loop problem. think of the Patriot fratricides in Iraq in 2003, where automated air defense engaged friendly aircraft and the human operators deferred to the system. if your standard is satisfied by a tired operator clicking confirm in three seconds because the machine is usually right, it isn't a control standard.
- it has to keep accountability coherent: if the human's real function is to absorb blame for a system they couldn't actually steer (the "moral crumple zone" move), the standard should flag that as failure, not count it as compliance.
- it has to preserve the judgements the laws of war demand -- distinction, proportionality, precautions -- and say who or what is performing them in engagements where no human is positioned to.
- it has to say something sensible about engagements that run faster than deliberation: electronic warfare, missile defense, swarms. "a human approves every shot" is not available there, so either the standard relocates control (to mission-level authorization, to design-time constraints, wherever) or it concedes those domains.
- and it can't just lean on the in-the-loop / on-the-loop / out-of-the-loop triad if that triad is too coarse to do the work, which i suspect it is. tell me.

two things i most want. first: is some subset of these jointly unsatisfiable? my hunch is "deliberative human judgement per engagement" and "credible defense at machine speed" can't both be hard requirements, and if the list itself forces that tradeoff i want it stated baldly -- that would be the paper's actual result. second: which single requirement knocks out the most proposals currently circulating in diplomatic and NGO discussions, and do the popular formulations (mission-level control, "appropriate human judgement," that kind of thing) survive it?

end with one line in exactly this form: \answer{...} containing the name of the requirement that does the most excluding work.
