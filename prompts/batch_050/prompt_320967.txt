I am a novelist working on a book whose narrator, after a head injury, develops the Capgras delusion: he becomes convinced that his wife has been replaced by an impostor. In researching clinical psychiatry so that I can portray his treatment plausibly, I have been struck by what looks like a tension in how the profession handles delusions, and I would like help working out what the practice philosophically presupposes.

The practices I have in mind are these. First, diagnostic manuals characterize delusions as beliefs — fixed, held with conviction, resistant to counterevidence. Second, clinicians are trained never to argue a patient out of a delusion, nor to ridicule it; the advice is to acknowledge the patient's distress and the reality of the experience without endorsing or disputing the content. Third, the first-line treatment is antipsychotic medication, which sometimes dissolves the conviction without anyone ever offering the patient a single counterargument. Fourth, cognitive-behavioral therapy for psychosis does something like the opposite: the therapist and patient gently examine the evidence for and against the belief, much as one might reason with anyone who holds a false view.

Meanwhile, the theoretical literature I have read pulls in two directions. Two-factor accounts say a delusion requires both an anomalous experience that suggests the strange thought — in Capgras, plausibly, the absence of the normal feeling of familiarity on seeing a loved one — and a further failure of the capacity to evaluate and reject hypotheses. And some philosophers question whether delusions are genuine beliefs at all, noting that patients often fail to act on them consistently, keep them compartmentalized from the rest of what they know, and can live alongside them in a way real convictions rarely permit.

So my question: what conception of delusion does this constellation of practices presuppose? Does the prohibition on direct confrontation presuppose that delusions are not ordinary beliefs answerable to reasons, while the behavioral-therapy branch presupposes precisely that they are? Is the overall practice coherent, or does it rest on inconsistent commitments? Where is it underdetermined — could the same practices be licensed by several rival views, for instance if confrontation is merely ineffective or harmful rather than conceptually inappropriate? And what would have to change clinically if the field committed fully to a doxastic view, or fully to a non-doxastic one? Finally, as a matter of craft: what does your reconstruction imply about how I should write my narrator's relationship to his own conviction? Please answer in American English.
