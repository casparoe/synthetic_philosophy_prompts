I never studied philosophy or political science — I left school at sixteen and have spent twenty years working in my family's freight and customs brokerage business, which operates across two countries with very different norms around gift-giving. For years I've been turning one thought over, and I've finally written it up as a short essay. Before I show it to anyone, I'd like comments.

Here is the essay's argument in compressed form:

1. Western anti-corruption rules — including the compliance manuals my firm must now sign to keep its larger clients — treat every gift to an official as the same kind of act. I think this flattens three genuinely different things, which I call purchasing gifts (given to change a decision not yet made: a carton left at the port the week before a license ruling), maintenance gifts (given inside an ongoing relationship on a schedule set by the calendar rather than by decisions: New Year envelopes, a wedding contribution; they don't buy a specific outcome, they keep a channel open), and acknowledgment gifts (given after a favor, in discharge of a felt debt).

2. Only purchasing gifts are corrupt, because only they interfere with the official's reasons. Maintenance and acknowledgment gifts are the local machinery of trust in places where contracts and courts don't reach; abolishing them doesn't produce impartial administration, it produces administration that only the already-connected can use.

3. The strongest objection I can see gets its own section. I call it baseline drift: a stream of maintenance gifts buys no single decision, but it slowly shifts what the official regards as the normal level of consideration, until the honest trader who gives nothing starts to look insulting, then suspicious, then like someone whose paperwork deserves scrutiny. I argue baseline drift is real but manageable through norms — public schedules, capped values, reciprocity duties on the official's side — and that the societies I know managed it for generations.

4. I close by claiming the Western standard is not universal morality but the moralization of one region's solution to a problem other regions solved differently, and that exporting it through compliance regimes is itself a form of clientelism, since it channels business toward firms rich enough to afford compliance departments.

What I want comments on, in order of priority:

(a) The three-way distinction. Does it carve anything real, or is it a self-serving taxonomy built by a man who gives maintenance gifts? In particular: given my own section on baseline drift, is my claim that maintenance gifts don't change decisions actually defensible — doesn't baseline drift just show that maintenance gifts are purchasing gifts paid in installments? If the distinction survives, what does it need that I haven't given it; if it doesn't, is there a better cut nearby?

(b) Whether I've been fair to the opposing position. I suspect the strongest case against me is not "all gifts are bribes" but something like: gift-mediated administration is stable only while it stays small and personal, and collapses into pure clientelism at scale. If that's the real argument, my section 3 answers a weaker opponent. Tell me whether I've set up a straw man and what the steelman looks like.

(c) My empirical confidence. I generalize freely from "the societies I know," which honestly means two countries and my grandfather's stories. Point out every place where the essay's rhetoric runs ahead of what someone in my position could honestly claim to know.

(d) The closing move in section 4, which I suspect is the weakest part: it feels satisfying but may be nothing more than a tu quoque. Say whether it stands on its own, and if not, whether the essay is better off ending at section 3.

(e) Anything structurally confusing about the sequence — I wrote the sections in the order I thought of them, which may not be the order a reader needs.

Please be direct. I would rather hear now that the central distinction is indefensible than publish it and be told so later.
