Act as a philosopher of biology with practical experience advising field researchers and citizen-science groups. Think carefully rather than giving a textbook overview. Begin with a brief brainstorming section identifying the conceptual distinctions that should matter here, and only then provide your rankings.

I recently became responsible for the catalogue at my neighborhood seed library, so a dispute I previously regarded as theoretical has become practically important. We must decide which of two bean varieties to describe as fitter in the catalogue and in a grant application. That description will affect which variety dozens of local gardeners plant next year.

The evidence is mixed:

- In raw counts from last season, Variety A produced an average of 23 mature seeds per plant, while Variety B produced 19.
- Variety A was unintentionally planted more often in irrigated raised beds. After grouping plants by soil, watering, and sun exposure, A averages 16 viable offspring per plant and B averages 21.
- A performs substantially better in unusually dry years, while B performs substantially better in wet years. Gardeners disagree about whether next year’s conditions, the recent local climate, or a changing long-range climate distribution is the relevant environment.
- A produces more seeds, but a smaller fraction germinate. B produces fewer seeds, but they germinate more reliably and their offspring are likelier to reproduce.
- Over several generations, A’s descendants appear more vulnerable to a recurring mildew, although individual A plants can be spectacularly successful when they avoid it.

Please evaluate the following six interpretations of what A is fitter than B could mean:

1. Actual reproductive success: the organisms or varieties that in fact left more viable offspring during the observed period are fitter.
2. Single-generation propensity: a variety’s fitness is its expected number of viable offspring per plant, relative to a specified distribution of environments and controlling for accidental placement.
3. Multigenerational lineage propensity: fitness is the expected contribution of a heritable type to the population over several specified generations, including the reproductive performance of descendants.
4. Trait-centered causal fitness: fitness is the degree to which heritable traits cause reproductive differences, whether or not those traits happen to predict success under current conditions.
5. Environment-independent capacity: fitness is an intrinsic robustness or capacity for reproductive success across the full range of biologically possible environments.
6. Operational predictive fitness: fitness is whatever combination of measurable traits best predicts future representation in the population, even if it is not itself the cause of reproductive success.

For each interpretation, give separate scores from 0 to 10 for:

- conceptual adequacy as an account of biological fitness;
- resistance to the objection that survival of the fittest merely means survival of those that survive;
- usability by a small organization with incomplete records and no controlled experiment.

Then produce an overall ranking using weights of 45 percent for conceptual adequacy, 30 percent for resistance to the tautology objection, and 25 percent for usability. Show the weighted scores. If one interpretation can be repaired by explicitly specifying a population, environmental distribution, time horizon, or unit of selection, explain exactly how.

Apply your top three interpretations to the bean case and state whether each makes A fitter, B fitter, or the comparison indeterminate on the available evidence. Conclude by recommending what our catalogue should say. It must be scientifically honest, intelligible to gardeners, and avoid presenting a contingent prediction as though it were a definition of fitness. Please do not include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list.
