My teacher keeps saying the outbreak of the war was "overdetermined" — meaning there were so many factors pushing toward war (the alliance system, the naval arms race between Britain and Germany, imperial rivalries, nationalism in the Balkans, the whole logic of mobilization timetables, etc.) that basicly any one of them on its own would have been enough to cause a war sooner or later, even if all the others hadn't existed. She said the assassination of Franz Ferdinand was just the spark, and if it hadn't happened, some other crisis would've lit the fuse eventually.

Here's what's confusing me though. If each of these factors was enough to cause the war on its own, then taking any one of them away wouldn't have stopped the war. But if removing something doesn't change the outcome, in what sense did it cause the outcome? Like, if two people shoot someone at the exact same time and either shot alone would've been fatal, you can't say "without his shot, the guy would've lived," because that's false — so my instinct is neither shooter really caused the death in the normal sense. By that logic, doesn't overdetermination actually mean that none of the so-called causes of WWI are real causes? Which would make my teacher's essay assignment on "the causes of WWI" kind of pointless, since by her own reasoning there aren't any, just a bunch of stuff that happened beforehand.

I talked about this with my friend at lunch and he said I'm overthinking it, but I honestly can't see where my mistake is. Am I missing something about how causation works, or is "overdetermined" just a word historians use that doesn't actually make sense if you push on it?
