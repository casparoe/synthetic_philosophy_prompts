I'm in the second year of a PhD in political philosophy (working on democratic backsliding, mostly) and I keep running into the line about the arc of history bending toward justice. It came up again at a workshop last week: I was presenting on institutional erosion in Eastern Europe, and in the Q&A a senior academic basically waved the whole problem away with it -- the implication being that backsliding is temporary noise on a long enough view, so alarm is overwrought. You also see it on protest placards and in op-eds, where it seems to be doing almost the opposite work, i.e. geeing people up to push harder. Same sentence, seemingly opposite morals.

So: what precise claims could that one-liner actually be making? I can gesture at a few candidate readings myself -- a straight empirical trend claim about moral progress; a statistical claim about variance and mean reversion; some kind of Hegelian or providential teleology where history has a direction built in; a claim about moral epistemology (that over time our moral judgements converge on the truth the way (allegedly) scientific ones do); or maybe just a normative exhortation dressed up as a prediction, so "bends toward" really means "ought to be bent by us". And possibly there's a reading where "long run" makes it unfalsifiable, since any setback can be absorbed as a local wobble. But I'm sure I've missed some, and I definately can't yet say which of these are platitudes, which are substantive but defensible, and which are just false or confused.

Could you lay the readings out as a menu, give a verdict on each, and -- this is the bit I actually need -- tell me which reading(s) would have to be true for the workshop use to work, i.e. for it to license complacency about democratic erosion? And which reading(s) the placard-wavers need instead? If it turns out the slogan can only do the comforting job on a reading that's false or empty, that's a useful thing to be able to say in a paper, so please be explicit about it rather than just gesturing at the ambiguity.
