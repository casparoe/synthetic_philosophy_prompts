I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about biological individuality by going on my gut verdicts about cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, lies behind them. A clonal stand of quaking aspen sharing one root system strikes me as a single organism, its trunks like so many branches. A Portuguese man o' war is likewise one organism to me, even though it is composed of many specialized zooids, since the zooids cannot survive independently. A Dictyostelium slug that aggregates out of genetically unrelated amoebae is, while it lasts, one organism too, despite its internal genetic heterogeneity. But a lichen is two organisms in intimate symbiosis, not one -- even for species in which neither partner is ever found free-living in nature. And a human being together with her gut microbiome is one organism plus a great many residents: the holobiont is not an organism, whatever the functional integration. The honeybee colony is the case I keep going back and forth on: it thermoregulates, makes colony-level foraging decisions, and concentrates reproduction in a single queen, which feels organismal, yet I resist calling it one organism outright. Is there a coherent principle here -- metabolic or developmental integration, being a unit of selection, immunological self-maintenance, or some combination -- and which of my verdicts would it force me to give up? In particular, I am uneasy that my man o' war verdict leans on inseparability while my lichen verdict denies it, and that my hesitation about the bee colony may be inconsistent with both. Please state your candidate principle(s) precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, and say what the resulting account predicts for two cases I have not listed: a mycorrhizal fungal network that shuttles carbon between the roots of several trees, and a human tetragametic chimera formed when two embryos fuse in the womb.
