I'm putting together a "which theory of originals do you actually hold?" quiz for a museum-studies reading group, to be taken before we've read anything, so it has to sort people by their reactions to cases rather than by labels. Please write an eight-question quiz where each question presents a concrete scenario involving a copy, fake, or restoration, followed by two or three answer options phrased as first-person reactions to the scenario (never as "do you believe aesthetic value supervenes on perceptible properties?"). The quiz must be able to sort takers into at least these five positions: (a) strict perceptualism, on which a truly indistinguishable copy has exactly the aesthetic value of the original; (b) origin-constitutive views, on which facts about who made a work, when, and how are part of what the work is, so even perfect copies differ in value; (c) the achievement view, on which the original's special value lies in the creative performance it records rather than in the object; (d) the deception view, on which forgery is a moral wrong that contaminates our experience of the work, but an honestly labeled perfect copy loses nothing; and (e) the view that originals matter only for non-aesthetic reasons (historical evidence, market value) and that anyone who reports an aesthetic difference is confusing themselves. Required scenarios, worked in wherever you like: a celebrated painting revealed to be a modern forger's work; a molecule-for-molecule duplicate produced by a non-deceptive process; a copy painted by the original artist later in life; and a work whose visible surface is now mostly restorer's work. After the questions, give a scoring key mapping answer patterns to positions, and flag any pair of positions your quiz cannot reliably separate, saying honestly why the cases can't pry them apart. Finish with a short section on the unoccupied regions of the logical space: which combinations of answers correspond to no recognizable position, and for each whether it looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.
