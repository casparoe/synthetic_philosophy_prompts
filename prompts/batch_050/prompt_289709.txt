My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the two-envelope paradox and reached an impasse that I now think would make an excellent exam question. Our dispute was roughly this: she insists the paradox is fully dissolved the moment you fix any proper probability distribution over the envelope amounts, because the switching argument then cannot go through for every possible observed value; I maintain that something paradoxical survives even with a prior in place, since there are distributions (for instance, ones where P(X = 2^n) is proportional to 3^-n) under which the expected value of the other envelope exceeds yours no matter which finite amount you observe, and the puzzle is then to explain why switching is not in fact rational. We could not agree on whether the flaw in the switching argument is a flaw in the probability theory (a covertly improper prior, an illicit conditional expectation) or a flaw in the decision theory (treating "the amount in the other envelope" as a fixed quantity when its distribution depends on what you hold, or applying dominance reasoning where the relevant expectations are infinite).

Draft one essay question on this dispute for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of probability, answerable in 50 minutes, that requires candidates to take and defend a position on which of us is right rather than merely recounting the paradox. The question should be impossible to answer well by summarizing the standard setup; it should force candidates to (a) state the switching argument precisely enough that its invalid step can be located, and (b) engage with the distinction between the closed-envelope version (no observation made) and the open-envelope version (you see your amount before deciding), since my friend and I disagreed about whether these even raise the same problem.

Provide, alongside the question:

A model answer of roughly the length a strong candidate could produce in the allotted time, which clearly locates the illicit step in the switching argument, explains why no proper prior can make switching favorable conditional on every observed value while acknowledging that distributions exist where it is favorable conditional on almost every value, and adjudicates the dispute between my friend and me with an actual verdict rather than a both-sides summary.
2. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, stating the marks each is worth out of 20 total, with a one-line note on the characteristic way weaker candidates fumble each point (for example, the candidate who thinks the paradox is just about risk aversion, or who claims expectations are undefined without showing why).

Close with a single sentence giving your verdict on the original dispute between my friend and me, as the very last line, in the form \answer{...}.
