Had a massive row with a friend last night about the seperateness of persons objection to utilitarianism and it's still bugging me. My position was that the objection is basically right: you can't justify torturing one person by pointing to tiny gains spread across a million others, because nobody actually gets the summed-up good. He countered with what he called his "prudence argument", and I'm annoyed to say I couldn't find the hole in it at the time. It went roughly like this:

(1) The separateness objection says a loss to one person can't be made good by gains to other people, because there is no single subject who experiences the combined total.
(2) But there's no single subject who experiences the combined total across one life either. The person-stage suffering through Monday's root canal is not strictly the person-stage enjoying healthy teeth ten years later; they're just suitably connected stages.
(3) So if (1) is sound, ordinary prudence is illegitimate too: putting up with a harm now for a bigger benefit later trades a loss to one stage for a gain to a different stage, and no stage enjoys the sum.
(4) Prudence is obviously fine. We do it constantly and it would be mad not to.
(5) So (1) is false: the separateness objection proves too much, since if it worked it would wreck prudence. Aggregation across persons is just prudence without the prejudice about which container the costs and benefits land in.

I'm fairly sure I don't want to concede (5). But when I tried saying the cases differ because in the prudence case the sufferer gets compensated by her own later gain, he shot back that "compensation" is doing all the work there, and that a separateness objector can't just help themselves to it without begging the question against him. I want to keep three things: that prudential trade-offs within a life are fine, that numbers still count sometimes between lives (I'd want the rescuer to save the five rather than the one), and that torturing one for millions of trivial gains really is wrong. What I presumably can't keep, on pain of agreeing with him, is that aggregation across persons is exactly as innocent as aggregation across times. So: which step do you actually reject, why does it look so plausible at first glance, and what does rejecting it cost me? If honest analysis means giving up one of the things I want to keep, tell me straight rather than rescuing everything at the price of mush. Also, please answer in British English.
