I sit on the professional conduct commitee of a mid-sized engineering society (we can censure or expel members) and I have to bring a recommendation to our next meeting. Here's the case.

One of our members, a senior engineer at a medical device firm, spent about two years quietly copying internal test data because she believed her bosses were massaging failure-rate numbers on a product already on the market. She never used the internal reporting channel and never went to the regulator. Then she got passed over for a promotion, and two months later sent everything to a journalist — and also to a rival firm, whose job offer she accepted shortly after. The reporting checked out: the product's been recalled, two executives resigned, nobody seriously disputes the disclosure did real public good.

The complaint in front of us isn't from the firm, though. It's from her former teammate. He co-developed much of the test methodology and had shared his own unpublished analysis with her under an explicit promise of confidentiality — he has the email where she promises. That material ended up in the article and apparently at the rival firm. His own reputation got scorched by association, and some of his now-leaked methods can't be patented. He wants her sanctioned for breach of professional confidence.

The committee keeps circling two things. She swears she'd have leaked eventually anyway and the promotion snub only changed the timing; he says that's awfully convenient and the whole thing was a grudge wearing a whistleblower costume. Second, our ethics code protects members who reveal genuine dangers to the public, full stop — it's silent on motives, on collateral harm to innocent third parties, and on leaking to competitors.

Write the memo. Recommend we dismiss the complaint, sanction her, or land somewhere in between (e.g., no action on the public-interest disclosure but censure for the competitor leak or the broken promise to the colleague). Give the actual reasoning, and take seriously the strongest version of whichever side you rule against, because we're genuinely split and a hand-wavy memo will get torn apart. Also weigh in on the code revision we'll have to do either way: should protection track what the disclosure accomplished, what the discloser was trying to do, or who got hurt along the way?
