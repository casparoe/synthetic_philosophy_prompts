Next spring I'm teaching a senior seminar in philosophy of mind on computationalism, and before we read a single theory of physical computation I want to hand the students a specification document: what any adequate account of what it is for a physical system to perform a computation must deliver, if the thesis that the brain is a computer is to be a substantive empirical hypothesis rather than a vacuous redescription. The occasion for the seminar is my own longstanding worry about triviality arguments: if, under some mapping of physical states to computational states, a wall or a pail of water can be described as implementing any finite computation you like, then the claim that the brain computes threatens to carry no more content than the claim that the brain is a physical system. I want the desiderata list built around exactly this pressure point. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether it strikes you as non-negotiable for any theory worth the name or as a preference of a particular camp (representationalists, mechanists, defenders of purely causal or syntactic accounts).

Please make sure the list engages at least the following, though you should feel free to add requirements I've missed. The theory must explain why attributions of computation in actual computational neuroscience are confirmable and refutable by experiment, rather than being true come what may; it must deliver a principled verdict on the pail-of-water and wall cases, and that verdict had better not make every rock a mind; it must handle analog and continuous systems without either excluding them by fiat or letting everything in; and it must say something sensible about whether the intentions of a designer matter — here is a test case I invented for the seminar and would like treated explicitly: suppose a neuromorphic chip functionally indistinguishable from a commercial processor were produced by a freak accident of lightning in a junkyard, with no programmer and no intended interpretation. Does it compute? If the theory says yes, it owes us an account of what fixes which computation it performs; if no, it owes us an account of why computational explanation in biology, where there is no designer, is legitimate at all. I would also like one desideratum connecting the theory to explanation: a good account should illuminate why computational descriptions of the brain explain anything at all, rather than merely relabeling causal facts we already had.

Close the document with two things. First, flag any subset of the desiderata you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable, with a reasoned argument — if, say, non-triviality, designer-independence, and explanatory payoffs cannot all be had at once, that is itself a result I want the students to see. Second, score the main families of accounts (simple mapping views, causal-mechanistic views, and semantic or content-involving views) briefly against the list, and identify the single desideratum that knocks out the most contenders. No citations or bibliography, please; I'll supply the readings myself once I see which requirements survive.
