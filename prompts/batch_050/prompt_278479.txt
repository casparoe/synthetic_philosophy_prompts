I am marking first-year undergraduate essays for my aesthetics module, and I would like a second opinion on one script before I finalise the grade. The question set was: "Are musical works identical to their performances? In answering, explain the type–token distinction as applied to music, and discuss what role scores and recordings play in identifying a work." Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

"Musical works are not identical to their performances. The standard view is that a work such as Beethoven's Ninth Symphony is a type, while any particular evening's performance of it is a token of that type. This explains how we can speak coherently of many performances of the same work, and how a flawed performance is still a performance of the Ninth rather than of some other, inferior work. Nelson Goodman, the classic source for this approach, held that musical works are eternal, uncreated Platonic objects which composers discover rather than create; on his view, a work is brought into the world when a composer first performs it. The score plays the role of a set of instructions: it tells performers what to do, and a performance is a token of the work whenever the performers intend to follow that score, regardless of how accurately they do so. Recordings are then simply further tokens of the work, on a par with live performances: when I play a CD of the Ninth, I am hearing the work itself, just as I would in the concert hall. A consequence of the type view is that the work exists independently of any of its instances, so the destruction of every score and recording of the Ninth would not destroy the work itself. I conclude that works and performances are distinct, and that anyone who conflates them has simply failed to grasp the type–token distinction."
