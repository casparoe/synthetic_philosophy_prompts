I am a retired physicist who has taken up the philosophy of technology in recent years, and I keep encountering a pattern for which I have no word. It is not the familiar case of a device designed to discriminate, nor that of a neutral tool put to bad ends. It is this: a technology that is, considered purely as a piece of engineering, morally inert, yet which can only be operated under a particular kind of social arrangement, so that adopting it amounts to quietly choosing that arrangement.

Two instances. A large nuclear power station cannot be run by a loose federation of amateurs; it demands a centralized, disciplined, security-conscious hierarchy wherever it is built, and any society that adopts it will grow such a hierarchy whether or not anyone intended this. Household solar panels, by contrast, function equally well under almost any ownership structure, while district-scale heating networks pay off only if residents are bound into a shared scheme. One technology is indifferent to its society; the other selects one.

Note what the phenomenon is not: no designer is smuggling in an agenda, and the effect is no accident of misuse; it follows from what the machine needs in order to work. Does this have an established name? If so, I would like to know how it differs from neighboring notions — from technologies that merely have political side-effects, and from the broader thesis that technology drives history — since I suspect different writers draw the boundaries differently. If it has no name, what would you call it, and where would you draw its edges: must the social requirement be strict, or does strong pressure suffice?
