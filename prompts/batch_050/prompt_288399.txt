In my graduate seminar on Nietzsche’s On the Genealogy of Morality, discussion has stalled over what kind of argument a genealogy is supposed to be. One group treats it primarily as historical debunking: if guilt, conscience, humility, and unconditional equality arose through resentment, domination, and contingent struggles for power, then their authority is weakened. Another says this confuses explanation with refutation; a value can have disturbing origins and still be true or justified. A third treats genealogy as exposing a mismatch between morality’s official self-image and the psychology actually sustaining it: morality presents itself as impartial, freely chosen, or universally commanded, while the genealogist presents it as satisfying needs for revenge, self-protection, or self-discipline. A fourth says Nietzsche’s causal claims matter only indirectly, as materials for an evaluative perspective concerned with health, strength, and the enhancement of life.

To clarify the disagreement, I proposed a two-key lock model. A genealogy has critical force only if two independent mechanisms are established: a causal mechanism explaining how a value or practice emerged and persisted, and a legitimating mechanism explaining why those causal facts should alter our confidence in its authority. The first key alone is history; the second alone is normative argument. My hypothesis is that the seminar’s apparent disagreement about Nietzsche is really disagreement about the second key. But I may be mistaken.

Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this is. Is it mainly historical—about what mechanisms actually produced Christian morality and bad conscience—philosophical—about when causal origins can affect epistemic or moral authority—or interpretive—about whether Nietzsche intends genealogy as explanation, debunking, self-understanding critique, or evaluation? Is any substantial part merely verbal, arising because origin, cause, function, and justification are being used interchangeably?

Use a counterfactual source test to sharpen the diagnosis. Suppose the same moral judgments had originated from beneficent and truth-sensitive processes. Would the historical debunkers accept them as legitimate? Suppose they had originated from exactly the processes Nietzsche describes but were later supported by independent arguments. Would genealogy still matter? Conversely, suppose a value’s official self-image were false even though the value reliably promoted admirable lives. Would the self-understanding reading still count this as a criticism? Explain what the competing positions imply in each case.

Finally, state what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that must be made, a historical or psychological question that could be investigated, a philological issue that needs resolution, or an argument showing that no further resolution is possible. Do not give a general survey of interpretations of Nietzsche. Where scholars broadly agree, identify that as settled; where competent interpreters genuinely divide, identify the dispute as contested rather than presenting one reading as the consensus.
