I am the managing director of a mid-sized philanthropic foundation. Under our fast-track charter, each of our nine program officers may commit up to $2 million to a single project without consulting the others, on the theory that the expected cost of a missed opportunity exceeds the cost of the occasional bad grant. Last month this arrangement produced its first genuine catastrophe: one officer, acting alone and in perfectly good faith, funded a small open-air field trial of a solar-radiation experiment in the territorial waters of a country whose government had not been notified. The science was defensible; the diplomatic and reputational fallout was not. I have since learned that two other officers had seen essentially the same proposal cross their desks and quietly declined it. The board has asked me to circulate a decision memo before next month's meeting recommending whether to retain, abolish, or modify unilateral fast-track authority.

In preparing, I have been reading about what some writers call the unilateralist's curse: when several agents can each independently initiate the same hard-to-reverse action, and their private estimates of its value differ, the action will tend to occur precisely when it should not, because it is the most optimistic estimate, not the median one, that pulls the trigger. The proposed remedy is that a rational unilateralist should discount their own favorable estimate in proportion to how many peers had the chance to act and refrained. I find the argument compelling and disturbing in equal measure, because it seems to prove too much: it would have instructed our officer to defer to colleagues he was under no obligation to consult, and taken literally it appears to imply that whoever hesitates longest ends up setting policy for everyone.

Please draft the memo. It should do the following:

1. Recommend a concrete decision procedure the board can actually vote on — whether that means preserving the status quo, requiring the initiating officer to obtain a fixed number of countersignatures, instituting a short mandatory structured canvass of the other officers before any qualifying commitment, or some hybrid — and defend that recommendation against the strongest alternative.
2. Give an honest hearing to the case for keeping unilateral authority intact: the value of speed and of idiosyncratic conviction, the fact that committees have failure modes of their own (the most persuasive optimist dominating the room, diffusion of responsibility), and the worry that any consent requirement quietly converts the curse into its mirror image, in which the most pessimistic officer holds an effective veto and cautious projects die by default.
3. Say what the analysis implies for individual conduct while the current rules remain in force — that is, what guidance the board should issue to officers in the interim, given that merely instructing them to be more careful presumably changes nothing.
4. Address how the recommended procedure should scale: whether the stringency of required consultation should vary with the irreversibility and external visibility of the action, and if so, how to specify that in a rule officers can apply in an afternoon rather than a forty-page taxonomy.

Keep it to the length and register of a real board memo: decisive at the top, readable by intelligent non-philosophers, and candid about the strongest consideration weighing against its own recommendation.
