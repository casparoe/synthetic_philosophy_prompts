Hey! I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about why a certain corner of the betting/quant-finance world talks about money in a way that seems totally alien to how economists do.

Here's what I keep tripping over. Standard economic theory says a rational agent maximizes expected value (or expected utility, if you're fancy). But the Kelly-criterion crowd says you should instead maximize the expected logarithm of wealth — equivilantly, the long-run geometric growth rate — and they say this even about bets where the expected-value-maximizing play would be to bet big. And lately I've been hearing a stronger argument (the "ergodicity economics" pitch) that this isn't just risk aversion in disguise: the claim is that when payoffs multiply over time, the average across parallel possible worlds (the ensemble average) and the average one actual person experiences over time (the time average) genuinely come apart, so chasing expected value can systematically ruin almost everyone who really plays.

So here's what I'd love from you: a careful comparison of expected-utility maximization and Kelly/geometric-mean maximization as rival accounts of rational choice under risk. Specifically:

1. In what precise sense does the Kelly strategy beat any "essentially different" strategy almost surely in the long run, and what assumptions is that claim quietly depending on (repeated independent bets, known probabilities, unlimited horizon, no ability to pool risk with others)?
2. Is the ergodicity argument a real rival to expected utility theory, or is it just a rediscovery of the old point that utility is concave in wealth (i.e., log utility all over again)? Where does the genuine disagreement live, if there is one?
3. If geometric rationality is so compelling, why can a rational agent still sometimes rightly take the higher-expected-value option — one-shot decisions, insurance and risk-pooling, or a small enough bet relative to total wealth?

Then give me a verdict: if you had to tell my readers which framework deserves to be called the rational one, which would you pick and why — and what's the strongest thing the losing side can still say in its defense? Don't just cheer for Kelly; I've read enough True Believer blog posts on this already.
