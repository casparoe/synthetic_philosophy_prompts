Before I circulate the position statement for my graduate seminar on practical reason, I want it audited for coherence rather than defended. My commitments are these:

(1) Reasons internalism, in roughly Williams's sense: an agent has a reason to φ only if she could come to be motivated to φ by some sound deliberative route starting from her existing motivational set.

(2) The standard correction for ignorance: a deliberative route counts as sound only if it proceeds from true beliefs, so the agent who mistakes petrol for gin has no reason to drink what is in the glass, whatever she actually wants.

(3) Internalism does not make moral reasons hostage to moral motivation: even someone wholly devoid of concern for others — no sympathy, no guilt, no standing disposition toward fairness — still has reason not to harm them.

(4) We may legitimately criticize people for failing to act on reasons they have; the language of reasons is not merely descriptive of what moves them.

(5) Sound deliberation is not exhausted by means-end calculation plus the correction of factual error: it includes imagination, reflection on what one's existing commitments actually involve, and the acquisition of new motivations in the process — otherwise internalism would be as restrictive as its critics claim, and it is not.

Tell me which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to, with the genuine tensions ordered by severity and, for each, exactly what would have to give. In particular, I want a view on whether (5) can do the work I need it to do for (3) without exacting a price elsewhere in the set. Please take a definite position on the single cheapest revision — which commitment should go or be weakened, and why — rather than laying out the menu of options for me to choose from.
