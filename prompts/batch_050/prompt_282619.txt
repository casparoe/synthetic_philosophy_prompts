I am writing the opening chapter of a book on the problem of evil, and I want the chapter to do one thing only: to state, before any particular theodicy is examined, what a theodicy would have to accomplish in order to count as successful. The literature seems to me almost never to do this. Authors present their favoured theodicy, defend it against objections, and move on, without ever saying what question a theodicy is supposed to answer or by what standard candidate answers are to be judged. I want the specification itself, written for a reader who will go on to assess actual theodicies against it.

The chapter is anchored to a passage from Part XI of David Hume's Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (1779). Cleanthes has just proposed that a finitely powerful deity might explain the world's evil, and Philo replies (I have kept the original spellings):

"Did I show you a house or palace, where there was not one apartment convenient or agreeable; where the windows, doors, fires, passages, stairs, and the whole œconomy of the building, were the source of noise, confusion, fatigue, darkness, and the extremes of heat and cold; you would certainly blame the contrivance, without any farther examination. The architect would in vain display his subtilty, and prove to you, that if this door or that window were altered, greater ills would ensue. What he says may be strictly true: The alteration of one particular, while the other parts of the building remain, may only augment the inconveniencies. But still you would assert in general, that, if the architect had had skill and good intentions, he might have formed such a plan of the whole, and might have adjusted the parts in such a manner, as would have remedied all or most of these inconveniencies. His ignorance, or even your own ignorance of such a plan, will never convince you of the impossibility of it. If you find many inconveniencies and deformities in the building, you will always, without entering into any detail, condemn the architect."

Begin by interpreting this passage as the seed of the specification. It contains, I think, two demands that can come apart, and I want them disentangled: a demand about the shape of a justification (that it must justify a plan of the whole rather than excuse each defect piecemeal) and a demand about the use of ignorance (that our inability to envisage a better plan cannot be treated as evidence that no better plan exists). State precisely what requirement each imposes on a would-be theodicy, whether each demand is fair, and whether a theodicist could accept the first while resisting the second. Note also what the passage does not demand: since Philo concedes that altering any single feature may make matters worse, the passage does not license the crude requirement that God should have removed any particular evil while holding everything else fixed.

Then give the full desiderata list: the requirements an adequate theodicy must satisfy, stated as requirements rather than as refutations of named authors. For each one, give the case or phenomenon that forces it, explain why it is a genuine constraint rather than the prejudice of one camp, and say whether it is negotiable. The list must include, at a minimum:

- the requirement or requirements extracted from the passage above;
- a requirement covering sufferers to whom the theodicy's favoured good does not apply: animals, infants, and the severely cognitively impaired exercise no morally significant freedom and, on many views, build no character, so a theodicy resting on free will or on soul-making must either extend to them or frankly confess its limited scope;
- a requirement of consistency with moral practice: a theodicy whose truth would give us reason to stop preventing or relieving evil fails, whatever its explanatory merits;
- a requirement drawing the distinction between justification and excuse — between showing that God's permission of evil is compatible with his goodness and showing that the permission was right — together with a view on which of the two a theodicy, as distinct from a mere defence, actually owes us;
- a requirement concerning distribution: suffering is not apportioned according to desert, and a theodicy must neither quietly entail that it is nor answer for the species as a whole while saying nothing to the individual sufferer;
- a requirement that the theodicy not merely relocate the problem: if evil is the price of freedom, of law-governed nature, or of soul-making, the theodicy must explain why the price had to be this high and paid by these people — otherwise the problem has been moved rather than answered.

Finally, put the list to work. Which single requirement eliminates the largest number of the standard candidates — free-will theodicies, soul-making theodicies, greater-good or aesthetic theodicies, and the sceptical-theist response (flagging any of these that is not, strictly speaking, a theodicy at all)? And are any two requirements on the list jointly unsatisfiable? If they are, say so plainly and state what follows for the project of theodicy as such, since that would be the chapter's real result.

Write in British English, roughly 1,500 words excluding the quoted passage. A table is acceptable for the scoring only; the requirements themselves must be argued in prose, with the motivating case behind each. No bibliography and no citations: I want the criteria and the reasoning for them, not a survey of who holds what.
