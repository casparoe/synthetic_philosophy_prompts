I'm a high school senior writing a case analysis for our school's philosophy and politics society, and I'd like you to produce the full draft. The case is the franchise of the 2016 UK referendum on EU membership, and the lens is the boundary problem in democratic theory: the question of who belongs to the demos before the demos is allowed to decide anything.

Please treat the following facts as given. The referendum used essentially the general-election franchise: British citizens resident in the UK could vote, as could Irish and qualifying Commonwealth citizens resident in the UK. Citizens of other EU member states living in the UK could not. British citizens living abroad could vote only if they had been registered in the UK within the previous fifteen years. Sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds could not vote, even though they had been allowed to vote in the 2014 Scottish independence referendum. The outcome was a narrow win for Leave.

For the theory, work with the three standard answers to the boundary question: the all-affected interests principle, the all-coerced (or all-subjected) principle, and the status-quo principle that the demos is the existing citizenry. Please do not cite particular authors or papers; refer to the positions by name and I will fill in references myself.

The analysis I want, in order:

1. A disciplined mapping. For each disputed group -- long-term expatriates, resident EU citizens, sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds, and citizens of other EU states whose lives Brexit would plainly affect -- state what verdict each of the three principles delivers. Where a principle gives no determinate verdict because it needs a metric it doesn't supply (how affected is affected enough? which coercive relations count?), say so explicitly instead of forcing an answer.

2. The pressure points. The all-affected principle seems to enfranchise much of a continent; the all-coerced principle seems to disenfranchise expatriates whose legal situation Brexit would directly change, while enfranchising resident EU citizens whom the actual franchise excluded. Explain whether these are reductios of the principles or just uncomfortable conclusions.

3. The regress made concrete. The franchise was chosen by a Parliament that was itself elected by a demos whose boundary nobody voted on. Is this a real instance of the boundary problem's circularity, and does the episode suggest the regress is vicious in practice, benign, or quietly solved by convention?

4. The reverse direction. What does the episode show about democratic theory itself? Consider specifically: does the fact that the franchise decision was political, and that a different but defensible franchise could plausibly have changed the outcome, show that the boundary question is constitutional rather than philosophical -- or does one of the three principles emerge from this case looking best?

Keep the tone academic but pitched so a strong high school reader can follow it, and flag clearly anywhere the theory runs out and the answer is genuinely undetermined.
