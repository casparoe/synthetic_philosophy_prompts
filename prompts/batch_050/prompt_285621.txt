After a long and unresolved argument with a friend last night, I need an outside referee. The dispute concerned evolutionary debunking arguments against moral realism. I had been pressing the familiar genealogical worry -- that our moral convictions are products of selection pressures rather than of any sensitivity to stance-independent moral facts -- when she turned the strategy around and claimed that any such argument defeats itself. Her case ran as follows:

(1) Our moral beliefs are largely the causal product of natural selection, which shaped them for their effects on cooperation and fitness, not for their correspondence with mind-independent moral truths.
(2) When the best explanation of why we hold a belief makes no appeal to that belief's truth, the belief is unjustified unless we possess independent grounds for thinking it true.
(3) Any grounds we could offer for our moral beliefs would themselves draw on further moral beliefs, so the confirmation is circular.
(4) Therefore our moral beliefs are unjustified.
(5) The same genealogy holds for our beliefs about epistemic norms -- including the norm stated in (2) -- since what we take ourselves to have reason to believe was likewise shaped by selection for fitness rather than for normative correctness.
(6) Any grounds offered in support of (2) rely on further epistemic judgments, so its confirmation is circular in exactly the same way.
(7) Therefore (2) is itself unjustified.
(8) So the debunking argument either rests on a premise it is not entitled to, or proves too much by sweeping away the very notion of justified belief it depends on; either way, it defeats itself and poses no threat to moral realism.

Each step seems defensible when I examine it individually, yet I cannot accept the conclusion -- it would mean that a whole research program in metaethics can be dismissed in eight lines, and that moral realism emerges unscathed essentially by a trick of self-reference. My friend insists I must name a step to reject, and I find I cannot. I would rather retain (2), which strikes me as a genuine and intuitive constraint on justification, than accept that genealogical challenges are uniformly toothless -- but I do not know what that retention costs me.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each no more than a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion: first, identify the single step you would reject; second, explain why that step seemed compelling in the first place; third, state what rejecting it costs, and to whom; fourth, say whether a debunker could simply concede the self-defeat charge and still undermine moral belief (for instance, by running the argument as a reductio aimed only at robust realism); fifth, say where this leaves the moral realist -- vindicated, or merely unrefuted. Write in American English.
