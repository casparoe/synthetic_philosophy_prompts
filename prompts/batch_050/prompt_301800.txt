I'm in my final year of secondary school and taking a philosophy elective on social ontology. This term we've been looking at whether social categories like "generations" (Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and so on) pick out anything real or are just marketing labels with a veneer of science. Our teacher sets practice essays and then has us peer-mark each other's work, with a grade out of 20 and written comments on each part. I've been assigned a classmate's answer and I've formed a provisional view of it, but I'd rather not anchor you, so I won't share my grade or comments. For context, the essay question was set around a framework of three tests for "kind-worthiness" that our teacher developed for the course: (1) Inductive support — does membership in the kind let you make reliable inferences you couldn't make without it? (2) Boundary structure — do the kind's boundaries track genuine causal discontinuities rather than arbitrary lines? (3) Classification effects — does applying the label to people change the people so labelled, and if so, does that strengthen or undermine the kind's claim to reality? The question itself was: "Are 'generations' real kinds? Apply all three tests, distinguish being socially constructed from being unreal, and draw on at least one thinker we studied."

Here is the answer I'm marking, reproduced in full:

"Generations are real kinds, but their reality is social rather than natural. Being socially constructed does not make something unreal: money is socially constructed, yet it supports prediction and explanation, and generations are similar in this respect. Test 1: marketers and employers reliably predict attitudes and behaviour from cohort membership, which shows the kind supports inductive inference. Test 2: the boundaries are admittedly conventional — there is no principled reason Gen Z should begin in 1997 rather than 1996 — but conventional boundaries can still be real, just as the border between two countries is conventional yet real. Moreover, as Mannheim argued, a generation is constituted by the shared birth years of its members, since those years fix who experienced which historical events at which age. Test 3: once people are labelled 'Millennial', they come to see themselves that way and behave accordingly; the label loops back and reinforces the very pattern it describes. This looping shows that generations were real all along, since the labels must have latched onto pre-existing differences in order to amplify them. Finally, many supposed generational differences dissolve on inspection: what looks like a cohort effect is often an age effect (young people have always been less trusting of institutions) or a period effect (everyone adopted smartphones at roughly the same time). Since generations pass all three tests, they are real kinds, on a par with natural kinds such as biological species."
