I have a set of intuitions about how one ought to revise one's beliefs upon discovering disagreement, and I would like to know whether a single principled account can deliver all of them — and if not, which intuition should be sacrificed. The cases below are all ones in which I initially take the other party to be roughly my epistemic peer: someone with comparable intelligence, comparable evidence, and comparable care.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. The restaurant bill. My friend and I each compute our shares of a check independently. I arrive at $47; she announces $43. I regard her as just as careful at mental arithmetic as I am. My verdict: I should split the difference and settle at about $45, pending rechecking.

2. The sleep-deprived colleague. A colleague whose judgment I normally rate as equal to my own tells me he believes a bridge design is safe; I had concluded it is not. I then learn that he has slept three hours a night all week. My verdict: I owe him no conciliation whatsoever; his sleeplessness breaks the symmetry between us.

3. The locked door. I seem to remember clearly that I locked the door this morning; my spouse, equally reliable in such matters, seems to remember clearly that I did not. My verdict: my confidence should drop substantially — to roughly a coin flip — even though my own memory still feels vivid from the inside.

4. The philosophy seminar. A colleague I respect as my epistemic peer defends moral realism; I defend anti-realism. We have read the same literature and know each other's arguments. My verdict: I am not rationally required to move toward 50/50. If I were, the same demand would seem to apply to my belief about how to respond to disagreement itself, and the position collapses into self-defeat.

5. The contested famine. A historian whose expertise and access to sources I take to match my own concludes that a particular famine was caused primarily by policy failure; I had concluded it was caused primarily by drought. My verdict: I should move somewhat, but nowhere near halfway — from high confidence to mild uncertainty, not to indifference.

The tension is obvious: cases 1 and 3 push toward splitting the difference, case 2 permits no movement at all, and cases 4 and 5 permit only modest movement. My tentative principle is this: conciliate fully unless you possess evidence about the disagreement that your peer does not possess about herself. I take this to handle case 2 (my knowledge of his sleeplessness is something he lacks) and, perhaps, case 4 (my first-personal access to my own reasoning is asymmetric in the relevant way). But I suspect it handles cases 3 and 5 badly, and I am not confident it survives scrutiny.

Please do the following. First, assess whether my tentative principle actually delivers my five verdicts, working through it case by case, and identify exactly where and why it fails. Second, state a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that does better. Each clause of your account should be motivated by a specific case, and you should say plainly which of my verdicts, if any, must be overturned to achieve coherence, with a justification for overturning that one rather than another. Third, apply your preferred principle to three cases I have not considered: (a) disagreement with my past self about a judgment I made before acquiring evidence I now possess; (b) disagreement about a logic puzzle in which one party can exhibit a step-by-step proof the other can check line by line; (c) disagreement with someone I regard as my superior in general intelligence but my inferior in familiarity with the specific subject matter. Fourth, tell me honestly whether your account commits me to conciliating about the correct theory of disagreement itself, and if so, whether you regard that as a defect of the account or a consequence I should accept.
