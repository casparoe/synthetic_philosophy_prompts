In J. B. Priestley's An Inspector Calls, the Inspector assembles a chain in which each member of the Birling household contributed to Eva Smith's death, but the links differ in kind: some are acts (a dismissal engineered out of spite), some are withdrawals (Gerald ending the affair), and some are pure failures to act (Mrs Birling's committee refusing help to a desperate applicant who was, unknown to her, carrying her own grandchild). Read the play through the philosophy of causation by omission. Does the Inspector's case treat the omissions in the chain as causes of the death in the same sense as the acts, and is Priestley entitled to that, or does his moral rhetoric elide the distinction between doing and allowing? Consider whether the play's logic depends on omissions being causal only against a background of expectations (a charity committee, a lover's arrangement) that the Birlings themselves helped create, and what that implies for distributing responsibility among several agents each of whom could claim the outcome was overdetermined. I'm leading a discussion group on the play and want to know where the philosophical reading sharpens its moral force and where it exposes the drama as trading on a confusion the Inspector never has to answer for.
