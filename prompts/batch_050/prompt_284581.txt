For a few years now I've been following the literature on the Allais paradox, and I've started to suspect the debate isn't really going anywhere. As far as I can tell both sides know the setup inside out (the classic 1A vs 1B, 2A vs 2B choices with the 0.89/0.90/0.11 probabilities), and both sides agree that most people's actual choices violate the independence axiom. The disagreement is purely about whether those choices are rational. One camp keeps producing arguments that independence violations lead to dynamic inconsistency or make you exploitable across sequential choices; the other camp keeps saying the certainty effect is a legitimate preference, that regret matters, and that the dynamic arguments assume you'll naively re-decide at each stage instead of sticking to a plan (resolute choice, sophisticiated choice, etc.). Nobody seems to be converting anybody.

My hypothesis, which I'd like you to engage with: the two camps are actually disagreeing about how to individuate outcomes. The independence defenders describe outcomes purely in terms of money and probability, while the Allais defenders smuggle things like "getting nothing when you could have had a sure million" into the outcome description itself, at which point the choices arguably don't violate independence anymore. So the debate has stalled because it's secretly about which description of the same choice situation is the right one, and neither side has a non-question-begging criterion for that.

Is that the right diagnosis, or is something else doing the work -- a factual disagreement about whether resolute choosers really can avoid exploitation, a normative disagreement about separability across states of the world, or just bedrock intuitions? And given your diagnosis: what would actually move this debate? A new thought experiment, a formal result, or should we expect it to stay stuck?
