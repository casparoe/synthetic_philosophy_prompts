My local rationalist meetup runs a film-and-discussion night, and I've proposed Twelve Angry Men with the framing that it's secretly a film about Bayesian convergence: twelve jurors walk in with wildly different priors, share the same body of evidence over one afternoon, and end up unanimous. Read the film seriously through the lens of convergence theorems and the washing out of priors. Where does the reading hold -- the jurors do receive essentially common evidence, and the film is careful to make the reversals turn on re-examined testimony, the knife, the old man's walk, the woman's glasses, rather than on bare sentiment? Where does it break down -- for instance, does the film smuggle in differences in likelihood functions rather than priors, since the jurors disagree about how probative each exhibit is, which is exactly the case where convergence results don't apply? And is Juror 8 actually a good Bayesian or just a skilled rhetorician: he wins the first vote by appealing to social comfort rather than evidence, and I'd like an honest accounting of which of his moves would survive scrutiny by the standards of someone who takes Aumann-style agreement theorems seriously. Finally: the film never shows us whether the defendant is innocent, and reasonable doubt is not convergence to the truth -- so what, if anything, does the film contribute to the philosophy of deliberation that the theorems themselves don't capture? I'll be leading the discussion afterward and want a reading I can defend against a room full of people who will notice if I overclaim.
