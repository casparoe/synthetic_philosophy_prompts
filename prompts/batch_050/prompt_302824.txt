I'm a philosophy PhD student putting together an application for a small summer research grant (3 months, one paper expected at the end). The topic is the old puzzle about whether you can assign probabilities to your own choices while you're still deliberating, but I have a half-formed angle on it that I want the proposal to develop.

The rough idea: people usually state the thesis as something like "deliberation crowds out prediction" -- while you're deciding what to do, you can't treat your future act as just another event to forecast, on pain of decision-theoretic weirdness. But it seems to me this is stated way too coarsely, and there are at least three different things the ban could be on: (a) having credences over your own acts at all mid-deliberation, (b) having them but being barred from using them as evidence for or against options, or (c) something about not being able to *settle* the question of what you'll do by finding out what you'll do. And I suspect the ordinary-life counterexamples people give (you notice mid-deliberation that you're obviously going to pick the pasta; your friend predicts you better than you predict yourself; you decide what to do by reflecting on what someone with your track record would end up doing) only put pressure on some of these readings, not all. So the real question becomes: what's the strongest version of the ban that survives the everyday cases, and is that version still strong enough to do the work decision theorists want it to do?
