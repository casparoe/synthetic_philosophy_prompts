I teach a second-year undergraduate course on theories of the good life, and next week I have one ninety-minute seminar on G. E. Moore's account in Principia Ethica of the two greatest goods: the appreciation of beauty and personal affection. The students will have just spent a week on Mill's higher and lower pleasures, so they will arrive primed to hear Moore as Mill with refined tastes. Plan the session rather than deliver it.

On the order of ideas: work out what must be in place before the specific goods will make sense -- presumably the intrinsic/instrumental distinction, the method of isolation, and the doctrine of organic unities -- and give me the sequence and what each stage is doing, in particular where organic unities must land so that friendship reads as its paradigm case rather than as a coda tacked onto the aesthetics.

On the carrying example: I see two candidates -- the pair of imagined worlds, one beautiful and one hideous, that no one will ever observe; and the matched pair of experiences that are identical from the inside, admiring a person for qualities they genuinely have versus admiring a secret fraud. Say which should carry the session and why, and where the other belongs. The main example has to let the existence requirement -- Moore's claim that the beauty or the admired qualities must really be there, and be believed to be there, for the full value to obtain -- emerge from the students' own judgments about the case rather than be announced as a rule.

On misconceptions: list the predictable wrong turns in the order students will take them, each with the point in the sequence where it should be headed off. My guesses: that Moore is a qualitative hedonist for whom deluded enjoyment is just as good if it feels the same; that 'organic' is a vague holism about everything being connected; that the view ranks our friends by merit and licenses loving only the worthy; that the two worlds are meant to prove beauty's intrinsic value rather than test for it. Correct and extend this list.

Finally: one closing question that forces them to apply both organic unities and the existence requirement to a case they haven't seen, so I can tell whether the position landed or only the slogan did; and what I should openly declare out of scope for a single session, and where to mark that boundary, without making the omissions sound like evasions.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- the one load-bearing decision the whole plan turns on -- then give the plan, with no preamble and no restating of my brief.
