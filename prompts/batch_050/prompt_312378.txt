PhD student in ML here, venting a bit before my quals. The whole field runs on benchmarks: a new model gets taken seriously if it beats the old state of the art on some standard suite, the leaderboard gets updated, and everyone knows in the back of their mind that within a year or two the suite will be "saturated" and quietly replaced. Meanwhile the same community treats the test set as sacred -- don't train on it, don't peek -- while collectively the entire field is optimizing against these benchmarks as hard as it can, through architecture search, data curation, and picking which results to publish. And everyone cites Goodhart's law in passing, then goes back to leaderboard-chasing anyway.

So my question: what theory of measurement does this practice actually presuppose? Like, does it only make sense if benchmark scores track some real, underlying capability that exists independently of the test -- and if so, what has to be true about the relationship between the proxy and the capability for Goodhart not to sink the whole enterprise? Or is the practice really operationalist -- the score just is what we mean by "good at language" or whatever -- and if that's the view, does the field have any right to be impressed when a model trained to ace MMLU does well on MMLU? Also: is the individual "don't touch the test set" norm even coherent given that the community as a whole does exactly the thing the norm forbids, just distributed across thousands of labs and papers? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories -- if the practice is compatible with several, tell me which parts are underdetermined), say whether the individual norms and the collective practice presuppose consistent things, and what would concretely have to change in how we evaluate models if we fully internalized Goodhart instead of just name-dropping it.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning -- no preamble, no restating my question. American English please.
