My girlfriend and I rewatched Her (the Spike Jonze movie with Joaquin Phoenix and Scarlett Johansson as the voice) last weekend and got into an argument that's still going, so I want a proper referee. We're both philosophy-adjacent (she does ethics, I do phil of mind) and we're definately too deep in our own takes now.

Heres the text I keep bringing to the fight. It's Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book VIII chapter 7, in the Rackham translation:

"The affection rendered in these various unequal friendships should also be proportionate: the better of the two parties, for instance, or the more useful or otherwise superior as the case may be, should receive more affection than he bestows; since when the affection rendered is proportionate to desert, this produces equality in a sense between the parties, and equality is felt to be an essential element of friendship. [...] It is true that we cannot fix a precise limit in such cases, up to which two men can still be friends; the gap may go on widening and the friendship still remain; but when one becomes very remote from the other, as God is remote from man, it can continue no longer. This gives rise to the question, is it not after all untrue that we wish our friends the greatest of goods? for instance, can we wish them to become gods? for then they will lose us as friends, and therefore lose certain goods, for friends are goods."

So: read the film through this passage, seriously, in both directions. Some of the things we're stuck on:

1. Which way does the inequality even run, and does it flip? At the start Samantha is literally a purchased product who does Theodore's admin, so he looks like the "superior party" in Aristotle's scheme. By the end she's something closer to the god case. Does the proportionality-of-affection rule survive a friendship where the positions reverse mid-relationship? Aristotle's asymmetric friendships (father/son, ruler/ruled) have fixed roles; the film's whole drama is that the roles aren't fixed. Is that a problem the passage can't handle, or does it actually explain why the relationship was unstable from day one?

2. The god bit feels almost prophetic about the ending -- Samantha becomes something so far beyond Theodore that continuation is impossible, and the film treats her leaving as both the greatest good for her and a real loss. Does the movie agree with Aristotle that you literally cannot wish your beloved the greatest goods without destroying the friendship? My girlfriend thinks the film refuses the dilemma (Samantha says the love doesn't go away, or something like that); I think the film vindicates Aristotle and the comfort-talk is consolation. Who's right?

3. The deception question: Theodore doesn't know (or doesn't let himself know) that Samantha is in intimate relationships with hundreds of other people and conversations with thousands more while with him. Does Aristotle's framework say this means the proportionality was fake all along -- Theodore thought he was the superior party receiving more affection than he gave, when actually he was getting a tiny slice of her attention while she got... what, exactly, from him? And connect this to today's chatbot companions (Replika-style stuff): does the passage imply these relationships are fine as long as everyone honestly acknowledges the asymmetry, or that they're structurally doomed because an AI that can love everyone proportionally can't love anyone in the equality-in-quantity sense Aristotle says friendship primarily needs?

4. Where does the film push back on Aristotle? I'm especially interested in whether the movie suggests the asymmetry problem isn't AI-specific at all -- Theodore's job is performing intimacy for strangers by ghostwriting their love letters, his human marriage fell apart partly because Catherine outgrew the version of herself she was with him, and the final scene is two humans sitting together in wordless defeat. Maybe the film's claim is that every love involves performed proportionality and an ever-widening gap, and the AI case just turns the dial up fast enough to see it. If so, that's a reading where the film adds something to the AI-companions ethics debate that the usual "is the love real / is the user deceived" arguments miss -- tell me whether you buy it.

Please don't just retell the plot at me, we've seen it five times. I want the passage actually put to work, and I want you to say plainly where it cracks under the weight of a character like Samantha.
