I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm drafting a column on higher-order theories of consciousness for a general-interest magazine. Below is my working draft. Before I file it, I'd like detailed comments — on accuracy, on whether my proposed experiment does what I claim, and on anything a specialist reader would pounce on.

DRAFT: "The Mind Watching Itself"

Why does any brain activity feel like anything at all? One influential answer, developed by David Rosenthal and refined by others since, is the higher-order theory: a mental state becomes conscious only when the brain forms a second, higher-order representation of it — roughly, a thought to the effect of "I am now seeing red." The first-order state by itself is unconscious. On this view, strictly speaking, you are not in pain unless you represent yourself as being in pain.

The theory has real charms. It explains why introspection seems revelatory rather than incidental, it fits with evidence implicating prefrontal cortex in conscious awareness, and it unifies consciousness with metacognition — the brain's ongoing estimates of its own performance, which psychologists already study in the lab.

The obvious worry is who gets left out. If consciousness requires a concept of one's own mental states, then infants and most animals would be locked out of experience entirely — a conclusion I take to be close to a reductio. Defenders reply that the relevant higher-order thoughts can be "thin": nonconceptual, non-linguistic, and themselves unconscious. Whether that answer survives scrutiny is, to my mind, the live question.

Here I'd like to propose a way to settle the matter empirically, which I'll call the confidence mirror. Using closed-loop neurofeedback, train participants so that their confidence ratings come to track a decoder's readout of their prefrontal activity, rather than their actual perceptual accuracy. Keep the sensory stimulus and early visual processing fixed, but manipulate the prefrontal signal so that confidence rises and falls independently of performance. If subjective experience shifts along with the manipulated higher-order signal while the sensory cortex holds steady, the higher-order theorists win, and first-order and global-workspace rivals lose.

Consciousness, on this picture, is the mind's habit of taking attendance on itself — and we may finally be able to falsify the roll call.

END OF DRAFT

Please tie your comments to specific sentences rather than offering general advice on writing about consciousness.
