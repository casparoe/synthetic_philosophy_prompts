I am a novelist serving, somewhat improbably, as the lay member of the jury for a national prize in typeface design; the founders wanted one practising writer among the type designers, and this year I drew the short straw. One entry in the category for new text faces has split us down the middle, and I have been asked to draft the jury's written opinion before we reconvene.

The entry, called Mercurial, is an alphabet with no fixed letterforms. Every character is designed to change shape according to its neighbours: the 'a' appears as a single-storey bowl in one word and a double-storey structure in the next; in several common words it collapses into a ligature, and in a few it becomes, so far as its outline goes, identical to the face's 'o', identifiable as an 'a' only because the surrounding word requires one. Set in continuous prose the face is, by the admission of everyone on the panel, perfectly legible — rather beautiful, in fact. Shown in isolation, however, a good many of its characters cannot be recognised as any letter at all.

The prize rules state that the category is open to 'a coherent alphabet of letterforms suitable for continuous reading'. Half the jury argues that Mercurial is the purest demonstration imaginable of what a letter is: an 'a' is whatever plays the role of 'a' in readable text, and the essence of a letter has always been a matter of role and recognition rather than of any fixed shape — a medieval scribe's 'a' and a modern sans-serif 'a' share almost no feature either, yet we call both by the same name. The other half replies that a letter must remain itself across contexts; that an 'a' which cannot be picked out of a line-up is not an 'a' at all but merely a mark doing a letter's work on sufferance; and that an entry whose characters dissolve outside of words belongs in the experimental lettering category, not this one.

Please draft the jury's written opinion, in British English and no more than 500 words. It should lead with a clear recommendation — admit the entry, reject it, or recategorise it — and then set out the reasoning, including a fair and serious statement of the strongest argument on the losing side, since entrants receive our opinions and this one will be read closely. Where the decision turns on the question of what makes a letter the letter it is, the opinion should say so plainly rather than hiding behind the wording of the rules.
