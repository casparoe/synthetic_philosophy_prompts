I run operations for a regional chain of residential care homes — twenty-two sites, a few hundred staff — and over the past decade I have more or less accidentally run the same experiment twice. For the first several years we hired for character: mature people, good judgment, real commitment to the residents, and then we trusted them and stayed out of their way. The best sites under that system were the best we have ever had, but the variance was enormous, and the failures were slow and invisible — one much-admired manager turned out to have been neglecting record-keeping for years, and when another left, her site collapsed within months because everything had lived in her head. So we rebuilt around the opposite philosophy: every role has a written specification of exactly what it owes, outputs are measured, bonuses and sanctions follow automatically from the numbers, and whatever staff report is cross-checked against independent data. Reliability became boring, which is what we wanted; mediocre hires now perform adequately. But something else happened that I have no word for, and it is the thing I actually want to ask about. Nobody will now do anything good that is not in their specification. Staff who used to reorganize their afternoons around a resident's bad week now point at the document. Unusual situations bounce between desks because no one's post owns them. And — this is the part I find strangest — nobody is angry or playing games. They sincerely believe they are doing their jobs well, because by the only standard in front of them, they are. It is not a slowdown or a protest, and it is not people exploiting badly chosen metrics, because in several areas the metrics genuinely measure what we care about and the phenomenon shows up there too. When we tried layering the old mission language back on top — "take ownership," "do right by the resident" — people used the vagueness to dodge the measurable duties, so we now have the worst of both.

A friend who reads classical Chinese tells me I have reinvented an old position and sent me a passage from the Han Feizi, chapter 50 (顯學, "Eminence in Learning"), with his own rough translation. The original: 夫聖人之治國，不恃人之爲吾善也，而用其不得爲非也。恃人之爲吾善也，境内不什數；用人不得爲非，一國可使齊。爲治者用衆而舍寡，故不務德而務法。夫必恃自直之箭，百世無矢；恃自圜之木，千世無輪矣。自直之箭，自圜之木，百世無有一，然而世皆乘車射禽者何也？隱栝之道用也。雖有不恃隱栝而有自直之箭、自圜之木，良工弗貴也。何則？乘者非一人，射者非一發也。不恃賞罰而恃自善之民，明主弗貴也。何則？國法不可失，而所治非一人也。故有術之君，不随適然之善，而行必然之道。 His translation: "In governing a state, the sage does not rely on people being good on his account; he relies on making them unable to do wrong. If he relied on people being good on his account, he would not find even ten within his borders; if he relies on their being unable to do wrong, the whole state can be made uniform. One who governs uses what works for the many and discards what works for the few; therefore he does not occupy himself with virtue but with law. Insist on arrows that are straight of themselves and a hundred generations will yield no arrows; on wood that is round of itself and a thousand generations will yield no wheels. Yet every age rides in carts and shoots birds, because the craft of the straightening-frame is used. A self-straight arrow that appears without the frame is not prized by the skilled craftsman, for there is not only one rider and not only one shot. Likewise the enlightened ruler does not prize people who are good of themselves over reward and punishment, for the laws of the state must not fail and those he governs are not one person. So the ruler who possesses method does not follow whatever goodness happens to occur, but walks the way that holds of necessity."

Here is my question. The passage tells me how to make wrongdoing impossible, and on that score it describes my second system accurately. But it seems entirely untroubled by the failure mode I am describing — the disappearance of uncommissioned good conduct, the atrophy of judgment, the way a population of rule-satisficers comes to feel no responsibility for anything the rules forgot to mention — and its closing line, about not following accidental goodness, reads almost like it considers that disappearance a feature. Does this phenomenon have an established name: in the scholarship on Han Feizi and the Legalist school, in the Confucian tradition that argued against it, or in modern organizational or political theory? If it is really several phenomena tangled together, please untangle them and tell me where the joints are; if nothing established fits, propose a term yourself, with a definition and a few boundary cases, because a bare label without the carving will not help me. And one subsidiary question: is my second system actually what this passage is recommending, or have I — or my friend's translation — flattened something? I notice, for instance, that the passage speaks of making wrongdoing impossible rather than of specifying duties, and those may not be the same design, and I would like to know whether the distinction matters for my problem.
