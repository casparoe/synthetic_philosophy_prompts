In debates over same-sex marriage, one frequently hears the argument that marriage is essentially a contract: an agreement between two consenting adults, which the state merely registers and enforces, much as it registers and enforces a lease or a business partnership. On this picture, excluding same-sex couples is as arbitrary as refusing to let them co-sign a tenancy agreement, and the state's proper role is neutral enforcement of whatever the parties have agreed. Audit this analogy. Set out the correspondence explicitly: what in a marriage plays the role of offer, consideration, terms, breach, remedies, and the contracting parties, and what corresponds to the state's enforcement role. Then identify where the mapping strains: contracts are terminable by mutual consent while divorce is not; contract terms are set by the parties while marital obligations are fixed by law and largely non-negotiable; contracts create obligations but not, in any thick sense, a status recognised by third parties; and contracts seldom exist partly for the sake of third parties who never signed them, as marriage arguably does where children are concerned. Distinguish which of these disanalogies matter for the specific conclusion the analogy was deployed to support and which are merely cosmetic. Finally, say whether the conclusion survives the audit -- perhaps in a weakened form -- and if the analogy is beyond repair, propose a better one that would carry the same argumentative weight. Please write your answer in British English.
