A friend of mine builds physics simulations for a living, and over a game of snooker last month he made an argument I've been turning over since. Causation, he said, is no more mysterious than a billiard-ball collision: one ball strikes another, momentum passes across, and you can watch the whole transaction with your own eyes. Philosophers who analyse causation in terms of counterfactual dependence — roughly, the idea that C caused E because E would not have occurred had C not occurred — or in terms of interventions — the idea that a cause is a handle such that wiggling it under the right conditions wiggles the effect — are, on his view, building elaborate machinery to explain something that a single afternoon at the snooker table makes plain. I later came across a passage that seems to cut against him, from Section VII of David Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding: "When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other." If Hume is right that all we ever observe is succession, then my friend's claim to simply see the causing looks like the one thing the billiard table cannot give him — and I also noticed that he himself lapses into the idioms he dismisses: to establish that the cue ball, and not a wobble in the table, moved the black, he says things like "hold the ball still and see what happens" and "the black would never have moved had the white missed."

Could you audit his analogy properly? I'd like the correspondence table made explicit: what, in the counterfactual and interventionist pictures, plays the role of the collision, the transferred momentum, the felt inevitability of the shot. Then the places where the mapping fails, separating the failures that matter from the ones that don't. Cases I suspect strain it: two balls arriving at the black at the same instant when either alone would have sufficed; one ball knocking aside a second that would have blocked a third; the pocket that stayed open because nobody closed it — an absence apparently doing causal work with no collision at all; and the fact that the physics of the impact looks reversible while the causation does not. I'd also like to know what the billiard picture quietly imports — that causes are events rather than omissions, that causal influence travels by local contact, that there is a mechanism underneath doing the real explanatory work — and whether those imports are assumptions my friend is entitled to.

Finally: does his deflationary conclusion — that counterfactual and interventionist accounts are idle ornaments on a plain fact — actually survive the audit, or does the billiard case, properly examined, turn out to need those accounts to say which collision did what? And where the two theories diverge in their reading of the snooker table, which of them does the analogy itself favour, if either?
