So I'm in a population ethics seminar and after class two people got into this long fight about Benatar's asymmetry argument for antinatalism, and now they've both demanded I pick a winner and I genuinely can't. Roughly how it went:

(1) Nora: the asymmetry is just common sense. We think it's seriously wrong to have a kid you know will live in agony, but nobody thinks a couple that *could* have a perfectly happy kid and just doesn't has done anything wrong. Best explanation: presence of pain is bad, presence of pleasure is good, but absence of pain is good while absence of pleasure is merely not-bad. So coming into existence is always a harm, never existing never is.

(2) Theo: you can explain those intuitions way cheaper. Nobody believes in a duty to pump out happy people because it would be insanely demanding, and we reject over-demanding duties on independent grounds. You don't need some weird four-part asymmetry of value to do that.

(3) Nora: demandingness explains why we aren't *obligated* to create happy people, but not why we think the childless couple's choice involves no loss whatsoever. If absent pleasure were straightforwardly bad, then passing up a blissful kid would be a real cost that we're just excused from caring about. But nobody thinks there's any cost there at all, not even a forgivable one.

(4) Theo: but then "the absence of pain is good even when no one exists to enjoy it" is doing all the work, and that's a category error. Good has to be good *for* someone. An empty world isn't better off, because there's nobody around to be better off. The everyday intuitions are all about harms and benefits to actual people, full stop.

(5) Nora: you're just asserting that the comparison between existing and never existing is impossible, while Benatar at least gives a principled table that handles it. Declaring comparisons illegitimate whenever they're inconvenient isn't an argument.

Who's right at each step here? And who's more right overall? Also curious what you think both of them are missing.
