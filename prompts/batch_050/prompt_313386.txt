I run risk review for a humanitarian NGO that does food and medical logistics in active conflict zones, and about once a month something lands on my desk of the form: if we do X, some small number of people will predictably be harmed or killed (a convoy route that will draw fire onto a village, dropping supplies by air in a way that might hit people, paying a checkpoint "fee" that funds an armed group), but if we don't, a larger number will die of things we're there to prevent. My board keeps sending me these cases with no framework at all, just vibes, and the lawyers won't touch them.

Here's the conceptual knot I keep hitting and that I want the procedure to actually engage with rather than paper over: if the reason I shouldn't run the convoy is that killing is bad, then it's weirdly self-undermining, because refusing to run the convoy means MORE killing happens overall, just not by my hand. It seems like the rule "don't kill" only makes sense if you think killings are bad, and if killings are bad, shouldn't you want fewer of them, even if that means doing one yourself? A philosopher friend told me this has an actual name -- the paradox of deontology -- and that the resolution people reach for is that the constraint isn't about minimizing killings in the world but about what kind of agent YOU are, what you can be answerable for, which is agent-relative rather than outcome-relative. Okay, but I can't put "be the right kind of agent" in a sign-off form.

So: write me a decision procedure I can actually run on these cases in under a day, that translates whatever is defensible in the deontological view into operational steps. It should include: the ordered questions to ask about a proposal (e.g., is the harm intended as a means, merely foreseen, a side effect of enabling someone else's wrongdoing, etc.), what the difference between intending a harm and merely foreseeing it is supposed to be and a workable test for telling which one a given case is (because in practice everyone claims everything is "merely foreseen"), where the procedure yields a hard no versus a judgment call that goes to the director, and how to handle the agent-relative point concretely -- i.e., how to weigh "deaths we cause" against "deaths we fail to prevent" without either treating them as identical (which dissolves the constraint) or refusing to compare them at all (which makes the constraint untestable). Then run it on two cases: the checkpoint payment that funds an armed group versus the convoy route that will predictably draw fire onto a village, and be explicit if the procedure gives different answers for them and why. Also flag where the procedure will misfire, because I know it will.
