I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature about how we treat victims of accidents, and I keep running into a puzzle I can't crack. In most countries, if you end up paralyzed because a delivery driver ran a red light and hit you, the legal system will (in principle) make you whole: full compenation for lost earnings, care costs, even "pain and suffering," extracted from the driver or their insurer. But if you end up paralyzed to the exact same degree because a blood vessel burst in your spine, or a tree fell on you in a storm, nobody owes you anything — you get whatever disability benefits and public healthcare happen to exist, which is usually a fraction of what the tort victim recieves. Same injury, same need, wildly different treatment, and the only difference is whether someone was at fault. Meanwhile, the same countries often run no-fault schemes for specific things — vaccine injuries, sometimes car accidents — where compensation flows precisely *without* anyone being to blame. So the system seems to believe two things at once.

There's a camp of political philosophers — the "luck egalitarians," people like Ronald Dworkin and G.A. Cohen — who argue roughly that the point of distributive justice is to neutralize bad luck that people aren't responsible for, while leaving outcomes traceable to genuine choices on the chooser. On that framework the tort/disability split looks exactly backwards: the stroke victim and the hit-by-a-car victim are identically unlucky, and the driver's negligence seems neither here nor there.

Here's what I want. Act as a political philosopher who's also genuinely fluent in how tort law works in practice. Think carefully, take your time — maybe start with a quick brainstorm of the possible implicit theories before you commit to a reading. Then:

(1) What view of responsibility and luck does the fault-based compensation system actually presuppose? Does it rest on luck-egalitarian foundations that got distorted somewhere, or on something else entirely — a practice for deterring carelessness, an old idea that wrongs create personal debts, a hunch about corrective vs. distributive justice?

(2) Which parts of the practice genuinely *require* that theory, and which are merely compatible with it? I want to know where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories would all license it.

(3) Are the fault-based system and the no-fault schemes actually inconsistent in what they presuppose, or is there one coherent theory that explains why we treat vaccine injuries differently from spinal strokes differently from negligent drivers?

(4) If a country rebuilt its whole compensation system on consistently luck-egalitarian foundations, what concretely would have to change — and are there features of the current setup that would survive even that rebuild, because they were never really about luck in the first place?

Also, please flag anywhere you think I've misdescribed the luck egalitarians' actual position — I'm going off secondary coverage here and I'd rather get it right now than in the corrections section.
