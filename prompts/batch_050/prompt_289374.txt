I'm preparing a position statement for a graduate seminar on democratic theory, and I want a stress-test of my commitments before I present them, since my classmates are good at finding the one weak joint in an otherwise plausible view. I'd like you to respond as a political theorist with expertise in democratic institutional design: take your time, consider each commitment individually and then in combination, and brainstorm possible tensions before settling on your verdicts. My view is a hybrid of sortition-based and competence-sensitive proposals, developed over a couple of years of reading and arguing with friends who range from committed electoral democrats to outright epistocrats.

Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) I support replacing one chamber of the national legislature with a body of randomly selected citizens, because random sampling yields a legislature that descriptively mirrors the population in class, education, occupation, and outlook, which election by party competition systematically fails to do. (2) I also hold that political decisions go better when made by the better-informed, so I favor weighting the selection lottery by a civic knowledge test, such that citizens who pass a basic exam on institutions, economics, and current affairs have a meaningfully higher chance of being seated. (3) I maintain that the legitimacy of the sortition chamber rests precisely on its being a statistical portrait of the citizenry — a miniature of the public whose judgments we can treat as what everyone would think under decent conditions — and not on the individual merits of its members. (4) I want the chamber's decisions to be binding rather than advisory; a merely consultative citizens' assembly seems to me a fig leaf. (5) I think selection must be strictly voluntary, so that any chosen citizen may decline without penalty or stigma, since conscripting people into political office is an objectionable infringement on their liberty. (6) I hold that serving members should be insulated from lobbying and from post-service career rewards, so that the chamber cannot be captured the way elected bodies are.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture on sortition generally. Identify which of these commitments are in genuine tension with each other and which pairs only look tense but actually dissolve on inspection. For each real tension, state precisely which commitments collide and why the usual motivation for one undercuts the other, then rank the tensions by severity. Finally, tell me which single revision would restore consistency at the lowest cost to the overall view, and what that revision would sacrifice. Please be concrete: if two of my commitments cannot both be held, say so directly rather than gesturing at a possible reconciliation, and do not flag tensions you cannot spell out.
