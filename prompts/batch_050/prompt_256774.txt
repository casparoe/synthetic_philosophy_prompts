Over the holidays I got into it with my brother-in-law about whether our state should keep its ban on a certain drug (won't say which, don't want this to turn into that argument). I made some point about how the ban ruins more lives than the drug does, and he just shrugged and said "well, you can't legislate morality" -- like that settled it, and moved on to asking about my job. The annoying thing is I'm pretty sure I ALSO believe something in that neighborhood, but when I pressed him later over text he said he meant that the law can't change what people think is right, so the ban is pointless -- which seems like a totally different claim than "the state shouldn't try," and honestly maybe a false one? Segregation laws seem to have changed what people think, or at least what they say.

Anyway I went down a rabbit hole and found out there's this whole mid-century fight between H.L.A. Hart and Lord Devlin about whether the law may enforce morality as such, with Devlin saying a society is held together by a shared morality so it can defend that morality legally the same way it defends itself against treason, and Hart pushing back with something in the tradition of Mill's harm principle plus a critique of the idea that society collapses whenever its moral code shifts. So now I have a menu of things my brother-in-law's one-liner might mean and I want it sorted properly.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses "you can't legislate morality" could be expressing? I'm guessing at least: an empirical claim that laws can't change moral behavior or belief, a normative claim that morality-as-such isn't the state's business, something about unenforceable laws breeding contempt for law in general, and maybe a conceptual claim that law and morality are just different categories. For each one: is it a platitude, a live substantive position, or just false; how does it map onto the Hart-Devlin dispute (who'd endorse it, who'd deny it, and does Devlin's social-cohesion argument actually answer it); and most importantly, which readings would actually justify my brother-in-law's conversational move -- i.e., which ones, if true, would show the drug ban is illegitimate or doomed rather than merely showing that one argument for it fails. I'd like to bring a cleaner version of this to him next time, so a list with verdicts is what I'm after.
