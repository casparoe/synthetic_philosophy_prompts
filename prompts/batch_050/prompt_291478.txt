I spent most of last weekend locked in an argument with a friend from the biology department over whether her study organism -- a colonial ascidian that fuses with genetically distinct neighbors and carries a stably inherited bacterial consortium -- is one organism, several, or a bad question. The frustrating part was that we never reached substantive disagreement: every time I pressed a metabolic-integration criterion she countered with shared evolutionary fate, and every time she pressed vertical transmission of the symbionts I countered with the chimerism of the fused colonies. Each of us treated our favored criterion as obviously the right one and the other's as a category error, and the argument ended in stalemate. I now want to salvage something from this. I teach an upper-level philosophy of biology seminar, and I'm writing the final exam: I want one essay question on biological individuality that reproduces this stalemate for the students and then forces them to diagnose it, rather than letting them recite the standard menu of criteria (bottlenecks in the life cycle, germ-soma separation, policing of cheaters, high relatedness, immunological cohesion) as if listing them settled anything.

Here is the shape I have in mind. The question should present a single concrete case -- you may use my ascidian, or design a fictional but biologically realistic case of your own, provided it has the crucial feature that physiological criteria and evolutionary criteria of individuality come apart: the entity is a coherent metabolic and developmental unit but not a unit of selection in any clean sense (or vice versa). Students should be asked to (i) state precisely what each of the two criteria is a criterion *of* -- what explanatory work the concept of an organism is doing in each research tradition; (ii) deliver a verdict on the case under each criterion, showing they can actually apply rather than merely describe them; and (iii) take a defended stand on whether the divergence shows that "organism" is a plural or pragmatic notion whose legitimate use varies with explanatory purpose, or whether one criterion has genuine priority and the other is tracking a merely derivative kind of unity. The level is third-year undergraduates who have done one term of philosophy of biology; assume a 75-minute essay in a closed-book exam.

Please deliver three things. First, the question itself, phrased as it would appear on the exam, including whatever case description you use. Second, a model answer of roughly 600 to 700 words. Third, a marking scheme out of 25 points built around five must-hit points, where for each point you state what it is worth, what distinguishes full from partial credit, and one sentence on how a weak candidate typically fumbles it (for instance, treating the cooperation-conflict framework as if it were a definition rather than a criterion, or announcing that "it depends on your perspective" without explaining *why* the perspectives diverge and whether that divergence is principled). Finally, add a short paragraph stating explicitly what separates an A answer from a B answer on this question -- my worry is that a B student can produce a fluent, accurate survey of the criteria, so the A/B line needs to turn on something the B answer structurally lacks, not on polish or coverage.
