Last weekend a friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about the gatecrasher problem and the blue bus case, and the argument ended in a way I want to turn into a research project. Her position was that refusing to find against the bus company or the stadium on the numbers alone is just probability illiteracy dressed up as jurisprudence; mine was that a verdict is a claim about this defendant, not about the class he happens to fall into, and that no base rate by itself ever supports such a claim. What emerged halfway through, and what neither of us could resolve, is that the law already seems to do the thing she defends in at least one corner: in the DES market-share cases, courts knowingly apportion liability among manufacturers in proportion to their market share, which looks like imposing liability on naked statistics. I'm a philosophy graduate student applying for a twelve-week summer research grant, and I need a two-page proposal built around this tension. My half-formed hypothesis is that the real distinction is not between statistical and non-statistical evidence but between evidence that bears some causal trace back to the particular defendant (as even fallible eyewitness testimony does) and evidence that merely partitions a population into base-rate classes — and that the market-share cases are the stress test, since they suggest the law relaxes the causal-trace requirement when the goal is distributing an unavoidable loss but not when the goal is assigning blame. If that hypothesis survives scrutiny, it would explain the civil/criminal asymmetry better than the usual move of just citing the higher criminal standard of proof, since the discomfort with naked statistics shows up even under the civil standard.

The proposal should state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, explain why the more familiar families of responses — those that diagnose the puzzle through knowledge-based norms like sensitivity or safety, those that appeal to an individualized-evidence requirement, and those that treat it as a side-effect of the special gravity of criminal punishment — each leave my version of it open, and lay out what I would actually do week by week (I'm imagining a mix of reconstructing the logic of the market-share decisions, testing the causal-trace hypothesis against hypothetical variants where it predicts the intuition should flip, and checking it against the lottery-paradox literature in epistemology). Say why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives, such as running experimental-jurisprudence surveys on lay intuitions, and be explicit about what a result would look like, what would count as the hypothesis failing, and which parts of the larger debate (for instance, the general epistemology of testimony) are too big to settle in twelve weeks and will be bracketed. One thing I care about: in motivating the project, separate what is genuinely settled in this debate from what is still contested among people who work on it — don't present any particular diagnosis of the puzzle, including my own, as though it were the received view.
