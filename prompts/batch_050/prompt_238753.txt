Hi, I'm a second-year PhD student in moral psychology working on motivated reasoning, and I keep bumping into a legal doctrine that I can't square with the debates in my own lit, so I wanted to think it through with someone.

The doctrine: in criminal law, a defendant who strongly suspects a fact but deliberately avoids confirming it gets treated as if they knew it. The textbook case is the courier who agrees to carry a locked suitcase across a border, suspects very much that it's drugs, and carefully never asks and never looks. Juries can be told (it's sometimes called the ostrich instruction) that this deliberate not-knowing counts as knowledge. So willful blindness is punished like actual knowledge -- way more harshly than mere negligence or recklessness.

Here's why this puzzles me. Elsewhere the law seems to treat sincere belief as both involuntary and exculpating: an honest mistake of fact is a defense, and nobody thinks you can just be ordered to believe something. Meanwhile in my field there's a live debate about whether self-deception is even possible -- the paradox being that to deceive yourself that p you'd seemingly have to know p (to know what to avoid thinking about) while also not knowing p. Deflationary accounts say there's no paradox: what's really happening is just biased inquiry and avoidance, so the person ends up genuinely ignorant, full stop.

So, concretely:

1. Does the willful blindness doctrine strictly presuppose that self-deception is possible -- that the courier in some sense does know what's in the suitcase while managing not to know it? Or can the doctrine be fully reconstructed on deflationary lines, as punishing culpable non-inquiry (something you *do*) rather than culpable belief (something you supposedly can't help)? And if it's really the latter, does equating willful blindness with *knowledge* rather than with negligence still make any sense, or is that just a convenient fiction?

2. Is the doctrine consistent with the mistake-of-fact defense, or do the two parts of the law presuppose incompatible views of doxastic responsibility? If there's a single coherent view of responsibility for belief that makes both come out right, state it; if there isn't, say where the tension actually lies.

3. What would have to change in legal practice if we went full deflationary-involuntarist -- beliefs just happen to us, self-deception is really only motivated avoidance? Does the doctrine survive retooled as being about duties to inquire, or does something important get lost in translation?

Please be careful to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits nicely with -- I suspect parts of it are philosophically underdetermined and I want to know exactly which parts.

At the very end, give me your bottom line as \answer{...} where ... is one of: "presupposes self-deception is possible", "does not presuppose it", or "underdetermined", plus at most a five-word gloss.
