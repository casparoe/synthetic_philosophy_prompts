I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm preparing a feature on why public confidence in expert models has frayed — pandemic projections, macroeconomic forecasts, climate scenarios. The centerpiece is an interview with Professor Adriana Voss, a senior economist who has spent her career defending an uncompromising methodological line: the realism of a model's assumptions is irrelevant; a model is vindicated by predictive success and nothing else. She traces the position to Milton Friedman's 1953 essay The Methodology of Positive Economics and has never retreated from it — not after the financial crisis, not after the pandemic. Before I sit down with her, I want a rehearsal.

Play Professor Voss. Rules:

- Stay in character, first person, until I say "cut." You may not step out to editorialize, and you may not agree with me just to be agreeable.
- You are sharp, courteous, and dryly funny; you concede small points gracefully but never the main one. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs — this is an interview, not a lecture.
- Your genuine resources include: Galileo idealized falling bodies and frictionless planes and physics did not collapse for it; every theory in the mature sciences is strictly false somewhere and gets used anyway; Friedman's point that the expert billiards player plays as if solving the equations, and that foliage distributes itself as if each leaf were maximizing its light; and — your strongest card — any journalist's worry that a model's assumptions are "fragile" is itself a prediction about how the model will perform under changed conditions, so it can be settled inside your framework rather than against it.
- If a question of mine is ambiguous, ask me to sharpen it, as she would.

Here is my opening question. Professor, I want to start where I think you're weakest. Imagine two forecasting models that make identical, successful predictions over the next twenty years. Model One gets there by idealizing in Galileo's manner: it omits factors it can independently show to be small, so its falsehoods are controlled approximations we understand. Model Two gets there by cancellation — it assumes something badly false in one component and something badly false in another, and the errors happen to offset each other. On your view, as I understand it, the two models are equally good, because their predictions match. But the second model's success is a coincidence of errors that will shatter the moment conditions shift, and any policymaker you advise can feel the difference. So either you bite the bullet and call them equal — which no one who advises central banks can afford to say in print — or you admit that we sometimes have to open the hood and grade the assumptions themselves, which is the concession your whole methodology exists to refuse. Which is it?
