I am a doctoral student in psychology drafting the analysis plan for my first solo empirical study, and the episode I keep returning to is Daryl Bem's 2011 paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, which reported a series of experiments on precognition — participants apparently anticipating future random events — most of which cleared the conventional p < .05 threshold. Bayesian critics reanalyzed the results and argued that the evidence was far weaker than the p-values suggested once the prior implausibility of psi was brought into the calculation; several pre-registered direct replications subsequently failed. I would like this episode analyzed as a case study in the frequentist–Bayesian dispute over statistical evidence, with three specific demands.

First, the mapping, done carefully: what exactly does a p-value condition on, and what does a Bayes factor condition on? Where does the prior enter the Bayesian analysis, and why can a result be conventionally significant while constituting weak evidence for the hypothesis even when no analytic misconduct has occurred? State what each framework concludes Bem's data actually licensed him to claim.

Second, an honest account of explanatory reach on both sides. The standard critique of Bem invoking optional stopping, undisclosed analytic flexibility, and multiple comparisons is itself frequentist machinery, so the episode is not a straightforward vindication of Bayesianism; yet the Bayesian objection appears to identify something the p-value structurally cannot, namely that an identical p-value for precognition and for a mundane effect does not constitute identical evidence. If that is correct, specify precisely where the frequentist framework falls silent; if it is not, explain why the intuition is mistaken.

Third, run the analysis in the reverse direction: what does the episode do to each theory? Do the failed replications settle anything, and for whom — given that a frequentist can attribute them to low power or hidden moderators, while the Bayesian reanalyses themselves rested on contested prior specifications? Does the episode show that the replication crisis is fundamentally a crisis of statistical inference or one of publication incentives, and does either framework even possess the resources to answer that question?

Finally, bring the analysis to bear on my situation: my pilot study has n = 40 and p = .03 on its key comparison, and my committee is pleased. Restricting yourself to what the Bem episode actually establishes rather than generic methodological advice, what do I in fact have, and how does the answer change under each framework?
