Alright, context: I retired from a physics lab two years ago and have been falling down philosophy rabbit holes ever since, currently the "norms of assertion" literature. I jotted down where I think I've landed and I'd like an audit before I embarrass myself at the reading group.

Here's my position:
1. Knowledge is the norm of assertion. You shouldn't flat-out assert what you don't know.
2. I'm a fallibilist about knowledge. You can know something even though you might be wrong; knowledge doesn't require Cartesian certainty.
3. Hedged claims are often the responsible ones. In science we constantly say things like "the data suggest p, though I could be wrong" or "p, as far as I know," and I think that's perfectly fine, sometimes mandatory.
4. Testimony does the job. When the relevant experts agree, I'm entitled to repeat their claims myself (vaccines are safe, the climate is warming) even though I couldn't reproduce the evidence on my own.
5. Assertion is like giving your word. That's why lying is so bad: asserting carries a kind of guarantee. Though I'd add that an honest, careful asserter who turns out to be wrong hasn't done anything blameworthy.

What I want: tell me which of these actually pull against each other and which only look like they do. My guess is 1 and 4 might clash but I'm not sure. Rank any real tensions by severity, say for each one what the cheapest fix is, and don't just flag everything as equally problematic -- part of what I want to know is wich pair is fine once you look closer.
