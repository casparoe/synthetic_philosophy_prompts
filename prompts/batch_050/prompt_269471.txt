I serve on the five-member steering board of a mid-sized city's newly created Office of Civic Process. The mayor has proposed replacing our expert-led planning commission with a standing citizens' assembly for land-use decisions, defended in explicitly Deweyan terms: zoning policies are to be treated as working hypotheses, ordinary residents as co-inquirers alongside technical staff, and every adopted measure given a mandatory sunset review in which a later assembly of affected residents evaluates the actual consequences and may revise or repeal it. Two board members oppose the plan. They concede the Deweyan framing but argue it fails on its own terms: genuine inquiry requires that inquirers learn from the consequences of their experiments, yet a rotating assembly dissolves before its policies' effects come in, so no public capable of learning is ever constituted — and land use is precisely the domain where consequences take a decade to appear. They would keep expert control with merely advisory public input. A third member wants to adopt the proposal unchanged. Draft the written opinion I will circulate before our vote. It should recommend adopting, rejecting, or modifying the proposal — with concrete modifications if you recommend any — and it must do three things: say what would have to be true of the assembly's design for the sunset reviews to count as genuine inquiry rather than ritual; give the strongest version of the skeptics' continuity objection rather than dismissing it; and explain, on Deweyan grounds, what role technical experts should play if they no longer hold decision authority, since treating them as mere consultants arguably reintroduces the separation of knowledge from its public consequences that the whole proposal is meant to overcome.
