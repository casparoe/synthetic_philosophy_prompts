The student wrote: "The dogmatism paradox, which Gilbert Harman introduced in his 1973 book Thought, works like this. Suppose I know some proposition p, for instance that my bike is locked up outside the library. Now consider any future evidence E I might get that appears to show not-p, like a friend calling to say the bike rack is empty. I can reason as follows: since p is true, E must be misleading evidence; since I know p, I therefore know E is misleading; so I'm justified in dismissing E without even checking it. But surely knowledge can't license me to ignore any possible future counterevidence in advance -- that's just pigheadedness. The argument crucially depends on the KK principle, the idea that if you know p then you know that you know p, because I have to know that my knowledge is real before I can use it to write off the evidence. The premise that the counterevidence must be 'misleading' is unproblematic, since evidence pointing against a true proposition has to be fabricated or erroneous somewhere along the line. Harman's own moral was that the paradox shows knowledge is impossible in most ordinary cases, which is why the paradox is usually treated as an argument for skepticism."
