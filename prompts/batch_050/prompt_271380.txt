I was on jury service last month and its done my head in ever since. We convicted a 22-year-old who glassed a bloke outside a pub. Drunk, grim upbringing, social services in and out of his life since he was eight. I voted guilty and I'd do it again. But since then I keep testing my gut on cases and it isn't consistent, and I want to know what I actually think:

- Same defendant, same pub, but the defence shows he's got a slow-growing tumour pressing on his frontal lobe, and apparently once it's removed he's gentle as anything. Not his fault. I'd have acquitted, no contest.
- A junior doctor at the end of a fourteen-hour shift misreads a chart and harms a patient. Not her fault, or only a bit -- anyone would have.
- A drunk driver who kills a cyclist. Completely his fault, even though at the moment he got in the car his judgement was every bit as gone as the doctor's was.
- My gran's a retired actuary and mildly unnerving, and she's been predicting my brother's choices in writing since he was ten, sealed envelopes, never once wrong. If his whole life goes like that, is he off the hook for anything? Gut says obviously not. But I can't see why the envelopes are so different from the tumour, causally speaking.
- A man who was horrifically abused as a kid goes on to offend violently himself. I genuinely can't land on this one. Sympathy says mitigate, but I didn't mitigate for our defendant and his childhood was grim as well.

So: is there a principle that gets most of this? Something about whether they could have done otherwise doesn't seem to work, because the doctor and the driver both "couldn't have" at the time and I treat them differently, and the envelopes suggest alternatives don't matter anyway. Maybe it's about where the action comes from, but then the tumour and the childhood look alike and I treat those differently too. Give me the best principle (or two candidates) stated properly, tell me which clause is doing which work, be honest about which of my verdicts have to go if I want consistency, and say what it implies for the abused reoffender, for our actual defendant, and for cases I haven't thought of -- addiction, say, or blackmail.
