I got into a dumb argument with a labmate last week and it's been bugging me ever since, so I want a proper essay-length treatment. We both work near mechanistic interpretability, and the fight was about whether any of it is *literally true*. His view, roughly: a neural network is a pile of weights and floating point activations, full stop. When researchers say they "found the model's representation of the Golden Gate Bridge" or "a deception feature," they're describing a compression that's useful *to us* -- poke a probe in, clamp some direction, watch the logprobs move -- but the network itself doesn't contain anything answering to those descriptions. Different methods even carve the same network into incompatible feature sets (change the sparsity penalty on your SAE and you get a different "ontology"), and he says if there's no fact of the matter about which carving is right, then none of them are real. My instinct is that he's being too austere: if steering along some direction reliably and specifically changes the model's behavior in the predicted way, that seems like exactly the kind of interventionist evidence we'd accept anywhere else in science for a causal structure being real. But then he hits me with the decodability point: a linear probe can often recover information from activations that the network never downstream *uses* (you can decode all sorts of stuff from early layers that seems causally inert), and surely "decodable by us" isn't sufficient for "represented by the system" -- otherwise my thermometer represents the weather in some philosophically juicy sense and the word stops meaning anything. And honestly I wasn't sure whether my steering argument actually escapes that objection or just relocates it.

So, the essay I'd like written: do neural networks have representations -- is realism about interpretability's posits defensible, or should we be thoroughgoing instrumentalists about them? Weigh the strongest versions of both sides, including (a) the interventionist/steering case for realism, (b) the "decodability isn't use" objection and what an adequate criterion of *use by the system itself* would even look like, (c) the multiple-incompatible-carvings problem, and whether a kind of pluralism (many equally real decompositions, like maps at different scales) saves realism or quietly concedes the eliminativist's point. Then reach an actual verdict -- I'm not interested in "it's complicated, both sides have merit" as a conclusion; some hedged or conditional verdict is fine (e.g., realist about X kind of finding but not Y), but defend it. Finally, say briefly what's actually at stake practically: if the instrumentalist is right, does anything change about how much we should trust interpretability-based safety audits, or were those fine all along?
