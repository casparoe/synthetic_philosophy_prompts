You're a philosophy professor who's spent years thinking about population ethics and the meaning of life stuff. Think carefully about this one before writing, and maybe sketch a couple of candidate scenarios before settling on the best.

Here's the background, in case the framing helps: Samuel Scheffler's "afterlife conjecture" is roughly the idea that our own lives and projects depend for their value and meaning on humanity continuing after we personally die -- that we care far more about the collective human afterlife than about our own personal survival, and that if we knew humanity ended with us, much of what we do (curing diseases, writing books, building institutions, even having kids) would lose its point. This is supposed to explain why human extinction seems so much worse than "just" the deaths it involves and the happy future lives it forecloses.

What I want from you: construct a thought experiment that cleanly isolates this conjecture from its two main rivals. So I need a scenario (or pair of scenarios) where:

- The number and timing of deaths is held completely fixed -- nobody dies earlier or differently across the scenarios
- Total lifetime welfare is held fixed too, including the welfare of any future people, so a straightforward welfarist has to judge both outcomes equally good or equally bad
- And yet the afterlife conjecture predicts a dramatic difference in how bad the two outcomes are (or in how the people in them should feel)

In other words, engineer a case where the ONLY thing that varies is whether the collective human project continues -- so that if the reader feels a pull toward one outcome being worse, the afterlife conjecture is what's doing the pulling, and if they feel nothing, that's evidence against it.

Constraints: keep it realistic-ish (no aliens inheriting the earth, no digital uploads, no omniscient aliens telling people the future -- if you need characters to *know* humanity's fate, find a plausible mechanism). The people affected shouldn't be philosophers who've read Scheffler; ordinary people with ordinary projects. And the difference in the outcomes has to fall out of the scenario's structure, not be stipulated ("and then everyone felt meaningless" is cheating).

After the scenario, do the demonstration: (1) show explicitly why the welfarist and the death-counting explanations are forced to give a verdict of "no difference," walking through the welfare math informally; (2) show what the afterlife conjecture predicts and why; (3) identify the single load-bearing feature of your case and say what happens to the verdicts if you remove it; and (4) steelman the critic who says the conjecture conflates what makes our lives *meaningful to us* with what makes outcomes *good* -- how would they redescribe your scenario to dissolve the intuition?
