I'm a science journalist working on a feature about the current wave of neural net interpretability work, and I keep running into the same analogy, from researchers and skeptics alike: that probing a network's internals is basically like doing neuroscience on it. Activation vectors are like neural firing patterns, layers are like brain regions, a probe that lights up for "Paris" is like finding the concept-cell for Paris, and sparse autoencoders are like an fMRI machine that finally gives clean resolution. The analogy does real argumentative work in my interviews: it's used to justify claims like "the model represents the user's emotional state in layer 12" or "this feature IS the model's concept of deception" -- i.e., that the networks genuinely have representations and we can read them off.

Here's what I want from you: audit the analogy, hard. Give me the full correspondence table first -- what plays the role of the neuron, the voxel, the brain region, the experimental stimulus, the decoder. Then tell me where it breaks, and be picky about which breaks actually matter for the conclusion being drawn. A few pressure points I already suspect: (1) in brains we can't rewrite neurons and watch behavior change, but we can ablate/edit network internals -- does that make the interpretability case *stronger* than neuroscience, or is it a different game entirely; (2) a brain's activity has to be there given what it's made of, whereas a network could carry an encoding of something it never uses -- does a probe detecting X show the model *represents* X in any sense that matters, or just that the information is recoverable; (3) the fMRI metaphor assumes the features are already in the net waiting to be found, but critics say autoencoder features are partly an artifact of the decomposition method -- does that worry map onto anything in neuroscience, or is it a uniquely neural-net problem?

End with the verdict I'd need for the article: when a researcher tells me "the model has a representation of X," is the neuroscience analogy licensing more than it should, and what's the most defensible deflationary vs. realist reading of that sentence? If there's a better analogy than the brain one, pitch it. I want this honest enough that the researchers I quote won't be able to say I set up a strawman, but I also don't want to launder hype through a flattering metaphor.
