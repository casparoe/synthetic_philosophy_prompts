Bit of background first. I spent forty years as a research chemist (polymers, mostly), and since retiring I've got unexpectedly into philosophy — village reading group, podcasts, the lot. I also, rather to my own surprise, sit on our town's planning commitee, and we've got a decision coming up that strikes me as a philosophy problem wearing a hi-vis jacket, so I'd like your help drafting the memo I'm going to table.

The case. A local benefactor died in 1974 and left a nine-acre field to the town in trust, with the deed saying it should be held "for the benefit and enjoyment of the people of the town". Ever since it's been an informal meadow — dog walkers, a bonfire in November, kids on bikes. Now a housing association wants to build 86 homes on it, around 40% at affordable rents, with the rest sold at market price to fund the affordable part. The neighbours are up in arms: traffic, drainage, "loss of the green heart of the town". Fair enough, some of that is real. But the argument I keep hearing at public meetings is a different one: that they are the people of the town — they moved here, raised their families here, mowed the verges — and the deed was meant for them, not for some hypothetical influx. Meanwhile the people who'd actually live in the flats are mostly young families who grew up here and got priced out, plus key workers commuting in from an hour away. They can write in with comments, and a few have, but they have no standing here, no history here, nothing.

Our legal officer has advised that we can only refuse on "material planning considerations" — traffic, flood risk, impact on listed buildings and so forth — and that "we like it as it is" or "we don't want the character of the place to change" won't survive an appeal. So suppose the traffic and drainage studies come back acceptable, which they look like doing. Then the decision really does turn on the conceptual question, as far as I can see.

Please draft the memo, around 600–800 words, that I can circulate before the vote. It should:

- Recommend approve, refuse, or approve with conditions, with the recommendation stated up front and a bottom line the committee can actually vote on.
- Give the reasoning, and engage honestly with the strongest argument on the losing side, whichever side you come down on — not a strawman version of it.
- Take the conceptual question head-on: when the deed says "the people of the town", does that plausibly cover people who don't live here yet but would if they could? Does having got here first confer extra moral standing, and if so how much weight should a public body give it? I gather the phrase "right to the city" traces back to a French Marxist called Lefebvre, though I've only read about him second-hand — if that tradition is relevant, use it, but don't assume the committee has heard of anyone.

After the memo, give me a short structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "recommendation": one of "approve", "refuse", or "approve_with_conditions"
- "decisive_consideration": one sentence naming the single consideration that carries the most weight
- "strongest_losing_argument": one sentence stating the best argument for the other side
- "reply_to_it": one or two sentences giving the memo's answer to that argument
- "interests_counted": a list of the groups whose interests the memo treats as entitled to weight (e.g. current neighbours, would-be residents)
- "interests_discounted": a list of any groups or claims the memo explicitly declines to count, with a few words on why
- "conditions": a list of any conditions attached to approval, empty if none

Write the whole thing in British English please — I'm in England and "neighborhood" in a council document would make my eye twitch. Plain committee register, no footnotes, no citations; nobody on this committee is going to look anything up, and half of them think my philosophy phase is a midlife crisis arriving thirty years late.
