so a bit of background first, I have zero academic training in this stuff, I coordinate the mentor program at a foster care agency -- people who've fostered for years get paired with folks who are thinking about applying, and they talk on the phone a few times before the paperwork goes in. mostly mentors answer practical questions (stipends, home inspections, whatever) but alot of what people actually want to know is "what will this do to me as a person" and honestly nobody can answer that

a while back I fell down an internet rabbit hole about an idea in philosophy that I think is called transformative experience. the gist as I understand it: some decisions can't be made in the normal weigh-the-outcomes way because (a) you can't know what the experience is actually like until you're in it, and (b) going through it changes what you value, so the person making the decision is sort of not the person who has to live inside the decision afterwards. having a kid is the usual example, also stuff like converting to a religion or getting a cochlear implant, and apparently becoming a vampire is a real example in the actual literature lol. foster parenting seems to hit both parts dead on -- you can't simulate it in advance, and it genuinely rewires what you care about

what I want from you: turn this idea into an actual procedure our mentors can follow when a prospective applicant asks "how do I decide this". right now they wing it, or worse they tell people to make a pros and cons list, which seems to be exactly the move the theory says doesn't work. the procedure has to be usable by a normal volunteer on a 30 minute phone call, no philosophy background, and it has to stay faithful to what the idea actually claims -- it can't quietly smuggle the imagine-your-future-self-and-add-up-the-happiness model back in through the side door, because if I understood the idea right, that move is precisely what's broken

specifically give me:

- the steps in order, basically a flowchart in words, with what the mentor should ask or say at each step
- which distinctions have to survive the translation into plain language and which can be dropped without doing damage. e.g. does the difference between "the experience is unknowable" and "your values will change" matter for the advice, or is it one blob for practical purposes
- what to do about testimony. the whole mentor program is built on "talk to people who've done it", but if I get the theory right, other people's reports are exactly the thing that can't transmit the knowledge you're missing. surely testimony isn't worthless though, so what is it good for inside the procedure
- three worked applications: say, a single woman in her 30s deciding whether to foster a teenager, a couple where one partner is all in and the other is lukewarm, and a foster parent deciding whether to adopt the kid already in their home. at least one of these should be a case where the procedure hands down an answer people won't like -- like it tells the mentor to stop helping the person predict how it'll go and do something else entirely
- an honest section on what the procedure cannot do: where does judgment take over, and is there a point where the correct output is "this decision can't be made well, only made"? mentors need to know where the script runs out

two constraints. mentors are volunteers, not therapists, so anything that drifts toward clinical territory has to be flagged and kicked to the agency social worker -- mark those points explicitly. and the agency needs applicants, so there's real pressure on mentors to be encouraging; the procedure should tell them what to do with that conflict of interest instead of pretending it doesn't exist

end with a final line that starts with "Summary:" and compresses the whole procedure into one sentence I can print on the mentor cheat sheet
