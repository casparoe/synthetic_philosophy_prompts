Pretend you're a professional moral philosopher who's spent years on promissory obligation and is now moderating an adversarial collaboration. Take your time and think carefully before you start drafting; maybe brainstorm a list of candidate agreed claims and candidate cruxes first, then write the document.

Here's the setup. Two colleagues of mine have been bickering for ages about why promises bind. One is a Scanlon-style expectation theorist: promises bind because, given social practices of relying on assurances, making a promise creates expectations in the promisee, and there's a general duty not to let people down when you've knowingly got them to rely on you. The other is a normative-powers theorist in roughly Raz's vein: promises bind because we simply have the power to create obligations by communicating an intention to do so, and that power is justified by the value of being able to bind yourself (giving gifts of commitment, planning a shared life, etc.). They argue past each other constantly, so I've made them sit down and produce a joint statement.

Draft the document they should produce. Concretely, I want:

1. A numbered list of claims both would sign -- about what promising involves (the communication, the uptake), about the obvious cases, about the difference between a promise and a mere prediction or statement of intention. These have to be substantive, not mush like "promises matter morally", but also not slanted towards either theory.
2. A disagreements table: each row states the disagreement in wording each side would accept as a fair statement of its own position, not the other side's caricature of it.
3. The cruxes -- the sub-questions where, if one side lost, they'd actually have to revise their view. Some candidates I suspect belong here: whether a promise still binds when the promisee never finds out or doesn't care (think promises to the dead); whether coerced promises bind and why exactly not; whether you can promise to do something immoral; and whether the obligation is really owed to the promisee or just somehow "out there". For each crux, give the concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts about that case and how they'd explain the prediction away if it went against them.
4. A final short section saying which bits of their disagreement, if any, they conclude were actually verbal (e.g. arguing over what counts as "the promise" vs "the expectation") rather than genuine.

One constraint: no citations or reading lists, just the substance. And if you find that on some crux the two theories give identical verdicts by different routes, say so explicitly -- that's the interesting bit, since it suggests the crux isn't load-bearing after all. Aim for something both parties could actually put their names to without wincing.
