Longtime lurker on LW/ACX/econ Twitter here, and I keep running into sloppy claims about discount rates, so I want a sanity check on something. A friend of mine (very smart, works in climate policy, not a philosopher) summarized the state of play on discounting the future for me like this, and I want to know how much of it to trust:

"Economists discount future benefits, and the way they do it has two parts. One part is pure time preference -- valuing people's welfare less just because they're born later -- and this is basically a settled question: philosophers since Ramsey have shown it's irrational, an incoherent bias like thinking Tuesdays matter less than Wednesdays, and Ramsey himself called it ethically indefensible, so no serious person defends it anymore. The other part is discounting because future people will be richer and money means less to them, which is obviously fine. The whole fight over the Stern Review's low discount rate vs. Nordhaus's higher one is therefore really just a fight about whether to sneak pure time preference back in, and the longtermist people are right that the only honest position is a zero rate. Also, since you can't justify valuing future people less, extinction risk dominates all cost-benefit math by construction, which is what Bostrom showed."

Now, my instinct is that like 70% of this is right and 30% is him laundering his longtermist sympathies into fake consensus, but I can't tell exactly where the seam is. In particular: is it actually true that pure time preference has been "shown" irrational, or is that Ramsey's (influential!) opinion being upgraded into a theorem? Is the Stern/Nordhaus disagreement really just about pure time preference, or is he conflating it with the uncertainty and growth parts? And does the move from "no pure time preference" to "extinction risk dominates" actually go through, or is there a step missing?

Please go through it claim by claim: what would a philosopher who works on this accept, what's a defensible simplification, and what's wrong. And be commited about it -- I don't want a "reasonable people disagree, here's the menu" answer. Tell me which parts you'd strike if you were editing his paragraph, whether the overall pro-zero-discount-rate conclusion survives fixing the errors, and where exactly his argument for it breaks if it does.
