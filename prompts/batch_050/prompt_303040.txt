In his 1860 Glasgow speech "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?", Frederick Douglass broke with the Garrisonian abolitionists and set out a rule of constitutional interpretation. The key passage runs: "it should be borne in mind that the mere text, and only the text, and not any commentaries or creeds written by those who wished to give the text a meaning apart from its plain reading, was adopted as the Constitution of the United States... the intentions of those who framed the Constitution, be they good or bad, for slavery or against slavery, are so respected so far, and so far only, as we find those intentions plainly stated in the Constitution... It was what they said that was adopted by the people, not what they were ashamed or afraid to say, and really omitted to say."

Nearly a century and a half after Douglass's death, American constitutional theory developed a doctrine he never encountered: original public meaning originalism. On this view, the meaning of a constitutional provision was fixed at the moment of its ratification by how a reasonable member of the ratifying public would have understood its words; that meaning binds judges today unless and until the people amend the text; and historical materials from the founding era (convention records, ratification debates, newspapers, dictionaries) are admissible not as statements of the framers' private intentions but as evidence of what the words publicly meant at the time. One consequence is that the Fourteenth Amendment, for instance, is said to carry today exactly the content an ordinary reader of 1868 would have given it, and that judges who update that content in light of modern values are illegitimately amending the document from the bench.

My question: had Douglass lived to see this doctrine fully articulated, would he have embraced it as the vindication of his rule, or rejected it as a betrayal of that rule's spirit? I want you to take a definite position here, not survey the possibilities. In defending your verdict, please grapple with the genuine complications on both sides rather than cherry-picking. On the one hand, his insistence that only the text was adopted, and his scorn for reading the Constitution through the secret motives of its drafters, seems tailor-made for original public meaning, and even anticipates its distinction between private intention and public understanding. On the other hand, consider at least the following: Douglass elsewhere read the Constitution in light of the Declaration of Independence and background principles of natural justice, which is not obviously the same as asking how a typical 1868 reader would have parsed the words; he chose reinterpretation of a fixed text precisely as the alternative to disunion and formal amendment, which raises the question of whether he really believed that unjust meanings must simply be endured until amended; and his hostility was directed at the Philadelphia Convention's closed-door debates, which modern originalists themselves mine for evidence of public meaning, so you should say whether his rule forbids that practice or merely redirects it. Note also that your verdict should survive the observation that Douglass was arguing against a specific opponent's specific use of history, not offering a theory of interpretation for its own sake: does that context shrink the passage's authority, or does the reasoning he gives transcend the occasion?

Please give me your answer as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, no tables.
