So I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on a bit of worldbuilding for my next book. In the story, humans make first contact, and the aliens turn out to be tiny — hummingbird-sized — but plainly smarter than us. Their brains are minuscule, just absurdly dense and efficient. Writing scenes with them keeps forcing me to think about whether brain size is morally relevant at all, and I keep flip-flopping, so I figured id ask.

Compare two ways of answering that question. The first is what I'd call the raw capacity view: what matters morally is cognitive capacity — neuron counts, processing power, flexibility of behavior, however you want to cash it out — and brain size only matters insofar as it's a rough proxy for those things. On this view my tiny aliens are obviously persons, full stop. The second is what I'll call the embodied scale view: brain size matters morally because it sets the scale at which a creature lives — how fast its thoughts run, how much of the world it can take in at once, what a long life is for it, how much "room" its inner world has. On this view, a big-brained whale and a small but neuron-dense crow might deserve different *kinds* of moral consideration even at equal intelligence, and maybe my aliens' moral status is genuinely strange, not just ordinary-personhood in a small package.

Which view holds up better? I especially want to know where each one breaks down. What does the raw capacity view do with animals that have small brains but weirdley efficient ones (corvids, parrots, octopuses)? Does it end up committed to the conclusion that a future pocket-sized superintelligence deserves more moral weight than all of humanity combined? And does the embodied scale view just smuggle in anthropocentrism by quietly treating human scale as the default, or is there something real there?

One more thing, since I'm a novelist and can't help myself: which view makes for better drama, and should that count as evidence for or against its truth? Half-joking, but only half.

Please answer in American English.
