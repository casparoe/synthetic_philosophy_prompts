So I've been teaching a unit on dispositions and I keep coming back to the Chernobyl accident as a real-world case, and I want you to actually work it through with me rather than give me the encyclopedia version.

Quick recap of the facts as I understand them (correct me only if I get something badly wrong, but I think this is all standard): in April 1986, during a safety test at low power, reactor 4 went into an uncontrollable power surge, and the operators pressed AZ-5, the emergency shutdown button that drives all the control rods fully into the core. That's the last-resort safety measure, the thing whose whole job is to kill the reaction. But the RBMK's control rods had graphite displacers on their tips, and for the first several seconds of insertion those tips displaced water at the bottom of the core and actually *increased* reactivity there. So pressing the shutdown button, in the conditions that held that night, initially sped up the very reaction it was supposed to stop. The standard account is that this "positive scram" effect contributed to the explosion.

Here's what I want. Take the scram system as a bearer of a disposition -- roughly, the disposition to shut down the chain reaction when AZ-5 is pressed. Now run it through the machinery of the dispositions literature: the simple conditional analysis, Lewis's reformed version, Martin's electro-fink cases, and the masks/antidotes idea (Johnston, Bird). The thing I can't decide, and where I want you to take a stand, is what *kind* of failure this is. Is the graphite-tip design a mask (the disposition is still there, something interferes with its manifestation)? Is it a fink (the very stimulus -- inserting the rods -- removes or reverses the disposition)? Or is it neither, because the case shows the disposition was misdescribed from the start and "shuts down the reactor when AZ-5 is pressed" was never the right disposition ascription to begin with? Pick one, defend it, and say what's wrong with the other two options rather than just listing them.

Then run it in the other direction: what does the episode do to the theories? Lewis reformed the conditional analysis precisely to handle finks, but does a case where the stimulus *reverses* the manifestation fit his story, or does it break it in a way ordinary finks don't? And does the fact that the disaster depended on the reactor being in a weird low-power, xenon-poisoned state support the view that disposition ascriptions always carry an implicit "in normal conditions" clause, or does it show that clause is doing too much work to be honest?

Be committed throughout -- I'd rather get a definite verdict I disagree with than a menu. Also, flag any place where the philosophy's verdict depends on an engineering detail you can't be sure of from memory, and just reason from the version of the facts I've given. Write it in American English.
