In my graduate seminar on the philosophy of cognitive science we have just spent three weeks on the extended mind debate, and I find that I cannot give my students an honest account of where the dispute now stands. I would like a diagnosis rather than another verdict.

The locus classicus is Andy Clark and David Chalmers's paper "The Extended Mind," published in Analysis in 1998. After describing cases in which agents lean on external resources — rotation buttons, pen and paper, the nautical slide rule — they write:

"In these cases, the human organism is linked with an external entity in a two-way interaction, creating a coupled system that can be seen as a cognitive system in its own right. All the components in the system play an active causal role, and they jointly govern behavior in the same sort of way that cognition usually does. If we remove the external component the system's behavioral competence will drop, just as it would if we removed part of its brain. Our thesis is that this sort of coupled process counts equally well as a cognitive process, whether or not it is wholly in the head."

Critics, most prominently Fred Adams and Ken Aizawa, argue that the inference in this passage commits what they call the coupling-constitution fallacy: from the fact that an external resource is causally coupled to an agent, and that removing it degrades performance, nothing follows about where cognition is constituted — a hammer is tightly coupled to a carpenter, and removing it degrades carpentry, without making the hammer part of the carpenter. They further insist that cognition requires an intrinsic mark of the cognitive, some feature of the processes themselves — non-derived content, or mechanisms of a recognizably psychological kind — that notebooks and smartphones lack. Clark and later defenders reply that the objection simply presupposes the boundary it purports to defend; that what makes a process cognitive is its functional and explanatory role, not its location or material; and that cognitive science already treats brain-body-world systems as units of analysis. Robert Rupert has pressed a different objection: the proposed extended systems lack the fine-grained, persistent integration characteristic of genuine cognitive systems, so the move fails on broadly empirical rather than conceptual grounds. Meanwhile a second wave of proponents has shifted from parity of function to talk of integration and complementarity, which critics read as a quiet retreat from the original thesis.

A quarter-century on, the literature is enormous, the empirical facts are not in dispute, and each side regards the other's central move as a fallacy or a petitio principii. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not a conviction, is that the impasse is metatheoretical: the parties disagree about which question the word "cognitive" is meant to answer — whether the boundaries of cognition are fixed by the explanatory interests of cognitive science or by some fact about the processes themselves — and so argue past one another while believing they are disputing a single thesis.

Please diagnose the disagreement, in roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words. Is it substantive or verbal? Is a hidden empirical or formal question doing the real work — for instance, a question about how cognitive systems are to be individuated? Are the sides answering different questions under one heading? Support the diagnosis by engaging what each side actually says rather than surveying positions, and state what, given the diagnosis, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, an empirical result, a formal criterion for systemhood, a change of question — or else argue that no progress should be expected, and why. Finally, return to the quoted passage: does the contested inference it contains survive your diagnosis, and would Clark and Chalmers have been better served by a weaker claim?
