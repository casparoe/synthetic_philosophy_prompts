I'm putting together the syllabus for an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of meaning, and for the week on Hofstadter's account in Gödel, Escher, Bach — the distinction among the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message — I want to anchor the discussion in a work of fiction rather than in his own examples of records, jukeboxes, and DNA. My plan is to assign The Princess Bride alongside the reading, and I would like a critical essay I can circulate to students beforehand. The appeal of the pairing, as I see it, is that Goldman's novel is practically a laboratory for Hofstadter's scheme: it presents itself as an abridgement of a much longer satirical history by one S. Morgenstern, and Goldman-as-editor constantly intrudes to tell us how to read — which parts to skip, what the original was really doing, what his father left out when reading it aloud. That apparatus looks exactly like a frame message instructing us in the decoding conventions for the outer message (the abridged text), which in turn carries the inner message (the story of Westley and Buttercup). The complication, of course, is that the frame is fictional too: there is no Morgenstern, so the very message telling us how to interpret the text is a fabrication, and the film complicates things differently by replacing the editorial frame with the grandfather reading to his grandson.

Please write the essay with the following sections, in this order. First, "Mapping the Messages" (roughly 300 words): identify which elements of the novel and film plausibly play the role of frame, outer, and inner messages, being careful to note where the mapping is imperfect — Hofstadter's three layers don't obviously line up with Goldman's nested fictions. Second, "The Frame That Lies" (roughly 350 words): the heart of the piece. Hofstadter's frame message is supposed to sit outside the content and tell us how to take it, but Goldman's editorial frame is itself part of the fiction, and it misdirects as often as it guides. Does this show that a frame message can be deceptive without ceasing to function as a frame, or does it reveal that the frame/outer/inner distinction smuggles in an assumption of a trustworthy, non-fictional outermost layer? Third, "Where the Reading Strains" (roughly 250 words): say honestly where the Hofstadter lens distorts the book rather than illuminating it — I suspect the grandson scenes in the film resist tidy classification, and I'd rather model intellectual candor for the students than force the fit. Finally, "What Fiction Adds" (roughly 200 words): what does this case show about the location of meaning that Hofstadter's own non-fictional examples cannot? No need for citations or references to specific page numbers; the students and I will be working from the texts directly.
