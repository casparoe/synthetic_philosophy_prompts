I'm a junior in high school and a member of our school's philosophy club. Last week we were debating utilitarianism, and I brought up the classic thought experiment in which a surgeon could kill one healthy patient to harvest organs that would save five dying ones, arguing that our reaction to it puts pressure on the idea that the right action is always the one that maximizes total well-being. My opponent replied with a line I'd never heard before: one person's modus ponens is another person's modus tollens. He explained, roughly, that just as I reason from "the surgeon's act is wrong" to "utilitarianism is false," he could equally well reason from "utilitarianism is true" to "the surgeon's act is right," so my thought experiment doesn't establish anything. The room seemed to treat this as a decisive move, and our faculty advisor let it stand without comment.

Something about the move bothers me, but I can't articulate what. Could you lay out the different precise claims that this slogan might be expressing? I suspect it ranges from a trivial observation about the logical form of arguments, to a substantive epistemological thesis about how intuitions and theories ought to be weighed against each other, to something like a license to dismiss any counterexample one dislikes. For each reading, I'd like to know whether it's a platitude, a defensible but contestable position, or actually mistaken, and what further assumptions it would need to be true.

Most importantly: which of these readings, if any, would actually justify what my opponent did, namely treating the transplant case as a wash rather than as evidence against his view? And if the slogan, properly understood, doesn't settle the matter, what does settle it — what makes it rational in some cases to trust the intuition and reject the theory, and in other cases to keep the theory and bite the bullet on the intuition? Please organize the readings as a numbered menu I can bring to the club's next meeting, since I intend to reopen the debate.
