I co-organise a fortnightly philosophy of science seminar at my university, and for next term we are trying something new with our two most argumentative regulars: a defender of counterfactual theories of causation, working broadly in the tradition of analysing causes in terms of what would have happened otherwise, and an interventionist who thinks in terms of causal models and hypothetical manipulations. After two years of watching them talk past each other — she keeps producing preemption and trumping cases she claims his framework handles only with ad hoc repairs, he keeps replying that her repairs multiply primitive similarity judgements while only his account actually underwrites the methods scientists use to infer causes — we have asked them to co-author a joint statement rather than continue debating. I would like you to draft that statement for them, as the starting text they will then argue over line by line.

The document should have three parts. First, a numbered list of claims both parties could genuinely sign: what causal claims commit us to beyond constant conjunction, how causation differs from mere correlation, what any adequate theory owes us on cases of preemption, and whether manipulability is necessary, sufficient, or merely evidence for causation. Second, the residual disagreements, each stated in wording the relevant party would accept as a fair description of their own position. I particularly want the dispute over reduction and circularity stated exactly — she aims at an analysis of causation in non-causal terms, while he happily defines it via interventions, which are themselves causal notions — because I suspect at least half their quarrels reduce to the question of whether that kind of circularity is a vice or simply honesty about what the concept is for. Third, the cruxes: for each one, a concrete case or argument both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Candidates I would expect to appear include late preemption, trumping (the classic case of two sergeants simultaneously ordering a major to advance, where he advances because of the orders but it is unclear whose order caused what), causation by omission or absence, and causation at the level of fundamental physics, where nothing looks much like an intervention variable. Where part of their disagreement strikes you as verbal rather than substantive, mark it as such and say why. I will fill in the references to the literature myself, so please do not cite particular books or papers by name — describe positions and arguments, not sources.
