Two colleagues of mine, both with some training in logic, got into a running argument about whether algorithmic information theory vindicates Occam's razor. (1) Anna claimed that Solomonoff induction settles the question: by assigning each hypothesis a prior of 2 to the power of minus its Kolmogorov complexity, it gives a mathematically precise sense in which simpler hypotheses are more probable before any evidence comes in, so preferring simple theories is not an aesthetic prejudice but a theorem-backed rule. (2) Ben replied that Kolmogorov complexity is defined relative to a universal Turing machine, and while the invariance theorem bounds the difference between machines by a constant, any particular finite string — including the statement of any actual scientific theory — can be made arbitrarily simple by an adversarial choice of machine. Since there are infinitely many universal machines, most of them encoding bizarre priors, the math vindicates *a* simplicity bias but not *our* simplicity bias. (3) Anna answered that the machine-dependence is only an additive constant, which is swamped as data accumulates: all universal priors converge on the same predictions in the limit, so the disagreement is a finite nuisance rather than a refutation of the razor. (4) Ben said this concedes his point — convergence in the limit says nothing about which of two live theories to believe *now*, and science is done entirely in the finite regime where the constant dominates; and if she falls back on the "natural" choice of machine, that just reinstates our pre-theoretic simplicity judgments under a technical label, with the razor doing the work and the math providing decoration. Neither would give ground, and they asked me to referee. Please go through the four moves in order, saying who is right at each step and why, note what if anything both sides are missing, and give an overall verdict on whether Solomonoff-style reasoning genuinely justifies Occam's razor or merely relocates it.
