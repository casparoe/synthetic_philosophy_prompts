I've pretty much bought Dennett's picture that all the design in nature gets built by cranes — bottom-up algorithmic processes like selection and learning — and that skyhooks, anything that lifts complexity in from nowhere, are cheats. Working on ML systems has only strenghtened this: gradient descent keeps producing capabilities that smart people insisted would need something skyhook-y.

But I'm starting to wonder whether the position actually risks anything, and that's what I want from you: what would count against it? Concretely:

- Is there any possible empirical finding that would force a cranes-only person to accept a skyhook? Or is "keep looking for the crane" just a methodological rule dressed up as a claim about the world, i.e. unfalsifiable?
- Where exactly is the line between good reductionism and what Dennett calls greedy reductionism? I want a criterion for when you've skipped explanatory levels you shouldn't have skipped, not just a list of examples of people doing it badly.
- Does the view have a self-application problem? If reasons and normativity bottom out in selection pressures and gradient descent, can the cranes person still say some arguments are genuinely better than others without quietly smuggling in a skyhook?

Also: which of these would a committed cranes person concede actually counts against the view, and which would they wave away as confusion? Rank the threats from fatal to merely awkward.

Last thing — keep settled and contested seperate. Selection being algorithmic is settled science as far as I know; whether meaning, normativity, and consciousness all bottom out in cranes is not. Don't write as if the experts have wrapped all that up.
