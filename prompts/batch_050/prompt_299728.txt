I work as a software engineer on multi-agent systems, and I've been reading a popular book about game theory and rationality. It summarizes the idea of superrationality — which it attributes to Douglas Hofstadter — like this:

"Superrationality is Hofstadter's solution to the prisoner's dilemma. If both players are perfectly rational and each knows the other is, either can reason as follows: 'The other player thinks exactly the way I do, so whatever conclusion I reach, they will reach the same one. My only real choice is between both of us cooperating and both of us defecting, and mutual cooperation is clearly better. So I cooperate.' Since this reasoning is available to any rational agent, superrationality shows that rationality by itself guarantees cooperation in the prisoner's dilemma — no contracts, repetition, or enforcement required. This is why game theorists regard the one-shot prisoner's dilemma as solved in principle, and why rational agents can be trusted to cooperate whenever their situation is symmetric."

When I described this to a colleague, he took it further. The agents in a system we're building are all fine-tuned copies of the same base model, and he argued that in symmetric situations they're effectively guaranteed to reach the same conclusions, so we can drop the cross-agent verification and monitoring layers from our design — superrationality, he says, gives us that guarantee for free.

Neither of us has formal training in philosophy or game theory, and I'm skeptical, but I can't pinpoint where the reasoning slips. Could you go through the book's summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts an expert would accept as accurate, which are common simplifications, and which misrepresent what superrationality actually claims? Three specific points bother me. First, the step from "we reason alike" to "we will reach the same conclusion" — is that licensed even between idealized reasoners, and does it depend on anything stronger than mere similarity? Second, is superrationality really the resolution of the dilemma that mainstream game theory accepts, or does it conflict with how standard game theory defines rational play? Third, whatever guarantee the summary describes between idealized agents, does it transfer to language-model agents that share weights but differ in prompts, context, and sampling randomness? Finally: does anything that survives correction in the summary actually support my colleague's conclusion, or is his argument leaning entirely on the parts that don't hold up?
