I'm taking a course on nineteenth-century German philosophy this term, and for my final paper I want to write on Schopenhauer's account of music. The two claims I keep coming back to are, first, his view that music is unique among the arts because it doesn't represent the world through the grades of the Will's objectification (as the other arts do via the Ideas) but is instead a direct copy of the Will itself — the voice of striving, satisfaction, and renewed striving — and second, his view that genuine aesthetic experience is a state of will-less contemplation, a temporary release from the tyranny of desire. I've been rewatching Kubrick's A Clockwork Orange (I've also read Burgess's novel) and I can't shake the feeling that the film is an unwitting test case for both claims, and possibly a counterexample to one of them. I'd like a careful reading of the film through this lens before I commit to a thesis.

Here's the tension as I see it. Alex's most intense experiences come while listening to Beethoven, above all the Ninth Symphony, but his transports seem inseparable from — indeed, they seem to accompany and even fuel — his violence. Music for Alex is not a Sabbath from willing; it looks more like an amplifier of it. That appears to vindicate the first claim (music as the Will's own voice, indifferent to morality) while falsifying the second (aesthetic experience as release from the Will). The obvious defense on Schopenhauer's behalf is to say that Alex never listens aesthetically at all — that he hears music egotistically, as a soundtrack to his desires, rather than as a will-less spectator. But I'm worried this defense is either ad hoc or else trivializes the theory by making it immune to counterexample: if any case of music intensifying desire gets reclassified as "not really aesthetic," what would falsify the second claim? Then there's the Ludovico sequence, where the state pairs the Ninth with the conditioning process and thereby destroys Alex's capacity to hear it without agony — the state treats music as a behavioristic lever, and the loss of his musical life is collateral damage no one in power even registers. And the endings diverge in a way that seems philosophically loaded: Burgess's Alex simply outgrows violence, while Kubrick's Alex is restored to himself, fantasizing to the Ninth in the final scene, "cured" in the worst sense.

So here's what I'd like from you. Read the film against both of Schopenhauer's claims separately rather than treating his aesthetics as a single package: where does it confirm him (the amorality of great art, the state's indifference to aesthetic experience, the final scene suggesting that music voices the Will without elevating it), and where does it genuinely complicate or pressure him (music as incitement rather than quietus, the status of Alex's listening)? Please address whether the "he isn't really contemplating" defense is available to Schopenhauer without rendering the theory vacuous, and whether either the novel's or the film's ending fits his framework better — bearing in mind that for Schopenhauer the only genuine escape is denial of the Will, which neither Alex nor the doctors nor the state comes anywhere near. Finally, I'd like your judgment on what, if anything, the film contributes to this debate that a philosophical argument alone couldn't — my hunch is that it dramatizes the separability of aesthetic rapture from moral character more vividly than any thought experiment, but I want to be pushed on that if it doesn't hold up.
