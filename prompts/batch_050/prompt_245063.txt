I've been reading posts and comment threads about judging the past for years, and I recently had an exchange that left me stuck. An interlocutor laid out the following argument, numbered roughly as he gave it:

(1) Fair blame requires that the agent could have recognized the decisive reasons against their action — not in a bare logical sense, but in the sense that those reasons were actually available given the information, concepts, and evidence their environment supplied.

(2) The ability to recognize moral reasons is a social achievement, like the ability to diagnose an infection: it depends on what your epistemic environment has accumulated. No one generates it alone.

(3) Historical agents we now condemn lived in environments where the decisive arguments and counterevidence were absent, marginal, or socially invisible. That is precisely what people mean by "the standards of their time."

(4) So blaming such agents by our standards is a category error — the historian's version of faulting a medieval physician for not prescribing antibiotics.

(5) Notice, though, that (1) and (2) never mention historical distance. They invoke only what was epistemically available to the agent. And that condition is local: every person's capacity to recognize reasons is fixed by their particular upbringing, education, temperament, and milieu.

(6) For nearly any present-day wrongdoer, the decisive reasons were, in exactly this sense, unavailable to them too — or at least we are in no position to establish that they weren't.

(7) Therefore fair blame is nearly always illegitimate, for the past and the present alike.

He cheerfully accepts (7); I can't. But every time I reach for a disanalogy — that a whole era being blind is different from one person being blind, or that some historical figures plainly had access to the right arguments and ignored them — I find the disanalogy either collapses under scrutiny or smuggles in a premise I've already conceded. I'd like to keep two commitments if possible: that blaming at least some contemporaries is legitimate, and that judging at least some historical figures by present standards is not obviously confused. Which step do you reject, why did it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost? If you think the argument is valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender — and whether the surviving view still lets a historian say anything evaluative about the past at all.
