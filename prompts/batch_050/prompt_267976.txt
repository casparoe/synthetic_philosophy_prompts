Next week I am giving the toast at the retirement dinner for Marjorie Quill, who for thirty-one years was the executive officer of our philosophy department and held sole power of countersignature over everything the department did: speaker invitations, grant submissions, curriculum changes, press statements, purchases over fifty pounds. She is famous, notorious, and beloved for almost never signing anything promptly. Forms sat in her tray for weeks. Enthusiasms expired waiting for her. Colleagues called her "the bottleneck" and "Dr. Pending," and someone once said at a faculty meeting, in frustration, that she was the reason this department has no legacy. The room I will be addressing is mixed: half philosophers, several of whom work on collective action problems and will catch any technical slip; half Marjorie's family, who know only that she was "in admin" and that her colleagues seem oddly, fiercely fond of her.

Please write the toast. It should be built around a single idea, explained plainly enough for the family but accurately enough for the specialists: the unilateralist's curse. When any member of a group can single-handedly bring about an outcome that binds everyone — send the email, book the speaker, submit the form — and each member privately judges whether it is a good idea, the outcome will tend to happen even when most of the group would have opposed it, because the most optimistic member simply acts. Unilateral power plus dispersed enthusiasm reliably produces collective mistakes. Marjorie's genius, the toast should argue, was to grasp this decades before anyone gave it a name: her legendary inaction was not timidity but a one-woman constitutional remedy — a mandatory cooling-off period, a human supermajority requirement, the reason our department alone among its peers has never had to issue a retraction, a public apology, or a redundancy round.

Requirements:

- Roughly five minutes when read aloud, about 700 words.
- Explain the curse in one passage the family can follow and the philosophers cannot object to; no jargon, but also no hand-waving a specialist would wince at.
- Invent two or three concrete near-misses she quietly prevented (in the vein of: a charismatic visitor someone nearly hired on the spot; a donor with conditions; a departmental rebrand). Each should be funny but plausible, and each should show a different colleague having been, at that moment, the most optimistic person in the room.
- Include the reveal that in thirty-one years she signed exactly one thing on sight, within the hour — and what it was. Invent it; it should reframe her character rather than merely get a laugh.
- End on genuine warmth: the room should come to understand that her refusal to act was a form of care, without the speech ever calling it that in so many words.
- Do not lecture. The philosophy should carry the sentiment, not compete with it.
