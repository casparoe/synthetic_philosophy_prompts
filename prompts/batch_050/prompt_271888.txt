I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about the long-running fight between professional archaeologists and historians on one side and the authors of what gets called alternative or fringe history on the other -- Atlantis literalists, ancient-astronaut writers, proponents of radically compressed or invented chronologies. What strikes me, after reading both camps for months, is that the demarcation debate itself seems completely stuck. The professionals keep publishing pieces explaining, roughly, the criterion that separates their work from the fringe: respect for the full body of evidence, willingness to abandon a thesis, engagement with peer criticism. The fringe writers respond that they do all of these things and that the academy simply refuses to look. Each side's argument has been essentially the same for decades, and as far as I can tell no one has ever been moved by the other side's statement of the criterion.

My working hypothesis, which I'd like tested rather than confirmed, is that both sides are misdiagnosing the disagreement by borrowing the frame of the demarcation problem in philosophy of science. Pseudoscience is often genuinely untestable or immunized against tests, but fringe history is different: its claims are usually quite concrete and locally checkable -- a specific civilization at a specific time, a specific map showing an ice-free coastline, a specific chronology requiring that certain documents be forgeries. Individual claims get checked and fail, yet the enterprise is unharmed because the author migrates to a new claim or adds an auxiliary explanation for why the check was inapplicable. If that's right, then the stalled debate is not about what the demarcation criterion is -- both sides might even agree on the criterion in the abstract -- but about what counts as an instance of satisfying it, which is a disagreement about which cases count as data. And beneath that, I suspect, sits a third disagreement that neither side states: whether the argument is really about epistemic standards at all, or about who is entitled to public credibility about the past -- in which case no criterion, however precisely stated, could end it.

So what I'd like from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict on Atlantis. Is this impasse a substantive disagreement about standards of evidence, a verbal one in which both sides use words like 'evidence' and 'open-mindedness' differently, a case of two groups answering different questions under one heading, or a disagreement about starting intuitions -- about how much anomaly a mainstream narrative can absorb, how much trust institutions deserve -- that neither side can actually argue for? Ground the diagnosis in what the two sides characteristically say and do, including the asymmetries between them (one side can lose a claim and survive; the other has to defend a whole edifice). And then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a worked case study both sides must reckon with, a sociological rather than epistemological reframing -- or an argument that no progress should be expected, because the debate is doing social work for both parties that resolution would destroy.
