So my roommate and her friend (both in the same phil mind seminar) had this long argument at our kitchen table last night about epiphenomenalism and I was just doing dishes and listening, and now I keep thinking about it, so I want a verdict. Here's the exchange as best I remember it.

Move 1: The friend (call her Maya) said epiphenomenalism -- the view that conscious experiences are real but cause nothing physical -- is self-undermining, because of what she called the judgment problem. If my feeling of red causes nothing, then it can't cause my belief or my utterance that I'm seeing red. A molecule-for-molecule zombie twin with no experiences at all would produce the exact same reports. So the epiphenomenalist is committed to saying we'd all go around sincerely reporting rich inner lives even in a world with no inner lives, which means our confidence that we're conscious can't be connected to the consciousness itself.

Move 2: My roommate (call her Jess) said that's too fast. Beliefs don't have to be caused by their subject matter to be true or even reliable -- we have plenty of true beliefs about numbers and numbers cause nothing. And more importantly, the epiphenomenalist isn't stuck with random disconnection: there's a common-cause structure available. Some brain state causes both the experience and the report of the experience, on parallel tracks. Then the reports track the experiences perfectly reliably, just like two clocks set to the same time agree with each other without either one affecting the other. The zombie twin lacks the experience but also, on a dualist view, its brain isn't hooked up to the experience-producing laws, so the comparison is rigged.

Move 3: Maya said the parallel-clock picture is exactly the problem. The reliability is a giant cosmic coincidence written into the psycho-physical laws, and nothing about the subject's beliefs is sensitive to the phenomenal facts. If the laws had paired pain-behavior and pain-reports with the feeling of warmth instead, everyone would still calmly say 'ouch, that hurts' while feeling cozy, and nobody could ever notice. A belief-forming process that delivers the same output no matter what the truth is can't count as knowledge, so on this view nobody knows they're conscious -- and Jess's own belief that she's in pain right now (she'd stubbed her toe earlier, it came up) would be unjustified by the thing that makes it true.

Move 4: Jess said the sensitivity demand proves too much, since lots of knowledge isn't counterfactually sensitive in that way -- my belief that I'm not a brain in a vat, or that the future will resemble the past, would be held fixed even in worlds where it's false. And she tried a different move: the connection between experience and judgment doesn't have to be causal at all. Maybe the phenomenal state partly constitutes the judgment, or serves as its justifier directly rather than as a cause -- the way the pain itself, not a causal chain from the pain, is what makes 'I'm in pain' reasonable to say. Knowledge by acquaintance rather than by causation.

Move 5: Maya's last move was that this is just giving up on the view. Once the experience gets to be a justifier or a constituent of the judgment, it's doing real explanatory work on a physical event, since judgments show up in behavior, and you've slid into interactionism or identity theory or panpsychism or something -- anything but epiphenomenalism. Jess said that conflates causal relevance with justificatory relevance and the two can come apart. Then my pasta was done and they moved on to complaining about the readings.

Please adjudicate this properly, move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it's clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible-but-with-a-problem, and justify each rating in a sentence or three. Then tell me who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing -- e.g. is there a way to save epiphenomenalist self-knowledge that neither of them tried, or a version of Maya's objection that's stronger than the one she actually gave? I'd like the whole thing as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, just like an email laying out the verdict.
