THE QUESTION (from the homework sheet):
Explain the control principle and why it seems to generate a paradox about moral assessment. Present the four kinds of moral luck Nagel distinguishes, with an example of each, and explain how each one puts pressure on the control principle. Finally, does Nagel think the paradox can be resolved, and does Williams's contribution to the debate help or not? Defend your answer.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER:

The control principle, as Nagel states it, is the idea that we are morally assessable only for what is under our control, or for what depends on factors under our control. The paradox arises because almost nothing seems to be fully under our control in the relevant sense, yet we constantly judge people morally for things shaped by luck. So ordinary moral practice conflicts with a principle that seems obviously true.

Nagel distinguishes four kinds of luck. First, resultant luck: luck in how our actions turn out. Nagel's example is the truck driver who hits a child through no fault of his own versus one who doesn't -- we judge the first more harshly even though the difference is pure luck. Second, circumstantial luck: luck in the kind of person you are, your character and temperament. Someone constitutionally timid might fail a moral test that a braver person passes. Third, constitutive luck: luck in the situations you find yourself in, like the person who never faces any great moral test versus someone who lived under the Nazi regime and failed it. Fourth, causal luck: luck in being determined by prior causes, the worry that everything you do is the causal result of things before you were born, so nothing is really up to you.

Each kind puts pressure on the control principle because in each case our moral judgments track outcomes, traits, and situations that the agent did not choose and could not control. If we took the principle seriously, Nagel argues, the area of genuine moral assessment would shrink almost to nothing.

On resolution: Nagel's view is that the paradox is basically an illusion and once we see clearly that the control principle is correct, we should simply stop blaming people for lucky outcomes, so the paradox dissolves. This is where Williams's contribution helps: in his 1976 paper Moral Luck, which introduced the term into the debate, Williams uses the Gauguin case to argue that rational justification of a life choice can depend on luck, supporting Nagel's conclusion that we should be less confident in our moral judgments. So between them they show the control principle should be rejected outright, which resolves the paradox.
