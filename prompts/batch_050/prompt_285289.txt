Next month I'm presenting a paper at a workshop split between Buddhist studies scholars and analytic philosophers working on desert and moral responsibility. My thesis: karma is best interpreted as a theory of moral desert rather than as a claim about impersonal cosmic causation, and a naturalized version of it -- where the desert-conferring mechanisms are psychological (character formation, guilt, habituation), social (trust, reputation, exclusion), and institutional rather than karmic rebirth -- preserves everything in the doctrine that is doing philosophical work. My argument runs through three premises: that the soteriological function of karma depends only on agents getting what they deserve, not on the mechanism delivering it; that doctrines of rebirth functioned historically as the best available mechanism story, not as the core commitment; and that naturalized karma explains moral luck better than secular desert theories because it makes desert partly self-administered through one's own dispositions. The Buddhist studies contingent will suspect I've laundered a religious doctrine into something its adherents wouldn't recognize; the analytic contingent will suspect that a desert theory without a guaranteed desert-delivery mechanism isn't a theory of desert at all but an expression of hope. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually be raised, phrased the way each camp would phrase them, marking which are mere annoyances and which are genuinely threatening, with what I should concede in each case, what I should hold, and how to reply in two minutes. Include the objection I'm most hoping nobody asks -- I suspect it concerns whether "self-administered desert" collapses my view into the claim that people deserve whatever psychology they end up with, which I don't know how to answer.
