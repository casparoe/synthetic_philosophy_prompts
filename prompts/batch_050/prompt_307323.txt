I'm a journalist working on a long-form piece about whether philosophy makes progress, and I've hit a wall that I now think is the real story: the debate about whether philosophy makes progress has itself been running for decades without visible movement. The arguments on both sides feel fully catalogued. The skeptics point to the persistence of expert disagreement — large surveys of professional philosophers show no convergence toward consensus on the central questions, in sharp contrast to the sciences. The optimists reply with a familiar toolkit: philosophy's progress takes the form of spinning off disciplines (physics, psychology, linguistics, logic, computer science all left home); it consists in refining questions rather than settling them; disagreement at the frontier is exactly what you'd expect in any healthy field, and science only looks consensual because we forget its long-settled parts and count them as background. Each side has heard the other's moves a hundred times. Nobody changes their mind.

My working hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but at least three running in parallel: a verbal dispute about what "progress" must mean to count (settled answers? cumulative results? better questions?); an empirical-historical dispute about what actually happened (did the natural sciences really emerge from philosophy by conceptual clarification, or is that a just-so story?); and a normative dispute about what standard a discipline like philosophy is fairly held to. But I may be wrong, and I'd like a genuine diagnosis rather than a survey. So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Use what each side really argues — not caricatures — to say whether the impasse is verbal, factual, normative, or a sign that the two camps are answering different questions under one heading ("is philosophy like science?" versus "what is philosophy for?").

Then, given your diagnosis: what, if anything, would count as progress in this meta-debate? A proposed metric for philosophical progress that both sides could accept as a fair test? A particular historical case study done carefully enough to force a concession? A distinction that dissolves the question? Or is there a principled reason to expect the meta-debate to be permanently stalled — and if so, does the stalledness of the debate about progress itself constitute evidence, one way or the other, about progress at the object level? I'd like that last reflexive move taken seriously rather than treated as a rhetorical flourish, since it may be the most quotable thing in my piece or a cheap trick, and I'd like to know which before I file.
