I teach an undergraduate seminar on decision theory and rational choice (about 18 students, one 75-minute session), and next month we reach Herbert Simon. Design an in-class activity that makes participants feel the difference between maximizing and satisficing from the inside, before either term is mentioned. Specifically, I want the structure of the task itself to produce two experiences: first, that once evaluating options is costly and time-limited, "find the best option available" stops being an executable instruction, so that each participant silently replaces it with some private "good enough" threshold without ever being told to; and second, that this threshold drifts with what they've already seen — an option that would have been snapped up early in the sequence gets passed over after a run of strong candidates, and vice versa — so that the class can discover, in their own recorded choices, that their standards were path-dependent rather than fixed. Give me complete rules, the materials (it should run on index cards or a spreadsheet, no special software), a scoring scheme that creates real pressure without real money, and a precise timeline for the 75 minutes. The debrief should map moments in the activity onto satisficing, aspiration-level adjustment, and bounded rationality, and then push the harder question I actually want them to sit with: was what they did a degraded approximation of an optimal-stopping rule (compare the secretary problem, where an explicit optimal policy exists and nobody in the room will have followed it), or something structurally different from optimization altogether — and does Simon's claim that this is how real agents actually decide survive the observation that some participants will feel irrationally bad about their choices afterwards? Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing at least one student will try to optimize anyway and blow the time budget, and one will game the scoring scheme), how to handle those cases without breaking the spell, and which feature of bounded rationality a 75-minute classroom exercise cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in lecture.
