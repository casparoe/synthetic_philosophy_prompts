In most U.S. jurisdictions, employment is governed by the at-will doctrine: either employer or employee may end the relationship at any time, for almost any reason or none, without owing the other party a justification. Defenders of the doctrine stress its symmetry — the employee is just as free to quit as the employer is to fire — and conclude that no one's freedom is compromised. Critics respond that a worker who can be dismissed for refusing an order she considers dishonest, or for her off-duty political speech, is not free in the same sense as her employer, whatever the formal symmetry. What conception of freedom does the at-will doctrine, and the symmetry defense in particular, presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view as precisely as you can, identify which rival conceptions of liberty (negative, positive, or republican) it quietly rules out, and say whether the doctrine is coherent on its own terms. Note too that the doctrine carves out exceptions — one may not be fired on the basis of race, for serving on a jury, or for reporting safety violations. Does that list of exceptions presuppose a different conception of freedom than the baseline rule does, and if so, is the resulting body of law consistent? Finally, describe what employment law would have to look like if the presupposed conception were replaced with freedom understood as the absence of domination rather than the absence of interference.
