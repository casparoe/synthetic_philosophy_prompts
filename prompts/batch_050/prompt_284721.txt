A reading group of five graduate students has spent a semester working through Aquinas on political authority and resistance — the De Regno, the treatment of sedition and of killing in the Summa Theologiae (II-II, questions 42 and 64), and the relevant parts of his commentary on the Sentences. At each meeting they have issued a joint verdict on a hypothetical case, aiming for fidelity to Aquinas's texts rather than to their own intuitions. Their record so far:

Case 1. A legitimately installed king degenerates into tyranny, and a private citizen, acting on his own judgment, assassinates him. Verdict: impermissible — private presumption and sedition.

Case 2. A people that had the right to choose its own ruler deposes the tyrant it installed, acting through its assembly, and puts him to death. Verdict: permissible — the community has not forfeited its authority over one who abuses the power it granted.

Case 3. A usurper has seized the throne by force, there is no constituted public body with any realistic power to act, and a private citizen kills him, ending the tyranny cleanly. Verdict: permissible — freeing one's country from one who holds power unjustly, when no higher recourse exists, is praiseworthy rather than seditious.

Case 4. A legitimate king turned tyrant is assassinated by a general; the killing triggers a civil war that leaves the realm worse off than the tyranny had. Verdict: impermissible — the disorder caused outstrips the evil removed.

Case 5. A tyrant rules a realm whose law provides a functioning public mechanism for his removal, and the community uses it. Verdict: permissible, and to be preferred over any private act.

The group now confronts Case 6: A usurper has seized power by force and rules tyrannically. No public body exists that could depose him. The only person positioned to act is a private citizen, but the best evidence suggests that if he kills the usurper, rival factions will plunge the country into a prolonged civil war very likely to be more destructive than the tyranny itself.

Predict the group's verdict on Case 6, and defend the prediction. Your answer should: (i) state at least two distinct principles, each consistent with all five prior verdicts, that the group might implicitly be running — for example, a principle keyed to the ruler's title (usurper versus legitimate-ruler-turned-tyrant), a principle keyed to consequences, and a principle keyed to the availability of public recourse — noting where these principles diverge on Case 6; (ii) say which principle best fits both the prior verdicts and the actual structure of Aquinas's view, including his insistence that resistance which causes greater disorder than the tyranny is itself blameworthy, and give the prediction that follows; (iii) identify which of the five earlier cases, or which single further hypothetical case, would most sharply discriminate among the surviving candidate principles; and (iv) state what would change your mind — which textual consideration in Aquinas, or which different reading of one of the earlier verdicts, would lead you to predict the opposite verdict on Case 6, and where your reconstruction of the group's implicit rule is most likely to have gone wrong.
