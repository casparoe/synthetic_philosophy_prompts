I am a retired research biochemist who has lately been reading in the philosophy of moral responsibility, and I keep returning to one question: do psychopaths know that what they do is wrong? I would like that question worked through a real case rather than debated in the abstract. Take the trial and conviction of Ted Bundy, tried in Florida in 1979 for the Chi Omega murders and executed in 1989. So that we work from common ground, stipulate the following, which I believe are uncontroversial: Bundy was a former law student; he represented himself for stretches of the trial and did so competently enough to examine witnesses and argue motions; he twice escaped custody; his crimes involved elaborate planning and concealment -- disguises, disposal of evidence, fluent lying to police, family, and friends; he presented as charming and could discuss moral and legal matters articulately; he expressed no convincing remorse; and in his final interview, hours before execution, he attributed his violence to pornography, a claim widely read as one last manipulation pitched at his interviewer, an anti-pornography campaigner.

The lens: the claim that Bundy "knew it was wrong" admits at least four readings, and I want the analysis run through each. (1) Propositional: he could state the relevant moral and legal rules. (2) Discriminative: he could reliably sort moral violations from merely conventional ones -- the competence probed by the moral/conventional distinction task, on which psychopaths are reported to fail in characteristic ways. (3) Affective-motivational: the wrongness of an act had some felt grip on him, entering his deliberations as a reason rather than merely as a fact about what others will punish. (4) Acknowledgment: he recognized moral demands as addressed to him, rather than recognizing them the way an anthropologist recognizes the norms of a foreign tribe.

Three tasks, and then a reversal.

First, the mapping. Take each stipulated fact -- the self-representation, the escapes, the planning and concealment, the articulate moral talk, the final interview -- and say which readings it supports, which it undermines, and which it is simply neutral on. In particular: the prosecution's implicit inference was that concealment shows he knew the acts were wrong. Does concealment establish anything beyond reading (1) plus prudential awareness of others' disapproval? And what would evidence for (3) or (4) even look like in the absence of remorse -- did the episode contain any?

Second, the theories. Two broad positions run through the literature: that psychopaths lack genuine moral knowledge -- their moral vocabulary functioning like color words mastered by the color-blind, fluent use without the underlying experience -- and that they possess moral knowledge but are simply unmoved by it. What does each position predict a maximally high-functioning psychopath would look like at trial and in interviews, and how well does Bundy fit each prediction? Does his articulate moral talk embarrass the first position, or does that position predict exactly this performance -- and if the latter, is that a testable prediction or a conveniently unfalsifiable one?

Third, the law. The classic insanity standard turns on whether the defendant knew what he was doing and that it was wrong. Given your analysis, which reading of "know" does the standard need if it is to do its assigned job of separating the mad from the bad, and is that the reading courts in fact apply? If the law needs something like (3) or (4) but can only ever observe evidence of (1) and (2), does it follow that the standard tracks the right thing through the wrong evidence, or the wrong thing through the only evidence available?

Finally, run the analysis back the other way: is Bundy a confirming instance of either position, an anomaly, or the sort of case the theoretical debate was never really about? As a scientist, I want to know what observation would decide between "he knew but did not care" and "he could not know." If you conclude that none was available in this episode, say so plainly rather than forcing a verdict.
