I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of biology and I've been roped into TAing an undergrad course, and next week the professor wants me to run one 50-minute section on the propensity interpretation of fitness and the tautology objection. I know this stuff cold as a researcher (or I like to think so) but I've realized I have no idea how to actually sequence it for second-years who mostly took the course to fill a distribution requirement. Don't just re-explain the theory to me -- I want a plan for how I should teach it.

Concretely: what's the order in which the pieces have to go in, and why? My instinct is to start with the tautology objection (survival of the fittest is circular because "fittest" just means "the ones that survive") since it's punchy and they've all heard the slogan, but a friend of mine argued that if you open with the objection, students anchor on it and never actually absorb the propensity definition as anything other than a patch. On the other hand if you do the propensity definition first, is there a risk it reads as just obvious, and the objection lands as a pedantic gotcha? Which order preserves the intellectual problem here, or is there some third option? Also I need one carrying example. Everyone reaches for Darwin's finches or dark/light moths, but I suspect I want something where the "propensity" part is visible -- like cases where two organisms differ in expected offspring but not in realized offspring, or where luck vs. capacity comes apart. What does the example need to have and need to NOT have (e.g., should actual numbers of offspring be in it at all, or does that just invite the confusion)?

Then the misconception map: I'm guessing they'll conflate fitness with survival rather than reproductive success, treat fitness as an intrinsic trait of the organism rather than relative to an environment, and think the propensity reading just renames the problem instead of dissolving it. Where in the sequence do I head each of these off -- before they form, or do I let them form and then break them? What's one end-of-section question I can ask that actually discriminates "they got it" from "they can parrot the definition" -- something like asking whether a sterile organism that's the last of its kind has high or low fitness, or is there a better probe? And what should I openly refuse to touch at this level (I'm thinking the whole long-run vs. short-run propensity debate, or whether fitness is even a causal property) and how do I flag those without making the thing I did teach seem shaky?
