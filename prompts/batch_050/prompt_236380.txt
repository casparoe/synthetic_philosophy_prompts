For a philosophy of psychiatry writing group, I have to circulate a one-page position note before we discuss whether delusions are beliefs, and I want it stress-tested rather than polished. My commitments are: (a) paradigmatic delusions such as Capgras and Cotard really are beliefs, not mere imaginings, avowals, or speech acts, because their subjects sincerely assent, draw inferences from them, and use them to explain other thoughts; (b) the two-factor picture is basically right in structure: a first factor supplies an anomalous felt presence or felt absence in experience, and a second factor explains why the strange hypothesis is adopted and kept rather than dismissed as a hallucination, misperception, or joke; (c) the second factor need not be a global reasoning defect, since many patients are lucid and even ingenious outside the delusional theme; (d) nevertheless delusions are not continuous with ordinary irrational beliefs like superstition or partisan wishful thinking, because their fixity has a lesion- or prediction-error-like basis rather than a merely motivational one; (e) many delusions are behaviorally cool: the patient may lock the door against the impostor yet still hand the supposed impostor a cup of tea, or insist the body is dead while eating breakfast, and this does not by itself show the attitude is not belief; (f) a delusion can be monothematic and encapsulated while still depending on background beliefs that remain intact, so the boundary of the theme is carved by affective salience rather than by logical content; (g) if a subject gives up the claim after a single calm counter-demonstration, that is evidence the attitude was only a hypothesis or obsessive worry, not a delusion in the clinical sense. Please audit this set rather than exposit the debate: identify which commitments genuinely pull against one another, which only appear to because I have run together levels of explanation, and which are harmless as stated. Rank any real tensions by severity, state exactly what would have to be revised in each case and what that revision would cost, and flag any place where my wording secretly assumes the very doxastic/non-doxastic issue the note is supposed to address. I am especially unsure whether (b) and (d) can both be true if the second factor is supposed to do explanatory work, and whether (a), (e), and (g) can share one notion of belief; but do not assume those are the only fault lines. End with the smallest set of edits that would leave the note coherent while preserving its main anti-continuist and anti-global-deficit instincts.
