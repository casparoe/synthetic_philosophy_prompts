My foundation has a rule that I'm increasingly convinced is broken, and a live case is about to force the issue. I'm one of eleven program directors at a mid-sized science foundation, and any single one of us can unilaterally approve grants up to $750k -- no second signature, no board vote. The rule exists for a real reason: the old consensus process was slow, and since any two skeptics could kill a proposal, anything genuinely high-variance died in committee. The single-signature rule was our way of keeping a channel open for bold bets.

Here's the live case. A team has applied for funding to run the first open-air release of a self-spreading engineered vaccine in a wild rodent population, aimed at a nasty livestock disease. The science is genuinely interesting, the ecological modeling is contested, and if something goes wrong it goes wrong in an ecosystem, not a lab. Most of us privately think the expected value is negative. But our one director with real technical background in this exact area -- call her Marta -- is enthusiastic, and she's told me she intends to approve it next month unless the board changes the rule first. Wrinkle: this team has already been turned down by two other funders, revised, and resubmitted. So in a sense the real decision-maker for whether this trial happens is just the most optimistic reviewer anywhere in the funding ecosystem, and our internal rule only decides whether our name is attached.

The board meets in two weeks and I've volunteered to write the memo. Draft it for me. It should recommend something concrete -- keep the single-signature rule, scrap it, or modify it (if modify, give the actual procedure, not a gesture at one) -- and it needs to engage seriously with a few things:

1. The structural problem: when approval just requires the most optimistic of eleven noisy estimates to clear the bar, the project gets funded even if its true value is negative, and adding more directors makes approval *more* likely, not better vetted.
2. The failure mode in the other direction: if everyone becomes curse-aware and reasons "someone better-informed than me would have approved this by now if it were good," you get a deference spiral where nobody ever pulls the trigger and we're back to committee paralysis by another name. Is that a real risk or am I overthinking it?
3. The Marta problem: curse logic treats the optimist's estimate as the noisy tail of the distribution, but she's also the most expert person in the room, so discounting her view means systematically discounting our best information. Though expertise in the science isn't obviously expertise in estimating deployment risks. Sort that out.
4. The strongest case for whatever the memo recommends against -- I don't want a memo that only argues one side, because Marta will read it and she deserves a fair answer.

Keep it short enough that people will actually read it, around 600-800 words, and give it a bottom line the board can vote on.
