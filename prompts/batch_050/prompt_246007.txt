For my grade 12 philosophy elective we got into Persson and Savulescu style moral bioenhancement, basically the idea that if tech gets dangerous, maybe we should not only make people smarter but also use medicine/gene stuff/brain interventions to make people more empathetic, less aggressive, better at fairness, etc. My friend said this sounds obviously good if it stops wars, but I keep thinking there is a difference between helping someone choose the good and quietly installing the good in them like a software update. Our teacher lets us invent one comparison case, so I want to test it against a made-up alternative I am calling a moral exoskeleton: instead of changing brains, society changes the choice architecture around people -- default rules, cooling-off delays, reputation systems, public nudges, transparent appeal processes, and opt-outs -- so selfish or violent options become harder while the person's actual desires and reasons stay mostly untouched. Assume both are medically/technically possible and neither involves literal mind reading.

Compare the two as answers to the same problem: a society that wants fewer catastrophes and less cruelty, but still wants adults to be able to dissent, be weird, make small selfish choices, and blame or praise each other for real. Please get into the messy parts, not just safety good vs freedom good: consent and who counts as competent to consent; whether enhancement bypasses giving reasons in a way the exoskeleton does not; whether nudgy environments can become coercive too, just less visibly; whose morality gets baked in and how minorities/dissenters fare under each; reversibility and exit rights; authenticity, meaning if I donate because my aggression was lowered or because the form made cruelty annoying, do I get less moral credit; elite capture, since governments or platforms would set the targets or defaults; responsibility after failure, like if an enhanced person still harms someone, do we blame them, the doctors, or the state; and whether a hybrid is only okay with opt-in consent, sunset clauses, and democratic control. End with a one-sentence summary and then \answer{BIO}, \answer{EXO}, \answer{HYBRID-CONSENT}, or \answer{UNDECIDED}.
