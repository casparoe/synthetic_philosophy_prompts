I'm a graduate student in philosophy of biology, and the central chapter of my dissertation defends the etiological (selected-effects) theory of function -- the view, associated with Wright and developed in different directions by Millikan and Neander, that a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected -- against causal-role rivals in the tradition of Cummins, on which function is just a matter of what a part actually contributes to a containing system's capacities. At my last committee meeting, one member who is broadly sympathetic to the causal-role picture asked me a question I couldn't answer well: what would you take to be evidence against your own view? I've been turning it over since. The stock objections I know how to bat away -- Swampman-style creatures with no history at all, the worry that selection acts on a trait's effects rather than on anything one could call its function, exaptations whose current use was never selected for -- but I'm worried that my ability to bat them away is itself the problem, and that the theory has accumulated enough amendments (modern-history versions that look only at recent selection, generous ways of counting populations and lineages) that it risks nothing.

So here is what I'd like: an honest map of the etiological theory's exposure, written as if for me to hand back to that committee member. Which observations, cases, or formal results would genuinely count against it, and which merely embarrass it? Distinguish what critics say counts against it from what a committed etiological theorist would herself concede as damaging. Where does the theory stand or fall with positions elsewhere in philosophy -- for instance, how much does it depend on teleosemantic accounts of mental content succeeding, since the same selected-effects machinery is doing the work there? And address the unfalsifiability worry head-on: if every counterexample can be absorbed by adjusting what counts as the relevant selection history, is that a vice for a theory of this kind, or is a constitutive account of a concept permitted to be unmovable in a way a scientific hypothesis is not?

One case in particular has been nagging me, and I'd like your verdict on whether it counts against the view or is handled by it. Imagine a bacterial lineage in which a mutation originally swept to fixation because it stiffened the cell wall; then the environment changed, and for the last several thousand generations the very same allele has been maintained solely because it happens to sequester a toxin that recently entered the habitat -- nothing in the lineage's recent history involves the cell wall at all. On an original-selection reading, the allele's function is wall-stiffening even though it no longer does that and hasn't been selected for doing it in ages; on a modern-history reading, the function silently flipped from one effect to another without any change in the allele itself. Both answers seem to me embarrassing in different ways, and my committee member thinks the case shows that function attributions track what traits currently do for their bearers, full stop. Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say where this case lands on that ordering, and tell me what would actually move the number for a reasonable person in my position. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
