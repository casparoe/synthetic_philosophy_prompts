Two friends of mine have been going in circles for months about "free-floating rationales" -- Dennett's idea that natural selection can track reasons without there being any reasoner around. One of them thinks this is one of the deepest insights in philosophy of biology: the reasons are genuinely *there* in nature (why the flytrap snaps shut, why the cuckoo chick mimics the host's begging calls), discovered by selection rather than by any mind, and any science that ignores them is missing real structure in the world. The other thinks this is just teleology in a trenchcoat: there are only causal-mechanical processes, "reason" talk is a convenient compression for creatures like us who can't do the mechanistic math, and calling these rationales free-floating adds mystery, not explanation.

Draft the joint statement they should have produced ages ago instead of arguing at the bar. Format: (1) a numbered list of claims both can actually sign -- I'm guessing these include stuff like "organisms reliably do what they need to do," "mechanistic explanations are never *wrong*," and "design-stance talk has real predictive success in practice" -- but figure out the real ones, don't just take mine. (2) A table of the remaining disagreements, each phrased so both would accept the wording of their own side, and importantly: separate the disagreements that are about words (e.g. what counts as a "reason") from the ones that survive any definitional truce. (3) The cruxes -- the sub-questions where settling it would force at least one of them to move. I'd expect one crux to be whether reason-explanations are in principle eliminable without epistemic loss, and another to be whether selection actually *responds to* rationales or merely produces things that line up with them, but again, you may find better ones. For each crux, give a concrete case or finding both agree bears on it -- candidates: the Venus flytrap's counting behavior, antibiotic resistance, a fully solved mechanistic account of some behavior, engineers reverse-engineering an organism -- and record in advance what each side predicts or concedes about that case.

Keep it neither vacuous ("both sides agree organisms are complicated") nor slanted. Where a disagreement turns out to be purely verbal, say so plainly in the table -- that alone would save them a dozen arguments.
