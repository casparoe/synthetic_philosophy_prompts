So I've somehow ended up on the governance committee of my housing co-op (long story, involves a leak and a feud about bike storage), and we've been arguing about whether decisions should go to a full vote of all 90-odd members or to a small elected panel, which sent me down a rabbit hole on the Condorcet jury theorem. The basic result is neat enough: if each voter is more likely than not to get it right, and they vote independently, the majority converges on the correct answer as the group gets bigger. Fine. But the more I read, the more it seems like the theorem itself is the least interesting part, and everything interesting lives in what happens when you poke the assumptions. And since I'm thinking of making this the topic of my master's dissertation in political theory (roughly eighteen months, one undergrad stats course to my name, so nothing requiring serious formal firepower), I'd like help working out which of the open problems around it are actually most worth digging into, and in what order.

Here are the lines of work I've noticed people circling, to seed things: (1) what happens when voters' errors are correlated because they all read the same newspapers or share the same information, which seems empirically like the normal case rather than the exception; (2) deliberation — does letting people talk before voting raise individual competence, or does it mostly just correlate their errors, and how would you even tell the difference conceptually rather than empirically; (3) what to do when most people are below 50% competence but a minority reliably isn't, i.e. whether the theorem has anything to say in favour of expertise-weighted or delegated systems rather than pure majorities; (4) the jump from binary choices to three or more options, where Condorcet's own stuff gets messier and cycling shows up; and (5) voters who vote strategically rather than sincerely, where apparently you get weird results like unanimity rules doing worse than majority rules. I've also had a possibly daft idea I can't place: if voters are better than chance at identifying who among them is competent (meta-competence, if you like), does a two-stage setup where the group first votes on who to delegate to, and then delegates, beat a direct majority vote — and does anyone's framework cover that, or is it actually open?

So: which of these (or something I've missed entirely) should come first for someone in my position, and why? I don't want a survey, I want a defended ordering — which questions are upstream of the others in the sense that you can't make progress on the downstream ones without settling them, which are tractable for a political theorist rather than an economist or statistician, which are neglected relative to how much they matter for real institutional design like my co-op's, and which you'd deliberately deprioritise because the answer wouldn't change much in practice. And tell me honestly whether the meta-competence thing is a real gap or just something that follows trivially from existing work once you see it properly — I definately can't tell, and I'd rather know now than after six months on it.
