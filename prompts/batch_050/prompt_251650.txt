As a journalist covering physics and the philosophy of science, I keep encountering the slogan “only gauge-invariant quantities are physically real.” In one context it is used to dismiss worries that the electromagnetic vector potential changes under gauge transformations; in another, to argue that two solutions related by a gauge transformation cannot describe different possible worlds. Please disambiguate the slogan into its strongest precise readings—an epistemic claim about observability, a semantic claim about redundant descriptions, an ontological claim about what exists, and a methodological claim about how theories should be formulated—and evaluate each. Using electromagnetism and, briefly, general relativity, say what each reading implies, whether it is trivial, substantive, or false, and whether it supports the inference from “not gauge-invariant” to “mere surplus structure.” Also consider a hybrid view I am calling relational realism: individual gauge-dependent values are not objective, but gauge-dependent variables and their interrelations may still represent real structure. Is that position coherent, and does it evade or merely rename the problem? Please answer in American English.
