Write an Onion-style news article — headline plus about 450-550 words — reporting that a local man has been circling the same four-mile stretch of highway for eleven hours because every time an off-ramp appears he can't remember whether it's the first exit or the second one.

Quick background so you don't have to look anything up (and please don't lecture it back at me): the road has two consecutive exits that look exactly alike. Taking the first one gets him home in time for dinner; taking the second gets him home in time for the game, wich he cares about a lot more; missing both means a night at the rest stop. The catch is that passing an exit wipes his memory of having passed it, so whenever he's sitting at an off-ramp he genuinely cannot tell wich exit he's at. Before he left the house he sat down and worked out the optimal plan: exit with some fixed probability p at whatever exit you find yourself at. But now that he's actually at a ramp, gripping the wheel, he re-derives the best thing to do on the spot and gets a *different* answer than his own plan told him — because his beliefs about which exit this is depend on the plan he was suposed to be following.

The jokes should come from getting the decision theory right, not from "lol amnesia." I want the planning-vs-acting inconsistency front and center: the note he taped to the steering wheel that he now refuses to trust because the guy who wrote it didn't know what he knows now (or knew exactly the same amount? he can't rule it out), his increasingly shaky reasoning about whether he's been here before, the passenger-seat sandwich he may or may not have already eaten. Include one quote from a bewildered county traffic engineer and one from his wife, who just wants him to pick a lane, any lane.

Commit to a side. The piece should read as written by someone who thinks one horn of the paradox is where the real absurdity lives — say, that re-optimizing at the ramp is obviously the mistake and you should just execute the plan, or the reverse, that only a sucker follows orders from a past self with no new information — and stay deadpan about it rather than both-sides-ing the thing. Then, after the article, add a short author's note (3-4 sentences) flagging where the piece takes liberties with the original absent-minded driver setup or leans on a contested reading of it, so I know what to defend if a game theorist writes in.
