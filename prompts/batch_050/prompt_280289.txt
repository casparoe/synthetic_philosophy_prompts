I'm in a reading group on post-war responsibility (we did Jaspers on German guilt a few weeks back) and we're each supposed to write down our position so the others can poke holes in it. Some background so this makes sense: my grandfather was a minor functionary in a regime that did genuinely horrific things, he died before I was born, and my country's government still pays restitution out of a budget my taxes feed into. So this isn't entirely accademic for me.

Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Guilt requires agency. Nobody can be guilty of something they didn't choose and couldn't have prevented. So the idea that I could be guilty of my grandfather's crime is just a category error.
2. Collective liability is real and legitimate. It was right that my country's state paid reparations after the war, and it's right that people born decades later still help fund that through taxes.
3. Criminal liability is individual only. It would be a profound injustice to charge, convict, or even officially blame anyone born after the events.
4. Nations genuinely do wrong. When I say "my country wronged those people" I mean it literally — it's not shorthand for "some officials wronged them". The nation itself is on the hook.
5. Shame yes, guilt no. Feeling ashamed of my country's past is appropriate, maybe even healthy; feeling guilty about it is confused and a bit self-indulgent.

What I want is a consistency check, not a lecture on collective guilt in general. Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? I've got a suspicion 2 and 3 are in tension but I might be wrong about that. For each real tension, tell me what the cheapest fix is and what it costs me, and rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. If one revision cleans up the most problems, say which one it is.
