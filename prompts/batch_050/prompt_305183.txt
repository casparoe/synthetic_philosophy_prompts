I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I host a small podcast where I interview people at the intersection of design and ideas. My next guest is a type designer and self-described "letterform essentialist": she argues that every lowercase 'a' — from a Garamond italic to a child's scrawl to a blackletter double-story glyph — shares a discoverable essential structure that is not reducible to a checklist of visual features (bowl, ascender, counter, and so on), since any feature list we write can be violated by some glyph that still reads unmistakably as an 'a'. She takes this to show that letterforms have a kind of abstract style or spirit that human readers grasp directly and that, in her view, no current machine-learning classifier truly grasps, because such classifiers only learn statistical correlations over pixels or vector points.

I'd like a sequence of interview questions that genuinely tests this position rather than just letting her state it. Specifically:

1. Build the questions around the actual substance of her view, drawing on the tensions a working software engineer would notice: for instance, I can write code that recognizes 'a's across hundreds of fonts, yet I cannot write down the rule I'm implicitly using — does that support her ineffable-essence claim, or does it suggest the "essence" is just a high-dimensional pattern with no clean symbolic description?

2. Press on the hard boundary cases and anticipate her likely replies, including follow-ups to those replies. Examples of pressure points: invented scripts and stylized glyphs where letter identity degrades gracefully rather than at a sharp boundary; the fact that a glyph counts as an 'a' partly because a community of readers treats it as one (does that make a-ness historical and conventional rather than essential?); and the possibility that her "spirit of the letter" is really the recognizer's contribution, not the letter's — in which case a trained neural network's behavior might be evidence about essence rather than a failure to capture it.

3. Include at least one question probing what would change her mind — what observation, artifact, or experimental result would count against letterform essentialism — since I want to know whether the view is falsifiable in practice.

4. Avoid generic icebreakers; every question should turn on the content of her position.

At the end, add a short meta-note for me as the interviewer: indicate how confident you are that each question targets a genuine weakness in the essentialist position (rather than a point she can absorb easily), and single out the question or assumption in your set that you are least sure of, explaining why.
