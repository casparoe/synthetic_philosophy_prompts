I sit on the grants committee of a private research foundation, and at our December meeting we must vote on whether to renew our flagship award: a five-year grant, roughly $4 million a year, to the Adaptive Heuristics Programme, a behavioral-science research programme we have funded for twelve years. I have been asked to circulate a decision memo before the meeting, and I'd like you to draft it.

The programme's hard core is the conjecture that every systematic departure from rational-choice models is produced by one of seven context-triggered heuristics governed by a shared triggering mechanism. Its record, as far as the committee has been able to establish it:

- Its founding prediction -- that loss aversion reverses under severe time pressure -- failed in three independent laboratories. The programme responded with an auxiliary hypothesis: the reversal requires stakes above a motivational threshold. That yielded a new prediction (reversals at high stakes), confirmed once and then failing replication. A second auxiliary -- reversals occur only for losses framed as expenses rather than as foregone gains -- predicted a previously unobserved framing effect in charitable giving, which two labs have since confirmed. This is the programme's one widely conceded success in a decade.
- The taxonomy of heuristics has grown from seven to forty-one, mostly by splitting existing categories to accommodate anomalies; no split has yet produced a confirmed novel prediction of its own.
- The programme's last eight publications each re-analyze results already published by rival laboratories. It has not anticipated a new anomaly before a rival group found one in six years.
- Its methods sections now routinely carry a standing clause to the effect that heuristic triggering is context-sensitive, so any null result can in principle be attributed to an uncontrolled contextual variable.

The renewal fight splits the committee. The renew side argues that, on Lakatos's own criterion, a programme still capable of occasionally producing confirmed novel predictions is theoretically and empirically progressive, and that Lakatos himself insisted research programmes can be judged only with hindsight: Copernican astronomy looked degenerating for decades, and a funder that cuts support the moment the protective belt thickens will reliably kill programmes one shift before their revival. The sunset side argues that one confirmed novel prediction in ten years, forty-one heuristics, and a standing clause that screens the hard core from refutation together make a textbook degenerating problem shift, and that our charter commits us to high-risk funding, which a programme this insulated from failure no longer is.

Draft the memo. It should state a definite recommendation -- renew as is, renew with restructuring, or sunset -- in its first sentence, with no preamble and no restating of the case, then give the reasoning: an application of Lakatos's progressive/degenerating distinction to this record that a committee member could actually use (not merely that novel predictions are good and ad hoc moves are bad, but what should count as a novel prediction and as a genuine problem shift in this specific history), an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the side you reject, and a short closing section specifying what the programme should be required to report at the next review so that this same dispute is decidable rather than re-fought.
