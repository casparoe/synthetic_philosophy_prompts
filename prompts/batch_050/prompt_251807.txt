You are a philosopher and historiographer who specializes in the ethics of commemoration and the problem of presentism. Think carefully before answering; begin by brainstorming the distinct philosophical issues the practice below raises, then organize your analysis.

Consider a practice that has become standard at many universities, museums, and municipal governments. When a building, prize, or street named after a historical figure becomes controversial because of things that figure did or said, the institution convenes a review committee whose charter instructs it to apply the following rule: the name may be retained if the conduct now objected to was not widely regarded as wrong by the standards of the figure's own time and place, and should be removed if it was. In application, committees treat this as a factual inquiry: they commission reports establishing what educated opinion in the figure's era generally accepted, they discount the views of the era's dissidents on the grounds that a person cannot be faulted for failing to anticipate later moral developments, and they sometimes note, without treating it as decisive, that the figure's contemporaries who stood on the receiving end of the conduct objected to it at the time. Committees also insist that the rule is neutral between moral viewpoints, since it asks only what the figure's contemporaries believed, not what we believe.

What view of moral judgment across time does this rule presuppose? Work through at least the following: (1) whether the rule presupposes that moral standards are indexed to times and places -- that "wrong" means something like "wrong by the lights then available" -- or whether it could instead rest on a non-relativist view about blame and what could reasonably have been known; distinguish these reconstructions and say which one the committees' actual procedures better fit. (2) The rule requires identifying "the standards of the time," but any era contains conflicting standards -- enslaved people and abolitionists held views that differed from those of the slaveholders' peers. What does a committee's choice of whose standards count presuppose about whose moral testimony carries authority, and is that choice itself a moral judgment made from the present, in tension with the rule's claim to neutrality? (3) Whether the rule can consistently be applied to itself: the willingness to excuse past wrongdoing on grounds of its era's tolerance is arguably a standard of our time, not of theirs, so does the rule judge the past by the present even as it forbids doing so? (4) Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: could a thoroughgoing presentist who denies that we can ever step outside our own standards endorse the rule, and could a thoroughgoing moral realist who thinks moral truth is timeless also endorse it? (5) Finally, say concretely how the committee's charter, evidence-gathering, and decisions would have to change if it instead adopted each of two alternatives: first, the view that presentist judgment is unavoidable and the committee should simply apply current values openly; second, the view that historical figures are responsible only for what they could have known, with the era's dissidents counting as proof that the knowledge was available.
