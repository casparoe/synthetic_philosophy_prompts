I work as a finds officer for a regional heritage agency. Several times a month I have to write the significance assessment for a newly excavated assemblage, and these assessments feed directly into catalog entries, museum labels, and occasionally press releases. My recurring problem is not identifying what an object is made of or when it dates to; it is deciding how far up the interpretive ladder a report is entitled to climb. There is a familiar progression: from the object's form to its function (this was a cutting tool); from function to the practice it participated in (these people butchered animals communally); from practice to the beliefs or institutions behind it (this was a ritual feast overseen by a priesthood). Each rung seems to demand a different kind of warrant, and in my field the top rungs are where careers are made and where the discipline's credibility is lost.

Please turn whatever the philosophy of inference and the methodology of archaeology actually establish about this into a working procedure I can run on any assemblage in about an hour. Specifically, I want:

1. An ordered sequence of questions to ask before assigning an interpretation at each rung, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each question (e.g., what kinds of evidence -- use-wear, context, analogy, replication, distribution across sites -- legitimately support each rung, and which kinds only appear to).
2. Explicit rules for the known failure modes. In my experience these include: the "ritual" wastebasket, where anything whose function is not obvious gets labeled ceremonial; the single-site fallacy, where a pattern is read as intentional from one deposit; and the analogy trap, where a superficial resemblance to an ethnographically documented practice is treated as identification.
3. Clear stopping conditions: where the procedure must halt and report "function unknown" or "no institutional inference licensed," rather than letting me reach for a story.
4. An honest marking of the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment -- where two competent archaeologists applying it faithfully could still disagree, and what should be recorded at those points so a later reviewer can see that a judgment call was made rather than a conclusion drawn.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First: a pit near a spring containing a dozen deliberately bent and broken bronze blades, no other goods. Second: a large building with no hearths, no storage facilities, and a single entrance facing the sunrise on the winter solstice. Third: a grave containing a child's skeleton, a miniature ceramic vessel too small for practical use, and twenty butchered piglet jawbones. Include at least one case where the procedure forces a deflationary conclusion I will not enjoy writing up -- where the exciting interpretation (a shrine, a cult deposit, a ceremonial burial) must be set aside in favor of something mundane, or in favor of reporting that the evidence underdetermines the question. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which disputes in my field it will not settle, and what I should say on the record when a journalist wants the top rung and the procedure only licenses the second.
