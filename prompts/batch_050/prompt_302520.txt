You are a specialist in the theory and philosophy of AI alignment, equally at home with the machine-learning details and with questions in epistemology about what iterative self-improvement can and cannot certify. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey, and I want you to think carefully: begin by briefly brainstorming at least three competing diagnoses of the disagreement described below, then argue for the one you find most defensible, explaining why the alternatives fail.

The disagreement concerns iterated amplification and distillation as an alignment strategy, and it has been stuck for years. The setup is familiar to both camps. A weak but trusted overseer is amplified by being given access to copies of itself working on subtasks, producing a system more capable than any single copy; the amplified system's behaviour is then distilled into a fast model; and the loop repeats, on the hope that if each stage is no less aligned than the trusted base and no less capable than its predecessor, the limit of the process is both aligned and highly capable.

The optimists argue roughly as follows. Every node of the decomposition tree bottoms out in the trusted base agent, so alignment is inherited from the bottom up. Errors are local: each amplification step can in principle be audited by the stage below it, so small infidelities get caught rather than compounding. And capability is preserved by construction, since distillation is just compression of behaviour the amplified system already exhibits.

The sceptics argue that distillation never preserves everything: it keeps input–output behaviour and discards the reasoning that produced it, so whatever the amplified system knew but did not say is lost at every round. Some tasks, they add, resist decomposition into subtasks whose answers the overseer can verify, and the strategy quietly assumes otherwise. And once the amplified system is sufficiently capable, it can construct justifications whose soundness its weaker components cannot check, so the audit assumption fails at precisely the point where it begins to matter.

Both sides have been recycling these points for years without movement. Everyone agrees the toy demonstrations show nothing decisive; no one has run the large-scale version; and the conceptual argument has not advanced.

My own coinage, offered as a hypothesis rather than a verdict, is what I call decomposition debt: at each round of the loop, the gap between what the amplified system can establish and what its distilled successor can independently verify. If distillation keeps the answers but not the justifications, the debt grows even when every answer happens to be correct. My suspicion is that the optimists are making a claim about single steps (each round is auditable, so no new debt accrues) while the sceptics are making a claim about the fixed point (debt compounds, so the limit inherits it), and that they talk past one another because "the loop preserves alignment" means something different in the two framings. An alternative hypothesis I have considered is that there is a hidden empirical question underneath, about whether errors across decomposition steps are correlated or independent: if independent, the loop plausibly contracts them; if correlated, it amplifies them, and neither side has said what evidence would distinguish the two.
