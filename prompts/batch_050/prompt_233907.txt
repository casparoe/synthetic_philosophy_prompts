i keep running into the same move in AI welfare discussions, most recently in a discord where someone used it to argue we should already be worried about how we train models. it goes: an RL agent is organized around a reward signal the same way an animal is organized around pleasure and pain -- dopamine is the brain's reward signal, RL reward is the same thing written in code, so if negative reward is flowing then something is in a state at least analogous to suffering, and we're being speciecist-ish to shrug it off. audit this analogy properly for me. i want the full correspondence table: what plays the role of dopamine, of the reward prediction error, of pleasure, of pain, of evolution, of the environment -- and whether anything in the RL setup even *has* a candidate to map onto pleasure, or whether the table has an empty cell there that people paper over. then the disanalogies. two i want pressed on specifically: first, the liking/wanting split in the neuroscience literature (dopamine seems to track wanting and salience more than hedonic enjoyment, the Berridge-and-Robinson line) -- does that finding strengthen the analogy (since RL has a clean wanting analog too) or destroy it (since it suggests reward signals were never the suffering-relevant thing in brains either)? second, the direction-of-fit point: animals come to want things because those things produced reward, whereas the RL agent's wants are just whatever policy the reward happened to sculpt -- does that inversion matter for whether the reward could be felt as good or bad from the inside? finish with a verdict: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that todays RL agents plausibly have valenced states survive, does the opposite conclusion survive, or was the analogy carrying everything -- and if neither survives, tell me what consideration or evidence would actually settle the question instead of leaning on the brain comparison.
