I run a small philosophy reading circle — six of us, none with any academic training — and we've just finished a month on medieval ethics, closing with Abelard's claim that sin lies in the consent of the will rather than in the deed itself. We worked from a translation of his Ethics (the one also known as Scito te ipsum) plus a couple of introductory summaries. To finish the unit, I promised to set a take-home exam question, answerable in about two hours without books, that would test whether people actually understand the view and not merely the slogan that it's the intention that counts.

Please draft (a) the exam question, (b) a model answer of roughly 600 to 700 words, and (c) a marking scheme.

Some requirements, since the first draft I attempted myself turned out to be answerable by reciting our reading notes:

- The question must force the answerer to work through at least two of the following cruxes, and I'd like you to build it around whichever two you think combine best: (1) Abelard's reason for holding that the outward deed adds nothing to the sin — that the same act can be performed with or without consent, so that a completed killing and a thwarted attempt can be morally on a par; (2) his distinction between consenting to something and merely desiring or being unable to avoid something connected with it — our group kept tripping over cases like a killing in self-defense, where the agent plainly wants to live but arguably does not consent to the death, and we want the question to make that distinction do real work; (3) the obvious objection that the view renders outward conduct morally indifferent, and what resources Abelard has to explain why deeds may still be punished and why it still matters what we actually do to one another.
- The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit points, with marks assigned to each (20 total), and a one-line note per point describing how a member who has only half-understood the reading typically fumbles it.
- End the marking scheme with a short paragraph naming the specific move that separates an excellent answer from a merely competent one — the thing a good summary-reciter will not think to do.
- Where the model answer uses Abelard's illustrative cases, please present them in your own words rather than quoting any translation.

One more thing. Everything above reflects my own lay reading of the material, and I may well have distorted Abelard somewhere — for instance, in how I've set up the consent-versus-desire contrast or in assuming the punishment point is even his. If any of the cruxes rest on a misreading, or if the exam as specified can't fairly be done in two hours by people without training, say so plainly and explain what should change, rather than politely drafting around the problem.
