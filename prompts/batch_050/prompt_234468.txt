So I'm in a philosophy club at my school and last week I tried to defend this argument I came up with after our bio unit, and everyone agreed the conclusion is ridiculous but nobody could tell me which step dies, and now it's bugging me. Background: our teacher is big on the idea that explaining something in biology means giving the mechanism — like, you point at the parts and what they're doing and how they're organized, and that's what produces the thing. Ok here's the argument:

(1) To explain a biological phenomenon is to show the mechanism that produces it — the parts, what they do, and how they're arranged so the whole thing happens.
(2) But the parts of any mechanism are themselves made of smaller parts, and at the bottom it's molecules doing chemistry. Nothing in a cell happens except molecules bumping and reacting.
(3) So every complete mechanistic explanation bottoms out in molecular interactions.
(4) If the real explanatory work is done at the molecular level, then talk about cells, organs, behaviors, ecosystems is just a convenient summary of the molecular story — it doesn't explain anything extra.
(5) So things like ecology or animal behavior or even physiology don't genuinely explain stuff. Only molecular biology really explains, and everything else is shorthand while we wait.
(6) But that's clearly wrong — my ecology teacher explained why the fish in the lake died using stuff about algae and oxygen and seasons, and it was a perfectly good explanation, no molecules needed.

I feel like (2) and (3) are doing something sneaky but I can't pin it down. Like, obviously everything is made of molecules, but somehow that feels different from saying the molecules do the explaining?? If I have to give something up I'd rather keep (1) and (6) than the middle stuff, since (1) is just what our teacher says explanation is and (6) seems obviously true.

So: which step do you actually reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost?
