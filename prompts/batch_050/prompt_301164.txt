I'm a PhD student in statistics with a side interest in the philosophy of probability, and last week my reading group was working through a chapter in a popular book on uncertainty (I won't name it, since my question is about the content rather than the book itself). The chapter gives the following summary of Bertrand's paradox and its significance: "In 1889, Joseph Bertrand posed a simple problem: pick a chord of a circle at random; what is the probability that it is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? He showed that there are three equally natural ways to pick a chord, giving three different answers — one-half, one-third, and one-quarter — and since all three methods pick chords at random, all three answers are equally correct. This proves that the classical principle of indifference, which says to distribute probability uniformly over symmetric cases, is logically inconsistent, and hence that the whole Laplacean approach to probability collapses: probability claims are only meaningful relative to a freely chosen method of generating outcomes, so there is no objective answer to such questions until someone stipulates a physical mechanism. The chord problem is essentially the same difficulty that arises in modern measure theory when conditioning on events of probability zero, which is why Bertrand's paradox is now understood as an early anticipation of the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox."

My own sense is that the first half of this is roughly right but that the later sentences overreach — in particular, I'm inclined to think the paradox is usually taken to show that "at random" is underdetermined rather than that the principle of indifference is strictly inconsistent, and I suspect the last sentence conflates two problems that are related but distinct. But I'm not confident enough in either suspicion to rely on them. Could you audit the summary sentence by sentence: which claims would a careful philosopher or mathematician accept as stated, which are defensible simplifications, and which misrepresent the situation — by conflating the chord problem with the distinct measure-theoretic issue, overstating what the paradox establishes about the principle of indifference, or treating one response to the paradox (stipulating a mechanism) as the forced conclusion when alternatives exist? And does the summary's closing picture — that probability questions of this kind have no objective answer absent a stipulated mechanism — actually follow from the parts of the summary that survive correction?

Finally, please say explicitly what would change your verdict on the disputed points: for instance, what consideration or argument would lead you to conclude that the stronger reading — the principle of indifference really is inconsistent, not merely underdetermined — is correct after all, or where in your reconstruction of the standard view you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
