Three years into retirement from a career in condensed matter physics, I now convene a philosophy of probability seminar in my university's continuing-studies programme, and I am setting the end-of-term assessment. I want one essay question on Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference that students cannot answer by reciting that the principle assigns equal probability to possibilities one has no reason to distinguish, and then noting that the chord problem embarrasses it. The crux I want them to reach is this: the three classical calculations of the probability that a randomly chosen chord exceeds the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle — random endpoints, random radius, random midpoint — are not rival answers of which two must be mistakes; each applies indifference legitimately to a different parametrization, so the paradox concerns the non-invariance of uniformity under redescription, not anyone's arithmetic. A top answer should then engage Jaynes's proposal that the correct prior is the measure invariant under the problem's symmetry group, which for the chord problem singles out the random-midpoint answer of one half, and should test that proposal against the wine-and-water mixture problem, where the symmetry considerations do not fix a unique measure. Crucially, the question should require students to take a stand: does the invariance approach genuinely rescue the principle of indifference as a logic of ignorance, or does it covertly substitute physical symmetry knowledge for ignorance, so that what survives is not indifference at all? I want the model answer to commit to a verdict on this, with reasons, rather than surveying the landscape and leaving it open.

The assessment is for upper-level undergraduates and master's students, answerable in 75 minutes, worth 25 marks. Alongside the question itself, please provide a model answer of roughly 600 words that argues to a definite conclusion, and a marking scheme distributing the 25 marks across five or six must-hit moves — for instance: stating precisely why none of the three chord calculations is an error; articulating the representation-dependence of uniformity, perhaps via van Fraassen's cube factory; explaining Jaynes's invariance solution and the result it yields; pressing the wine-and-water objection; and delivering the reasoned verdict. The scheme should also name the specific mistakes that should cost marks — treating two of the three calculations as blunders, presenting Jaynes as having simply solved the paradox, or conflating the claim that the experiment is underdescribed with the claim that there are no rational constraints on priors — and it should state explicitly which moves separate an A answer from a B answer.
