The exam question was the following. A three-member hiring committee must decide whether to offer a candidate a position. By the committee's own rules, an offer is to be made if and only if the candidate is judged strong on research (R) and strong on teaching (T). Member A judges R true and T true; Member B judges R true and T false; Member C judges R false and T true. (a) Determine the committee's decision under a premise-based procedure (majority vote on R and on T, with the decision derived from those outcomes) and under a conclusion-based procedure (each member votes directly on whether to make the offer, majority wins). (b) Explain why this divergence constitutes a genuine dilemma for the committee rather than a mere curiosity, and (c) briefly assess which procedure the committee should adopt, or whether some third option resolves the problem.

Here is the student's answer, verbatim: "This is the discursive dilemma. Under the premise-based procedure, R passes two to one and T passes two to one, so the committee must make the offer. Under the conclusion-based procedure, only Member A votes for an offer, so the offer fails one to two. Essentially the same thing happens in the Condorcet paradox: majority rule applied across several questions produces an incoherent group verdict — just as Condorcet showed that pairwise majorities can cycle over three alternatives, here the majority 'cycles' between endorsing each premise and rejecting the conclusion they jointly entail. The dilemma is real rather than curious because both procedures are defensible: the premise-based route gives the committee transparent reasons it can defend to the candidate, while the conclusion-based route is efficient and lets members weigh the criteria holistically, and yet they deliver opposite verdicts on identical votes. The cleanest fix is to vote separately on R, on T, and on the offer, and to adopt the majority verdict on each: since every individual vote is decided by majority, the resulting set of committee judgments is collectively rational, and the tension disappears."
