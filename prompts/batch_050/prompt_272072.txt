I spent forty years running experiments in a university chemistry department, and in retirement I have been working my way through the classical Chinese political thinkers, Han Feizi most recently. What drew me to him was a certain temperamental affinity: he proposes to govern by public, uniform standards of reward and punishment — fa — applied impersonally, rather than by the cultivated judgment of virtuous ministers, and this appealed to me as the political analogue of trusting a calibrated instrument rather than the postdoc's practiced eye. But in my notebook I drafted an argument, extrapolating from what I take to be his own commitments, that appears to prove his central ideal impossible. I cannot find the misstep, yet the conclusion cannot be right — not because it offends morality, but because it would dissolve the very distinction his system is built on. Here it is:

(1) A state is well governed to the extent that conduct is directed by clear, promulgated, uniformly enforced standards, rather than by the private judgment (si) of any person, minister or ruler alike.

(2) Whenever an official decides a matter by his own assessment of the particulars rather than by a fixed standard that settles it in advance, he is ruling by private judgment in the sense (1) excludes.

(3) Every application of a standard to a case requires a judgment that this case falls under this standard — that this defendant's act counts as "theft," that this official's report counts as "fulfilling his charge."

(4) That subsuming judgment is not itself dictated by the standard; a rule for applying rules would merely push the problem up one level.

(5) Therefore every application of a standard is, by (2), an exercise of private judgment.

(6) Therefore there is no difference, in the end, between rule by fa and rule by men: every legal order is discretionary all the way down, and the Legalist ideal of an impartial, self-operating mechanism of government is a chimera.

I note that (3) and (4) are doing the work, and they have the ring of something my grandchildren would call "that Wittgenstein thing about rules," which I have not read and do not wish to lean on. What I would like from you: identify the step where the argument goes wrong, if it does; explain why that step looked sound to a man trained to prize impersonal measurement; and then price the alternatives. Specifically, if I refuse to give up (3)–(4), what must I say (2) actually excludes, and is that reading still faithful to Han Feizi's hostility to ministerial discretion — or does it quietly reintroduce the virtuous judge he thought unnecessary and dangerous? Conversely, if I keep (2) as stated, can the Legalist still distinguish the good official applying the statute from the bad one bending it, using only resources internal to the system — verifiable records, the matching of performance to the stated charge, the calibration analogy itself — without smuggling in the very discretion he abolished? I would rather surrender the instrument analogy than the distinction between applying a standard and legislating one, but I want to know what that preference costs.
