ok so this is a thing I've now watched happen twice at work and once in my personal life and it's driving me nuts because I can't figure out if it's one phenomenon or three different ones that just look alike.

Instance one: those penny auction sites (a friend got into them a while back, like quibids before it died). You bid a cent at a time on like a TV, but every bid costs you 60 cents whether you win or not. So the TV "sells" for $41.20 and everyone goes wow what a deal, except the winner paid 60 cents x however many bids on top, and everyone else paid for their bids and got nothing. My friend knew exactly how it worked and still kept bidding because quitting meant eating the $80 he'd already spent. The site made way more than the TV was worth.

Instance two: two teams at my company basically got into an internal war over which of their tools becomes the official internal platform. At every review meeting both teams could point to how much work they'd sunk in, and leadership kept saying ok one more quarter to decide. Both teams kept staffing up. Everyone involved agreed privately that total spending had long passed any plausible value of winning, but for each team, dropping out now meant all that budget and two years of their careers counted for zero, while staying in still had *some* chance of paying off.

Instance three: me and a coworker, honestly embarrassingly, got into a bidding war on ebay over a vintage synth and I paid like 30% over what I told myself was my hard max. Every individual bid felt fine. +$5 to stay in the lead. Sure.

So here's my question. The common explanation I'd reach for is the sunk cost thing -- people irrationally weighting money that's already gone. But in instances one and two I'm not sure that fits, because if you actually run the numbers at each decision point, continuing genuinely can be the locally better move given where you are. The irrationality (if there is any) isn't at any single step, it's in... the shape of the whole game? Like the trap is structural, not psychological, and yet the psychological version and the structural version seem to feed each other.

Is there a name for this specific setup -- where the loser also pays, so "stopping" and "losing" are different options with different costs, and the collectively insane outcome is built from locally sane moves? And separately: is the thing in instance three (where stopping is basically free, you just feel the pull anyway) actually the same phenomenon or a different one that people lump together with it? I'd also love to know whether being able to see the structure in advance actually protects you, or whether the whole problem is that the structure only becomes visible once you're already invested. If there's no clean established term that covers all this, feel free to just define the thing properly yourself and give it a name -- I mostly want the carving, not a citation.
