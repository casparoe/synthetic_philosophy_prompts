For our school debate society's end-of-year exhibition, my teammate and I have been preparing the case that "general intelligence is a myth," and our centerpiece has been the famous comparison, drawn from evolutionary psychology, of the human mind to a Swiss army knife: not one all-purpose blade, but a bundle of special tools, each shaped by natural selection to solve a specific recurrent problem faced by our ancestors. In preparation I read parts of Leda Cosmides and John Tooby's Evolutionary Psychology: A Primer, where the idea is put roughly as follows (I am paraphrasing, not quoting): because there is no such thing as a general problem, only particular adaptive problems like finding mates, detecting cheats, avoiding predators, and choosing food, the mind could not consist of a single general-purpose problem-solver and must instead be a collection of distinct, specialized mechanisms, each with its own proprietary procedures and representations. On stage, we used this to argue that IQ tests measure a fiction, that someone's being "smart" is really just their being well stocked with particular tools, and that worrying about machines becoming "generally" smarter than us is confused, because there is no such axis to move along. The other team objected that the analogy was doing all our work for us, and after thinking about it, I am no longer sure they are wrong: a Swiss army knife does have a handle that every tool shares, the tools do get chosen and deployed for purposes none of them was built for, and it seems possible in principle to rank knives as better or worse overall. So I would like a careful audit of the analogy before we commit to it.

Please respond as a philosopher of mind with real expertise in cognitive science and evolutionary psychology, and think carefully rather than giving me a pep talk. Begin with a short brainstorm of candidate correspondences, then give me an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the handle, the individual blades, the knife maker, and the situations the knife is carried into. Then separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten our three debate conclusions (no meaningful general intelligence, IQ as fiction, machine intelligence fears as confused) from the disanalogies that are real but harmless to those conclusions. Pay particular attention to what the analogy quietly imports that the mind may lack: a manufacturer with intentions, a fixed inventory of tools, tools that cannot modify themselves or be used off-label, and the fact that one knife cannot design another knife. Also tell me whether the primer's reasoning about specialized mechanisms actually requires the knife picture, or whether the modularity claim could stand even if the analogy collapses. End with a verdict on which of our three conclusions, if any, still stand once the disanalogies are on the table, and suggest a better analogy if one exists.

Strict limit: 800 words maximum, not counting the paraphrased passage above; open with a one-sentence bottom-line verdict before anything else.
