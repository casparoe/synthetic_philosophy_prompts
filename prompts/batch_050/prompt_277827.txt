I am a PhD candidate in political theory helping to teach a 75-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar on distributive ethics. There will be 18 students, and no prior knowledge of the utility-monster objection can be assumed. Most can state the objection once it is described, but they tend to regard it as a fanciful case about an unusually greedy person. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel it as a structural problem generated by welfare aggregation, interpersonal comparison, and limited access to other people’s experiences.

The activity should proceed through several short rounds in which students allocate indivisible resources among participants whose private welfare schedules differ. At least one participant should naturally become the monster because of the structure of their schedule, not because they are instructed to behave greedily. Please isolate three distinct ways in which a utility monster can arise: first, an exceptional capacity to derive total welfare from resources; second, unusually strong marginal sensitivity to additional resources; and third, an ability to make welfare claims that cannot be checked against anyone else’s experience. The activity should show that these are not equivalent and that different allocation rules respond differently to them.

Include a phase in which the group chooses an allocation procedure before knowing their own welfare schedules, followed by rounds in which they must operate the chosen procedure under uncertainty. There should also be a contrast between rounds with sincere welfare reports and rounds in which reports can be strategically inflated, so that students must distinguish the metaphysical problem of comparing welfare from the epistemic problem of verifying reports. The resources and rewards need not have monetary value, but the choices should have enough consequence to make participants care about the outcome.

Do not introduce the term utility monster or standard names for distributive principles until the debrief. Participants should discover the pressure toward increasingly unequal allocations through the rules themselves, then be asked whether the result is optimal, unfair, manipulable, or some combination of the three. Provide exact materials, setup, payoff rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, instructions the facilitator can read aloud, and a description of what is likely to happen in each phase.

The debrief should map concrete moments in the activity onto the relevant distinctions: total versus marginal welfare, ordinal versus cardinal information, sincere experience versus strategic reporting, and maximizing aggregate welfare versus protecting a minimum share. It should also ask students where each distributive rule encountered in the activity breaks down and what additional assumption would be needed to defend it. Finally, anticipate what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run—including performative exaggeration by the designated monster, confusion between points and welfare, and premature recognition of the philosophical target—and explain how to handle each problem. Be explicit about which feature of the philosophical case the activity cannot reproduce, particularly the reality and comparability of phenomenal intensity, and about what the facilitator must therefore supply in discussion.
