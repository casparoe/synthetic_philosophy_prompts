I'm applying for a small summer research grant at my university (10 weeks, me plus a faculty mentor, ending in a paper of about 8-9000 words) and I need a two-page proposal. The general topic is Wang Yangming's doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action (zhi xing he yi).

Here's my half-formed idea to build on. Most readings I've run into treat the doctrine as either (a) a motivational thesis -- genuinely knowing what's right already involves being moved to do it, so anyone who "knows but doesn't act" doesn't really know -- or (b) a normative ideal we should strive toward, with the unity as something achieved through cultivation rather than something already true of knowledge as such. I suspect both readings import a picture where knowledge is a self-standing inner item and action is something that comes after it, and that Yangming's point is better understood as a claim about the conditions for attributing knowledge at all: roughly, that in the moral case we don't count someone as knowing filial piety or knowing that a smell is bad unless their evaluative responses and conduct line up, because the "knowledge" just is the reliably-patterned response and not a separable mental state that causes it. So the doctrine would be closer to a point about what knowledge-talk is for than a point about what knowledge does. What I don't know is whether this reading can handle the hard cases -- akrasia, self-deception, the person who seems to sincerely avow a moral belief while their behaviour flatly contradicts it -- without collapsing back into reading (a) with extra steps.
