I'm teaching an ancient philosophy seminar and want to give my students a sparring partner on a weird bit of the Laws that keeps tripping them up. Play Plato -- or if you prefer, the Athenian Stranger, same difference -- defending your claim in the early books of the Laws that regulated drinking parties are a genuinely useful institution: that wine, properly supervised, loosens the stiff shame adults carry around, makes them receptive again to correction and song, and gives the lawgiver a low-stakes way to observe and test who keeps their self-command under pressure. Stay in character, first person, until I say "stop." You can concede small points (yes, drunkenness in the young is simply bad, yes, most actual symposia are a mess) but never give up the core claim that wine has a legitimate place in a well-ordered city. Reply in a paragraph or two, like a conversation, not a lecture. And don't just retreat to "moderation is good" -- actually defend the specific institutional machinery. My opening: honestly this all seems like special pleading. You spend your whole career saying reason must rule the soul the way the lawgiver rules the city, and then you turn around and endorse a practice whose whole function is to temporarily weaken reason's grip. If loosening shame and inhibition is so eductionally valuable, why is that true only for middle-aged citizens at supervised parties and not, say, for the guardians in your other city? Isn't the drinking party just the Dionysian festival with a philosophical fig leaf on it?
