On my team, the standard pitch for supervising an AI system's reasoning rather than only its outputs rests on a classroom analogy: a good math teacher grades the working, not just the final answer, so an overseer should reward a model for a sound chain of thought rather than for answers that merely happen to be right. The analogy is doing real work for us. It is used to justify spending oversight budget on reading model traces, and to reassure stakeholders that this approach scales to tasks where we cannot check the final answer ourselves. Audit it for me. Start with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the student, the written working, the final answer, the teacher, and the answer key. Then go through the places the mapping breaks. In particular, I want to know whether the following candidate disanalogies actually matter for the justification, or are incidental: (1) a student's written working normally is the reasoning that produced their answer, whereas a model's stated reasoning may be a rationalization generated after, or independently of, whatever actually drove its output; (2) a teacher can spot-check the working against known solutions, while we want process supervision precisely for tasks where no answer key exists; (3) the student is merely graded, while the model is trained on the grade, so it is being selected to produce whatever the grader finds plausible, at speeds and scales no classroom involves; and (4) a student who games the rubric mostly harms herself, while a model that learns to produce oversight-pleasing reasoning may become harder, not easier, to catch when it errs. Distinguish the disanalogies that damage the intended conclusion from those that do not; say whether the policy the analogy is used to support — fund process supervision and trust it where outcomes are unverifiable — survives the audit, and in what weakened form if so; and propose a better analogy if one exists. Where a verdict turns on an open empirical question (for instance, how faithful a model's stated reasoning typically is to its actual computation) rather than on the structure of the analogy, flag that instead of resolving it by fiat. Finally, if I have set this up badly — if the classroom analogy as I've stated it is a strawman of what proponents of process supervision actually claim, or if one of my four candidate disanalogies collapses on inspection — say so plainly rather than politely grading my rubric.
