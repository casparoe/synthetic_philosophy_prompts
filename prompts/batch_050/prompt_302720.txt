I'm a high school senior drafting a position paper on forgiveness for my ethics class, and before I commit to it I want to check whether the views I've assembled actually fit together. Here is where I've landed: (1) Forgiveness is a change of heart -- giving up resentment and ill will toward the wrongdoer -- not a change of conduct, so you can fully forgive someone while still cutting them out of your life. (2) Forgiveness loses its value the moment it is owed: because it is a gift, no one can have a duty to forgive, and it is confused to blame someone for refusing to. (3) Some atrocities are unforgivable, and a victim who forgave them would be doing something morally bad, not something generous or supererogatory. (4) Unconditional forgiveness -- forgiving someone who has neither apologized nor repented -- is possible and sometimes admirable. (5) Resentment toward unrepentant wrongdoers is often morally valuable, because it protests the wrong and expresses the victim's self-respect, so letting it go too easily cheapens both. (6) Someone who refuses to forgive a sincerely repentant friend over a minor betrayal is acting badly, not merely understandably. Which of these commitments are in genuine tension and which only appear to be? For each real tension, please say how severe it is and what the cheapest revision would be, rank the tensions rather than treating them all as equally bad, and tell me which single change would clean up the most.
