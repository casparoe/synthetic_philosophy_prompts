Write a deadpan product-roundup article, in the style of a British consumer magazine's annual gift guide, reviewing a fictional range of household goods called the Phi-Home collection, each of which is certified for its level of consciousness under Integrated Information Theory. Items on the test bench might include: a wall-sized grid of interconnected logic gates, marketed as the most conscious object ever sold for under ten thousand pounds; a state-of-the-art smart speaker whose feed-forward architecture earns it an official certificate of total unconsciousness despite its ability to discuss its own feelings fluently; and a premium model whose consciousness rating drops sharply if a single internal connection is rewired, even though nothing about its behaviour changes. Include a buyer's guide section on how to interpret the ratings, a sidebar of frequently asked questions (Is my dog more or less conscious than the hallway unit? Why did the laboratory refuse to certify the large models on grounds of computational cost?), and star ratings that reward internal causal organisation rather than anything the object actually does. The humour should come from taking the theory's counterintuitive consequences entirely seriously — that consciousness is a matter of internal structure rather than function, that a system behaving identically to a conscious one may rate zero, and that simple grids can outscore brains — so that a reader familiar with the IIT literature recognises the satire as technically fair, while a reader who isn't simply enjoys an increasingly absurd catalogue. About 700 words.
