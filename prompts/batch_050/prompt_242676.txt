After forty years working as an evolutionary ecologist — mostly on insect life histories — I have spent my retirement reading philosophy of science, and I keep running into the indispensability argument for mathematical realism: the claim, associated with Quine and Putnam, that we ought to believe mathematical objects exist because they are indispensable to our best science. A recent variant interests me more: the claim that mathematics does not merely describe nature but genuinely explains it. The standard exhibits are the periodical cicadas, whose 13- and 17-year emergence cycles are said to be explained by the primality of those numbers (primes minimize synchronization with predator life cycles), and the honeybee's comb, whose hexagonal cells are said to be explained by the theorem that a hexagonal tiling partitions a plane into equal areas with the least perimeter.

Here is my difficulty. When a philosopher says the mathematics explains, I automatically hear this in my native vocabulary — Mayr's distinction between proximate and ultimate causes, adaptation versus developmental or architectural constraint, optimality models and their known excesses — and I cannot tell whether the philosophical debate survives that translation or evaporates into a distinction I already drew every working day: the difference between a genuine functional explanation and mere bookkeeping.

So: translate. Restate the indispensability argument, and its explanatory variant, in the vocabulary of a working evolutionary biologist, as precisely as that vocabulary allows. I am fluent in the target vocabulary, so do not simplify it for me. Two cautions. First, do not smuggle the biological vocabulary's own assumptions in and call the result a translation; where biology is simply silent — on whether numbers exist, for instance — say so rather than inventing a biological counterpart. Second, the residue matters to me as much as the translation.

Please structure your response in five sections, in this order:

1. The Phenomena (roughly 200 words): state the cicada and honeycomb cases as a field biologist would describe them, before any philosophy enters, so I can check you have the biology right.
2. The Translation (roughly 500 words): the indispensability argument and the explanatory variant, rendered in terms of proximate and ultimate causation, adaptation, constraint, and optimality.
3. The Residue Report (roughly 400 words): three lists — what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no counterpart in the biological vocabulary at all — with your verdict on whether the residue indicts the vocabulary or the argument.
4. A Field Test (roughly 300 words): apply the translated framework to the Fibonacci spirals of sunflower florets, and to a case where the mathematics is plainly bookkeeping, such as Hardy-Weinberg proportions in a randomly mating population. Does the framework sort these cases where my instincts do?
5. My Hunch, Examined (roughly 150 words): my instinct has always been that mathematics in biology is the ledger in which selection's work is recorded, not the worker. Tell me plainly whether that hunch survives your analysis.
