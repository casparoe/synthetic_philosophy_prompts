I'm a PhD student working on practical rationality, and I have a draft paper with an argument my advisor says proves too much. I think she's right, but I can't find the step to cut, and I'd rather someone else locate it before my committee does. Background is Quinn's self-torturer puzzle: you've got a device permanently attached to you with a dial from 0 to 1000, each notch up pays you more money and increases your pain by an amount too small to notice, and setting 1000 is agony you'd never accept for the money if it were offered as a package deal. Here's the argument, slightly cleaned up from the draft:

(1) For any setting n, a rational agent at n should move to n+1: the money gain is real, and an imperceptable increase in pain cannot rationally outweigh a real benefit.

(2) If a rational agent should take each step of a sequence, each considered on its merits at the time, then it is not irrational for him to take the whole sequence.

(3) So it's not irrational for the self-torturer to keep turning the dial up to 1000.

(4) But at 1000 he's clearly worse off than at 0, and he knows this in advance. A theory of rationality that licenses knowingly walking into a clearly worse, fully avoidable outcome is broken.

(5) So one of (1) or (2) has to go.

(6) (1) can't go. Refusing at some particular n means turning down an option that's perceptibly better, for the sake of a cost that's invisible at n. Rational requirements can't outrun what the agent can in principle detect.

(7) So (2) goes: rationality is not closed under sequences of locally rational steps. Some step has to be refused even though nothing distinguishes it from the steps that were rightly taken.

(8) But agents can only be guided by differences they can discern, so a requirement to refuse an indistinguishable step is a requirement that can't guide anyone.

(9) Human life is full of self-torturer structures: workload creep, hedonic adaptation, tiny price increases, giving up five minutes here and there until your evenings are gone. Anywhere negligeable costs accumulate against visible benefits, we're on the dial.

(10) Therefore huge swaths of human choice are governed by rational requirements that are necessarily non-action-guiding, and rational choice theory, as an account of what we should actually do, fails for creatures like us.

(10) is absurd, or at least way more than I set out to prove. Which step do I reject, and why did it look fine? For what it's worth I want to keep (1), since it seems like the whole point of the puzzle that each step is compelling, and I really don't want to abandon the idea that rational requirements have to be able to guide us. My gut says something slips between (7) and (8) but I can't articulate it. Also, when you tell me what to cut, price the main alternatives for me: what does it cost to reject (1) outright, what does it cost to reject (2) via something like resolute choice or plan-based rationality, and what does it cost to just bite (8)'s bullet and accept that some rational requirements can't guide us locally.
