So here's a position I've talked myself into and I'd like to know what it would take to talk me out of it. The position: when it comes to the big collective harms -- factory farming, climate emissions, elections -- the sheer scale of the system means my individual act almost never changes anything. One fewer chicken bought doesn't reduce production (the industry works in batches and forecasts, and the odds my purchase is the one that tips a retailer's order are miniscule), one tonne of CO2 doesn't measurably change anyone's flood exposure, and no serious election is decided by a single vote. So, the view goes, the only honest ways to criticise my act are (a) expected-value reasoning -- tiny probability of triggering a threshold, multiplied by a huge effect -- or (b) some non-consequentialist rule about doing your part, which to my eye just assumes the conclusion it's meant to establish. And (a) cuts both ways: if the expected-value calculation is doing the work, then in plenty of cases it might come out in favour of the act, and the moralising was never about the harm at all.

What would count against this? I don't want the standard replies rehearsed back at me (I know about the harmless torturers, I know about threshold-triggering), I want a map of where the view is actually exposed. Specifically: which empirical findings about how supply chains, grid systems, or electorates actually respond at the margin would embarrass the premise that one act never makes a difference -- and are there real discontinuities (rounding, batch sizes, tipping points in demand forecasting) that make the "imperceptible difference" framing just descriptively wrong rather than philosophically contestable? Which formal results would hurt it -- e.g., if it turns out that under any remotely plausible credence distribution the expected contribution doesn't wash out the way I think, or that you can't coherently hold the view case-by-case without licensing paralysis across the board? And are there cases the view simply cannot describe -- overdetermination cases, cases where everyone reasoning exactly like me guarantees the bad outcome -- where any theory that endorses my reasoning has to bite a bullet I'd refuse to bite?

I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one, say whether a committed defender of the view would concede it as a genuine cost or dismiss it as a change of subject. Finally: has the position been quietly insulated -- e.g., by always being able to retreat to "well, the expected value is still negative" whenever a case goes against it -- and if so, is that kind of flexibility a vice for a view like this or just what hedging under uncertainty looks like? What I'd personally find most persuasive is a case where following the view's own logic, applied consistently by everyone it applies to, produces a result the view itself has to call bad. But I'll take whatever you've got.
