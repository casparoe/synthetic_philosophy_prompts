Went down a rabbit hole on pain asymbolia last night and I keep hitting the same problem with how philosophers use it, maybe you can straighten me out.

My understanding: after damage to certain brain regions (the insula gets mentioned a lot, or after cingulotomy) patients stop feeling pain entirely. Pinpricks, burns, whatever -- nothing registers, the sensation just doesn't arise anymore. Okay. But then philosophers cite these patients in debates about the badness of pain, like whether pain's badness is essential to what pain is, or whether the badness really comes from some seperate attitude or desire aimed at the sensation. And I can't see how the cases are even relevant to that. If the patients don't feel pain, then there's no pain there whose badness could be up for debate. Someone who feels literally nothing tells you nothing about what it's like to feel something. Honestly the case the philosophers seem to want would be the opposite: a person who has completely normal pain sensations but is somehow indifferent to them, just doesn't mind them at all. Now THAT would actually bear on whether badness is essential to pain. But asymbolia isn't that, it's just a broken alarm system.

Two follow-ups. First, these patients sound exactly like people born with congenital insensitivity to pain, so why does asymbolia get treated as this special window into the metaphysics of suffering while CIPA gets treated as just a (horrible) disability? Second, if the condition genuinely switches pain off, why isn't targeted surgery for it an accepted last resort for chronic pain patients -- or is it, and I just haven't heard?
