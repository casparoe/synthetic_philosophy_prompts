Had a long argument with a friend last night about the Meno problem -- why knowledge is supposed to be worth more than mere true belief when both get you to the same destination. She thinks the whole "extra value of knowledge" thing is a philosopher's invention; I think there's something real there but I couldn't produce a case that proved it. So build me one to order. I want a scenario with two people who hold the same true belief, arrived at the same way, except that one of them believes luckily (true belief, no knowledge) and the other knows -- and where the knower ends up demonstrably better off, in a way that isn't just "the knower happens to have more true beliefs down the line." Constraints: an everyday setting (a job site, a kitchen, a road trip), one short paragraph for the scenario itself, and no stopped clocks or fake barns, those are done to death. The better outcome has to fall out of the epistemic difference itself, not be stapled on afterwards. Then show the work: what exactly makes the lucky believer worse off, which single feature of the case is doing the work (stability under new information? guidance across changed circumstances?), whether the gap collapses if you remove that feature, and how my friend would attack the case. And take a side at the end: does your scenario actually show that knowledge has value beyond true belief, or does it show that the value was located somewhere else all along? Commit to an answer, don't just lay out options.
