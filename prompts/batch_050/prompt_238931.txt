A chapter in a popular neuroscience book I have been reading summarizes Integrated Information Theory as follows: Integrated Information Theory, developed by Giulio Tononi, holds that consciousness is integrated information: a system is conscious to the degree that its current state specifies more information than its parts do independently, a quantity denoted Φ. Consciousness is therefore not all-or-nothing but comes in degrees corresponding to Φ, so that many simple systems -- a photodiode, a thermostat -- possess a faint glimmer of experience, and IIT is a form of panpsychism. Since Φ grows with the sheer amount of information a system takes in, a grid of photodiodes such as a camera sensor, which registers an entire detailed image, would be highly conscious. The theory does have one striking success, though: it explains why consciousness is associated with the cerebral cortex rather than the cerebellum, since the cerebellum, despite containing most of the brain's neurons, has a largely feed-forward, modular architecture and hence low Φ. Finally, because Φ cannot be computed for anything remotely as complex as a human brain, the theory's central claim is untestable, and its pronouncements about particular cases rest on intuition rather than evidence.

The author is a practicing neuroscientist and the rest of the chapter struck me as careful, so I am inclined to trust this summary. Two things give me pause, however. I had been told that IIT's defenders explicitly resist the panpsychist label, and the camera-sensor example seemed to me to sit oddly with the theory's emphasis on integration, though I cannot say why. Please audit the summary claim by claim. Which parts would a proponent of the theory endorse as accurate? Which are defensible simplifications of a technical apparatus that a popular book cannot be expected to reproduce? And which parts misrepresent the theory -- whether by using its notion of information in the everyday or Shannon sense rather than the technical one, by overstating a thesis its defenders state more cautiously, or by presenting a contested objection as if it were a settled verdict? In particular, I would like to know three things: what the theory actually predicts about the camera sensor, given its own postulates; whether the charge of untestability follows from anything true in the summary or is an independent and disputable criticism; and whether the errors you identify, once corrected, undermine the author's broader conclusion that IIT is closer to metaphysics than to science.
