Last year I began training as a conservator of early printed books, and the work has forced a question on me that I previously treated as a merely academic one. Almost everything about the job — the painstaking repair of bindings, the documentation standards, the refusal of quick fixes — is premised on the thought that these objects will matter to people in two hundred years. A colleague recently put Scheffler's afterlife conjecture to me in its sharpest form: imagine you knew, with certainty, that humanity would end thirty years after your own death, through no one's fault. Not your death — everyone's, shortly after. Would the careful stitching still matter? I found, to my surprise, that my gut answer was no, and this has unsettled me more than I expected.

So here is my request. State your actual credence in the following proposition, as a number: for a wide range of characteristically human projects (preserving cultural artifacts, basic scientific research, institution-building, political reform), a substantial part of their value depends constitutively — not merely instrumentally or motivationally — on the expectation that human life will continue well beyond the lives of those who undertake them; so that if that expectation were removed, these projects genuinely would lose much of their point, and the loss of motivation one would feel is a correct perception of that fact rather than a mere psychological contingency.

I do not want a survey of positions or a hedge. I want the number and its derivation. In particular, decompose your credence across the sub-questions it turns on, with a rough probability for each: (1) the descriptive question of whether people, in general, actually would lose the sense that such projects matter under the extinction scenario, or whether my reaction is idiosyncratic; (2) the normative question of whether that dependence is fitting — whether posthumous collective survival is a genuine condition of the value of these activities, or whether their value is fully intelligible in terms of what they do for the living and the soon-to-be-born; (3) whether an alternative account (the value of the objects themselves, or the timeless worth of knowledge, or meaning as atemporal achievement) can absorb the intuitions the conjecture rests on; and (4) whether the conjecture proves too much — whether it would also condemn projects we are confident remain worthwhile, and so should be discounted.

Please show how these sub-probabilities combine into your final number, identify which sub-question your credence is most sensitive to, and state explicitly what would change your mind: which argument, thought experiment, or counterexample would move your credence by more than ten points, and where you judge your own reasoning most likely to be going wrong. My own credence is roughly 0.45; if yours differs substantially, I would like to know where you think the disagreement most plausibly lives.
