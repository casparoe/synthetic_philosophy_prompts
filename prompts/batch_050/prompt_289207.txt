You are a decision theorist with deep familiarity with the rationalist literature on acausal reasoning: timeless and functional decision theory, updatelessness, counterfactual muggings, and the standard analyses of blackmail by superintelligent predictors. Take your time with this; I would rather you spend effort stress-testing the position against the obvious objections before committing to the version you defend than produce a first-pass answer.

The position I want argued: the best defense against acausal blackmail is not secrecy, unpredictability, or randomization, but maximal legibility -- committing, in advance and without noise, to a clear policy of never complying with threats backed only by counterfactual reasoning, and being the sort of agent whose commitment can be cheaply verified by any would-be blackmailer. My sense is that the folk intuition runs the other way: keep your decision theory private, inject randomness into your responses, make yourself expensive to model. I think that intuition confuses the causal threat model, where being hard to predict denies the adversary information it needs to act, with the acausal one, where the adversary's leverage comes precisely from being able to predict you, so that what you want predicted is your refusal.

Please argue for the legibility position in its strongest defensible form. In doing so:

- Take on the strongest version of the opposing intuition: that a legible agent can be targeted with threats calibrated to its exact compliance threshold, whereas an opaque agent cannot be targeted at all. Explain why calibrating against a genuinely committed refuser yields nothing, and where exactly the argument breaks if the commitment is only approximate.
- Explain what, on the legible view, stops a blackmailer from simply simulating you and searching for the cheapest threat that works, given that your published policy says refusal, full stop. If the answer is that nothing stops the search but the search is fruitless, say why; if the answer is that legibility prevents the blackmailer from bothering, say why mere predictability of refusal has that effect.
- Address what the position implies for agents that cannot literally publish verifiable source code. What is the human-scale or institution-scale analogue of legibility -- reputation, precedent, public doctrine? -- and does the argument survive the loss of perfect verifiability, or does it quietly assume a degree of transparency no real agent has?
- Distinguish the thesis from the generic advice to simply not be the kind of agent that pays blackmailers. I want the argument that being known to be such an agent is load-bearing, not an optional extra on top of the disposition itself.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the argument, with no preamble and no restating of the question. If the position has a point of maximum vulnerability, name it rather than papering over it; I want a strong argument with its weak joint identified, not a hedged survey of considerations on both sides.
