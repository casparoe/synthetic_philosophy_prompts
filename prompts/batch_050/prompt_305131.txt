I'm workshopping an argument for a blog post and I've managed to reason myself into a corner. Quick background: some longtermist writers claim we might be living at the "hinge of history", a stretch of time with outsized influence over how the entire future unfolds, and that this should radically reorder our priorities. Here's my version of that arguement, then why I think it proves too much.

(1) Other things equal, you should put your altruistic resources wherever they do the most good in expectation.
(2) The long-run future is astronomically large in expectation: if humanity survives, the overwhelming majority of sentient beings who ever exist will exist after us, so even a tiny improvement in the future's trajectory swamps almost anything we could do for people alive today.
(3) This century is unusually high-leverage: transformative AI, engineered pathogens, and great-power war could plausibly either end the whole story or lock in institutions and values that persist for a very long time, and what gets built in the next few decades will largely determine which way it goes.
(4) So we should redirect a large fraction of altruistic effort -- talent, money, political capital -- away from present-day welfare and toward shaping the long-run future.
(5) But every ingredient of this argument was available to earlier generations. A sharp observer in 1450 could point to the printing press, in 1800 to industrialization, in 1950 to nuclear weapons, and run (1) through (4) verbatim, with the evidence feeling from the inside exactly as strong as ours feels now.
(6) It's absurd that rational altruists in 1450 were required to skimp on plague relief to fund "long-run trajectory work" they couldn't even conceptualize, and it's absurd that every generation in history gets to conclude that its own century happens to be the decisive one. That looks less like a discovery and more like a permanent excuse to ignore the present.
(7) So the argument proves too much, and something in (1)-(4) has to give.

If I have to choose, I most want to keep (3), the empirical claims about this century genuinely seem solid to me, and I suspect the rot is somewhere in (4) or in what (2) actually entitles me to conclude. But I can't pin it down. Which step do you reject, and what's the honest price? If I reject (2), what do I lose? If I reject the move from (3) to (4), what am I committed to instead?
