I co-teach a causation seminar with a colleague from the law school, and we've been going back and forth all semester about causation by omission. He keeps insisting his intuitions are just "the obvious common-sense ones," so I've been collecting his verdicts to prove he's actually running a theory without knowing it. Here's his track record so far. A gardener who was paid to water the orchids and simply never showed up: yes, his not watering caused the orchids to die. The Queen of England, who also did not water my orchids: obviously not a cause of thier death. A night watchman who skipped his rounds the night of the break-in: his omission caused the theft. A passerby across town who happened to be awake at 3am and could in principle have wandered by and scared off the burglar: not a cause, even though the theft wouldn't have happened if he'd shown up. A doctor who withheld a cheap, available antidote: caused the patient's death. A stranger in another country who could have donated the $50 needed for the antidote and didn't: not a cause, and my colleague is very firm about this one even though the patient would have lived if the guy had donated.

Now here's next week's case, which we're using on the exam. Two lifeguards are both on shift, both under identical contractual duties to watch the pool. Each notices the other is nearby and figures the other one is paying attention, so neither watches, and a swimmer drowns who would have been saved if even one of them had been watching. Predict my colleague's verdict -- are both omissions causes, one, or neither? Tell me which principle you think his earlier verdicts are tracking, because I can see at least two theories that fit his record so far (something duty-based, something more counterfactual, maybe something pragmatist about what we bother to mention), and I want to know which of them this case separates and which of them it leaves tied. And if there's a further case that would settle the remaining ambiguity, name it -- I'd like to put that one on the exam too.
