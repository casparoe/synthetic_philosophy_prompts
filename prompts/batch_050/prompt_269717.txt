I'm on the design review board of a small type foundry, and we vote on whether to publish commissioned typefaces glyph by glyph. One submission has caused a real fight and I need to draft the board's decision memo before Friday.

The face is a display sans, generally lovely, but the lowercase 'a' is the problem. It has no bowl and no counter -- it's basically a vertical stroke with a hook curling off the top right and a short tail bottom left. In our test panel, shown the glyph in isolation, only about 4 in 10 people identified it as an 'a' (most guessed 'd', 'q', or a music note). But here's the twist: embedded in words and sentences, readers parsed it flawlessly. Reading speed and comprehension were statistically identical to a conventional 'a', and afterwards most panelists couldn't even tell you what the 'a' had looked like.

The designers argument is that a letter is whatever does the letter's job inside the system, and that context is part of every letter anyway -- we misread 'rn' as 'm' constantly and don't notice. Half the board agrees and says pushing boundaries is why foundries exist. The other half says a glyph you can't recognize on its own has lost whatever makes it an 'a', and that the role-test logic means we could swap in a picture of a duck anywhere as long as sentences stayed readable, which is absurd.

You're a consultant with a background in both type design and the philosophy of categorization. Think hard before writing -- brainstorm the strongest version of each side first, then commit. The memo should recommend accept, reject, or accept-with-conditions, and it has to engage: what our test results do and don't show; whether the role theory really implies the duck conclusion and whether that's actually so bad (aren't emoji and rebus puzzles already that?); and whether there's any principled line -- intrinsic shape, recognizability in isolation, fluent readability -- we could reuse, because this designer's 'g' and 's' land on our desk next month.

Format, roughly: (1) "Recommendation" -- bottom line, ~3 sentences; (2) "What the evidence shows" -- one paragraph; (3) "The case for accepting" and (4) "The case against" -- a solid paragraph each, no strawmen; (5) "Where I come out and why" -- ~2 paragraphs; (6) "Rule going forward" -- a short operational test for future borderline glyphs. Don't just split the difference; I want a verdict.
