So I'm circulating a draft of a short essay (aiming at something like a Analysis-style note, maybe 3500 words when grown up) and I'd love your comments before I embarrass myself with it. It's on the identity of indiscernibles and Max Black's two-sphere counterexample. A bit of context: my reading of Leibniz is that in the Monadology (around §9) he isn't offering a definition of identity but a metaphysical claim grounded in the principle of sufficient reason -- roughly, that nature never contains two perfectly alike beings, because for any two distinct things there must be an internal difference, some intrinsic denomination, on pain of God having no reason to place one here rather than there. My essay tries to take that grounding seriously in a way I haven't seen in the literature I've covered, though the lit is big so tell me if I'm reinventing a wheel.

Here's the core of the draft, more or less as it stands:

---
Black asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres, a mile apart, in otherwise empty space, and says: here are two things sharing every property, so the identity of indiscernibles is false. The standard Leibnizian replies all seem to grant Black the description of the world and then fight over whether "being a mile from a sphere" is a property both spheres share. I want to attack the description itself.

When Black says "two spheres," he has already individuated them by position in an infinite Euclidean space. But nothing in the observable content of the world forces that reading. The same total qualitative facts are equally captured by a model with ONE sphere living in a space that wraps around -- take Euclidean space and identify points separated by a mile in some direction; a single sphere then appears, from inside, as if it had a twin a mile away, the way a person between two mirrors seems to see a crowd. On the one-sphere model, every fact Black cites (there is a sphere a mile in that direction, and a mile in the other) comes out true. So Black's "two" is not data; it is an artifact of assuming the ambient space is simply connected. And that assumption is precisely the sort of thing Leibniz's principle says needs a sufficient reason. If the two-sphere model and the one-sphere-in-wrapped-space model are indiscernible from each other, then by Black's own lights the difference between them is no difference at all -- in which case he cannot insist on the two-sphere reading without abandoning the spirit of his own argument.

The upshot: Black's case refutes the identity of indiscernibles only if the distinctness of regions of empty space can be taken as given, prior to and independent of what's in them. That's substantivism about space, and it's doing all the work. A Leibnizian relationist, who thinks space is nothing over and above the relations among bodies, has no resources to even state Black's world as a two-sphere world, and so no reason to concede the counterexample.
---

A few specific things I'd like comments on:

1. Accuracy first. Is my gloss of Leibniz fair -- in particular, am I right that his claim rests on sufficient reason rather than being a semantic thesis about what "identical" means? And is it fair to Black to say his argument quietly presupposes substantivism? I have a nagging memory that someone in the Black literature (maybe in the replies to his original article?) says almost exactly this, and I don't want to claim novelty I haven't earned.

2. The wrap-around move. Does it actually work? My worry: on the wrapped model, the single sphere is a mile from ITSELF, and maybe self-distance is different enough from distance-to-another that the models aren't genuinely indiscernible after all. Is that a fatal difference or a restatement of the point at issue? I'd especially like to know if the move collapses into the old "relational properties" reply and I've just dressed it up in topology.

3. Whether the conclusion is as strong as I think. I say the Leibnizian relationist "has no resources to state" Black's world. Is that right, or can the relationist state it and just deny it's possible? If the latter, my essay is less interesting than I thought -- it's just "relationism is false, so nyah."
