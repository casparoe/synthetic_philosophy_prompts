Two policies of the same government appear to rest on opposite assumptions about how one person's welfare compares to another's, and I would like to know whether that appearance is correct.

The first policy is regulatory cost-benefit analysis. When an environmental or safety agency evaluates a rule, it monetizes the costs and benefits falling on everyone affected, sums them, and proceeds if the net figure is positive. A hundred dollars of benefit to a wealthy person counts for exactly as much as a hundred dollars of benefit to a poor person; the agency asks how much is at stake, not for whom. Defenders sometimes present this even-handedness as a virtue: the method is neutral between persons, they say, because it declines to weigh one individual's welfare against another's.

The second policy is progressive income taxation. The standard justification for taxing higher incomes at higher rates is that an extra thousand dollars means less to someone earning a million a year than to someone earning twenty thousand, so that transferring resources down the income scale sacrifices less welfare at the top than it creates at the bottom. This seems to require not merely comparing but effectively quantifying the welfare that different people derive from the same quantity of money.

What, exactly, does each practice presuppose about the possibility of interpersonal comparisons of welfare? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind each, and in each case separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. On the cost-benefit side: could the analyst insist that dollars function purely as a measuring rod, that no comparison of welfare across persons is being performed at all — and if so, what is the resulting sum actually a measure of, and does it still support the decision it is used to justify? On the tax side: could progressivity be defended without any interpersonal welfare comparisons, on grounds of social insurance, incentive effects, political equality, or something else — and do those defenses genuinely avoid comparing welfare across persons, or do they merely relocate the comparison one step back?

Then take up the consistency question directly. Can one government coherently operate both policies, given the grounds on which each is standardly defended? Or must it either introduce distributional weights into its cost-benefit analyses, or else abandon the diminishing-marginal-utility rationale for its tax code? If you find the practices in genuine tension, say which of the two you take to rest on the sounder implicit theory, and why.

End your answer with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of these labels: compatible-as-practiced (both policies can be defended without interpersonal welfare comparisons), compatible-only-with-comparisons (the two can be defended together, but only by embracing interpersonal comparisons), or inconsistent (no single view of interpersonal welfare comparison licenses both).
