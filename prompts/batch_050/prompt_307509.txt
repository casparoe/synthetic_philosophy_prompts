Exam question: State the thesis and antithesis of Kant's Third Antinomy and briefly sketch his proof for each side. Then explain how Kant resolves the conflict and what that resolution contributes to his broader case for transcendental idealism.

Student's answer: The third antinomy is about causality. Thesis: causality according to laws of nature is not the only kind of causality in the world — we also have to assume a causality through freedom. Kant's proof is that if everything happens only through natural causality, then every event presupposes an earlier state from which it follows, so there could never be a first beginning and the causal series stays incomplete; but the law of nature itself says nothing happens without a sufficient cause, so natural causality contradicts its own completeness requirement unless there is a spontaneous, self-beginning cause. Antithesis: there is no freedom and everything in the world happens solely according to laws of nature, because a free cause would be an event with no prior determining ground, which breaks the rule that every event has a cause and would make coherent experience impossible. Kant resolves it the way he resolves all four antinomies: thesis and antithesis are both false, since both assume the world of appearances is a thing in itself with fully determinate features, and once we accept transcendental idealism the contradiction vanishes. He also shows that each proof contains a fallacy — the thesis trades on an equivocation about "beginning" and the antithesis begs the question by simply assuming universal determinism — so the whole conflict is sophistical, like Zeno's paradoxes, and falls apart once you spot the trick. The payoff for the Critique is that the antinomies amount to an indirect proof of transcendental idealism, since transcendental realism is what lands reason in these contradictions in the first place.
