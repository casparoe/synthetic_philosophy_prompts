After a four-hour argument with a friend that ended in stalemate, I want a neutral third party to draft the document we should have produced. My friend, trained in behavioral economics, holds that loss aversion and the endowment effect are straightforward cognitive biases: the fact that people routinely demand far more to give up a good than they would pay to acquire the identical good (as in the classic experiments where owners of randomly distributed coffee mugs demand roughly double what non-owners offer) shows that arbitrary ownership distorts valuation, and that the asymmetry between losses and gains is a kink in the mind rather than a feature of the world. I hold the opposing view: that at least a large portion of what gets labeled the endowment effect reflects legitimate, defensible preferences. Ownership genuinely changes one's relationship to a good (hedonic adaptation, sentimental attachment, and integration into one's plans), transaction costs and uncertainty about replacement quality rationally widen the gap between selling and buying prices, and treating a foregone gain as less bad than a loss of the same magnitude need not be any more incoherent than preferring the near to the far or the certain to the risky.

Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two positions. Structure it as follows:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — statements about the experimental record, about what would count as a preference versus a bias, and about the limits of the standard mug-and-ticket paradigms — that both sides could sign without reservation.
2. A disagreements section in which each remaining dispute is stated precisely, with each side's position phrased in words that side would accept as fair (no strawmen, no question-begging labels like "irrational" or "merely sentimental").
3. The cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of us. I suspect one crux is whether reference points are fixed by actual ownership or constructed by framing and context, and another is whether the diminished endowment effect observed in experienced traders shows the effect is a trainable-away error or instead that expertise legitimately changes one's preferences. Confirm, refine, or replace these.
4. For each crux, a concrete case, experiment, or observation both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance — including, where relevant, how each side would interpret the cases of sentimental heirlooms, default enrollment in retirement plans, and otherwise identical choices framed as losses versus foregone gains.

Close with a short section identifying which parts of our disagreement, if any, are merely verbal, and justify that classification.
