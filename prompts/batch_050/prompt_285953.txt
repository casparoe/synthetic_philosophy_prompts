I'm a postdoc in political theory, and next month I'm presenting a job-market paper to the faculty of a law school -- about fifteen tenured professors, most of them doctrinal scholars in constitutional and administrative law, two or three who do law-and-economics and are comfortable with formal social choice, and one empiricist who thinks anything without data is armchair speculation. The paper defends a specific proposal: when multimember bodies (courts, hiring committees, tenure committees) must decide a conclusion that logically depends on several premises, they should vote premise by premise using a "discursive supermajority" rule I develop -- each premise needs three-fifths support, but if the premises pass and the conclusion is nonetheless rejected by a majority of members voting on the conclusion directly, the body must either adopt the conclusion anyway or formally re-vote one premise under a higher threshold. I argue this thread-the-needle rule preserves both the doctrinal consistency that premise-based procedures deliver and the responsiveness to bottom-line judgment that conclusion-based procedures deliver, and that it answers the standard strategic-voting objection because manipulation now requires coordinating across two voting stages.
