I teach early modern philosophy and, in alternate years, a graduate seminar on the ethics of communication. Next term I want to put Augustine's two treatises on lying -- De Mendacio and the later Contra Mendacium -- on the syllabus alongside the contemporary analytic literature, and I've been drafting a handout that recasts his position in the framework my students actually command, namely post-Gricean pragmatics and speech-act theory: assertion as a move governed by norms, conversational implicature, maxim exploitation, the distinction between what is said and what is merely meant. What I want from you is a systematic translation of Augustine's account into that vocabulary, followed by a residue report. His position, in brief, is that a lie is a false statement made with the intention of deceiving; that lying is wrong absolutely, not merely on balance; that his eightfold taxonomy ranks lies by severity, from lies told in matters of religious doctrine down to "officious" lies told to protect someone from harm; that jokes and fictions are not lies because no one is deceived; and that a Christian must sometimes prefer silence or even martyrdom to any lie whatsoever -- while apparently tolerating strategic non-disclosure.

Please map the components of this view onto the pragmatist's toolkit as faithfully as you can: where the intention-to-deceive condition falls relative to the saying/implicating distinction; whether Augustine's absolute prohibition amounts to a claim about the norm of assertion itself or about the ethics of implicature; how the eightfold ranking would look if redescribed in terms of which conversational mechanisms each lie exploits; and whether his distinction between lying and mere concealment survives once misleading by true implicature is on the table. Then the part I care about most: what does not survive the translation? Augustine's framework leans heavily on the speaker's inner orientation -- what one wills in one's heart, the direction of one's mind toward God -- whereas pragmatics is resolutely about publicly recoverable meaning. Tell me whether this residue reflects a genuine phenomenon (a private dimension of lying that speech-act theory structurally cannot capture), a defect in Augustine's account that the translation usefully exposes, or a case where the target framework's assumptions have been smuggled in so that the "failure to translate" is really a prior disagreement. I'd like the handout to leave my students uncertain about which of those three verdicts is right, so argue for the one you find most defensible and say where its weakest joint is.
