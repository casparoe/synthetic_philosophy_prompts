My college's honor code is a single sentence: no student shall lie, cheat, or steal, nor tolerate those who do. Each autumn, on the evening before new students sign the code, one speaker delivers the keynote at our Honor Assembly. This year the speaker is me, and I would like you to write the address — roughly 1,300 words, about ten minutes out loud.

The occasion has a live controversy attached to it. Last spring the honor council investigated a student whose roommate had been photographing an exam in their suite's common room. Asked directly whether she had seen anyone in the common room that night, she answered that she had gone to bed at ten and slept straight through. Every word was true: she did go to bed at ten, woke briefly around two, saw her roommate at the desk with the exam, and went back to sleep without saying anything. The council concluded that it could not charge her with lying under the code, since she had asserted nothing false. The case has split the campus. Half say she got away with lying on a technicality; half say what she did was wrong but was simply not the thing the code forbids, and that a code which punishes a false assertion with expulsion while remaining silent on a deceptive true one is exactly as coherent — or exactly as incoherent — as it sounds.

The address must take a position on two questions: whether there is a genuine moral difference between lying and deliberately misleading by means of true assertions, and whether an honor code that expels for the first and cannot touch the second is defensible or in need of amendment. It should engage the strongest version of each side. On one side: the idea that a lie invites the hearer to rely on the speaker's own word and thereby wrongs the hearer in a way that misleading does not, since a misleading truth leaves an inferential gap the hearer was free to notice and close. On the other: the view, defended prominently in the recent literature, that where the deception, the stakes, and the breach of trust are identical, the linguistic means cannot change the moral verdict. Whatever position the address takes, it must answer the hardest reply to that position: if it defends the distinction, it must face the objection that the student deceived her council exactly as much as any liar could have; if it denies the distinction, it must explain why ordinary tact — the truthful but engineered answer one gives a host about a bad dinner party — does not thereby become a lie.

The audience is mixed: faculty who know this literature, trustees, and several hundred eighteen-year-olds who will sign the code tomorrow morning. The address should give those students a concrete answer to the question of what, exactly, they will be promising. It should be rhetorically fit for the room — jargon-free or jargon-explained, with at least one moment of genuine warmth or humor — rather than a lecture wearing a gown.

Finally, after the address itself, append a separate note of 150–250 words identifying the single premise on which the speech's verdict most depends, and stating what argument, case, or consideration would lead you to have written the speech with the opposite conclusion — that is, where the address's reasoning is most exposed, and what would change your mind.
