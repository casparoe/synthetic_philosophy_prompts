So I'm a journalist, I mostly cover science and ideas stuff, and I'm writing this from a mid-sized European city where I've spent the last two weeks covering a physics conference. And I've gotten myself into a genuinely confusing situation about buses, of all things. Here's the deal: at the bus stops near my hotel, nobody queues. It's not chaos exactly — there's clearly some kind of system, because elderly people and parents with strollers get waved to the front and everyone seems to know whose turn it roughly is — but there's no line, and boarding is basically a polite-ish scrum. My first three days I did what I'd do at home: stood in an orderly line, let others go first. Result: I never got on a bus before it filled up, not once. A local colleague finally laughed at me and told me to just push in like everyone else, that I was "playing a game nobody else is playing."

Here's where it gets philosophically weird for me, and I might actually pitch a column on this, so help me think it through. Part of me feels like following the local custom is just practical advice — do it or miss your bus, your call. But another part of me wonders if there's an actual moral obligation here, like the scrum-system is a kind of unwritten agreement everyone present is relying on, and my little line was me refusing to sign it while still benefiting from the order it creates. Meanwhile my colleague insists it's the opposite: she says as a visitor I should copy local norms even if they seem worse, because I'm a guest and guests don't get to unilaterally renegotiate the house rules. But then I noticed something that complicates her view: the scrum is genuinely bad for people with mobility issues who aren't visibly elderly, and one woman I talked to said she avoids the bus entirely because of it. So now I'm wondering — does "when in Rome" actually hold up as a moral rule, or is it just etiquette dressed up? Does it matter that I'm a short-term visitor who can just leave, versus a resident who'd have standing to push for change? And is my colleague's guest principle actually consistent, since she'd presumably never tell me to follow a local custom that was, say, cruel?

Give me your actual take on what I should do tomorrow morning at the bus stop, not just the abstract theory — though I do want the theory too, since my editor will want an argument and not just an anecdote. And please write in American English, since that's the house style of the outlet I'm pitching to.
