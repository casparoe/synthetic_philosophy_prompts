So here's my problem. I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and my editor has voluntold me to run a ninety-minute session in our newsroom's internal training series — "maths reporters actually need" — on Good's theorem: I. J. Good's late-sixties result that a rational Bayesian decision-maker can never be worse off, in expectation, for recieving free information before choosing. The audience is twenty-odd journalists and editors: clever, quick, allergic to notation, and — this is the wrinkle — people whose daily information diet comes from sources with agendas (press officers, campaign groups, whistleblowers with books to sell), so the theorem's assumptions fail in more or less exactly the conditions they work in. Plan the session for me; don't deliver it.

What I want, concretely: the order the pieces have to come in and why — I'm guessing you can't even state the theorem until expected utility and updating are both in place, but tell me what else depends on what; the one worked example to carry the whole thing, which needs a decision where the free information sometimes changes what you do (otherwise the point evaporates into a triviality), must have no cost and no strategic source, and should ideally be newsroom-flavoured rather than the usual medical test or umbrella; the misconceptions they'll form in roughly the order they'll form them — "so information is always good, full stop", "so turning down a free test is irrational", "so this is a licence for hoarding data", plus whatever muddle they'll make of ex ante versus ex post — and exactly where in the sequence I should kill each one off; how to handle the elephant in the room, namely that their sources are strategic actors, without either pretending the theorem covers that or letting the room conclude it's useless maths that doesn't apply to real life; what I should openly refuse to touch at this level (the proof? Blackwell's ordering of experiments? the stuff about non-Bayesian agents rationally refusing information?) and say so out loud; and one closing question that would show me whether they've grasped it as a theorem — assumptions, weak inequality and all — rather than absorbed it as a slogan about curiosity being virtuous.

Reply in British English, please.
