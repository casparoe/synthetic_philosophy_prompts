I direct the applied ethics segment of a master's program in public policy, and next month I have a single two-hour seminar on individual responsibility for collective harms. My students arrive trained in cost-benefit analysis, and most take the inefficacy objection to be decisive: where one person's choice is practically certain not to alter the aggregate outcome — one vote in a national election, one long-haul flight, one purchase in a continental meat market — talk of individual moral responsibility strikes them as rhetoric rather than reasoning. My aim is not to convert them to a position but to leave them unable to state the objection in its naive form. I want a plan for the session, not the lecture itself.

Plan the explanatory arc. I intend to build the whole session around a case I have invented: a drought year in which a city's reservoir sits just above the level that triggers mandatory rationing; each household's garden watering makes an imperceptible difference to the water level, the utility does not adjust prices or quotas, and whether rationing is triggered depends only on the aggregate draw. Say what this case must have and must lack to carry the session — I suspect it must admit both a reading on which each act genuinely does nothing and a reading on which each act runs a small chance of being the marginal one that trips the threshold, and I want the tension between those readings to drive the hour — and state what distinction has to be in place before the case can do its work. I take it the difference between making no difference and making no perceptible difference has to come first, but convince me or correct me, and say what depends on what thereafter.

Then give the misconception map: the errors these particular students will form, in the order they will form them — my guesses are that imperceptible contribution gets conflated with no contribution, that expected-value reasoning gets treated as settling the normative question rather than merely answering the causal one, and that causal inefficacy gets conflated with moral permissibility — and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than merely rebutted after it has hardened. Include the check I should run at the end to tell whether they have learned to separate the causal question from the permissibility question, as opposed to having simply adopted a side, and name what I should openly decline to cover in a single session.

Present the plan as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
