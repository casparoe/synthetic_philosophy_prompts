Three weeks ago I began my first semester as a TA for a first-year philosophy seminar on knowledge, evidence, and public speech, and I now have my first stack of take-home essays to grade. The professor has asked each of us to come to Friday's meeting with a proposed grade and a written justification for every essay. I am reasonably confident about the strong papers and the weak ones, but I keep going back and forth on the one below, and I don't yet trust my own eye enough to know why. The topic has also stopped feeling abstract to me: deciding what this student may fairly be marked down for saying turns out to be a version of the very problem the essay is about.

Here is the exam question, exactly as I set it:

Read the following passage from W. K. Clifford, "The Ethics of Belief":

"The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him."

(a) Explain in your own words the principle Clifford is defending here and how it applies to his case of the shipowner. (b) Some philosophers hold that one may properly assert only what one knows (the "knowledge norm" of assertion). Is Clifford committed to anything like this norm? Defend your answer, attending carefully to the passage.

And here is the student's answer, in full:

The passage states Clifford's central principle: whether a belief is right or wrong depends on how it was formed, not on whether it happens to be true. His shipowner convinced himself that his vessel was seaworthy by dwelling on her past voyages and trusting Providence, while deliberately pushing aside the doubts that had been raised about her. Clifford's verdict is that the man is guilty of the deaths of the passengers, and that his guilt would be unchanged even if the ship had crossed safely, because the wrongness lies in the stifling of doubts rather than in the causing of a wreck. What matters is that the shipowner had no right to hold his belief on the evidence available to him.

The question is whether this commits Clifford to the knowledge norm of assertion, the view that one may properly assert only what one knows. I think it does. Clifford insists that belief must be earned through patient investigation of the evidence; a belief so earned is one the believer is entitled to hold and to express. But a belief honestly earned from a thorough inquiry is simply what we mean by knowledge, since the believer has done everything required to rule out error. So Clifford's standard amounts to the claim that we may assert only what we know to be true. His remark that rightness has nothing to do with whether a belief "turned out to be true or false" reinforces this reading: what licenses assertion is the quality of the inquiry, which is also what makes a belief knowledge.

A further implication is that once a belief has been honestly earned, its rightness is permanently secured, because Clifford locates rightness in the belief's origin. A scientist who reaches a conclusion through years of careful work may therefore go on asserting it with a clear conscience even if later results begin to point the other way; the new evidence bears on whether the belief is true, not on whether she had a right to it, and that right was fixed at the moment of honest inquiry.
