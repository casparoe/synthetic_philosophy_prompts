I am a novelist finishing a book set in a fictional country during a campaign of mass violence, and I have run into a dispute that has stopped my draft cold. My story is loosely modeled on real twentieth-century atrocities: a government arms one ethnic group against another, villages are emptied, children are taken and raised in the majority's language and religion, and the targeted group survives only in exile. In my manuscript I have a character, a journalist, call the events by the gravest available word for the destruction of a people. My editor wants me to cut the word or hedge it, on two grounds. First, she says, the legal use of that word requires proving that the perpetrators intended to destroy the group as a group, and my fictional officials never quite say that out loud; they speak of security, relocation, and assimilation. Second, she argues that the word has become less a description than a prize: once it is granted, the victims' suffering is officially certified as the worst kind, and once it is withheld, the same dead are somehow demoted. A friend whose family fled a real atrocity takes the opposite view with equal force: to her, refusing the word is itself a kind of violence, a second erasure, and she thinks novelists who flinch from it are collaborators in forgetting.

I am not asking you to adjudicate any real historical case, and I would rather you did not: I want help with the conceptual knot underneath my editorial dispute, because how I resolve it will shape the ending of the book. The questions I keep circling are these. Does the moral gravity of mass killing actually depend on the perpetrators' intent toward the group as such, or is that intent requirement a lawyer's technicality that has been allowed to colonize our moral thinking? When people fight over whether the word applies to some event, are they really disagreeing about facts and definitions, or is the fight itself the point — a struggle over whose suffering gets institutional recognition, in which the word is a weapon rather than a label? And for my purposes specifically: is there a defensible sense in which a novelist, who is neither a court nor a government, owes the dead either the word or its restraint? If you think my editor is right that intent is doing legitimate work, explain what work and why it survives the obvious objection that perpetrators rarely announce their deepest aims. If you think my friend is right that withholding the word is a further harm, explain what makes a classification capable of harming, and whether that argument proves too much by making every terminological caution a betrayal.

Please take an actual position rather than surveying the options symmetrically; I can generate the survey myself. But I would also like you to write as someone who knows their view is contested: tell me explicitly what would change your mind — which argument or consideration, if it held up, would lead you to the opposite verdict on whether the intent requirement is a moral insight or a moral evasion — and identify the point in your own reasoning where you think it is most likely to break.
