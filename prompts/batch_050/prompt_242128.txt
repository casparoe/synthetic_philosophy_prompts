Since retiring from a career in materials science I have been reading in the philosophy of technology, and I keep returning to the question whether artifacts can themselves embody political arrangements. I find that my verdicts on particular cases do not obviously cohere, and I would like to know what principle, if any, underlies them. Here are the cases, with my verdicts as they stand:

1. A manufacturer in the 1880s installs pneumatic molding machines that are more expensive and no more efficient than the hand methods they replace; their real virtue, for the owner, is that they can be run by unskilled workers, which lets him break the skilled molders' union. Once the union is broken, the machines are quietly abandoned. Verdict: this artifact has politics — the political aim is the whole explanation of its adoption.

2. The low overpasses on certain parkways, built so that buses cannot pass beneath them, with the effect that those dependent on public transport cannot reach the parks and beaches beyond. Set aside the historical dispute about the designer's intentions. Verdict: even granting innocent intent, I feel the concrete is doing political work — but I cannot say whether that feeling survives scrutiny.

3. A mechanical tomato harvester whose adoption requires fruit bred for hardness and uniformity and whose cost structure favors large farms, so that smallholders and field laborers are squeezed out. Verdict: no politics here. Foreseeable distributional consequences, certainly, but no rule or exclusion is encoded in the machine; the politics sits in the credit markets and the research-funding decisions upstream of it.

4. A speed bump. A speed limit is a rule stated on a sign; a speed bump is the same rule enforced in asphalt. Verdict: politics embodied in matter — yet it was transparently and publicly decided upon, so perhaps it is mere administration.

5. The QWERTY keyboard: an arguably suboptimal standard locked in by path dependence, serving no one's current interest. Verdict: constraint without politics. I take this as a counterexample to any easy slide from "the artifact constrains us" to "the artifact is political."

6. Nuclear power, and the claim that it is workable only under centralized, security-intensive, quasi-authoritarian institutions. Verdict: strong compatibility, yes; requirement, no. Artifacts bias the feasible set of social arrangements but rarely fix a member of it.

Two tensions trouble me: case 3 against case 2 (if distributional consequences do not make the harvester political, why does exclusion make the overpass political?), and case 4 against case 1 (if delegating enforcement to matter is politics, then the speed bump and the molding machine are alike, yet they feel crucially different — and if they are not alike, what does the differentiating work?).

What I want from you: a principle, or two or three candidate principles, that sorts cases into something like (a) politics embodied in the artifact, (b) politics located only in the circumstances of adoption, (c) a neutral artifact with political side-effects, and (d) constraint or determination without politics. Please state which clause of your principle each of my cases motivates, which of my verdicts the simplest viable principle forces me to abandon, and what the principle predicts for two cases I have not listed: an automated hiring screen that scores job applicants, and a footbridge reachable only by stairs. Finally, I would like an honest assessment of whether the whole "politics of artifacts" framing survives this exercise, or whether my scientist's instinct — that politics is always in the people and institutions, never in the stuff — can after all be made good.
