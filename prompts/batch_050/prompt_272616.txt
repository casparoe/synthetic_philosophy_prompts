Okay so some context: I'm a journalist, I cover science and weird ideas for a general-interest magazine, and in a moment of weakness I agreed to give a short talk at a small workshop on AI forecasting next month. The audience is mostly ML researchers and engineers, maybe a third of them, then some economics-of-AI people, and a handful of the usual forecasting-community crowd who already think about takeoff speeds all the time. Crucially, the ML people are the majority and they are, to put it gently, not sympathetic to journalist takes on this stuff.

My talk defends a specific thesis, which is my own framing and probably the thing they'll want to push on: that the whole fast-takeoff vs. slow-takeoff fight is secretly a fight about what the binding constraint on AI progress actually is. My claim is that slow-takeoff arguments almost always assume the constraint is atoms -- experiments, hardware, deployment, institutions -- while fast-takeoff arguments assume the constraint is insight, i.e. the research itself, and that once AI systems can do the research the insight constraint dissolves. Neither side, I argue, states this assumption out loud, and most of the standard data points people throw at each other (compute trends, GDP growth rates, historical tech adoption curves) don't actually arbitrate between the two pictures because they were all generated under a regime where humans did the research. So my conclusion is that the debate is currently unanswerable with the evidence people keep citing, and the productive question is instead: which bottleneck is real, and what would observationally distinguish a world where the bottleneck is insight from one where it's atoms.

I have about 20 minutes of talk and then a Q&A that I fully expect to be the painful part. Prep me for it. I want the objections in the rough order they'll actually come, phrased the way a skeptical ML researcher would phrase them (they will not say "takeoff speeds," they'll say "you're just describing scaling" or something dismissive about recursive self-improvement), and separately the objections I'll get from the economists, who'll come at it from elasticity-of-substitution and complementarities angles I understand only halfway. For each objection: how much damage it does if it lands, what I should just concede up front, and a two-or-three-sentence reply. Also flag where my own framing is genuinely soft -- the spot where a smart questioner could get me to admit that "the bottleneck is insight vs atoms" is itself too binary, because I do worry that's true. And tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks, because I suspect the forecasting-community people in the room are the only ones who'll think of it and it's worse for me than anything the ML crowd has.
