I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general readership, and I am preparing a long piece on the old question of why some nations are rich and others poor. I have read enough of the literature to know the standard camps -- those who credit inclusive versus extractive institutions, those who point to geography (tropical disease burdens, agricultural endowments, access to trade routes), and those who appeal to culture or deep historical factors -- but I keep having the nagging feeling that all three camps are doing the same questionable thing and describing it in different words.

Here is what I would like. I am fairly fluent in the vocabulary of causal inference as statisticians and econometricians use it: potential outcomes and counterfactuals, confounding and backdoor paths, instrumental variables and the exclusion restriction, selection effects, measurement error, attenuation bias, and the distinction between identifying an effect within a sample and extrapolating it elsewhere. I would like you to restate the core of this debate in that vocabulary, as precisely and honestly as you can. That means reconstructing what each camp is actually claiming when it says institutions "cause" growth or geography "determines" development: what is the treatment, what is the unit, what would have to be held fixed, what identification strategy is implicitly being relied on, and whether the historical record -- a few hundred national cases, observed once each, over centuries -- can in principle deliver what the strategy requires. Please do not be gentle here: I want the translation to be unsparing about things like using settler mortality as an instrument, the problem that institutions and prosperity co-evolve so there is no clean pre-treatment period, and the fact that "culture" often functions as a label for whatever variance is left over.

Then, and this is the part I care most about, give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly into causal language and turn out to be straightforward (perhaps trivially) true or false once translated? Which claims can only be translated with distortion, and what exactly is the distortion? And which parts of the debate seem to have no counterpart in the causal-inference vocabulary at all -- for instance, questions about why a particular region developed its institutions in the first place, or whether "the nation" is even the right unit when the causal story spans empires and migrations?

Finally, I want a verdict on the residue itself: where the translation fails, is that a defect of the causal-inference framework -- evidence that there are legitimate historical questions it simply cannot formalize -- or is it a sign that the original claims were vaguer than they sounded and were gaining rhetorical force precisely from their untranslatability? Please commit to a view rather than presenting both sides as equally respectable, and then tell me what would change your mind: which argument, example, or methodological development would most plausibly overturn your verdict, and at which step of your own reasoning you think the analysis is most likely to fail.
