I am a senior software engineer at a company that operates a commercial matter-transportation service between Earth and the Mars habitat. I have no formal philosophy training, but I sit, as the technical member, on our standing Continuity Certification Board, which rules on disputed cases. Our standard process scans the traveler destructively at the departure terminal -- the original body is disassembled cell by cell during the scan -- and reconstructs the person from the transmitted data at arrival. Until last month, we had never had a serious dispute. Now we have Case 41, and the board's general counsel has asked each of us to submit a written opinion before we vote.

The facts, as the board has accepted them, are these. A customer was scanned for transport to Mars. Due to a fault in the disassembly stage, his original body was not destroyed: he survived the scan in a coma and was kept alive in our Seattle clinic for six days before dying of the damage the scan had caused. The Mars reconstruction, meanwhile, succeeded without error. The man who woke up there has all the customer's memories, believes himself to be the customer, has been living the customer's life, and is a party to these proceedings.

The question the board must answer is whether to certify the Mars individual as the continuation of the customer -- the same person for purposes of his employment contract, property, and marriage -- or to declare that the customer died in Seattle, in which case a buy-sell clause transfers his stake in a joint venture to his business partner, who has appeared before us and argued forcefully for exactly that conclusion.

Please draft the written opinion I will submit, roughly 800 words. It should:

1. Recommend one of the two certifications, clearly, with reasoning the other board members (a physician, a lawyer, and a customer advocate, none of them philosophers) can follow and act on.

2. Give the strongest case for the losing side before answering it. In particular, take seriously the business partner's argument that what died in the Seattle clinic was not a copy or a leftover but the customer himself -- a man who, for six days, was undisputedly alive, and who therefore cannot also have been on Mars.

3. Address the fact that keeps me up at night as an engineer: for six days, both versions existed. Whatever the board decides, our reasoning should not collapse into saying that the Mars individual's status retroactively depended on whether the Seattle patient happened to survive or die quickly -- that seems to make who someone is hostage to a hardware failure and a heart monitor. If your recommended reasoning does have that consequence, say so honestly and explain why the board should accept it anyway.

4. Close with a short paragraph on the precedent we would be setting: whether the same reasoning binds us in a future case where the departure-side survivor recovers fully and walks out of the clinic.

I want the opinion to rest on the conceptual question -- what does survival actually consist in, and does exact physical continuity matter, or psychological continuity, or something else -- rather than on legal technicalities or on who deserves the money. Do not pad it with citations or references to named thinkers; I want arguments I can defend in the room, not authorities I cannot verify.
