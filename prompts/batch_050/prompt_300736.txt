An eliminative materialist and an intentional realist about beliefs and desires have been arguing in our philosophy of mind reading group for two semesters, mostly past each other. Draft the joint statement they could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration.

The eliminativist holds that "belief" and "desire" are posits of a stagnant folk theory that a mature neuroscience will discard rather than reduce, the way pre-scientific posits like phlogiston and caloric were discarded. The realist holds that belief-desire psychology is among the most successful predictive instruments we possess, that its posits are no worse off than the posits of other special sciences (Mendelian genes were unobserved theoretical posits for decades before anyone found their molecular basis), and that cognitive science presupposes content-bearing states rather than threatening them.

The document should contain three parts.

1. A numbered list of agreed claims: what both sides accept about folk psychology's actual track record (where it predicts well, where it reliably fails), about the immaturity of neuroscience, about what each takes the mind-brain physically to be, about the fact that neither camp has a worked-out theory of content, and about which common-sense intuitions each side concedes it must explain away rather than accommodate.

2. A disagreements table in which each dispute is stated in a single sentence, phrased so that the side whose view it describes would accept the wording. I expect the genuine disagreements to include at least: whether belief- and desire-talk picks out anything like natural kinds; whether the right historical analogy is phlogiston or genes; and whether the eliminativist can state their thesis without self-undermining (does asserting that there are no beliefs itself presuppose belief?). If you conclude that part of their long-running dispute has been verbal rather than substantive, identify which part and explain why.

3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, give the case, argument, or class of evidence that both parties accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Candidate cruxes to assess (keep, refine, discard, or add to): whether connectionist and other distributed-representation architectures vindicate or undermine discrete content-bearing states; whether the fragmentation of the folk category "memory" into working, episodic, and procedural systems is a precedent for outright elimination or merely for revision within a realist framework; and what a mature cognitive neuroscience would have to look like for each side to concede — that is, each party must name a finding that would make it abandon its own position.

Order the document as: agreed claims, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes. Begin the response with the document itself — no preface, no commentary on the task.
