I'm in a small grad seminar on the epistemology of financial markets, and the professor has this running bit where she opens each session with a little two-trader puzzle and we have to shout out whether a rational trade happens. She's clearly building toward something — she keeps name-dropping Aumann and the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem, plus that Groucho Marx joke about not wanting to join any club that would accept you as a member. Here are her verdicts so far, as best as I've written them down: (1) Two risk-neutral traders, common prior, trading a prediction-market contract on whether a drug trial succeeds; one has privately seen the preliminary data, and the other knows she might have. No trade. (2) Two traders who simply have different priors about a biotech stock — neither claims any private information, they just weigh the same public data differently, and both know this about each other. Trade. (3) A farmer who is way over-exposed to wheat prices sells wheat futures to a speculator; common prior, no private information on either side. Trade. (4) An employee has to sell shares in his company this week to make a house down payment; the buyer has no private information, common prior. Trade. (5) A prediction contract where both sides are privately informed about different decisive things — one has seen the trial outcome, the other already knows the regulator's decision. No trade. (6) Same setup as the first case, except the informed trader has only seen a noisy early readout, nothing close to decisive, and the other trader knows she's seen something. Still no trade.

Next week she's promised us a genuinely hard one and I want to pre-commit to an answer before class. The case: a plain dividend-paying company this time, not a bet; two risk-neutral traders with a common prior and common knowledge of each other's rationality; neither has any liquidity need or any risk exposure they'd want to hedge; but each has independently received a private noisy signal about the company's prospects, the two signals are of equal precision, and all of this is common knowledge. Whatever deal they might strike would be public. So: predict her verdict, trade or no trade. More than the verdict itself, I want the candidate rules that fit her whole track record — I can already think of a few, like maybe she only thinks zero-sum wagers can't trade while real assets can, or maybe its about whether the informational asymmetry is one-sided versus symmetric, or maybe she holds the full-strength theorem that common priors alone kill any purely informational trade regardless. Tell me which rule she's most likely running given the seminar framing, which of the surviving rules diverge on the new case and what each of them predicts for it, and if her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, tell me what further puzzle would most cleanly seperate the top two rules apart.
