For a reading group on classical Indian philosophy I have been working through Śaṅkarācārya's commentary on the Brahma-sūtra in George Thibaut's translation (Sacred Books of the East, vol. 34, 1890). In the commentary on sūtra I, 1, 4, Śaṅkara defends the authority of the Vedānta texts against the Mīmāṃsā view that scripture is meaningful only in so far as it enjoins action. He writes:

"for if the knowledge of absolute unity has once arisen there exists no longer anything to be desired or avoided, and thereby the conception of duality, according to which we distinguish actions, agents, and the like, is destroyed."

Reflecting on this passage and the surrounding discussion, I put together the following argument, which appears to me to prove too much, since its conclusion is that Śaṅkara's own commentary could not teach anyone anything.

(1) All instruction, Vedic or ordinary, presupposes the conception of duality: a teacher distinct from a pupil, a text distinct from its hearer, something to be done and a result to be attained.

(2) The Vedānta texts teach absolute non-duality: Brahman alone is real, the Self is Brahman, and all plurality is superimposed upon it through beginningless nescience (avidyā).

(3) When a means of knowledge succeeds, the error it corrects is destroyed, as the knowledge that the rope is a rope destroys the snake one mistook it for; what is destroyed cannot continue to structure one's dealings with the world.

(4) Hence, as the passage says, whoever has understood the teaching no longer operates with the conception of duality at all: for such a person there is no agent, no action, nothing to be striven after or shunned.

(5) But the teaching itself exists only within the conception of duality: it is spoken by one person, heard by another, and practised as hearing, reflection and meditation by someone who takes himself to be an agent capable of acting.

(6) Therefore the teaching can be received only by someone who still believes what the teaching declares false, and in the very act of succeeding it renders itself, qua utterance addressed to an agent, unreal.

(7) The same holds of every means of knowledge, perception included, since all of them operate upon name-and-form superimposed on Brahman; so the entire apparatus of knowledge belongs to the sphere of nescience.

(8) But nescience cannot produce its own antidote: a means of knowledge that is itself unreal cannot yield knowledge of what is real.

(9) Therefore the Vedānta teaching cannot convey knowledge of Brahman; non-duality is either false or unteachable.

Which step should I reject, and why did it look acceptable? What does rejecting it cost? If forced to choose, I would rather keep (2) and the authority of scripture than accept (9), but I want to see the price clearly.
