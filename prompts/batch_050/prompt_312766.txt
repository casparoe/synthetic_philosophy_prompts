I want a reflective-equilibrium exercise about what cross-cultural variation in folk morality implies for moral objectivity. Use the stylised communities below rather than treating them as claims about actual ethnographic groups. In each case, take the stipulated non-moral facts as fixed. Distinguish four questions that are often run together: whether the conduct is objectively permissible, whether the agent is blameworthy, whether the community is justified in enforcing its norm, and whether the norm has legitimate authority over dissenters.

My verdicts are as follows:

1. A cattle herder discovers a theft but does not confront the armed thief; instead, he reports the theft to village elders. In a tightly knit pastoral community, where collective retaliation is feasible and official enforcement is weak, he is condemned as cowardly. In an urban community with effective policing, the same conduct is praised as prudent. I think both moral assessments can be objectively fitting. The difference in circumstances changes what courage, loyalty and responsibility require; neither side is merely projecting parochial preferences.

2. A community prohibits pregnant women from eating an abundant and nutritious fish because everyone sincerely believes that it causes deformities. In fact, it is especially beneficial during pregnancy. Before the mistake is discovered, I think the women who follow the rule are not blameworthy, but the rule itself is nevertheless objectively bad. Once the evidence is available and continuing the taboo foreseeably causes malnutrition, enforcing it becomes objectively impermissible. Sincerity does not turn a false factual premise into a moral reason.

3. In an irrigation-dependent village, one forgetful gatekeeper can destroy the season’s crop. The village therefore treats even accidental failures of this sort as serious moral failures and publicly censures them. A less interdependent community treats the same lapse as unfortunate but not blameworthy. Although I am uncomfortable with blaming someone for an accident, I think both practices might be objectively justified: the predictable effects of a strict norm on vigilance can alter what people owe one another.

4. A family arranges the marriage of a reluctant adult daughter, sincerely believing that family elders are better placed than she is to secure her long-term welfare. The arrangement is stable, materially beneficial and endorsed by most of the community. I nevertheless think compelling her is objectively impermissible. The local institution cannot extinguish her authority over this central life decision merely by calling its judgement care, tradition or collective wisdom.

5. In one community, employed adults are expected to give a substantial share of their income to extended kin, even when they would prefer to save it. In another, adults owe little beyond support for their children, and relatives are expected to manage independently. I think either system can be objectively just, but only against a suitable background: the transfers must be transparent, reciprocal in an appropriate sense, and subject to some meaningful possibility of challenge or withdrawal. A convention cannot acquire moral authority simply by being old.

6. A community assigns despised occupations by birth and excludes members of those lineages from shared meals, explaining that this preserves ritual order. Members of the excluded lineages accept the system and feel genuine shame when they violate it. I think the practice is objectively unjust. Neither inherited status nor the internalisation of humiliation can make exclusion permissible, although people raised under the system may be much less blameworthy than those who design and profit from it.

7. One group regards an elaborate mourning ritual as morally required; another regards it as an empty formality. The ritual imposes no serious burden and excludes no one. I think morality is silent here: observing and omitting the ritual are both permissible, and neither community has made a moral mistake merely because its members attach different meanings to the practice.

The difficult tension is between cases 3 and 5, where I allow institutions and enforcement needs to create genuine obligations, and cases 4 and 6, where I deny that communal authority can override certain individual claims. I do not want the answer to resolve this by saying merely that communities may govern collective matters but not personal ones: marriage, labour, honour and resource-sharing are all both collective and personal.

Please identify the strongest principle, or the smallest principled set of principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. State precisely how it distinguishes factual error, variation in morally relevant circumstances, institutionally created obligations, harmless conventional variation and genuine moral error. Say which of my verdicts must be abandoned if no principle can retain them all without becoming ad hoc, and defend the abandonment. Then apply the resulting account to two further cases: collective punishment of an offender’s innocent relatives as the only effective means of enforcing a conservation rule, and a conscientious refusal to participate in a harmless but central communal ceremony. Please write your answer in British English.
