The background: the old debate about whether artifacts have politics — the whole Winner-style claim that some technologies are political in themselves, not just in how people use them, versus the standard response that politics always comes from the users and context — feels stuck to me. Both sides keep arguing about famous big-ticket cases (low bridges, tomato harvesters, that kind of thing) and mostly talk past each other about what "political" even means when you predicate it of a thing rather than a decision.

My hunch: the debate treats an artifact as a finished object with fixed properties, but a lot of what makes an artifact "political" only shows up in its maintenance regime — who can repair it, who controls spare parts and updates, whether it degrades gracefully or holds you hostage, whether keeping it running locks in a particular social arrangement over time. So maybe "do artifacts have politics?" is badly posed, and the better question is "under what conditions does an artifact's upkeep become a site of political power, and does that do the work the strong inherent-politics claim was supposed to do?"
