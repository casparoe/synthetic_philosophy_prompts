I advise a standing constitutional-reform commission in a mid-sized parliamentary democracy, and my small team of analysts screens every institutional-design proposal before it reaches the commissioners. The proposals recur in three families: replace the membership of some public body with people chosen by lot; impose or lengthen term limits on an office that currently lacks them; abolish or relax term limits that already exist. At present each is argued from first principles, and the same confusions repeat: 'the people should decide' is invoked simultaneously for elections and for sortition; a body's descriptive resemblance to the population is treated as proof that it will act in the population's interests; and everyone complains about career politicians without specifying which failure — entrenchment, capture, or mere unresponsiveness — a lottery or a term limit would actually repair.

Write me a reusable decision procedure that my analysts can run on any such proposal in about two working days, producing a three-page brief. It should operationalise whatever the serious literature on sortition, representation, and term limits establishes that survives translation into practice: what the electoral sanction of non-re-election actually buys and when it fails; what selection by lot secures (independence from party machines, descriptive representation, a selection process no one can capture) and what it forfeits (the accountability chain, the filtering effect of campaigning, the consent of citizens accustomed to voting); what term limits fix (incumbency advantage, entrenchment) and what they cost (lost expertise, power migrating to permanent staff, lobbyists and the executive, lame-duck final terms). The procedure should pose its questions in order, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, identify which features of an office are decisive — reversibility of its errors, the value of continuity and accumulated expertise, whether the main threat is capture at the selection stage or misbehaviour in office — and mark where it halts with a recommendation and where it must hand the question to the commissioners as one of values rather than institutional mechanics. Say explicitly which distinctions must survive the simplification (descriptive versus substantive representation; responsiveness versus accountability) and which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three cases: a reconstituted second chamber of the national legislature; lay panels approving major planning consents; and the central bank's monetary policy committee. At least one run should yield a recommendation most commissioners will dislike. Close with what the procedure cannot settle.
