In a comparative ethics discussion group I attend, we were debating whether moral education should primarily nurture tendencies already present in children or primarily impose restraint on them, and a fellow member closed the exchange by declaring that this is just the old dispute in which Mencius says human nature is good and Xunzi says human nature is bad — and that, since nature plainly contains both generosity and cruelty, both were wrong and the whole debate was a stalemate. I suspect the slogan is doing more and less than he thinks, but I could not articulate the options on the spot.

Please unpack that one-liner into the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, treating the Mencius side and the Xunzi side separately. On the Mencius side, distinguish at least: the claim that humans are born already virtuous; the claim that humans are born with incipient dispositions toward virtue that require cultivation rather than invention; the claim that the capacities making virtue possible are innate even if the dispositions need training; and a normative reading on which calling nature good merely sets the standard for what counts as healthy human development rather than describing infants. On the Xunzi side, distinguish at least: the claim that spontaneous human desires are actively vicious; the weaker claim that untutored desire reliably produces conflict given scarcity, so that nature is bad only in its consequences; the claim that nature is morally indifferent raw material and all goodness is artificial, the product of deliberate convention and training; and the methodological claim that we should define nature as precisely what education does not supply. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or plausibly false, and note which pairs of readings, one from each thinker, would make the two genuinely disagree rather than talk past each other by using nature and goodness in different senses.

Then address my groupmate's use of the slogan: which of these readings, if any, would his both-are-wrong verdict actually engage, and does the verdict survive once the cultivation-versus-invention reading is on the table? I would like the readings laid out as a labeled menu I can bring back to the group, with your verdicts marked, rather than an essay that commits to a single interpretation.
