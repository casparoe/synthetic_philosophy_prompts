A colleague of mine has spent years working through Dharmakīrti's epistemology and now pronounces, for any cognition I describe, whether it counts as a genuine means of knowledge (pramāṇa) on his system. Her verdicts so far:

1. Seeing a patch of blue, before any judgment about it is formed — yes.
2. Inferring fire on a distant hill from the smoke rising there — yes.
3. Concluding that there is no pot on the table because, looking carefully in good light, none is seen — yes.
4. Taking a scripture's word for the existence of an afterlife that no one could observe — no.
5. Remembering the face of yesterday's visitor — no.
6. One's own direct awareness of presently being in pain — yes.
7. Taking a rope for a snake in dim light — no.

She also insists, with Dharmakīrti, that everything real is momentary and that to be real just is to be capable of producing effects.

Now consider three new cases: (a) a second, identical glance at the same blue patch, an instant after the first; (b) inferring, from the fact that a pot is a produced thing, that it is impermanent — indeed momentary; (c) inferring rain tonight from ants moving their eggs, on the strength of a long-observed correlation with no known causal or essential connection between the two.

Predict her verdict on each of (a)–(c), and identify the general rule she is most plausibly applying, given that her seven verdicts are jointly consistent with at least two different rules — for instance, that a cognition qualifies only if it is non-belying, i.e., confirmed by successful action; that it qualifies only if it discloses something not known before; or that inference qualifies only if it rests on causal or essential pervasion rather than mere correlation. For each new case, say which candidate rules diverge on it and why your favored rule wins. Finally, describe one further case, not among the ten above, that would most decisively discriminate between the two rules you consider most plausible.
