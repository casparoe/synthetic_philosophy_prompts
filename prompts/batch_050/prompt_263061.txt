I'm not an academic, but I've been reading about forecasting and the so-called outside view, and I keep coming back to one case. In the mid-1980s almost no professional Sovietologists predicted that the USSR would cease to exist within a decade, and as far as I can tell almost nobody made the forecast by asking how long states like it usually last. Analyze the Soviet collapse as a test case for forecasting from historical base rates. First, do the mapping work the theory demands: which reference class should the late USSR have been placed in — great powers, multi-ethnic land empires, one-party communist states, something else — and roughly what would each choice have implied about its remaining lifespan? Second, the harder question: if the choice of reference class does nearly all the work, and several choices look defensible in hindsight, does this episode actually vindicate the outside view, or does it show that the method can be made to say anything after the fact? Third, say where the outside view would genuinely have helped and where it is structurally silent — in particular, whether the things insiders fixated on (the Afghan war, falling oil revenue, Gorbachev's reforms) are the sort of information a base rate quietly absorbs or the sort it simply cannot see. I'd rather have an honest account of where the method breaks than a defense of it.
