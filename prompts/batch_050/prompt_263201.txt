Two colleagues of mine fell into an argument at a reading group over Chapter 25 of Machiavelli's The Prince, the chapter on fortune, and specifically over whether Machiavelli's account leaves room for a stable human excellence at all. The exchange went as follows.

(1) Anna argued that the chapter's famous division — fortune governs roughly half of human affairs while the other half is left to us — together with the image of the flooding river that can be contained by dikes and levees built in calm weather, shows that Machiavelli treats virtù as a genuine and durable capacity: foresight, discipline, preparation. On her reading, fortune is an external force that can be largely neutralized in advance, so the share of events we control can grow with better dikes.

(2) Ben replied that this reading collapses against the chapter's own examples. Machiavelli says that men prosper when their way of proceeding matches the character of the times — the impetuous man thrives in times that reward impetuosity, the cautious man in times that reward caution — and he explicitly denies that men can change their natures or be talked out of habits that have so far served them well. If virtù is really just a fixed temperament that happens to fit the moment, then success is fortune all the way down: the dikes only hold if you were lucky enough to be born with the disposition the era happens to call for.

(3) Anna answered that Ben has merely relocated virtù, not eliminated it. Even granting that temperaments are fixed, the wise ruler discerns which game is being played and presses his own temperament at the right moment and scale. Machiavelli's praise of Cesare Borgia — whose arrangements were so thorough that only an unforeseeable coincidence of events undid him — shows that Machiavelli still distinguishes sharply between agents who prepared well and agents who merely got lucky, which only makes sense if preparation is a real, assessable excellence.

(4) Ben countered that the Cesare case cuts the other way. Machiavelli's own verdict is that Cesare's ruin came from fortune in the form of events no preparation could have foreseen, which concedes exactly Ben's point that everything depends on luck at the decisive margin. Moreover, if discerning which game is being played were itself a learnable virtù, Machiavelli would not insist that a man who has prospered by one method cannot be persuaded to abandon it; and the chapter's closing preference for impetuosity over caution is advice about odds, not about excellence — it is a recommendation to bet on the temperament fortune statistically rewards, which presupposes rather than refutes the luck-dependence of success.

Please adjudicate this dispute move by move. For each of the four moves, say whether it is clearly right, roughly right, roughly mistaken, or clearly mistaken, and justify each rating briefly in terms of fidelity to Machiavelli's text and soundness of reasoning. Then give an overall verdict on which party is closer to Machiavelli's actual position on the relation between virtù and fortune, and on whether his view can sustain the notion of a stable excellence. Finally, say what would change your mind: identify the textual consideration or interpretive argument that would lead you to a different overall verdict, and indicate where your own adjudication is most likely to have gone wrong.
