THE EXAM QUESTION (20 points): Hume holds both that beauty is not a quality in objects themselves but exists only in the mind that perceives them, and that there is nonetheless a standard of taste by which some aesthetic judgments can be judged genuinely better than others. Explain how these two commitments fit together. What is the standard of taste, who are the true judges and what qualifies them, and what work does their verdict do in Hume's account? Does Hume leave room for blameless aesthetic disagreement, and if so, does that concession undermine the objectivity he is aiming for? Support your reading with at least one example Hume himself uses.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER: Hume's essay defends two theses that look like they can't both be true. The first is sentimentalism: beauty is no quality in things themselves but exists entirely in the sentiment of the perceiver, so one person's genuine feeling of beauty cannot straightforwardly contradict another's. The second is that tastes are not all equal, and Hume's proof that a standard exists is that time has already delivered one. Works like Homer's have pleased readers across centuries and cultures; the durable agreement of mankind, winnowed by time, is what fixes what is genuinely beautiful, and this is what Hume means by the joint verdict of the true judges. Those judges are critics with delicacy of sentiment, long practice, experience of comparisons, freedom from prejudice, and good sense. Hume illustrates delicacy with the story of Sancho Panza's kinsmen, who taste the hogshead of wine and detect the iron key and the leather thong that turn out actually to be in it. Hume does admit two cases where even good judges diverge blamelessly: differences in age and temperament (the young prefer amorous works, the old philosophical ones), and differences in the manners and opinions of different nations. But these residuals are minor, and since good sense is the qualification that does the real work, in principle enough comparison and conversation would reconcile even them. One worry about the whole account is circularity: we identify the true judges by the correctness of their verdicts and the correctness of verdicts by the judges. Hume's best reply is that the qualifications are independently testable -- delicacy can be checked against trials like the wine-tasting, where the right answer is known in advance -- so the standard doesn't collapse into relativism.
