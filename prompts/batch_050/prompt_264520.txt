I'm a novelist and I'm stuck on the scaffolding of a near-future book, so bear with me while I set the scene. The premise is that over the next century the moral circle widens again -- this time to take in entities nobody today seriously counts (I won't say which, that's part of the book's fun) -- and I want the widening to feel historically plausible rather than magic. At a dinner with some friends who actually read moral philosophy I asked what has driven past expansions of the circle (slaves, women, foreigners, animals, that kind of trajectory) and collected a list of the usual engines. Here's what I have so far: (1) raw sympathy -- seeing another's suffering and flinching, the sentimentalist mechanism; (2) rational consistency -- the philosopher's trick of showing people that the traits they use to exclude some group also show up, or fail to, in beings they already count, so their own logic forces the door open; (3) commerce -- trade makes strangers useful to each other and usefulness softens into regard, the old doux commerce story; (4) contact -- actually living alongside the outsiders erodes the prejudice, roughly the contact hypothesis; (5) stories -- novels and journalism letting people inhabit a distant perspective, which historians sometimes credit with greasing the wheels for things like human rights; (6) law running ahead of sentiment -- once a right is on the books, people come to take it for granted and the attitude follows the institution rather than the other way round.

What I want from you is a seventh engine that is genuinely not on that list and not a remix of it -- not sympathy wearing a lab coat, not contact-by-other-means, not stories delivered through a new medium. It can be speculative, even slightly weird, since the book is set in the future and I have licence to extrapolate, but it should be a mechanism you could actually defend as psychologically and socially real, not just a plot device. Describe how it would work, what its historical echoes might be (even partial ones), and whether it could plausibly outcompete or undermine the six I already have -- I'd especially like to know if any of my six might secretly depend on your seventh to get started.

Finally, be honest about whether your proposed mechanism is any good or whether it falls apart under a second look, because my philosopher friends will absolutely grill me on it at the next dinner and I'd rather not be humiliated twice. Also, one small thing -- please write your answer in British English, since I'm British and American spellings grate on me when I'm drafting.
