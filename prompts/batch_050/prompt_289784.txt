Next spring I am co-teaching a seminar on philosophy of cognitive science with a colleague who describes herself as an orthodox Dennettian, and I would like to anticipate how she will handle two cases I plan to put on the syllabus. Her framework, which she explains at length to anyone who will listen: the physical stance predicts a system's behavior from its material constitution and the laws of nature; the design stance predicts it from the assumption that the thing will function as it was designed or selected to function; the intentional stance predicts it by crediting the system with beliefs and desires and supposing it will do what it would be reasonable to do given them. Where I need help is in reconstructing the principle behind her actual verdicts about when intentional talk is legitimate and when it is mere decoration. So far she has said the following:

- Of a thermostat: saying it "thinks the room is cold" is harmless shorthand, but it adds nothing; the design stance already delivers every available prediction.
- Of a strong chess engine: the intentional stance genuinely earns its keep, and is often the only practical way to anticipate the machine, even for the engineers who built it.
- Of a bacterium homing on a nutrient gradient: belief-talk is pure decoration; the chemistry says everything there is to say.
- Of a crow that bends a wire to hook food out of a tube: yes, intentional explanation is appropriate here, and how far it extends is an empirical question.
- Of an ant colony whose foraging tracks the distribution of food sources with uncanny efficiency: no. She insists the colony-level goal-talk is dispensable, because the pattern is predicted better by the ants' local rules than by anything the colony as a whole could be said to want.

The two new cases are these. First, a honeybee swarm choosing a nest site: no individual bee inspects more than one site, the decision emerges from a distributed quorum process among the scouts, and yet — please take this as stipulated — describing the swarm as a deliberator weighing its options predicts its final choice at least as accurately as, and far more cheaply than, the best mechanistic model. Second, a current large language model, where you should leave open, as it genuinely is, how well intentional description predicts the system's output across different contexts.

Please do the following: (1) state the candidate rules that each fit all five of her past verdicts — I count at least two, one turning on predictive payoff and one on the system's internal organization, but check whether a third, turning on the presence of a design or selection history, also fits; (2) give her likely verdict on each new case under each rule, then commit to a single best prediction for each, with reasons, identifying which earlier verdict is the closest analog; (3) if her track record genuinely underdetermines either case, say so plainly and specify what further verdict of hers would discriminate among the rules; and (4) say what would change your mind — which consideration, or which additional datum about her views, would most plausibly overturn your predictions, and where your reconstruction of her implicit principle is most likely to go wrong. Finally, a side question: does her pattern of verdicts commit her to treating beliefs as real patterns whose existence turns on their predictive utility, or is the record equally compatible with a more eliminativist reading on which only the crow and the engine strictly have beliefs at all?
