I'm a law student in a combined evidence-and-philosophy seminar, and we've spent the last few weeks on statistical proof — the blue bus hypothetical, the gatecrasher problem, cold-hit DNA cases, the whole literature on whether liability can rest on numbers alone. For next week's session each of us has to circulate a short statement of where we've landed, and before I embarrass myself I'd like an audit of mine for internal consistency. Here are my commitments:

(a) The beyond-reasonable-doubt standard should not be reduced to a numerical probability in jury instructions, and I'm suspicious of any account that treats the standard as nothing but a high threshold, however high.

(b) In the blue bus case — the plaintiff is sideswiped by a bus and can prove only that a large majority (say 80%) of the buses operating on that route belonged to the Blue Bus Company, with no eyewitness, no driver log, nothing else — judgment for the defendant is correct. And I don't think the verdict flips even if the figure rises to 99%.

(c) Preponderance of the evidence in civil cases just means that the plaintiff's version is more likely than not — anything over 50% — and there is no further ingredient beyond the probability of the claim given the evidence.

(d) A cold-hit DNA database match with a random-match probability of one in tens of millions can, standing alone, be sufficient to convict beyond a reasonable doubt.

(e) The correct diagnosis of why bare statistical evidence fails is that it is insensitive: the evidence would have been exactly the same whether or not the proposition it supports is true, whereas good evidence tracks the fact.

What I want from you is a consistency check, not a survey of the field. Tell me which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other and which pairs only look like they do, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating everything as equally problematic. For each genuine tension, identify the cheapest revision that resolves it and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. One thing I suspect but haven't worked out: (e) seems to be doing a lot of load-bearing work in reconciling (b) with (d), and I'd like to know whether the whole package collapses if (e) is rejected, and whether (d) really does pass (e)'s own test — a match pulled from trawling a large database strikes some of my classmates as no better than the bus statistic, and I'm not sure they're wrong. Finally, please be careful to distinguish what is broadly agreed among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested: for instance, whether something like (e)'s tracking diagnosis, as opposed to rival explanations in terms of normic support or explanatory connection, is the right account of these cases is itself a live dispute, and I don't want a contested diagnosis presented to me as settled doctrine.
