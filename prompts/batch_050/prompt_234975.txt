I have been thinking about when a person's small contribution to a large collective harm is morally significant, and I have a set of case verdicts I am fairly confident about, but I cannot find a principle that generates all of them. I would like you to try. Here are the cases and my verdicts.

(1) My eating a chicken from the supermarket makes no perceptible difference to how many chickens are raised; the industry does not adjust production for one purchase. Still, I think buying the chicken is wrong, and not merely distasteful.

(2) A factory discharges pollutant into a lake. Each additional unit of discharge does nothing measurable until a threshold is reached, after which the fish die. The firm that releases the unit that crosses the threshold is clearly at fault. But I also think every earlier discharger did something wrong, even though, holding the others fixed, each could truthfully say the fish would have died anyway.

(3) Ten guards each fire one bullet at a condemned prisoner; any one bullet would have been lethal. I think each guard is fully a wrongdoer, not one-tenth of a wrongdoer.

(4) I refrain from voting in a large election because my single vote is practically certain not to be decisive. I think this is permissible -- burdensome actions whose expected effect rounds to zero are not obligatory.

(5) A company offers me a large sum to sign a petition I know will have no effect, since the decision has already been made in secret. Signing is harmless and changes nothing, yet taking the money to sign feels wrong to me in a way that merely taking money for nothing would not.

(6) A billion people each emit a trivial amount of a harmless-seeming gas, and the aggregate causes a catastrophe. I think each emitter bears some blame, though very little -- certainly less than any individual guard in (3).

The pair I struggle with is (1) and (4): in both, my act is causally inefficacious with respect to the outcome, yet I condemn the purchase and excuse the non-vote. I also struggle with (3) and (6), where the structure of overdetermination looks similar but my assessment of each agent's share differs wildly.

Is there a single principle -- or a small family of them -- that delivers all six verdicts? I have in mind candidates like expected marginal contribution, complicity or participation in a collective venture, and rules about what would happen if everyone did likewise, but each seems to fail at least one case. Please state your best candidate precisely, identify which clause of it each case motivates, and say honestly which of my verdicts, if any, it cannot accommodate and should therefore be revised. Finally, tell me what your principle predicts for a case I have not listed: a programmer whose individual commit to a large software system introduces no bug, but whose team's combined sloppy practices reliably do.
