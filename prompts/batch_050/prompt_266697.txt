I'm assembling the syllabus for a graduate seminar in philosophy of physics, and for the final paper assignment I want to force students past the textbook summary of Maxwell's demon that most of them absorbed as undergraduates. I'd like you to write the essay I have in mind as the model answer, pitched at the level of a strong term paper. The question: does the information-theoretic exorcism of the demon — the line of argument running from Szilárd through Landauer and Bennett, on which measurement and memory manipulation can in principle be made thermodynamically free, and the demon's bill comes due only when it erases a bit, at a cost of at least kT ln 2 of entropy — actually save the second law, or does it merely relocate the puzzle? The essay must engage seriously with the critique associated with Earman and Norton that the exorcism is circular: that one cannot establish a universal erasure cost, holding for all possible physical implementations of memory, without already assuming the very statistical second law the demon was supposed to threaten. Defenders of the exorcism think the critique overreaches; your essay should say precisely where, if it does.

But I don't want the essay to stop at refereeing that exchange, because there is a wrinkle I raised in seminar last year and never got a satisfying answer to. Imagine a demon equipped with an effectively inexhaustible supply of blank memory: a very long tape of registers all initialized to zero, long enough that within the lifetime of the gas cylinder, the laboratory, or indeed the civilization, the demon will never need to erase anything. If erasure is where the entropy bill comes due, this demon appears to produce a genuine, ongoing entropy decrease while deferring payment indefinitely — the debt is real but never collected. The standard reply is that the availability of blank tape is not free: usable, reliably-zeroed memory is itself a thermodynamic resource, ultimately financed by the low-entropy past of the universe, so someone paid in advance. Your essay should work out whether that reply is satisfactory. Does it show that the second law is genuinely safe, or does it concede that violations are always possible provided they are funded out of a pre-existing entropy surplus — which is arguably just what a local, temporary violation of a statistical law looks like? And what does the exchange reveal about whether information is doing real physical work in these arguments or is merely a bookkeeping device redescribing ordinary thermodynamic resources?

The essay must reach and defend a verdict: the Landauer-Bennett exorcism succeeds, or it fails, or it succeeds only under some stated condition (for instance, only relative to a background cosmological assumption about the past), and you should say which and why rather than hedging. At the same time, keep the epistemic bookkeeping clean. Distinguish what is genuinely settled among people who work on this topic — the theoretical and experimental standing of the erasure bound itself, for example — from what remains contested, such as whether the bound is independent of the second law and what ontological weight the notion of information can bear. Do not present a contested interpretive position as the consensus view, and where the literature itself is divided, say so explicitly.
