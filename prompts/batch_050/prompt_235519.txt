So I've been in a long-running reading group / comment-thread fight about Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, and it has that familiar LW-ish smell of two smart camps using one handle for different things and then being confused that nobody updates. I want a diagnosis of why it's stalled, not another round of who-wins.

The live question is roughly: does Smith's impartial spectator actually give moral judgment independent leverage over actual public opinion, or is it just an internalized average of the people around you?

Camp one, the social-mirror reading: for Smith, sympathy is imagination-based fellow-feeling, moral approval tracks what spectators would feel, and the impartial spectator is built up out of actual social feedback. So even when it feels like an inner tribunal, it's mostly a prediction of internalized approval, laundered through an idealization step. This camp thinks the spectator inherits the biases of the surrounding moral culture, can entrench status-quo and distance-based partiality, and is basically a conformity engine with good PR. In rationalist terms: it's an approval-prediction module that can be Goodharted, not a truth-tracking one.

Camp two, the corrective reading: the whole point of the spectator is that it's not actual observers. It's supposed to be informed, steady, impartial between near and far, corrected for the distortions of partiality, grief, resentment, and social proximity. Smith clearly wants cases where the crowd applauds the wrong things and conscience still condemns. So the spectator is a justificatory standpoint that can override the actual audience, not merely compress it.

There's also a quieter third position that the spectator is mainly a motivational device -- the man within the breast makes distant or abstract harms feel salient -- and both camps keep recruiting that as evidence for their epistemic story.

My current hypothesis is that this is partly a disguised disagreement between a causal-genealogical question (where do moral sentiments come from? social feedback, mostly) and a normative-justificatory question (what makes a moral verdict right? a standpoint that can in principle indict the society that produced it). But I might be letting a tidy distinction do too much work. It could also be bedrock: camp one thinks anything internalized from a flawed society can't transcend it, camp two thinks idealization can do real corrective work, and neither has an argument the other must accept.

What I want from you: diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is -- verbal, textual-interpretive, empirical about moral psychology, normative about what justification requires, or different questions under one name. Use what each side actually appeals to rather than a textbook survey. Tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction (actual vs idealized spectator?), a test that separates what the two readings predict about hard cases, a piece of moral psychology that would settle the genealogy, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the crux is brute intuition. If you think Smith himself is genuinely ambiguous or changed emphasis across the book, say that plainly rather than picking a team for him.

Two requests on how to answer. First, epistemics: clearly separate what's basically settled among people who know TMS well from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested reading as the scholarly consensus -- I'd rather have an honest low-confidence call than false convergence. Second, format: after the prose diagnosis, give me a JSON summary with exactly these fields: "diagnosis" (one of: verbal, textual, empirical, normative, different_questions, mixed), "real_crux" (one sentence), "what_each_side_is_doing" (object with keys "social_mirror", "corrective", "motivational", each one sentence), "progress_moves" (array of things that would actually shift the debate), "settled" (array), "contested" (array), and "confidence" (low, medium, or high). This definately needs the settled/contested split -- that's the part people keep fudging.
