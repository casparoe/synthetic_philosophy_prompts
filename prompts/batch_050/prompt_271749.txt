I am a novelist at work on a book whose central figure is a disgraced actuary, a man who ruined himself not at the tables but by trusting his own arithmetic where arithmetic should not have been trusted. Late in the novel he fills the last pages of his commonplace book with a single poem about the wager that undid him: a game in which a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads, paying two ducats if heads comes on the first toss, four if on the second, eight if on the third, doubling without end — so that the expected value of playing is infinite, though no sane person, he admits, would pay even fifty ducats for a seat at the game. I want this poem to be the thing itself, not merely to mention it, and I need your help writing it.

Here is the constraint I have settled on, and I want you to honor it strictly: the poem should have four stanzas of one, two, four, and eight lines respectively, so that the form doubles exactly as the payouts double, and the fact that the poem must end after fifteen lines — while the game's expectation does not — should be made to do the philosophical work. The final stanza should register, without lapsing into exposition, the gap between what the calculation commands (stake any fortune, since the expectation exceeds them all) and what the character's whole ruined life testifies. Render the mathematics accurately enough that a reader who knows the paradox will recognize it, but keep the language that of a broken man, not a textbook. Give the poem a title.

Please make definite choices rather than offering me variants: commit to a meter, a diction, and an ending, and stand behind them. After the poem, append a note of no more than three sentences stating plainly where you took liberties with the paradox or leaned on one contested reading of it (for instance, whether you treated the actuary's refusal as a point about diminishing marginal utility, about the impossibility of an infinitely deep bank, or as plain inarticulate dread). The poem itself must not exceed fifteen lines; I would rather have it too short than too long.
