I am a second-year undergraduate taking a course on collective action and rational choice, and I also sit on the nine-member allocations committee of my university's student activities fund. Under our constitution, any single member may unilaterally approve a 'micro-grant' of up to £150 from our shared pool; anything larger needs a vote at our monthly meeting. The arrangement was designed to cut bureaucracy, because event organisers usually need an answer within 48 hours in order to book rooms and speakers, and we handle two or three such requests a week. Over the past year, however, the fund has repeatedly paid for things I am fairly sure the committee would have voted down: a speaker who cancelled and kept the deposit, professionally printed flyers for an event that drew eleven people, a database subscription that nobody now uses. Each time, the approving member sincerely judged the grant worthwhile. Nobody is being careless or corrupt; something structural is going on.

In the course I came across a principle that I think explains it, sometimes called the unilateralist's curse. When an outcome requires only that at least one of several agents acts, and each agent acts whenever their private estimate of the action's value clears their own threshold, then the action is taken whenever the most optimistic estimate clears the bar. Even if every member's estimate is individually unbiased, the more members there are, the more likely the action goes ahead when its true value is negative. The principle says a rational agent should correct for this: the fact that you are the one acting is itself evidence that you are the most optimistic of nine, which is some evidence against acting. Failing that, you should defer to the group.

I would like you to turn this principle into a working procedure that our committee members — volunteers with no training in probability — could actually run before approving a micro-grant unilaterally. It should specify the steps in order: how a member should estimate their own chance of being mistaken; how to account for how many other members will realistically see the request before the 48-hour deadline; a correction rule for when no numbers are available; the conditions under which the procedure requires deferral to a vote despite the deadline pressure; and a separate rule for recurring items, since last year's approval is not evidence for this year's. Please mark honestly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say which distinctions in the principle must survive the simplification and which can safely be dropped.

Then run the procedure on three real cases from our log: (i) a £130 grant for a guest speaker, submitted by a society with a decent track record, which one member rates at about a 60 per cent chance of being money well spent; (ii) a £90 annual journal subscription that renews by default unless a member actively cancels it, which I mention because the curse seems to run in reverse here — nobody reads it, yet nobody cancels it, each assuming someone else uses it; (iii) a £150 grant to print an anthology of undergraduate philosophy essays, which I co-edited and very badly want funded. Please include at least one case where the procedure delivers an answer I will not like, and end by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do — for instance, where members disagree about what the fund is for rather than about facts, I assume the procedure has to hand the decision back to people.

Please answer in British English throughout. Give each of the three worked cases a one-line verdict, and end the whole response with a final line in the form \answer{...} containing the three verdicts in order, separated by commas, each drawn from the options: approve unilaterally, defer to a vote, decline.
