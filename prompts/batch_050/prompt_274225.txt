I serve on the steering committee of a small private fund that supports philosophy of biology, and this cycle we've decided to concentrate our money -- roughly two postdoctoral positions for three years -- on the old question of whether talk of genetic "information" and the genetic "code" is literal, metaphorical, or something in between. The committee is divided. Some members think the question was essentially settled decades ago one way or the other and that further work is scholastic; others think the biology has moved so far that the philosophical literature is arguing about a picture of the genome that working biologists no longer hold. Before we open the call, we need to decide which specific open questions within this area the money should chase, and in what order.

For context on where the debate stands as I understand it: one tradition argues that information in biology can only mean something like Shannon information -- mere correlation or causal covariation -- which is everywhere in nature and so makes DNA nothing special. A rival tradition tries to ground genuinely semantic content in evolutionary history, so that a gene "means" its protein roughly the way a signal means something, because of what it was selected for. Critics on the other side complain that the code-and-message picture systematically misleads: it exaggerates DNA's causal privilege relative to the cell's other machinery, treats development as the execution of a program rather than a distributed constructive process, and borrows the notion of a "reading" where no reader exists. Meanwhile defenders point out that the mapping from triplets to amino acids really does look arbitrary and symbolic in a way ordinary chemistry doesn't. On top of this, there are newer developments the older literature barely anticipated: machine learning systems that predict protein structure from sequence, synthetic biology that writes and boots up whole genomes, the expanding functional roles assigned to noncoding RNA and regulatory elements, and the routine clinical use of sequence data where talk of "what your genes say" carries real weight with patients. Which of these developments genuinely sharpen the philosophical questions, and which merely add noise, is itself unclear to us.

What I'd like from you is a defended agenda, not a survey of positions. Identify the open conceptual questions in this area that you think most deserve three years of philosophical attention, rank them, and justify the ranking in terms of explicit criteria: how much downstream work depends on each question, how tractable each is for philosophers as opposed to working biologists or computer scientists, and how neglected each is relative to its importance. Where the criteria conflict -- say, a question that is hugely consequential but likely intractable -- say how you weigh the trade-off. I'd also like you to name at least one question in this area that you think is overrated or likely to dissolve on inspection, and explain what you think people are confusing it with. Finally, for each of your top items, indicate what developments over the next few years -- experimental, computational, or philosophical -- would move it up or down your list, since we can redirect funding mid-cycle. Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long memo to the committee: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
