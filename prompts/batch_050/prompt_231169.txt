Last sunday my reading group finished part V of Spinoza's Ethics (we used the old Elwes translation) and the final hour turned into a fight between two regulars, Chris and Marta, over proposition 6. I stayed out of it, but now I want an outside verdict, because I honestly cant tell who won.

The passage they fought over, part V, prop. 6, plus the first sentence of its note:

"The mind has greater power over the emotions and is less subject thereto, in so far as it understands all things as necessary."

"The more this knowledge, that things are necessary, is applied to particular things, which we conceive more distinctly and vividly, the greater is the power of the mind over the emotions, as experience also testifies."

Chris's opening move: this proposition is the tell that the whole grand finale of the Ethics -- the intellectual love of God -- is really just a self-help technique. Understanding that things are necessary calms you down the way a nervous flyer calms down by learning the physics of turbulence; it is exposure therapy for the human condition, and when Spinoza later calls this state love of God he is putting incense around emotional regulation. The note gives him away: no genuine theorem needs to lean on "as experience also testifies."

Marta said he had read the "in so far as" backwards. The proposition is not advice, it's a theorem: it descends from part I's claim that everything follows from God's nature necessarily, and it describes what the mind is doing when it understands, not some trick we can perform on ourselves. And the love of God can't be a coping technique directed at an external thing, because God is not external to us and is not conceived as contingent -- the joy in question just is the understanding itself, with the idea of its cause built in, not a consolation prize you collect afterwards for stoically accepting fate.

Chris: then it's worse, not better. If everything is necessary, the "greater power" in the passage is decorative -- whether I come to understand necessity or stay confused was itself necessary, so part V's talk of a path to freedom is the determined congratulating themselves on their determination. And a proposition about what the mind necessarily does when it understands can't coherently come with a scholium urging us to go apply it to particular things more vividly, as if that were up to us. Either Spinoza is smuggling back the free will he abolished in part I, or the ethics of the Ethics is strictly impossible to follow, and love God is the weirdest possible name for noticing you never had a choice.

Marta's reply: freedom for Spinoza was never an exemption from necessity -- from part I onward, being free means acting from the necessity of your own nature instead of being pushed around by external causes, so "the determined congratulating themselves" just restates his definition as if it were an insult. And scholia are exactly where Spinoza addresses readers still stuck in the confused state; the imperative mood is pedagogy, not metaphysics. She did concede there's something genuinely odd about making freedom a matter of degree inside a geometric proof, but said the oddity is in the text itself, not in her reading of it.

So please go through the exchange move by move and say who is right about what. The things I most want sorted: (1) does the "in so far as" make the proposition weaker than Chris's therapy reading needs, or stronger than Marta's theorem reading wants? (2) does the note's appeal to experience actually undercut the deductive status, or is objecting to it a category mistake about what scholia are for? (3) the big one -- given that on Spinoza's view everything, including my coming to understand, is necessary, is the intellectual love of God best read as a consolation technique, as a description of what understanding already is, or is that a false dichotomy? And (4) is there anything important both of them are missing? Tell me who is more right overall, and definately don't split it down the middle just to be polite.
