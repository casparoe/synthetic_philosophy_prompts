I teach philosophy and have lately been thinking about proposals for making powerful AI systems checkable by decomposing hard questions into small pieces that a human overseer can verify one at a time, then recombining the verdicts. In working through this I've stumbled on a phenomenon I keep needing a word for, and I want to know whether the literature already has one. Here is the pattern, concretely. Ask a competent judge whether a long philosophical paper is any good and you get one verdict. Now take the same paper, split the evaluation into dozens of small questions a checker can answer in isolation -- is this premise plausible, does this inference follow, is this objection addressed -- and aggregate the answers, and you sometimes get a systematically different verdict. Crucially, the divergence is not noise: it tracks features of the decomposition itself. Carve the paper at the section boundaries and it passes; carve it at the level of individual sentences and it fails, because the paper's real weakness lives in how the sections hang together, a property no single piece exhibits and no aggregation rule I've tried recovers. The same thing happens when I assess a policy by having each affected department report only its own costs: every report is accurate, the sum is arithmetically correct, and the conclusion is wrong, because the decisive consideration was an interaction nobody was assigned to look at.

What interests me philosophically is that this doesn't fit neatly under the nearby concepts I know. It resembles the aggregation problems of social choice theory, but there the inputs are preferences of different people and the trouble is combining them fairly; here there is one judge, one object, and the trouble is that the act of dividing the object into assessable parts destroys or creates information. It is adjacent to gestalt phenomena -- the whole being more than the sum -- but gestalt talk usually just notes the existence of emergent properties, whereas my puzzle is normative: when the holistic verdict and the decomposed-and-aggregated verdict conflict, which one is the judgment, and on what grounds could we say the decomposition introduced an error rather than revealing one? And it is near to framing effects, but the divergent answer here is not a psychological glitch in the judge; it is produced by a fully explicit, individually scrupulous procedure. In the AI-safety context this matters enormously, because the whole hope of oversight-by-decomposition rests on the assumption that an answer assembled from verified parts inherits the reliability of its pieces, and this phenomenon is precisely where that inheritance fails -- not through any bad answer, but through the choice of where to cut and how to recombine.

So my question: does this configuration have an established name -- the systematic dependence of a verdict on the decomposition used to reach it, where every step of the decomposed procedure is individually correct? If it does, I want more than the label: I want to know how the phenomenon is carved up in that literature, what its boundary cases are (does it require emergent properties of the whole, or does any interaction effect qualify? does it count when the aggregation rule is fixed in advance versus chosen after seeing the parts?), and how it differs from the nearest neighbors I've gestured at. If nothing established fits, then please coin a term, define it carefully enough that I could classify new cases with it, and say something about which of the two verdicts -- holistic or decomposed -- has the better claim to authority, and whether that claim can ever be adjudicated without smuggling in a third, un-decomposed judgment.
