Two weeks ago my nine-year-old asked me over cereal how she knows her whole life isn't a dream, I mumbled something about pinching yourself, and now I've somehow volunteered to run a one-off session for her school's philosophy club (ages 9-11, one hour, nothing printed except what I read aloud). I want to do dream skepticism through Zhuangzi instead of Descartes, and the bit I want to anchor everything on is the dream passage near the end of chapter 2 of the Zhuangzi, the Qiwulun chapter -- NOT the butterfly itself, but the paragraph just before it where he says that while you're dreaming you don't know you're dreaming, that a great awakening might one day show this whole life was a great dream, that fools strut around certain they're awake, and then -- the move that broke my brain -- that he, the one telling you you're dreaming, is dreaming too. (I'm working from Legge's public domain translation if it matters.)

Here's my problem. The kids will show up fluent in Matrix: you can't prove you're awake, so maybe you can't know anything, spooky. But Zhuangzi doesn't sound spooked at all, he sounds cheerful, and by including himself in the dream he seems to kneecap the very argument the kids think he's making, and two sentences later he's onto the butterfly and his point about the transformation of things. So the passage seems to do the opposite of what they'll expect, and I don't know how to teach that without either flattening him into a skeptic or lying to ten-year-olds.

Don't give me the lesson, give me the plan, and commit -- pick one order of ideas and one carrying example (this passage, the butterfly, or some classroom stunt I haven't thought of) and tell me why your pick beats the obvious alternatives rather than handing me a menu. I assume I need "how would you prove you're awake right now?" genuinely live in the room before the passage can land, but argue me out of that if its wrong. Tell me the misconceptions to expect -- I'm guessing "Zhuangzi says life is just a dream," "he's basically Descartes in a robe," and "pinching settles it" -- and exactly where in the sequence each one gets defused, plus what I should openly leave out at this age. Last, one closing question that shows me whether they got the actual point (that he's oddly fine about the whole thing) and not just "you can't know anything."

Format: exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no introduction and no wrap-up.
