A colleague presented me with the following argument about behavioral criteria for understanding, and although I find the conclusion unacceptable, I cannot cleanly identify where the chain breaks. I would like a diagnosis.

(1) Every Turing-style test is finite. It consists of a bounded exchange of messages, so there are only finitely many transcripts of that length, and only finitely many exchanges any judge could actually conduct.

(2) For any such bounded exchange, one can in principle construct a playback machine: an enormous lookup tree that stores, for every possible sequence of the judge's messages, a prerecorded reply of the sort a thoughtful human might give.

(3) Such a machine would pass the test as well as any human could, since its observable contribution to the conversation would be indistinguishable, message by message, from that of someone who genuinely understands.

(4) Yet the playback machine understands nothing. It merely retrieves stored strings; nothing inside it is responsive to the meaning of what is said.

(5) Therefore, passing any finite behavioral test is not sufficient for understanding.

(6) The only evidence we ever possess for the understanding of other humans is, likewise, a finite record of their verbal and nonverbal behavior — longer than a test transcript, but finite all the same.

(7) Evidence that does not suffice for a conclusion cannot justify confidence in that conclusion.

(8) Therefore, behavioral evidence never justifies us in taking anyone — machine or human — to understand anything.

The conclusion strikes me as proving too much: it seems to land us in a skepticism about other minds that nobody actually lives with, reached purely by reasoning about machines. But each premise looks defensible in isolation, and my colleague insists the argument is valid.

Which step do you reject, and why did that step appear plausible in the first place? If more than one step is contestable, please rank them by what rejecting each would cost. For what it is worth, if I must sacrifice something, I would rather give up the sufficiency claim in (5) than abandon the commonsense position that I am justified in taking the people around me to understand me — but I want to know what that choice commits me to, and whether some other exit is cheaper.

Please give your answer in American English.
