Most wealthy countries now run a strange hybrid drug regime: alcohol and nicotine are sold openly, cannabis is legal or decriminalized in more and more places, Portugal-style systems treat personal possession of hard drugs as an administrative matter but keep dealing fully criminal, and cocaine and heroin stay banned outright. Meanwhile the same states that criminalize buying cocaine let you buy unlimited booze, gamble your savings away, and sign up for genuinely risky elective surgery. What picture of harm and autonomy does this patchwork actually presuppose? It doesn't look like Mill's harm principle, since self-regarding harms are sometimes criminalized and sometimes commercialized, and it isn't straightforward paternalism either, since the state happily lets you ruin yourself in plenty of other ways. Reconstruct the implicit theory if there is one -- is it something like the claim that some intoxications are legitimate ends and others are pathology, or that addiction voids consent, or that coercion is justified only against suppliers who exploit addicts? Or is the practice just historically frozen and philosophically underdetermined, compatible with several rival views at once? And say what would have to change in the alcohol regime if the implicit theory really is "protect people from exploitation," and what would have to change about punishing users if it's really "harm to others only."
