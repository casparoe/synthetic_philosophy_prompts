I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I serve as the graduate-student member of the screening committee for an interdisciplinary journal in my field. Each issue cycle brings roughly a dozen submissions claiming to have identified, or to have found new evidence for, a dedicated cognitive module — a module for face perception, for cheater detection, for grammar, for moral judgment, for number. My job is a first-pass assessment: within about forty minutes per paper, and without being a specialist in every subfield, I must decide whether the evidence offered supports the modularity claim as the authors actually make it, supports only a weaker claim than they advertise, or is equally compatible with a non-modular explanation. I pass a recommendation to the faculty editors, who make the real decision, but a sloppy screen wastes referee time and my credibility with them.

I would like you to turn the philosophy of modularity — Fodor's original account, with its criteria of informational encapsulation, domain specificity, mandatory operation, speed, fixed neural architecture, characteristic breakdown patterns, and characteristic development, together with the massive modularity hypothesis that followed — into a screening procedure I can actually run. Specifically:

1. Give me an ordered sequence of tests, beginning with claim reconstruction (what proprietary computation and what proprietary database is the proposed module said to have, and what information is it said to be sealed off from) and proceeding through encapsulation, dissociation evidence, domain specificity, and developmental and neural evidence. For each test, state what counts as passing, what counts as failing, and what counts as the evidence simply being silent — including what I should do when a paper reports no evidence of the relevant kind, for instance no developmental data at all.

2. The output of the procedure should be one of three verdicts: modular in the strict Fodorian sense; modular only in the weaker computational sense used by defenders of massive modularity, where domain-specific mechanisms need not be informationally encapsulated; or not established. Say explicitly which of Fodor's criteria your procedure treats as necessary and which it treats as merely typical, and justify that choice rather than assuming it, since the classification verdicts depend on it.

3. Distinguish clearly between single and double dissociations as evidence, and build in a check for the standing alternative explanations — general-purpose learning operating on a structured environment, and expertise — before any modularity verdict is returned.

4. Mark explicitly the steps where judgment cannot be eliminated. I expect at least two: how much demonstrated top-down influence is compatible with the kind of relative encapsulation that is still worth calling modularity, and whether a cortically recycled system, acquired in development rather than selected for, should count as a module at all.

5. Run the finished procedure on three cases and show the verdict and reasoning for each: (a) cheater-detection performance on the Wason selection task, where I suspect the procedure will return a verdict the evolutionary psychologists in my field will not like; (b) acquired prosopagnosia with spared object recognition, offered as dissociation evidence for face processing, where the expertise objection has to be confronted; (c) the visual word form area as a candidate acquired module, given that writing is too recent for dedicated selection to have produced a system for reading.

6. Be honest about where this procedure will misfire — what kinds of real cases it is likely to misclassify, and what it cannot settle no matter how carefully applied.

Finally, state how confident you are that the procedure preserves what actually matters in the theoretical debate rather than flattening it into a box-ticking exercise, and single out the one step of the procedure you are least confident in — the step most likely to misclassify a genuine case — together with what evidence or argument would lead you to revise it.
