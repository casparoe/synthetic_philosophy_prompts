I did my PhD in analytic metaethics (normative reasons, grounding, the usual suspects) but I've recently got sucked into classical Islamic theology via a lecture series and some encyclopaedia articles, and I keep trying to map the Mu'tazila–Ash'arite fight onto debates I actually know. Can you do the translation properly for me?

The debate as I understand it: the Mu'tazilites held that acts are good or bad in themselves, knowable by unaided reason, and that God, being wise, must command what's good — He can't lie, can't punish the innocent, and is in some sense bound by justice. The Ash'arites pushed back: good and evil are what God commands and forbids, full stop; reason can't discover moral truths before revelation; and nothing binds God, so if He punished the innocent that would be just, since justice just is whatever accords with His will.

So: restate both positions in my vocabulary — robust realism vs response-dependence, the two horns of the Euthyphro, grounding priority, buck-passing accounts of value, the normative/motivating reasons distinction, constitutivism if it fits anywhere. And is Ash'arism basically contemporary divine command theory (Adams, Quinn etc), or is it more radical given that they deny obligations apply to God at all?
