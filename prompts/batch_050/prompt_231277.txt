I cover science and medicine for a magazine, and I keep running into fights about what counts as a disease -- drug companies expanding categories, doctors resisting, patients wanting their thing recognized. I've been trying to figure out what I actually think by just going case by case. Here are my gut verdicts:

High blood pressure: disease, no question. Homosexuality: not a disease, and it never was, even in 1950 -- the medical establishment was just wrong, not wrong-for-their-time. Aging itself: not a disease, though it causes plenty. Menopause: not a disease. Mild nearsightedness: not a disease, just a variation, but severe vision loss is. Chronic low back pain with no identifiable lesion: disease, or at least a genuine medical condition. Alcoholism: disease. Shyness: not a disease. Social anxiety so bad you can't hold a job: disease. Jet lag: not a disease. Obesity: I wobble on this one but lean disease. Male pattern baldness: not a disease. Having the sickle-cell trait (one copy, no symptoms): not a disease. Pregnancy: obviously not.

The pair I can't reconcile: I want to say baldness is just a normal variation that doesn't harm you, so not a disease -- but by that logic mild nearsightedness and aging make sense... yet aging kills you and I still say it isn't a disease, while a harmless hormonal quirk like, say, losing your period from too much distance running, I'd actually call a medical problem. And alcoholism feels like a disease partly because calling it one seems to help people, which is a weirdly social reason that would have made homosexuality a disease back when calling it one was supposed to help too.

Is there a principled line under these verdicts, or am I just tracking stigma and insurance coverage? Give me the best principle (or two) that fits, tell me which verdicts I'd have to surrender, and say what it implies for the ones I dodged: chronic fatigue syndrome, caffeine dependence, and grief that won't lift after two years.
