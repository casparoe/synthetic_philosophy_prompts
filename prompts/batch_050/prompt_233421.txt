Ok so I'm in a second-year political science course and we've been doing the rational choice stuff on voting -- the Downs argument that since the chance of your one vote deciding a big election is basically zero, the expected payoff of voting can't beat the cost of doing it, so turnout shouldn't happen unless you add some kind of duty or satisfaction term into the equation (our prof wrote it as R = PB - C + D). Fine. But I tried stress-testing my own intuitions and they're a mess and I want to know what principle, if any, is underneath them.

Here are my gut verdicts:

1. My twelve-person seminar voting on whether to move the meeting to Fridays: obviously worth showing up and voting. My vote might genuinely matter.
2. Voting in the national election: also worth doing. I'd feel genuinely bad skipping it. But per the lecture math the P term is basically zero, so on the instrumental logic this should look exactly like a waste of an afternoon.
3. A faculty committee vote my roommate's mom sat on, 40ish people, where everyone already knew the outcome was going to be like 35-5: her skipping it seems completely fine to me, no guilt warranted.
4. Staying home from the national election because it was raining: this feels lazy and bad to me, worse than the committee case, even though the rain costs are real and the pivot chance is identical (zero).
5. Voting third party when the candidate has no chance at all: still worth it. It "says something." But then I notice my reason here has nothing to do with changing the outcome, so why doesn't that same reason cover the rainy-day case and excuse the rain-skippers as long as they, idk, post about the issue instead?
6. Voting in a municipal midterm for some comptroller race I know nothing about and don't care about: skipping feels totally fine. Same near-zero P as the national, same ballot box, and I feel zero guilt.

So the pair that breaks me is 2 vs 6. Any instrumental principle seems to force me to say skipping both is fine (which I won't accept for 2) or that both are required (which I won't accept for 6). And the "it expresses something" move handles 5 and maybe 2 but then I can't explain why the committee case (3) is skippable -- presumably her vote would have "expressed" something too.

So: is there one principle that gets all six, or at least five? If it needs to distinguish 2 from 6, what does the distinguishing -- stakes? audience? how close the polls say it is? -- and does that factor actually justify the difference or just track it? Tell me honestly which of my verdicts you think has to go, and what your principle predicts for two cases I haven't decided: (a) the state mails everyone a pre-filled ballot so voting costs nothing, does skipping become fine; (b) the night before the election, polling makes clear my preferred candidate will win by 20 points -- am I off the hook?
