Right, a bit of background first. I spent the best part of forty years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, and since retiring I've been catching up on all the philosophy I skipped as a student. One thing that has genuinely thrown me: back in the day I learnt Noether's theorem as a gorgeous bit of maths — every continous symmetry of the action comes with a conserved quantity, time-translation invariance gives you energy conservation, spatial translation gives you momentum, rotations give you angular momentum — and it never once occured to me to ask which side of that correspondence was doing the explaining. Now I find philosophers of physics have been fighting over exactly that, and my reading group has fallen into two camps I'd like to force into some kind of constructive document.

The first member (call her symmetry-first) thinks the symmetries are the deep facts: energy is conserved because the laws don't change over time, and symmetry principles are a kind of meta-law constraining what the first-order laws could even be, so the direction of explanation runs from symmetry to conservation law. The second (laws-first) thinks that's exactly backwards: Noether's theorem is a mathematical equivalence internal to Lagrangian mechanics, it runs in both directions, and a symmetry is only ever as real as the actual laws that instantiate it — so if anything explains anything, it's the dynamical laws explaining the symmetry, and talk of meta-laws is metaphysical window dressing. Write the joint statement these two should be able to sign after an honest adversarial collaboration: first a numbered list of claims both can put their names to (what Noether's first theorem actually establishes, what assumptions it needs, what it stays silent about), then the residual disagreements, each phrased in wording that side would itself accept, then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would genuinely move at least one of them.

For each crux I want a concrete case or argument that both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance, and I'd also like you to flag any part of the quarrel that strikes you as merely verbal. Some candidate cruxes they keep circling: whether explanation can run at all between mathematically equivalent claims and, if so, what fixes the direction; what to make of dissipative systems with no Lagrangian, where the theorem doesn't directly get a grip; whether gauge symmetries — which plenty of people regard as surplus mathematical structure rather than real physical symmetry — poison the symmetry-first view or are just a special case; and spontaneous symmetry breaking, where the laws have a symmetry that actual states lack, which seems to matter for which symmetries are even candidates to do explaining. If you privately think one camp has the better of it, keep that to yourself — I'm after a document both of them would sign, not a verdict.
