I sit on the licensing board for real estate agents in my state, and we've got a disciplinary case coming up that has split the panel down the middle, so I need to draft the opinion we'll vote on at next month's meeting. Here's the situation. A buyer's agent asked the listing agent directly, in an email that's now exhibit A, whether the basement of the property had ever flooded. The listing agent wrote back, "The current owners have had zero water issues in the nine years they've lived there." That's literally true. It's also true, and the agent knew it, that the basement flooded badly twice under the previous owners, including once during a storm that hit the whole street. The buyers found out after closing when a neighbor mentioned sandbags, and they've filed a complaint alleging professional dishonesty. The problem is our code of conduct. It prohibits "knowingly making a false or inaccurate statement of material fact to any party in a transaction," and says nothing else about deception. Nobody disputes the facts; the fight is entirely about whether the code reaches this.

One camp on the panel says the answer is obviously no: the statement is true, the code says "false statement," and if we sanction someone for a true statement we've basically rewritten the rule by fiat, which isn't fair to members who relied on its wording, and besides, buyers' agents have their own duty to dig. The other camp says this is pedantry, that the whole point of the clause is to stop agents from deceiving buyers, and that a rule you can evade by choosing your words carefully protects nobody. Underneath that, though, there's a real conceptual disagreement I want the opinion to engage rather than paper over. Some of us think misleading someone with a literally true statement is actually worse than lying in at least one respect, because it tries to keep the benefit of dishonesty while keeping clean hands, almost exploiting the hearer's trust in the practice of telling the truth. Others think that's backwards, that lying is worse because it's a more direct violation, and that misleading at least leaves the hearer a chance and keeps the norm of literal truth intact, so even if both are sanctionable the penalty should differ.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate beforehand, maybe 600 to 900 words. It needs a clear bottom line on whether the conduct falls under the code as written, plus what the board should do about the gap (sanction, dismiss, refer for a code amendment, or some combination), and it has to give the strongest version of the losing side rather than strawmanning it, including the part about fairness to members who read the code literally. I'd also like it to take a position, with reasons, on whether any lying-versus-misleading distinction should affect the severity of sanction even if it doesn't affect liability, since that question will definitely come up on the floor. Write it as continuous prose, like a memo in letter form -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold text or tables, just paragraphs.
