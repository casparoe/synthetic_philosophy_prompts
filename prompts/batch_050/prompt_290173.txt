A guy in my reading group has been running this arguement all semester and I can't find the break, though the conclusion has to be wrong.

Setup we all accept: the standard Frankfurt case. Black has a device that will force Jones to shoot Smith if Jones shows any sign of deciding not to. Jones deliberates, decides on his own, and shoots; the device never does anything. Most of us in the group accept Jones is blameworthy even though he couldn't have done otherwise.

Now the argument:

(1) Jones is blameworthy despite lacking alternatives, because the actual sequence -- his own deliberation and decision -- is exactly what it would have been with no Black in the picture, and Black's readiness plays no causal role in what Jones does.

(2) So blameworthiness depends only on the actual causal sequence leading to the act, never on what would have happened in scenarios that didn't occur.

(3) Now take a bank teller who hands over the cash at gunpoint. The gunman never has to fire -- she complies -- so by (2) the gun is just like Black's device: a counterfactual arrangement that never enters the actual sequence. Her handing over the money is her own deliberated act, done for her own reasons (she wanted to live), so she's blameworthy for it -- an accomplice in the robbery.

(4) Same for every case of duress or coercion: if the victim complies, the threatened harm never materializes, so the threat stayed causally inert and the excuse fails; if the victim refuses, no excuse was ever needed. Either way duress never excuses.

(5) Generalizing: no external circumstance ever excuses anyone -- threats, blackmail, coercion, desperate need -- because in every case where the person actually acts, the forcing condition remained in the background and never touched the causal sequence.

(6) But this is absurd. The teller isn't an accomplice, hostages who comply aren't collaborators, duress is a genuine defense.

(7) My friend says (7) just means we have to give up the Frankfurt verdict in (1) and admit the principle of alternative possibilities survives after all -- or else swallow (5). He insists every step after (1) only reuses logic we already granted in the Frankfurt case.

So: where does it break? If I have to choose I'd rather keep the Frankfurt verdict than accept (5), but I want to know exactly what rejecting the generalization costs, and whether whatever blocks the step to (3) ends up destabilizing the standard reading of the original Frankfurt case too.
