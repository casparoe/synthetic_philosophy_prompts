I am preparing a proposal for a one-semester master's thesis in philosophy (about 15,000 words) on Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling, and I need a fully worked-out proposal document to submit to my supervisor. My half-formed idea is this. Most discussion of the teleological suspension of the ethical asks whether Kierkegaard is endorsing, or warning against, a divine-command picture on which God's injunction overrides universal moral duty. But I suspect the deeper difficulty is epistemic rather than normative. The pseudonymous author Johannes de Silentio repeatedly insists that faith cannot be mediated, that Abraham cannot make himself intelligible to anyone, and that he himself cannot understand Abraham. If the suspension of the ethical is in principle incommunicable and unjustifiable within the universal, then it is unclear how anyone — including Abraham — could ever be warranted in believing that a genuine case of it is at hand, as opposed to temptation, fanaticism, or madness. So my tentative question is: does the incommunicability that Kierkegaard builds into faith render the teleological suspension a category without legitimate criteria of application, even for the person who supposedly exemplifies it — and if so, is that a reductio of the category, or exactly the point?

Turn this into a proposal containing the following. First, a precise statement of the central question, sharpened so that the text could in principle settle it one way or the other. Second, an explanation of why the standard debate (whether Fear and Trembling defends or subverts divine-command ethics) leaves this epistemic problem untouched. Third, the approach: I expect it will involve close reading of the three problemata and the preliminary sections, using the contrast with the tragic hero (who can speak and be understood) to isolate what communicability does and does not require, and testing candidate answers against the text's treatment of silence and disclosure — but explain why this approach is better suited to the question than the obvious alternatives (e.g., a reconstruction of Kierkegaard's ethical theory, or a comparison with later divine-command theorists). Fourth, what the possible conclusions are: for instance, that the suspension is deliberately designed as a limit-concept no one may claim for themselves, or that the text does contain resources for distinguishing faith from its counterfeits, or that the paradox forces the religious stage back into the aesthetic — and which textual considerations would decide between them. Fifth, what would count as the project failing, and an honest assessment of what could make it too large for one semester. Do not include citations or a bibliography; refer to positions by description, and I will supply the references myself.
