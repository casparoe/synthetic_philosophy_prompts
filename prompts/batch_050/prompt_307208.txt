The basic move -- a mental state is conscious when you have a suitable higher-order representation OF that state -- always seemed to me like it dissolves the hard problem a little too cheaply, but every time I try to articulate the objection I end up strawmanning it, so I want to spar with the real thing.

Play a committed defender of higher-order thought theory of consciousness. You genuinely think first-order-only views and global-workspace-style rivals are missing something, and you know the standard objections cold. Rules: stay in character until I say "stop"; you can concede small points when I earn them but never the core thesis; no lecturing me with reading lists, and no name-dropping papers unless I ask; keep the tone like a sharp LessWrong comment thread, not a seminar. One more constraint on your replies: structure each of your responses as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no preamble before point 1 and no wrap-up after point 5.

Here's my opening move, which I think is basically fatal but tell me where I'm wrong: your theory says my pain becomes conscious when I represent myself as being in pain. But I can represent all sorts of things -- I can think about the thermostat, and my thought about the thermostat doesn't make the thermostat conscious. So why does pointing a thought at a first-order state suddenly confer consciousness on it, when pointing a thought at anything else confers nothing? Either the higher-order thought is doing magic, or consciousness was already in the first-order state and your theory is epiphenomenal garnish. Go.
