Two colleagues of mine — Maya, a machine learning engineer, and Daniel, who works on policy — have been arguing for weeks about what the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness actually show, and they have asked me to settle it. I cannot, so I am passing it to you. Here is their exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Maya: When base rates differ between groups, no risk score can simultaneously be calibrated within each group and produce equal false positive and false negative rates across groups. This is a theorem, not an opinion. So when a newspaper reports that some tool is biased because its false positive rate is higher for one group, it is making a mathematical error: the tool could be perfectly calibrated — its scores could mean exactly what they claim to mean for everyone — and the headline would still be true. A large share of public outrage about algorithmic bias is just the impossibility theorem wearing a trench coat.

(2) Daniel: That is a motte and bailey. Calibration is a weak condition, and it is cheap: a lazy model that outputs nothing but each group's base rate is perfectly calibrated while being useless and discriminatory in effect. So the theorem does not vindicate any actual tool — it merely shows that one particularly undemanding notion of fairness is compatible with unequal error rates. The interesting question is which fairness criterion deserves priority, and that is a moral and political question. Embedding one answer to it in the math and then announcing that the math has spoken is not rigor; it is evasion.

(3) Maya: You have it backwards. The theorems are symmetric: they cut against your preferred error-rate criterion just as much as against calibration. Once you grant that, you have granted my real point — that no tool can be shown to be unfair simpliciter, only unfair relative to a chosen criterion. That is a disciplining result, not an evasive one. It forces the accuser to say explicitly which criterion they take to be morally correct, and to argue for it, instead of treating "bias" as a self-certifying technical finding. If anything, the math is the only thing in this debate that forces anyone to be honest.

(4) Daniel: Partially granted — but notice that the same symmetry also means no vendor can claim fairness simpliciter, which is not how these tools are marketed. More importantly, you are still inside the frame the theorem gives you. Calibration, error-rate parity, predictive parity: all of these are properties of a score, conditional on a fixed population, a fixed outcome definition, and a fixed decision pipeline. The deepest unfairness usually lives upstream — in whether the recorded outcome (say, arrests) even measures the thing of interest (say, offending), in who enters the dataset at all, and in what the score is used to do. The impossibility theorems are completely silent about all of that. So a tool could satisfy whichever criterion you crown, be certified fair by your lights, and sit at the center of an indefensible practice.

Please adjudicate. For each of the four moves, state whether it succeeds outright, succeeds only in part, or fails, and justify your assessment of the specific argumentative step it makes — not of the speaker's general outlook. Then give an overall verdict: who is more right, about what, and whether there is a point in this dispute that both parties are missing.
