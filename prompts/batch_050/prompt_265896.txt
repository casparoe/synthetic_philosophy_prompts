Me and my buddy got into it last weekend over the fine-tuning argument and realized we actually disagree about something more basic: when does a freakishly improbable outcome call for an explanation at all? So here are my gut verdicts on a pile of cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, is generating them. My cousin wins the state lottery at one in three hundred million odds: zero explanation needed, somebody had to win. Same cousin wins again two years later: now I want an investigation. I write down the exact order of a shuffled deck in advance, seal it in an envelope, and the deal matches all 52 cards: this absolutly demands an explanation, even though that particular order is exactly as improbable as any other order, and any other order wouldn't have raised an eyebrow. I'm in front of a firing squad of forty expert marksmen, they all fire, they all miss: demands an explanation (they were ordered to miss, blanks, something) -- even though, I've noticed, if they hadn't missed I wouldn't be around to be puzzled, which is exactly the situation with the constants. If I learn there were actually forty million lottery draws last year, my cousin's two wins stop being suspicious. But if I just walk past a casino and happen to see a stranger roll three sixes, I don't take that as evidence the casino has secretly been running millions of rolls all night. And then the actual case: the constants of physics landing in the tiny life-permitting window. My gut says this is like the firing squad and demands an explanation (multiverse, design, whatever), but I also couldn't be around to be surprised in any universe where they didn't, which makes it feel like the lottery. I'm stuck.

So what's the principle? Give me one, or a small set, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause each case is motivating, that reproduces as many of my verdicts as possible. Tell me what it ends up saying about fine-tuning and whether my torn verdict there has to resolve one way or can honestly stay torn. If no simple principle gets everything, tell me which of my verdicts is the weakest and has to go, and why that one rather than another. And say what the resulting account predicts for a case I didn't list -- like a party guest who correctly guesses my birthday on the first try, or whatever case you think best shows off what the principle really commits me to.
