I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas desk, working on a long piece about whether philosophy's armchair survives the trip abroad, and I want an actual number out of you, not a survey of the debate.

Here's the proposition I want your credence on: by 2040, mainstream analytic philosophy will have meaningfully changed how it uses thought experiments -- treating 'the' intuitive verdict on cases like Gettier, Kripke's Gödel/Schmidt scenario or trolley dilemmas as evidence only after asking whose intuition it is -- specifically because cross-cultural experimental work showed the relevant judgements vary across populations in ways that matter. Take 'meaningfully changed' to mean something concrete: top generalist journals and standard textbooks routinely flag cultural-demographic caveats when an argument leans on an intuition, the way psych papers now flag WEIRD samples.

Decompose it for me. Roughly the terms I have in mind, but recarve them if you think I've sliced it wrong:

1. The empirical term: your probability that careful, properly translated, adequately powered studies would find robust, non-trivial cross-population differences in at least some of the core cases philosophers lean on. Weigh up the actual track record as you know it: the early-2000s studies suggesting East Asian and Western students split on Gettier-style cases and on the Gödel/Schmidt reference case, versus the later big multi-country efforts that (as I understand it) found the Gettier verdict remarkably stable and the Knobe side-effect effect replicating pretty much everywhere. Its a messy record, so tell me how you weight it.

2. The philosophical term: conditional on such differences existing somewhere, the probability that they genuinely undermine the method rather than being absorbed -- philosophers are very good at saying intuitions are defeasible evidence and carrying on, or at narrowing the relevant population to 'competent reflective judges' who happen to be Western philosophers.

3. The sociological term: conditional on a real methodological problem, the probability the field changes its actual practice by 2040, as opposed to citing the critique in a footnote and proceeding exactly as before. Psychology needed a decade of replication crisis to budge, and philosophy has no equivalent forcing mechanism.

Give me a probability and one line of justification for each term, say how you're combining them and why that combination rule is right (do you treat the terms as independent? they don't feel independent to me), then the final number. Then tell me which term your number is most sensitive to, and what specific study, argument or event would shift it most.

For calibration: my editor has this at under 10%, on the grounds that philosophy metabolises every external critique without changing. I'm nearer 40%, mostly because I think term 1 is less damning for the armchair than the early headlines suggested. Tell me where our disagreement most likely lives.

No bibliography or reading list, please -- reason from what you know. And write it in British English; my subs will only change it otherwise.
