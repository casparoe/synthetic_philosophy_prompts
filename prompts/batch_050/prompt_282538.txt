Last month a close friend of mine received a terminal diagnosis, and in the long conversations we have had since, I have become preoccupied with Lucretius's symmetry argument: the stretch of nonexistence after death is no different in kind from the stretch of nonexistence before birth, and since the latter troubles no one, dreading the former is irrational. The usual rebuttal — that we simply care more about the future than about the past — strikes me as a confession of the asymmetry rather than a justification of it. I want to know whether the asymmetry can be defended on some other basis, so I would like a case built to order.

Construct a thought experiment in which a single individual rationally minds her future nonexistence while not minding her past nonexistence, and in which the asymmetry falls out of some feature of her situation other than a brute preference for the future over the past. Constraints: the protagonist must be psychologically ordinary (no foreknowledge of her own death, no time travel, no stipulated afterlife); the scenario must fit in one paragraph; and what she minds must be nonexistence itself — not the manner of dying, not anticipated pain, and not what her death would do to other people.

After presenting the scenario: (a) identify exactly which feature of the case breaks the symmetry, and explain why that feature cannot be run in the reverse, past-directed direction; (b) say whether the case, if it succeeds, vindicates the view that death is bad because it deprives the person of future goods, or instead shows that this deprivationist view is dispensable; (c) describe what happens to the asymmetry if the case is modified to remove the working feature — in particular, whether it collapses back into one where only a temporal bias could justify the differing attitudes; and (d) give the strongest objection a defender of Lucretius would raise against the construction, and say whether it succeeds.
