You are a professional philosopher specializing in bioethics, with particular expertise in reproductive ethics and the ethics of enhancement. Work through this carefully and methodically; I want a structured analysis, not a verdict reached in the first paragraph.

In debates about embryo selection via polygenic screening and, prospectively, germline gene editing, I keep encountering an argument built on an analogy with ordinary parental investment. Proponents of what is sometimes called liberal eugenics or the principle of procreative beneficence put it roughly like this. Nobody thinks it is wrong -- most think it admirable -- for parents to spend heavily on tutoring, music lessons, language immersion, and good schools to improve their children's cognitive capacities and life prospects. Selecting among embryos for favorable genetic profiles, or editing an embryo toward the same end, is simply doing the same thing earlier and more efficiently. The intention is identical: giving one's child the best possible start in life. Only the delivery mechanism differs, and a bare difference in mechanism is not a moral difference. Hence, if enrichment is permissible (perhaps even obligatory), selection is permissible (perhaps even obligatory) too.

I want you to audit this analogy rather than simply accept or reject it.

First, brainstorm, in a compact list, every structural feature the two practices share and every feature in which they differ, without yet judging which differences matter. Then construct an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role, on the selection side, of the tutor, the tuition fees, the child's improved capacities, the child's consent or resistance, the competing children, and the parent's motive.

Second, identify where the mapping breaks. Consider at least the following candidate fault lines, but do not limit yourself to them: (a) in enrichment there is a fixed, identifiable child who is benefited or burdened, whereas selection determines which child comes to exist at all, leaving it unclear who the beneficiary of the best start is and whether the unchosen embryos count as losers; (b) tutoring can be quit, resisted, or outgrown by the child, while a genomic endowment cannot; (c) some of the traits at stake confer advantage partly by being scarce, so that universal success at enhancement would be self-defeating in a way that may or may not also hold for education; (d) choosing against an embryo bearing a given trait arguably expresses a judgment about the worth of existing people who bear it, in a way that declining a violin teacher does not; (e) enrichment operates on a person already embedded in a relationship with the parent, whereas selection operates on a range of possible persons before any relationship exists.

Third, for each disanalogy you find, say whether it actually undermines the analogy's intended conclusion -- that selection inherits the moral status of enrichment -- or is merely cosmetic. Note also that disanalogies can cut both ways: some make selection look worse than enrichment (no possibility of the child renegotiating the choice), others arguably better (no exhausted afternoons, no instrumentalized childhood). Sort each onto the correct side of the ledger.

Finally, state what survives. Does the audited analogy still support a presumption of permissibility for selecting uncontroversially beneficial traits, such as disease resistance? Does it support the stronger claim of an obligation to select? And if the enrichment analogy cannot carry the strong conclusion, is there a better vehicle available to the proponent -- for instance, an analogy to parents choosing when to conceive, which likewise determines the identity of the resulting child and which virtually nobody regards as objectionable?

I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of named positions; reason from the structure of the cases themselves, and be explicit about any place where you are genuinely uncertain rather than papering over it.
