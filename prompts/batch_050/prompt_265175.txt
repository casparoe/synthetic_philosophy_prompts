I teach a college seminar on aesthetics and the philosophy of fiction, and every year the unit on imaginative resistance goes the same way: I present the puzzle, students split into those who find it obvious and those who find it nonexistent, and the discussion dissolves because we never agreed on what a solution would even look like. Next semester I want to front-load the standards instead of the theories. So before I assign anything, I'd like a specification: the desiderata a theory of imaginative resistance must meet to count as an adequate account of the phenomenon, whatever the phenomenon turns out to be.

Please give me the full requirements list, with the case or consideration motivating each one. To get you started on the territory I have in mind: the theory should have to say something about the apparent asymmetry between morally deviant fictional worlds (where we balk at going along with the narrator's stipulation that cruelty is good) and descriptively deviant ones (where we happily accept faster-than-light travel, speaking animals, or even some contradictions); it should have to handle the question of whether the resistance is a failure of imagination, a failure of willing participation, or a failure of the fiction to do its proper work, and it should say which of these is even the right explanandum; it should account for the gradience of the phenomenon, since some deviant moral claims in fiction are easy to entertain and others aren't, and for the role of genre, tone, and narrative distance in making the same content acceptable in one work and jarring in another; it should explain why authorial authority seems to extend over some aspects of the story but stall at others; and it should be able to distinguish genuine imaginative resistance from mere disagreement with the author, confusion about what's being stipulated, or dislike of the book. For each requirement, tell me whether it's a genuine desideratum any adequate theory must meet or a presupposition of one particular camp in the literature, and say which single requirement, in your judgment, rules out the most existing approaches.

Two further asks, because this is where I expect the list to earn its keep. First, if some subset of the desiderata looks jointly unsatisfiable -- for instance, if treating the phenomenon as a puzzle about imagination proper is in tension with treating it as a datum any theory of fiction must predict -- flag that explicitly, since a list that contains its own impossibility result is a finding, not a failure. Second, briefly and without turning this into a literature survey, indicate how the two broad families of response I know of (views on which the resistance is a real imaginative block grounded in how moral commitments work, and views on which it's an artifact of what fictions can successfully make fictional) fare against the requirements. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scoring I can redo in class.
