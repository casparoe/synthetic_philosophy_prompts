After forty years as an atmospheric chemist, I have spent my retirement reading political philosophy, and for some months I have been circling Sen's impossibility result concerning Pareto efficiency and minimal liberalism. My layman's gloss: if a collective decision rule must respect each person's authority over some purely personal matter, and must also never select an outcome that everyone prefers to avoid, then no such rule exists, because people's preferences reach into one another's private spheres. The standard illustration involves two citizens and a scandalous book — one would rather the other not read it, the other would rather the first be made to read it — and the conjunction of rights with unanimity collapses into contradiction.

I have arrived at a position, and I want to know what it would take to dislodge me from it. The position is that the paradox is an artifact of representation. Sen's framework encodes rights as restrictions on which social states a rule may select, which forces rights to be cashed out as preferences over end-states. If rights are instead modeled procedurally — as entitlements to make certain moves, with the social outcome emerging from everyone's simultaneous choices — then a Pareto principle properly applied to what people voluntarily bring about cannot conflict with rights, since each right-holder endorsed the outcome given the others' choices. The impossibility shows only that one cannot judge institutions both by their end-states and by their procedures; and since the procedural reading is the one that matches what a right actually is, the conflict dissolves rather than binds.

What would count against this? I want the exposure mapped honestly. Are there formal results suggesting the impossibility re-emerges even in the procedural framework, for instance when one person's welfare depends on moves inside another's protected sphere? Does the position merely redefine the Pareto principle so that it cannot bite, and would a defender of the end-state reading concede that redefinition as legitimate or dismiss it as changing the subject? Which intuitions does it sacrifice — say, about cases where everyone's voluntary exercise of rights leaves all parties worse off by their own lights — and which of its commitments would a less sanguine version of me find unacceptable? Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what the position's own defenders would concede from what only its critics press, and tell me plainly whether it has been qualified into something that risks nothing at all.
