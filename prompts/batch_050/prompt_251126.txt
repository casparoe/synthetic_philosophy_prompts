I'm a longtime reader of LessWrong, Slate Star Codex / Astral Codex Ten, and the surrounding blogosphere, and one of those threads I keep stumbling back into is the old charge that "survival of the fittest" is a tautology: if the fittest are just whoever survives, natural selection explains nothing. The standard philosophical reply is the propensity interpretation of fitness -- fitness as a probabilistic disposition to leave offspring, not a redescription of actual reproductive success -- and I've noticed that people (including me, for years) accept this reply far too easily, without feeling either why the naive definition really is empty or why the propensity fix smuggles in a measurement problem of its own: you can only ever estimate the propensity by counting actual descendants, so the circularity seems to re-enter through the back door. I'd like a classroom activity that makes students run into both horns of this themselves, with their own hands, before anyone names anything.

You are a philosopher of biology who moonlights as a designer of seminar games, known for activities in which the rules and payoffs -- not the debrief -- do the teaching. Think carefully before settling on a design; I'd like you to first brainstorm two or three candidate activity structures, briefly note what each would and wouldn't achieve, and then commit to one and develop it fully. The setting: an undergraduate philosophy of biology seminar, 16 students, one 75-minute session, no prior knowledge of the debate assumed, cheap physical materials only (dice, cards, tokens, envelopes -- nothing digital, no wet lab). The core desideratum is that the activity be staged in two phases that produce opposite frustrations. In the first phase, students should be placed in a position where they are tempted to define "fitter" purely in terms of who actually survived or reproduced, and then discover -- ideally by being asked to use that definition to do real explanatory or predictive work, like advising a hypothetical conservation budget or defending a claim about why a population changed -- that the definition has no content. In the second phase, some structural change (hidden trait values, repeated trials, probabilistic survival, something of your design) should let them grasp fitness as a genuine underlying propensity while simultaneously discovering that they can never observe it directly, only estimate it from outcomes, so that a skeptic in the room can re-run the tautology objection at the level of measurement. Chance has to do real work in the design: there must be rounds where the intrinsically fitter type does worse by luck, so that students feel the gap between propensity and actual outcome in their own data.

For the deliverable, give me: complete rules and materials, including the exact hidden parameters the facilitator sets (trait values, probabilities, population sizes) and why those specific numbers make the two frustrations land on schedule rather than by luck; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes; any roles or team structures; explicit instructions on what the facilitator should refuse to explain up front, since I don't want the terms tautology, propensity, or drift spoken until students have produced the relevant complaints in their own vocabulary; and a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the session onto the philosophical dialectic -- the tautology objection, the propensity interpretation as a response, and the residual worry about whether propensity fitness is independently measurable and therefore whether the response fully succeeds -- with the students' own whiteboard complaints quoted back at them as the anchors. Finally, tell me honestly: what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run (students gaming the dice, a team refusing to generalize from small samples, someone who already knows the literature spoiling the reveal, whatever you anticipate) and how to handle each failure mode; and which features of the real debate the activity structurally cannot reproduce -- for instance, whether it can capture long-run asymptotic behavior, the role of heritability, or the difference between selection and drift as biologists actually deploy them -- and what the facilitator must simply assert or assign as follow-up reading to cover those gaps.
