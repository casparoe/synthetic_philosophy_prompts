Consumer AI assistants these days mostly follow the same design rules: confirm with the user before doing anything irreversible (sending the email, deleting the file, spending money), don't act beyond what was asked, keep actions reversible where possible, and when unsure, do nothing and ask. People describe this as keeping the assistant "low-impact" -- it should do what you want without causing side effects.

What theory of impact does this practice presuppose? It seems to assume there's a baseline of "what would have happened anyway" that the assistant shouldn't disturb beyond fulfilling the request, but defining that baseline without a full account of what's valuable seems hard -- "don't change much" measured how? Number of atoms moved, options foreclosed, people's plans altered? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the practice actually needs it or is compatible with several rival accounts of impact (deviation-penalty views, option-preservation views, "just defer to the user" views), and where it's just underdetermined. And tell me what would concretely have to change in these design rules if it turned out that "side effect" can't be defined at all without smuggling in a substantial chunk of a value function.
