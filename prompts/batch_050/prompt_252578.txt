I'm a philosophy professor, and I also sit (unpaid, more fool me) on the grants commitee of a small community foundation in my town. We've landed on a genuinely hard case and I've been volun-told to write the memo the board votes on next month. Help me draft it.

Back in the 80s a local factory owner left us an endowment whose trust instrument restricts the income to "the relief of suffering within [our county]." It now throws off about $400k a year. For decades we've funded the county food bank, a domestic violence shelter, a heating-assistance fund. All fine. But a new program officer ran the numbers and pointed out that the same money, sent to top-rated global health charities (malaria nets, deworming, that whole world), would plausibly avert several child deaths a year plus a lot of serious illness -- whereas what we fund locally mostly makes hard lives somewhat less hard. Real good, but nobody in our county is starving to death. Half the board now says: suffering is suffering, a drowning child is a drowning child whether she's in the pond in front of you or on another continent, and we are effectively choosing to let kids die for the sake of a dead man's zip code. The other half says: we agreed to administer his money on his terms, we're fiduciaries not moral philosophers, and if donor restrictions become mere suggestions the whole charitable trust model collapses and future donors take their money elsewhere.

The wrinkle that makes this hard: our lawyers say we could petition the court to modify the restriction under the cy-près doctrine if the restriction has become impracticable or wasteful -- but "wasteful" is doing all the work there. The trust is perfectly practicable. It's just dramatically less effective per dollar than the alternative. So the real question is whether "we could do vastly more good if we ignored you" is the sort of thing that *should* count as defeating a donor restriction.

The memo should: (1) make an actual recommendation -- petition the court to modify, keep funding locally, or petition for a partial release (say half the income unrestricted) -- I'm honestly torn but the memo can't be; (2) give the strongest version of the losing side. If you recommend staying local, you have to face the drowning-child argument head-on: "but she's not right in front of you" is exactly the response the thought experiment is built to dissolve, so don't just reach for it; (3) say something honest about whether a fiduciary duty to a dead donor can weigh against duties of rescue owed to strangers, and whether the board's role morality even permits its members to act on their personal duties of rescue while wearing their trustee hats. About 800-1000 words, board-ready, not a term paper.
