I've never studied philosophy, but for years I've kept a private list in a notebook of actions sorted into two columns: things a person owes, and things that go beyond what anyone could demand of them. Lately I've started to suspect my columns don't rest on any coherent line, and I'd like help finding the principle — or being told honestly which of my entries has to move. Here are my verdicts. Wading into a shallow pond to save a drowning child, ruining good clothes in the process: plainly owed, and someone who walked past would be a bad person, full stop. Donating a kidney to a stranger: wonderful, but nobody owes it. Running into a burning car to pull out a stranger, at real risk of death: beyond what's owed. Running into the same car to pull out my own child: owed — a parent who stayed on the curb would be a monster in a way the bystander who stays is not, though I can't articulate why the relationship changes the category rather than just the stakes. Donating fifteen percent of my income to charities that demonstrably save children's lives: this is the entry I keep moving between columns, because it looks structurally identical to the pond case — a modest, survivable cost to me, a child's life on the other end — and yet calling it obligatory feels like an accusation against nearly everyone I know, including me, while calling it optional feels like letting the pond-walker off. Returning the extra twenty a cashier hands me by mistake: owed, trivially. Holding the elevator, letting a driver merge, texting a friend on a hard day: neither owed in a way that makes refusal blameworthy, nor saintly — there's a whole middle tier my two columns can't even represent. And a soldier who falls on a grenade to shield his unit: I want to call it heroic and beyond duty, but I've heard soldiers describe it as simply what the job required, and I'm not sure they're being modest.

So: is there a principled account of the line between the obligatory and the beyond-the-call that captures most of these, and what does it do with my pond-versus-donation pair, which is where I feel the contradiction most sharply? I'd rather be told that one of my verdicts is wrong than be handed a baroque principle that flatters all of them, so if the cost of consistency is overturning something, tell me which entry is the cheapest to sacrifice and why. I'd also like to know whether the parent case is best explained by special obligation or whether it shows my original sorting was confused in a deeper way, and whether the middle tier of everyday decency needs its own category or can be folded into one of the two. Finally, test whatever principle you arrive at on a case I haven't sorted: a bystander carrying an EpiPen who watches a stranger go into anaphylaxis when calling an ambulance would probably suffice — does it matter to the classification that the bystander could achieve the same result more cheaply, and does the principle say something sensible about it?
