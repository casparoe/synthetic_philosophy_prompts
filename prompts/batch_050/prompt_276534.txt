So I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about why Keynes's famous leisure forecast never landed. In his 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," John Maynard Keynes waves off the unemployment panic of his day like this: "We are suffering, not from the rheumatics of old age, but from the growing-pains of over-rapid changes, from the painfulness of readjustment between one economic period and another." He figured this readjustment was temporary -- technology would keep compounding, the economic problem would get solved, and his grandkids would work maybe fifteen hours a week and have to figure out how to live well with the rest.

Here's my episode: nearly a century on, rich countries did get roughly as rich as he projected, but working hours basically stopped falling somewhere around the 1970s, and now the biggest labor fights are over four-day-week trials and whether AI is about to eat everyone's job -- with people apparently just as anxious about "labour absorption" as they were in 1930. I want you to analyze this whole arc through Keynes's own framework. What exactly does he think the "growing-pains" line explains, and what does he assume has to happen for the pains to end rather than just keep recurring every time a new technology shows up? Where does the episode vindicate him (living standards, the return of technological unemployment anxiety) and where does it break him -- I'm thinking of his distinction between absolute needs and needs that are really about keeping up with or topping your neighbors, which he mostly brushes past, plus stuff like status competition in consumption, who captures productivity gains, and the fact that many people seem to like work or at least need it for reasons beyond money. Finally: if the pain Keynes describes is still with us almost a hundred years later, is that because the readjustment isn't done yet, or because his theory of what people actually want was wrong in a way his own essay quietly contains the materials to diagnose? Write in American English, and don't be afraid to say where the framework just runs out.
