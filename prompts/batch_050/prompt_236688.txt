In a seminar on conceptual engineering, we were debating whether a city’s predictive-policing system should be classified as “surveillance” even when it analyzes only information already collected by public agencies. Someone ended the discussion by saying, “It’s just semantics”: the police either are or are not doing something objectionable, and what we call it cannot change that. But our instructor suggested that this slogan can mask several very different claims—some obviously true, some philosophically controversial, and some plainly mistaken. It matters especially because the classification would determine whether the system falls under a statute requiring public notice and independent review.

Please distinguish the precise claims that “It’s just semantics” might be making. Consider at least these possibilities: the parties attach different meanings to the same word and are merely talking past one another; they agree about the relevant facts but disagree about a word’s correct extension; they are offering rival analyses of one shared concept; they are engaged in a metalinguistic negotiation over how a term should be used in a particular institutional setting; and they are asking which concept, among several available ones, would be most morally or practically useful. For each reading, explain what would count as evidence that it is the right diagnosis, whether the resulting disagreement is trivial or substantive, and whether it would justify the advice to stop arguing about words. Finally, apply your menu to the predictive-policing case and explain how a conceptual engineer could revise or replace “surveillance” without merely stipulating away the underlying normative dispute.
