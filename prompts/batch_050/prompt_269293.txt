I keep getting into arguments with a friend about whether DNA is "really" a code, and I want to settle it by figuring out what I even think makes something a code in the first place. I'm a software engineer, no philosopy training, so half my examples are from work. Here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases -- tell me what principle (if any) unifies them, or which ones I have to give up.

1. A player piano roll is a program. Holes at positions map to notes, read off mechanically. Yes, code.
2. A vinyl record is NOT code, even though grooves get mechanically turned into sound. The groove is just a squashed-down version of the sound wave. No symbols, just a smaller copy of the thing. (Though I admit 1 vs 2 already feels shaky -- is the difference just "digital vs analog"? That seems too thin.)
3. A cake recipe is code, but only because a person reads it. Printed on paper nobody ever reads, it's just ink.
4. A mold for casting metal parts is not a code. The mold's shape determines the part's shape, sure, but nothing gets "read" or "interpreted" -- it's just physics pushing back.
5. A house key -- I want to say not a code, but you could totally read the pin heights of the lock off the key's teeth, so by my piano-roll logic it might be one. This one bugs me.
6. The weights of a trained neural net are not a program. They causally produce the model's behavior, but nothing "looks anything up" in them. Except, hmm, the forward pass is just as mechanical as the player piano, so I'm not sure this survives contact with #1 either.
7. A lookup table printed on a poster -- say a Caesar cipher chart -- is a code even when it's just sitting on the wall unused. So maybe being read isn't required? Which conflicts with #3.

And then DNA itself. My instinct says it's obviously a code: codons map to amino acids, there's a start signal, and the mapping is even arbitrary-ish in the sense that it could have been different -- apparently mitochondria actually use a slightly different codon table than the rest of the cell, which is exactly what you'd expect if the mapping is a convention rather than physics. But here's what keeps nagging me: nothing inside the cell consults the codon table. There's no parser, no lookup. The amino acid gets attached to the tRNA by enzymes doing ordinary shape-fitting chemistry, and the ribosome just chains them together. So the "interpretation" of the code happens by the same kind of physical shape-matching that made me say the mold in #4 is not a code. When the piano plays my roll, the roll is a program; when metal fills my mold, the mold isn't. Which one is the ribosome doing?

So what I want: a principle (or two or three candidate principles) for when something counts as a code or program versus just a physical shape that determines an outcome. State it precisely enough that I could implement it as a decision procedure. Tell me which of my cases motivates each clause, and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop -- I suspect I can't keep all seven. Then apply the winning principle to two cases I haven't listed: a CRISPR guide RNA (the ~20-letter sequence that tells the Cas9 protein where to cut) and the amino acid sequence of a protein itself (which determines how it folds). Bottom line I'm after: on my own best principle, does DNA come out as a code, and if it comes out as something in-between -- like "a code relative to a reader that doesn't exist" -- say that plainly rather than rescuing my instinct.
