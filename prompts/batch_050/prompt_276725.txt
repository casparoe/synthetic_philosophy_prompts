ok so I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, but I keep bumping into this "Pascal's mugging" thing and it's been bugging me. For anyone who needs the recap: someone offers you an astronomically huge payoff in exchange for a small cost (your wallet, a $5 donation, whatever), the odds they can actually deliver are microscopic but nonzero, and naive expected value math says take the deal because huge times tiny is still huge. It feels like the same failure mode as engineering decisions where you chase some tail-risk upside and get burned.

Here are the fixes I've collected so far, mostly from blog posts and one very long forum thread:

1. Cap the utility function. If utility is bounded above, then bounded payoff times tiny probability stays tiny, problem solved.
2. Ignore probabilities below some threshold. If the chance is smaller than like one in 10^20, round it to zero and move on with your life.
3. Complexity penalty. The probability you should assign to "this guy can really deliver 10^50 utils" should shrink faster than the promised payoff grows, since delivering ever-more utility requires ever-more specific and elaborate mechanisms, so the expectation never blows up. I think Bostrom wrote something along these lines but I might be misremembering.
4. Weight outcomes by attitude to risk instead of going linear. There's a formal version where you rank possible outcomes worst to best and systematically overweight the bad end, so tiny chances of gigantic gains dont dominate. I forget the exact name, rank-something expected utility.
5. Refuse on strategic grounds. Agents who pay out on unverifiable promises paint a target on their own back, so a sane decision policy just declines the whole category, the same way you never pay ransomware even when paying might be cheap in isolation.

What I want: one more proposed solution that isnt on this list and isnt a rewording, special case, or mashup of anything on it (so "cap utility AND apply a threshold" doesnt count). Ideally it should defuse the mugging in a structurally different way, e.g. by changing what probabilities or expectations even are rather than by clamping the inputs. And be honest about whether it actually works or just shoves the wierdness somewhere else.
