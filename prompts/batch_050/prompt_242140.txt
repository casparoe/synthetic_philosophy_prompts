I'm in a philosophy of action reading group and we keep circling back to weakness of will, and I've gotten pretty attached to a view I want properly stress-tested. The view: akrasia is fully explained by present-biased (roughly hyperbolic) discounting, and there is no separate phenomenon of failing to do what you judge best. On this picture, the smoker who at noon judges she shouldn't smoke tonight and then smokes anyway isn't acting against her judgment at the moment of smoking — her preferences have flipped as the cigarette got closer, and at each moment she does what she then most wants. The ancient puzzle about how someone can act contrary to their own all-things-considered judgment dissolves: there's only diachronic preference reversal, which merely *looks* irrational if you illegitmately assume a person's preferences must be constant across time. I like how deflationary this is.

So: what would count against it? I want a map of the view's exposure, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Some things I'm already chewing on: (a) are there clear-eyed, synchronic cases of akrasia the discounting story can't describe — where at the very moment of acting I'd still say, and mean, that I ought all-things-considered not to? (b) Does the empirical discounting literature actually favor hyperbolic over exponential discounting, and could the view survive if the hyperbola turned out to be largely an artifact of how discount rates get elicited? (c) If the agent always does what they then prefer, what's left for regret and self-blame to attach to, and does the view's prediction about *when* people feel regret (only once preferences flip back, presumably) match how regret actually behaves? (d) The worry that keeps me up: if "preference" just means whatever the behavior reveals, then any action whatsoever can be fitted by some discount function, and the theory explains everything and nothing. Has it been qualified into unfalsifiability — and is that actually fatal for a theory of this kind, or do we tolerate that shape of theory elsewhere in the social sciences?

Two more requests. First, distinguish what defenders of the view (the picoeconomics crowd, say) would themselves concede as genuine counterevidence from what only critics think should count — I don't want a strawman immune system. Second, there's an argument floating around that hyperbolic-ish discounting can be perfectly rational given that uncertainty about future payoffs grows with delay, which seems to cut both ways: it strengthens the view as a description of behavior but undermines the idea that there's any pathology or failure left to explain. Can the view absorb that, or does it quietly change the subject at that point? If your honest verdict is that the view survives most of this, say so — I'd rather hear it's well-insulated than get a list of fake defeaters.
