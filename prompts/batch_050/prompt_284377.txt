I run a second-year undergraduate module on free will and I'm putting together this year's exam. Last year's Frankfurt question was a disaster in a boring way: everyone had memorised the Black-and-Jones set-up (the neurosurgeon who'd have intervened but didn't need to) and just regurgitated it, so the marker couldn't tell who actually understood the argument from who'd revised the Wikipedia summary. I want a question that makes students do the thing rather than describe it.

So: draft one exam question on Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities, answerable in about an hour, that can't be done by recounting the case. My instinct is the crux should be the dilemma facing the counterfactual intervener device itself: either the "prior sign" Black waits for causally settles what Jones will do (in which case the example presupposes determinism and the incompatibilist will just deny that Jones is responsible), or it doesn't (in which case Jones retains some thin alternative possibility — a "flicker of freedom" — and the case fails against PAP). The question should push students to run that dilemma themselves and then take a view on whether a flicker that thin could be the kind of alternative that responsibility actually requires. If you think a different crux separates understanding from memorisation better, use yours instead, but say briefly why.

Along with the question, give me a model answer of around 500 words and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with marks per point and a one-line note on the characteristic way a weak candidate fumbles each. The scheme should make clear what seperates a first-class answer from a 2:1 in terms of actual moves made, not coverage. Finally, flag which of your must-hit points is the most contestable — i.e. where a colleague might reasonably mark on different assumptions — and say what consideration would persuade you to weight it differently.
