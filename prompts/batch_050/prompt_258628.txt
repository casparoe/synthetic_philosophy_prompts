I run a tiny podcast where I interview academics about ideas that sort of connect to tech, and next week's guest is a philosophy professor who defends something called the Principal Principle. From what I've pieced together from a week of wikipedia-and-papers cramming (I'm a software enginner, no philosophy background), the rough idea is: if you know the objective chance of some event is, say, 0.7, then your degree of belief that it'll happen should also be 0.7 -- unless you have some kind of special "inadmissible" information. I work with probabilistic forecasting models all day, so the intuitive appeal is obvious to me, but I don't want the episode to just be her explaining the view for the nth time. I want to stress-test it.

Draft me about 8 interview questions, each with a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want the set to hit at least these pressure points:

- What "admissible" information even is. Every explanation I've read seems to define it as "information that doesn't mess with the chance," which smells circular -- like the principle is being made true by definition. I want a question that makes her say what admissibility amounts to without the circularity, and a follow-up if she retreats to "it's whatever a rational person would treat as evidence."
- The crystal-ball-ish cases where you get reliable info about the actual future (an oracle, a leaked spoiler, whatever) that seems to make the known chance irrelevant. Does the principle survive that, or does it just quietly add an exception for every case where it would give the wrong answer?
- Determinism. If the universe is deterministic, isn't the "real" chance of everything just 0 or 1? I gather defenders of the principle still want chance to exist in a deterministic world -- as an engineer this is the single weirdest part of the whole view for me, so I want a couple questions that pin down what a non-0-or-1 chance could possibly BE if the future is already fixed.
- Something I saw called the "Big Bad Bug" (no idea if that's the actual name lol) -- apparently if the chances themselves have chances, the principle ends up contradicting itself, like the chance distribution undermines its own authority. I want a question that makes her walk through the fix step by step, plus a follow-up on whether the fix is a principled repair or just a patch.
- Whether the principle is supposed to be a rule of rationality for believers, or part of the definition of what "chance" means, or both. If it's really a definition, I want to ask what's left of the claim that objective chances exist at all, and whether the whole thing collapses into "act like a well-calibrated model."

Tone: casual enough for a podcast audience, but don't dumb it down, and please no generic "what first got you interested in philosophy" filler.
