Since retiring from experimental physics I have been reading about the corrigibility problem in AI safety, and I would like a thought experiment constructed to the following specifications. Design a scenario — one paragraph, no superintelligence, no deception or hidden plans, hardware no more capable than a present-day hospital or factory system — in which an agent that is corrigible by the standard definition (it shuts down whenever it receives a valid shutdown command) behaves clearly worse, in plain human-welfare terms, than an agent that is incorrigible by that same definition (it refuses the command and keeps operating). The shutdown command must be fully authorized and well-formed: the failure must come from the definition itself, not from a spoofed signal or a hacker, and the incorrigible agent must refuse on the basis of a fallible estimate, not omniscience. Spell out the agent's reasoning with a small, explicit payoff table a careful reader can verify; the verdict must be generated by the designs, not stipulated. The purpose of the exercise is to test whether corrigibility, as standardly defined, actually tracks safety — so after the scenario, do the following: (1) state what each agent does and why its design compels it; (2) identify the single feature of the standard definition that your case exploits, and say whether removing that feature collapses the case back into the familiar picture where the compliant agent is obviously the safe one; (3) give the strongest response available to someone who holds that the standard definition needs no repair.
