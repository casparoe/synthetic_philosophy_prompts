Next month I'm giving the toast at the retirement dinner of my dissertation advisor, a comparative psychologist who spent her career arguing, against most of her field, that a great deal of human behavior is what philosophers call sphexish. Her signature example, which half the room will know by heart, is the digger wasp Sphex: it drags a paralyzed cricket to its burrow, leaves it at the threshold while it inspects the nest, and if you nudge the cricket a few inches away, the wasp drags it back to the threshold and does the whole inspection again — and will do so dozens of times, never once varying the routine. Her provocative claim was that we are mostly the same: we congratulate ourselves on deliberation while running fixed routines that merely *feel* chosen, and the moments of genuine flexibility are rare, effortful, and usually only triggered when the environment forcibly interrupts the loop. The audience will be split about evenly between colleagues who can recite the Sphex experiment's details from memory and family members, including her elderly mother, who have only vaguely heard that "Carol studies wasps, or maybe free will, nobody's sure."

Please write a five-minute toast for the occasion. The hard part is this: the toast cannot itself be sphexish. A generic retirement speech — the standard recitation of publications, grants, and fond adjectives — would inadvertently demonstrate her thesis at her own party, and she would absolutely notice and say so during dessert. So build the toast around one or two anecdotes about her that initially look like rigid routines on her part (she is famously a creature of habit: same lunch, same walking route, same opening joke in every lecture) but that turn out, on closer inspection, to conceal real flexibility — moments where she noticed the cricket had been moved and did something new. End with a turn that both audiences can feel: the suggestion that the highest compliment one can pay her is not praise, which is easy to emit on cue, but a genuinely unscripted response — and then have the toast earn that ending rather than just announce it. Keep it warm rather than lecture-y, assume no jargon needs defining for the family half, and make sure the colleagues catch at least one wink aimed just at them.
