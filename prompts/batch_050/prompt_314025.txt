Right, this one's a bit personal, so bear with me. I'm a philosophy professor (bioethics, mostly) and my oldest friend and former colleague, Martin Hale, died last month — or rather, was declared dead, and with Martin that difference is sort of the whole problem. He spent twenty years working on the definition of death, and the view he's known for is that the standard circulatory criterion doesn't report a biological fact so much as enact a decision: when clinicians declare death a few minutes after the pulse stops, it's the permanence of the cessation (it won't be restarted, because nobody is going to try) doing the work, not irreversibility (it couldn't be), and that makes the declaration partly a normative choice wearing a lab coat. He thought the practice was defensible anyway, provided we were honest about what we were doing. Half the people who work on this think he was right and half think he collapsed a distinction that matters, and as far as I can tell it's genuinely unresolved.

Martin had a catastrophic brain haemorrhage, never regained consciousness, and was declared dead by the circulatory criterion so that his organs could be donated — they waited the standard interval after his heart stopped, declared him, and retrieved his liver and both kidneys. His kidneys are in two strangers as I write this. His own view, stated at my kitchen table more than once, was that someone in his situation is not dead by any purely biological criterion at the moment of declaration, that what justifies retrieval is that the person is dying, beyond meaningful recovery, and consented — and that this is fine as long as nobody pretends otherwise.

I'm giving the eulogy on Saturday. The room will contain his widow, his two kids (early twenties, not philosophers), his mum, who thinks philosophy is "what Martin did at work," a few of my colleagues, and — the bit I'm sweating — several of the team who cared for him at the end, including the intensivist who made the declaration. His daughter asked me last week, not rhetorically, when did Dad actually die, and I told her I'd try to answer in the eulogy rather than around it.

So: write the eulogy, about four minutes at a shaky reading pace. The idea that a death declaration is partly a human decision has to do the structural work — I want it to land as honesty that comforts, not as a suggestion that his death certificate is a fiction. It must honour his actual view (he'd haunt me if I smoothed it into "the doctors knew exactly when he died"), it cannot be a seminar, and — this is the crucial constraint, given the intensivists in the room — it must not put in my mouth, as settled fact, anything that people who work in this area would recognise as still contested; keep what's genuinely agreed seperate from what's a live dispute, and where I gesture at his position make clear it was his position. Structure it as exactly five numbered sections, a short paragraph or two each, with no framing beyond the five — I lose my place easily and want natural pauses. British English please, and sentences short enough to say out loud while crying a bit.
