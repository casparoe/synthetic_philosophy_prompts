So I'm a PhD student in archaeology (third year, working on settlement patterns in the southern Maya lowlands, for context), and I keep hitting the same wall in the collapse literature. The Easter Island case is the worst offender but it generalizes: you have one camp (Diamond-adjacent, ecocide framing) saying societies degrade their environment and fail, and another camp (resilience/transformation people, and on Rapa Nui specifically the Hunt/Lipo vs. the earlier consensus fight) saying the society didn't collapse, it transformed, adapted, was pushed by external shocks like contact and disease, and anyway 'collapse' smuggles in a value judgement. Both sides know the same paleoenvironmental data, the same chronology disputes, the same population estimates. Nobody is converting anybody. The Bronze Age literature has the same shape -- was 1177 a collapse or a systems reorganization, etc.

What I want is not a verdict on Easter Island or the Maya. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. My hunch, offered as a hunch: it's mostly a verbal dispute about what 'collapse' has to mean -- population crash vs. loss of political complexity vs. loss of cultural continuity -- papered over a real normative disagreement about whether depopulation-with-continuity counts as failure. But maybe that's too easy and there's a genuine empirical core I'm missing (e.g. chronology resolution on Rapa Nui actually could settle part of it).

So: what kind of disagreement is this? Are the camps talking past each other on definitions, disagreeing about which evidence is even relevant, or divided by bedrock intuitions about what counts as a society 'doing well'? Use what each side actually argues, not a strawman. And given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress -- a better chronology, a cleaner definition, a different question entirely -- or argue that no progress should be expected.
