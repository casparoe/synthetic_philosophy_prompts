I host a small interview series at the university philosophy society — the audience skews heavily toward longtime readers of LessWrong, Overcoming Bias, and the rest of the rationalist blogosphere, so the baseline familiarity with decision theory is high, and questions that merely rehearse the textbook contrast between maximizing and satisficing will go nowhere with them. Our next guest is a moral philosopher whose new book argues that Herbert Simon's account of bounded rationality has been misread as a claim only about instrumental choice: on her view, satisficing applies all the way up the ladder of practical reason, including to the selection and revision of our ends themselves. Our life-projects, moral commitments, and even our conception of what counts as a good enough life are, she argues, themselves objects of boundedly rational choice, set by aspiration levels rather than by any optimizing procedure.

The book opens with a passage from Simon's Administrative Behavior (1947) that she treats as her central text:

"In the process of decision those alternatives are chosen which are considered to be appropriate means of reaching desired ends. Ends themselves, however, are often merely instrumental to more final objectives. We are thus led to the conception of a series, or hierarchy, of ends. Rationality has to do with the construction of means-ends chains of this kind."

Draft eight to ten interview questions that work this passage hard rather than letting her summarize the book at me. Some constraints on what I'm after:

The opening questions should force her to say what Simon's passage does and does not license. Read flatly, the passage makes rationality a property of chain-construction — of linking means to ends — and is silent about how the ends at the top of the hierarchy get there. I want to know whether she takes Simon to be smuggling in an unstated premise that some ends sit outside rational assessment, or whether her extension of satisficing to ends is a departure from Simon rather than a development of him.

At least one question should press the regress worry: if the construction of each level of a means-ends chain is itself a decision requiring a criterion, and that criterion is set at a higher level, where does satisficing actually terminate — and is her answer that it terminates in aspiration levels just another way of saying it terminates in whatever we happen to want, given our history?

At least one question should confront the adaptive-preferences objection her critics keep raising: if the agent sets her own bar for "good enough," and the bar adjusts downward under frustration, then her view appears to ratify sour grapes as fully rational. Include a follow-up keyed to her most likely reply, which I expect will be some version of the claim that aspiration levels are responsive to evidence and feedback rather than freely adjustable.

At least one question should ask what her view makes of the cases where the rationalist community would find satisficing about ends most alarming — existential risk, great-power decision-making, or a person quietly abandoning a moral commitment they could have kept — and whether she thinks there is any principled line between a rationally set modest aspiration and simple complacency.

Close the sequence with a question about what could count against the view: what evidence or argument, from psychology or from normative theory, would she accept as showing that ends cannot be satisficed in the way she claims?

For each question, give me the follow-up I should have ready for the answer she is most likely to give. No biographical questions, no "what drew you to this topic," and nothing that could be answered by reading the book's back cover.
