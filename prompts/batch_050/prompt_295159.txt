In The Rule of Law and its Virtue, Raz derives a list of rule-of-law principles — prospective, clear, and stable laws; an independent judiciary; access to courts; limits on police and prosecutorial discretion — from a single premise: the law must be capable of guiding human conduct, and everything else about the ideal follows from that functional requirement. He also insists, notoriously, that a wicked legal system can satisfy the ideal perfectly, comparing the rule of law to the sharpness of a knife, which says nothing about what you cut with it. Take that derivation as your starting point and write the desiderata that any adequate conception of the rule of law as a political ideal must meet: the cases it must classify correctly (emergency rule by decree, lawless but benevolent administration, retroactive war-crimes statutes, a regime with impeccable formal legality and abhorrent laws), the distinctions it cannot collapse (legality versus justice, predictability versus discretion, the ideal's existence versus its degree), and the conclusions about the ideal's moral value it must be able to state rather than merely assert — with the consideration behind each requirement. Mark which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable and which are prejudices of one camp, flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied (the demand that the ideal be purely formal sits awkwardly with the demand that it explain why the rule of law is worth having), and identify which single requirement excludes the most contenders. Then, briefly, say how Fuller's inner morality of law, Raz's own account, and Waldron's procedural, courts-centered conception fare against the list — a paragraph each is enough; the criteria are the deliverable.
