I've been asked to design the single week on heritability for an undergraduate course in psychological science — the students are mostly second-years who have had one statistics course and one intro biology course, and this is their last serious encounter with genetics before they scatter into majors. The department's informal diagnosis, which matches my experience, is that students leave the unit having memorized a definition (the proportion of variance in a trait attributable to genetic variance in a population) while retaining every misconception the definition was supposed to displace: they treat heritability as a property of a trait rather than of a trait-in-a-population-in-an-environment, they read a heritability of 0.8 as meaning a trait is "80 percent genetic" for an individual, they infer that highly heritable traits can't be changed by intervention, and they slide from within-group heritability to genetic explanations of between-group differences. I get one ninety-minute lecture and one discussion section, and I'd rather teach three ideas well than six badly.

What I want from you is a teaching plan, not a lecture script. Specifically: the order in which the components have to be introduced so that the definition, when it finally arrives, feels like a natural answer to a question the students are already asking — I suspect variance partitioning has to come before the word "heritability" is ever spoken, but I want to know whether you agree and what depends on what before that. Then the one carrying example to build the whole lecture around: I've seen people use height across well-fed and underfed generations, and also the thought experiment of sowing genetically variable seed in two uniform but different soils; tell me which example you would choose (or propose a better one), what features it must have to carry the load — and what it must lack, since an example that is too realistic invites digressions — and how to extract from it the point that a trait can be perfectly heritable and entirely environmentally malleable at the same time.

Finally, the misconception map: list the four or five wrong inferences this audience predictably brings or forms mid-lecture, identify the point in the sequence where each one is naturally born, and say where and how to defuse it — including whether the phenylketonuria case (a "genetic" condition neutralized by diet) is worth the class time it costs, and whether I should touch group-difference reasoning at all in week one or explicitly defer it. Close with the check for understanding: one question or task for the discussion section that would show the concept has actually landed rather than the definition having been re-memorized, and a note on what I should openly refuse to cover at this level (twin-study estimation, missing heritability, GWAS-based methods) and how to say so without making the unit feel truncated.
