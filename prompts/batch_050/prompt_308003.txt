I write novels for a living and my current manuscript has developed a philosophical problem, which is not what I signed up for. The narrator spends the whole book not admitting to himself that he knew his buisness partner was skimming money. He sort of knows — he leaves the bank statements unopened, he schedules vacations over audit season — and the actual drama of the book is the maintenance work of not-knowing. A guy in my writing group who nearly finished a philosophy PhD heard the plot and told me my narrator isn't just unreliable, he's impossible, and handed me an argument. Cleaned up, it goes:

(1) Self-deception is deception with the same person in both roles: whatever it takes for me to deceive you, it takes for me to deceive myself, mutatis mutandis.
(2) Deceiving someone means getting them to believe something you take to be false. (If I'm honestly mistaken and pass my mistake along, I've misled you at most, not deceived you.)
(3) So the self-deceiver, in his deceiver capacity, believes p, and in his deceived capacity comes to believe not-p.
(4) The deceiver capacity never clocks out — the thing only works if he keeps steering himself away from the evidence — so he believes p and believes not-p at the same time, and knowingly.
(5) But you can't attribute flatly contradictory beliefs to one person at one time; that isn't a description of a confused mind, it's a failure to describe a mind at all.
(6) Worse, deception is something you do on purpose, and nobody successfully runs a con on a mark who's read the playbook, which the self-deceiver has, being its author.
(7) So self-deception can't happen. Everything we call that is really something else — ignorance, wishful hoping, lying to other people while half-suspecting, take your pick.

I refuse to accept (7). And the thing seems to prove too much in a way that specifically bugs me as a writer: the same machinery looks like it would rule out deliberately putting something out of mind (hiding the spare key so future drunk-you can't find it), and it would make a narrator who lies to the reader coherent but a narrator who lies to himself a logical contradiction, which gets actual literature exactly backwards.

So: which step do I reject, and why did it look so reasonable on the way in? Then price the exits I've heard floated, because I can't tell which one I'm supposed to want:

- Split him across time: he believed p when he set up the avoidance, believes not-p later. Except my guy does upkeep the entire time, that's the character.
- Split him into parts, one subsystem deceiving another. Except then is it self-deception or just two tenants, and haven't I populated his head with little people.
- Drop the on-purpose part: his motivation quietly rigs what he notices, opens, and remembers, no plan anywhere. Except then what separates self-deception from plain motivated reasoning, and does the phenomenon dissolve into that.
- Drop the belief part: the endpoint isn't believing not-p but something thinner — saying it, acting as if, imagining it vividly. Which might explain why he'd pass a polygraph but not why he flinches at the partner's name.

Tell me which exit is cheapest, what each one costs, and whether the cheapest one still lets me keep my narrator roughly as written. If forced, I'd rather hold onto (2)'s ordinary picture of deception and the plain fact that people do this to themselves, and let the impossibility conclusion go — but I want the bill itemized, ideally in terms a novelist can use rather than a seminar.
