I've come to accept the strong reading of Sen's liberal paradox: that the impossibility of a Paretian liberal demonstrates a genuine structural conflict between even minimal individual rights and the weakest Pareto principle, so that any adequate theory of social choice must give up one or the other. Tell me what would count against this position. Consider at least: proposals to recast rights as constraints on the set of admissible alternatives rather than as considerations within the social ordering; domain restrictions under which meddlesome or nosy preferences simply don't count; the claim that the paradox is an artifact of modeling rights as decisiveness over pairs of social states rather than as freedoms over actions or strategies; and the objection that the result has no practical bite because real-world rights conflicts lack the structure of Sen's two-person example. For each, say whether a defender of the strong reading would concede it as a genuine defeater or dismiss it as changing the subject. Rank the threats from fatal to merely awkward, and conclude by assessing whether the strong reading, once suitably qualified, ends up risking anything at all -- and whether that would be a virtue or a defect.
