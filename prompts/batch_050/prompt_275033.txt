ok so i'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book whose main character is basically stuck in a loop -- one response to everything, no matter what life throws at him -- and i can't figure out if that's tragic or if it's the only honest form of freedom. which is what got me thinking about Bartleby the Scrivener. read it through the sphexishness idea from the free will literature: the digger wasp that drags its cricket to the burrow entrance, pops inside to check things out, and if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while it's in there, it comes out, drags the cricket back, and does the whole check again. and again. and again. it never just hauls the cricket straight in, even though that would obviously work. philosophers use it as the picture of behavior that looks purposive but is really a fixed subroutine -- and the scary suggestion that maybe we're like that too. so: is Bartleby sphexish? he's got exactly one move, that calm little formula of preferring not to, and the more the narrator pushes, the more he repeats it, like the wasp re-checking the burrow. but the story keeps insisting it's a preference, not a compulsion, and his rigidity ends up costing him absolutely everything, which a wasp's loop never does. does that make his fixedness mechanism dressed up as agency, or agency that can only express itself as mechanism? and flip it around: the narrator is endlessly flexible, reasonable, accommodating, bending around Bartleby until the day he quietly walks away. is the flexible one the free one, or is he just sphexish about avoiding conflict, running his own loop of indulgence and retreat? i want a reading that takes the philosphy seriously but also tells me what the story knows about fixedness that the wasp example can't teach me -- because as a writer, not just a reader, i need to know whether giving a character a single unbreakable move makes him less free or more himself.
