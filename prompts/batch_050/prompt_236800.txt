So my mom's oncologist told us last month that there's a drug that might buy her another year or so, but insurance won't cover it because it doesn't clear the cost-effectiveness bar — something about it costing too much per QALY compared to whatever threshold they use (I think we read around $100-150k?). Fine, I understand health systems have to draw lines somewhere, even though it stings.

But then a few days later I'm reading an article about some EPA air pollution rule, and it says the government values a "statistical life" at like $13 million when it's deciding whether a regulation is worth the cost. So I'm sitting there doing the math and it makes no sense to me. If the government thinks a life is worth $13 million, how can the same government run a health system that says a $400k treatment to keep my mom alive isn't worth it? Isn't that just flatly contradicting itself? One agency says $13 million, another says not even half a million, and nobody seems to think that's a problem.

What I'm actually asking: is there a way to make the healthcare side use the $13 million number instead? My brother thinks this could be a lawsuit angle — like, the state has already conceded in its own cost-benefit analyses what a life is worth, so denying treatment below that amount should count as inconsistent or even discriminatory, and a decent lawyer could make hay of that. I don't know if that's realistic, but at minimum I'd like someone to walk me through why the two numbers are allowed to be so wildly different, or whether one of them is just flat-out wrong. Right now it feels like the left hand and the right hand value my mom's life at totally different prices and nobody has to answer for it.
