Okay so I need help thinking through where to spend my time. I'm starting a philosophy PhD in the fall and I've basically committed to working on imprecise / indeterminate probabilities (credal sets, sets of priors, whatever you want to call them). I have maybe four or five years and my background is more formal epistemology + decision theory than econ or stats.

The thing is, the field seems to have a bunch of genuinely open problems and I can't tell which ones are load-bearing. Here's my rough sense of the candidates, but push back on the list itself if you want:

1. The decision rules problem: once your credences are imprecise, how do you choose? Maximin, E-admissibility, interval dominance all give different answers and I don't see a principled way to pick between them, or even to say what would count as settling it.
2. Dilation: there are cases where learning something apparently makes your credences *less* precise, which strikes a lot of people as absurd. Is dilation an actual problem for the framework or just a counterintuitive consequence we should live with?
3. The "does any of this matter" objection: arguably for any realistic decision, the imprecise and precise analyses recommend the same action, so the whole framework is philosophically cute but practically inert. Is that true? Are there domains (climate policy? AI risk? medical decisions with sparse evidence?) where it genuinely changes recommendations?
4. Whether imprecise credences are supposed to be a description of an agent's actual state of mind or just a way of representing incomplete evidence -- and whether the framework even makes sense if agents never literally have sets of probability functions in their heads.
5. Updating: full Bayesian conditioning vs. other rules, and how this interacts with the dilation stuff.

So: which of these is most worth four or five years of a PhD student's time, and in what order should I attack them? Don't just rank them -- tell me what criteria you're using. Some things I'm unsure how to weight: how many of the other questions turn on getting this one right first (my hunch is the decision rules question is upstream of basically everything, but maybe that's wrong), how tractable it is for a philosopher as opposed to an economist or statistician, and how much good work has already been done vs. how crowded it is. Also: what would change your ranking? Like if it turned out that empirical work showed real agents' choices are better modeled by imprecise credences than precise ones, would that reshuffle things?

One more thing: if you think the honest answer is "the whole research program has a foundational problem nobody talks about and you should work on something else," say that too and defend it. I'd rather hear it now than in year three.
