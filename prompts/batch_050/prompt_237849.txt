A friend of mine defends a view of the self in the spirit of Hofstadter: an "I" is a self-referential pattern rather than a substance, and such patterns can be instantiated, at varying fidelity, in more than one place. Her considered verdicts so far: her late husband persists in her, dimly but genuinely, as a coarse-grained copy she keeps running; a dog has a small but real soul and a mosquito essentially none, because souls vary in size with the richness of the self-model; a being whose every neuron is realized by a redundant coupled pair of simpler units is one person, not many, since personhood belongs to the pattern's level rather than the substrate's; and the child she once was is not her in any strong sense, only a low-resolution ancestor of the present pattern. Now the new case: a company offers to construct, from her husband's complete digital remains — decades of messages, letters, and recordings — a conversational system that reproduces his dispositions and turns of phrase more faithfully than her own recollection now does. Would she say he persists in it? Identify two or more rules, each consistent with all four verdicts, that diverge on this case; state what each predicts; give your best guess at her verdict, with reasons for preferring it; and name the further case whose verdict would distinguish the rules from one another.
