I’m a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I’m trying to write a column without turning it into a seminar handout. I’ve been listening to a transcipt of a philosopher-of-medicine guest, call her M, ruling on whether things count as disease. Her verdicts so far:

- A person with high blood pressure, no symptoms, no damaged organs yet, but a much bigger risk of stroke later: disease.
- A pro cyclist whose heart is way outside the population average in size and resting rate, but works beautifully and predicts longer life: not disease.
- Ordinary grief after a parent dies, brutal for months but not spiraling into inability to eat, work, or care for kids: not disease.
- Teeth slightly crooked enough that the person hates photos and gets teased, but chewing, speech, pain, and infection risk are all fine: not disease.
- Early presbyopia, the totally standard midlife lens-stiffening almost everyone gets: not disease, even though it is annoying and people buy reading glasses.
- A heterozygous sickle-cell carrier in a malaria region: usually fine, mild anemia risk, strong protection from malaria; M says not disease in that environment.
- A lung lesion found on a scan, not causing symptoms, statistically very rare, but with a 70 percent chance of becoming invasive within five years if untreated: disease.

New case for the column: there’s a common gene variant affecting circadian timing. Carriers are healthy, fertile, long-lived, and cognitively normal if they live on a late schedule, roughly sleep 3am to 11am. In a society built around 9-to-5 work and 7am school, they get chronic sleep loss, depression, accidents, and metabolic trouble. Give them control over schedule and the problems mostly vanish. The variant is not rare, not a lesion, not toxic by itself, and in some flexible gig-economy contexts it is mildly advantageous. Would M call it a disease? More importantly: what rule is she probably running, what other rules fit the seven verdicts just as well, and where do those rules split on this case?

I want the piece to do three things. First, predict her verdict and name the closest earlier analogue, but be honest if the track record underdetermines it. Second, distinguish at least three candidate principles she could be using, like internal dysfunction even without present harm, harm plus social valuation, current state as risk for future breakdown, or trait-environment mismatch rather than broken machinery, and say what each implies for the circadian case. Third, design one extra verdict question that would separate the best two rules, something she could be asked next week.

Please don’t give me readings or citations. And for the column I need the epistemic temperature right: mark what is fairly settled in this debate versus what is still genuinely contested among philosophers, clinicians, and nosology nerds, and don’t make a contested naturalist or normativist move sound like the consensus default.
