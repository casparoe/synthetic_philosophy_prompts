I lead product at a mid-size company building an "agent" that acts on behalf of users -- booking, negotiating, renewing, canceling. The hard cases we keep hitting are ones where the user's stated preference is, by their own lights, kind of garbage: they click "renew" out of inertia, they say they want the cheapest flight but consistently regret red-eyes with three layovers, they tell the agent to buy the extended warranty because the sales page scared them. So the team keeps proposing we act on what the user would want if they were better informed / had thought about it more, instead of what they literally ask for. Philosophers seem to have versions of this idea (full-information accounts of what's good for someone, "laundering" distorted preferences, the whole "coherent extrapolated volition" thing in AI circles), but we have no way to translate any of it into enginneering decisions, and right now every override case gets argued from scratch in Slack.

Write us a procedure. Specifically: a sequence of tests our product reviewers (smart people, no philosophy background, ~30 min per case, decisions get revisited quarterly) can run to classify a proposed override into (a) clearly fine -- implement, (b) needs human sign-off from the user each time, (c) never allowed. The procedure should make explicit which notion of "idealized preference" it's relying on at each step -- just correcting factual mistakes? enforcing consistency across the user's own preferences? weighting the preferences they'd have after reflection, whatever that means? -- because I suspect those come apart and have very different justifications, and I want that difference to survive the simplification rather than get flattened into "what the user really wants."

The procedure needs to handle: how much evidence of the user's *actual* dispositions is enough before we treat a pattern as "theirs" rather than noise; what to do when idealization is underdetermined (two coherent selves the user could grow into, pointing different directions); whose values get baked in when we "extrapolate" (I worry the idealized user ends up suspiciously resembling a product manager); and reversibility -- how wrong we're allowed to be before an override is unforgivable. Mark the steps where the procedure genuinely bottoms out in a value judgment and no further checking will help.

Run it on three cases: (1) declining the extended warranty the anxious user asked for, (2) not booking the multi-layover red-eye the user keeps selecting and regretting, (3) a user with early dementia whose current requests conflict with the detailed preferences they recorded with us two years ago -- and I'd like at least one of these to come out with an answer I won't enjoy, so the procedure is clearly doing work rather than blessing my intuitions.

Also: please keep straight what philosophers actually agree on here versus what's live and contested. I don't want the procedure presented as if "the idealized preference is what matters" is settled doctrine if the literature is mostly people disagreeing about which idealization is legitimate, or whether any of them are. Where the contested parts force a design choice, say so and say which horn we picked.
