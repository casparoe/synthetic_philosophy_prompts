So my roommate and I got into this huge argument last night about Mill's Subjection of Women, specifically the part where he goes after appeals to nature, and we got absolutely nowhere, so I'm outsourcing the adjudication.

It started when I said Mill basically proves that the differences people observe between men and women are made, not born. My evidence: he describes what currently gets called women's nature as an artificial product of training and repression, and he compares it to a plant that's been forced into some twisted shape rather than growing the way it would on its own. So my read: society manufactures femininity and then points at it and goes "see, it's natural."

She said I'm reading way more into the passage than the arguement supports. Her line was that Mill's actual argument is purely epistemic: since no woman has ever lived outside a system that shapes her from birth, nobody actually knows what women's nature is, so appeals to nature can't justify anything. On her reading Mill is agnostic about what natural differences exist, and agnosticism doesn't establish that observed differences are artificial -- it just blocks defenders of the status quo from citing nature as a reason. For all the argument shows, she said, there could be real natural differences; Mill's point is only that we can't find out until the artificial setup is removed.

I pushed back that he's clearly not just agnostic, because he doesn't merely say we can't tell -- he says the existing traits are artificial, which is a positive claim about their origin, not a confession of ignorance. And I said the plant analogy only makes sense if there's a real uncultivated shape underneath that society is deforming, which is exactly the nature-talk he's supposed to be attacking, so he's either inconsistent or he's a constructionist who's borrowing nature-talk for the metaphor.

She said the plant analogy cuts against me: if Mill needs a natural uncultivated shape for the metaphor to do its job, then he can't think everything is constructed, and the passage is really about our inability to separate the natural from the cultivated given the evidence available. She also argued that Mill needs some notion of natural capacities for the rest of his philosophy anyway (all the stuff about freedom letting people develop along their own bent), so reading him as a thoroughgoing constructionist makes him incoherent, and you shouldn't read a thinker as incoherent when a consistent reading is on the table.

My last move was that "artificial" is doing real work for him and can't be reduced to "we can't tell": the claim is that the traits exist because of the power arrangement, and you don't get that from mere agnosticism. She replied that even granting that, it follows only for the specific traits he's talking about, not the sweeping conclusion about gender differences in general that I wanted.
