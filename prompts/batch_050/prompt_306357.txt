So I just had a massive argument with my freind about whether "better than" has to be transitive -- she thinks the sorites-style cases basically prove it isn't, I think they just prove our judgments get fuzzy. Anyway I teach an upper-level ethics seminar and this is going straight onto the final exam. Help me write it.

I want ONE essay question (students get 2 hours) on spectrum arguments against the transitivity of "all things considered better than." They've already seen the standard pain-intensity spectrum in class (a state of excruciating agony vs a state of mild discomfort, connected by a long series of adjacent pairs that differ imperceptibly), so the question can't just ask them to regurgitate that. Instead, build the question around these two cases, which I'm going to state in the question text myself:

(1) The static spectrum: 10,000 possible headaches, ordered by intensity, where each adjacent pair is indistinguishable to the person having it, but headache #1 is clearly better to have than headache #10,000. For each adjacent pair it seems false that one is worse than the other, yet the endpoints clearly differ in betterness.

(2) The dynamic case: an agent offered a sequence of trades, where each single trade is a tiny, real improvement (say, a slightly richer life in exchange for one extra second of mild discomfort), but after accepting all of them the agent is plainly worse off than where they started. Each step looks rational; the whole sequence looks like a disaster.

The question should force students to (a) explain precisely which conjunction of claims generates the contradiction in case (1) -- I want them to actually identify that "imperceptibly different" is doing the work of "equally good" and that these are not the same relation; (b) take a stand on which claim to reject and defend it against the strongest rival option (I expect the serious options to be: reject that imperceptible differences can't ground betterness, deny transitivity, or treat betterness as vague/indeterminate at some point in the series); and (c) say whether the diagnosis they gave for case (1) even transfers to case (2), given that (2) involves sequential choice with changing context rather than a single betterness ordering -- this is the bit my friend and I couldn't resolve, so I want to see what the students do with it.

Give me: the exam question itself (worded so it can't be answered by reciting lecture notes), a model answer of roughly 600 words that takes a definite position, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with a one-line note on the typical way students bungle each point (e.g., treating indistinguishability as if it were strict indifference without argument, or answering (c) by just repeating their answer to (a)).
