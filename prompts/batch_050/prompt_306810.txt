ok so i've been chewing on quine's gavagai argument and something isn't adding up for me. the standard story: field linguist, native points at a rabbit and says gavagai, and the linguist cant tell whether it means rabbit, undetached rabbit part, rabbit-stage, or manifestation-of-rabbithood. fine. but two claims seem to get run together in the usual presentations and i want them pulled apart:

1. ostension alone underdetermines translation — the linguist's behavioral evidence fits multiple renderings of the word
2. translation as a whole is indeterminate — even with a complete translation manual for the entire language, a rival manual could fit all the same speech dispositions just as well

here's my actual question. regarding claim 1: can't the linguist just learn more of the language? once she can ask the native things like "is that the same one we saw yesterday?" or "is this a part of that?", she can test whether gavagai behaves like a count noun, a mass term, a stage-sortal, whatever. so maybe ostension-underdetermination is just a practical problem for impatient linguists, and the real thesis has to be claim 2, the holistic one. but then gavagai seems to collapse into ordinary Duhem-style underdetermination of theory by evidence, applied to semantics — which feels way less spooky than how the argument is usually sold.

so please explain, at a gut level: (a) does clever questioning actually fix ostension, or does quine have a response? (i suspect it's something about how the translations of the questions themselves are only as good as the manual they're embedded in, so you can't bootstrap your way out — but spell out how that circle actually works). (b) is there a genuine difference between indeterminacy of translation and garden-variety scientific underdetermination, and if so what's the extra ingredient? my hunch is it has something to do with quine's physicalism — the idea that all the facts are physical/behavioral facts, so if those don't settle translation then nothing does and there is literally no fact of the matter — whereas in physics we just say we lack the evidence, not that the reality is missing. but maybe i'm wrong about that.

no need for a literature review or anything, i just want the conceptual core. and if one of my framings above is off, tell me and explain why.
