I am designing the final unit of a graduate seminar on reparative justice, and I want my students to evaluate accounts of official state apology against an explicit standard rather than by intuition. Please write that standard: the desiderata an adequate philosophical theory of state apologies for historical wrongs must satisfy, with the case or consideration motivating each one. The theory's job is to answer three questions, and adequacy should be judged relative to them: what makes such an apology morally serious rather than ceremonial; what could make it wrong to offer or wrong to accept; and how it relates to compensation, truth-telling, and institutional reform.

Work through the following pressure points at minimum, and add any I have missed.

First, the temporal problem. For many of the wrongs in question, the direct victims and the direct perpetrators are dead. The theory must say who apologizes, to whom, and on what conception of the state as a continuing agent that persists through total turnover of its citizens and officeholders.

Second, successor regimes. Sometimes the apologizing government is the institutional descendant of the perpetrator regime; sometimes it is a government that displaced or even fought that regime, as when a postcolonial state addresses wrongs of the colonial power it replaced. The theory must say whether and why these cases differ in standing and in what is owed.

Third, corporate remorse. States do not feel. If sincere remorse is constitutive of apology in the interpersonal case, the theory must either supply a state-level analogue or deny that remorse is constitutive -- and if it denies this, it must explain what distinguishes an apology from a press release expressing regret.

Fourth, the laundering objection. An apology can function to close the books cheaply: a performative that discharges obligation rather than acknowledging it, after which further claims are recast as ingratitude. A serious theory must have resources to distinguish apologies that reopen the moral ledger from apologies that launder it, and must identify which features -- naming the wrong specifically, non-repetition guarantees, material measures, the distribution of authorship and consent -- do that work.

Fifth, the decoupling of admission and compensation. Imagine two states: one apologizes in full moral terms and offers nothing further; the other pays substantial compensation while officially denying any wrongdoing. The theory must either rank these or give a principled reason for treating apology and rectification as independently valuable, and it should confront the possibility that the second state's payment does more good.

Sixth, acceptance. Apology, unlike acknowledgment, invites a response. When direct victims are dead, the theory must say who, if anyone, can accept or refuse on their behalf, and whether anyone can forgive.

Seventh, legal drafting. Real apologies are often written by lawyers to avoid admitting liability, producing formulas of deep regret that stop short of any admission of wrong. The theory must classify these: failed apologies, a distinct genre with its own value, or a corruption of the practice.

For each desideratum, give the motivating consideration and mark it as non-negotiable or as a contested commitment of a particular camp, noting where expressive and consequentialist approaches to apology would part ways. Then do two things I especially care about: identify which single desideratum eliminates the most accounts currently on offer, and determine whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. My suspicion is that the corporate-remorse requirement and the temporal problem interact badly -- that whatever makes remorse possible for a corporate agent is exactly what the passage of generations dissolves -- but I want the argument, not my suspicion. Close with a brief scoring of the main styles of account against your list: interpersonal-apology models extended to the state, forward-looking reconciliation accounts, and benefit- or complicity-based accounts of inherited responsibility. A table is fine for the scoring; the reasoning belongs in prose.
