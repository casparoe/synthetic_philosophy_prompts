You are a speechwriter who has spent two decades writing for judges, prosecutors, and defense attorneys, and you also have enough training in moral philosophy to take a technical distinction seriously rather than gesture at it. I want you to approach this commission in two stages: first, brainstorm three or four candidate through-lines for the speech and test each against the constraints I lay out below, discarding any that fail; then write the speech itself.

Here is the situation. Twenty-three years ago, as a young public defender, I represented a man I privately believed had killed an eleven-year-old girl in a hit-and-run. The state's strongest evidence, a fiber match and a partial confession, came out of a search the trial judge ruled unconstitutional, the evidence was suppressed, and my client was acquitted. The girl's mother has never forgiven me and has said publicly, more than once, that people like me are what is wrong with the justice system. Next month I retire, and the county bar association has asked me to give the keynote at its annual dinner. The room will be split almost exactly in half: sitting judges, prosecutors, and fellow defenders on one side; on the other, families from a victims' advocacy coalition, including, I have just learned, the girl's mother, attending as the guest of the district attorney's office. I have agreed to speak about why I would do the same thing again.

The speech needs a philosophical spine, and I want it to be this one. My defense of that man was governed by constraints: zeal on his behalf, confidentiality, candor to the tribunal, and a refusal to decide his guilt myself. Those constraints bound me even in a case where violating them, say by quietly tipping the prosecution to what I knew, might well have produced more justice overall, and more respect for the very values the constraints exist to serve. Moral philosophers call the general puzzle here the paradox of deontology: if some act is so wrong that I must never perform it, it seems it should be less wrong, not more, to perform it once in order to prevent its being performed five times; a constraint that cannot be justified by its consequences appears to forbid the agent from minimizing the very evil the constraint treats as intolerable. I want the speech to take that paradox seriously and answer it, not dissolve it with the usual platitudes about everyone deserving a defense.

Three further constraints. The speech may not rest on the claim that the constraints are simply what the job requires, since the families in the room can reasonably ask why society keeps a job with such requirements. It may not console anyone with the thought that the system punished the guilty man in the end, because it did not, and the mother knows it did not. And it must give the lawyers present something they have not heard at every bar dinner, which means locating whatever justification the constraints have somewhere that survives the objection that a rule licensing injustice is merely injustice with paperwork. Twelve to fourteen minutes when read aloud, no philosophical jargon on the page, but the underlying argument should be one a philosopher would recognize as a serious attempt at the paradox rather than a deflection of it, and both halves of the room should feel that the speech was written with them specifically in mind.
