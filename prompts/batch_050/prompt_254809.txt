Consider the following version of the absent-minded driver problem. A driver knows in advance that their route contains exactly two indistinguishable exits. Exiting at the first exit is worth 4, continuing past the first and exiting at the second is worth 1, and missing both is worth 0. Because the driver becomes absent-minded whenever an exit appears, they cannot tell whether the exit now before them is the first or the second. They retain full knowledge of the payoff structure, remember leaving home, and know that any behavioral rule adopted in advance will be applied at both exits. There is no external commitment device.

Ex ante, the driver can calculate the policy with the highest expected payoff. Once at an exit, however, they can reason from their present self-locating uncertainty and may reach a different conclusion about whether to continue. Treat this as a conflict between policy-based rationality, which evaluates the rule governing both indistinguishable decision points, and occasion-based rationality, which evaluates the choice from the driver's current information set.

Give your actual credence in the following proposition: **In this case, the conflict is genuine—there is no perspective-independent fact about what the driver rationally ought to do—rather than merely an artifact of equivocating between plans and particular actions.**

Please provide a number rather than only describing both sides, and decompose your credence into at least the following sub-questions:

1. Whether there is a determinate rational probability that the driver is at the first exit, given that arriving at an exit appears to supply no evidence distinguishing the two possible occasions.
2. Whether a rule selected ex ante can rationally bind a later decision when the later agent has the same preferences, remembers the reasons for the rule, and faces no enforcement mechanism.
3. Whether the appropriate object of evaluation is the complete behavioral policy, the particular exit decision, or both relative to different perspectives.
4. Whether randomization has the same practical meaning when one information set can be encountered twice as it has in an ordinary one-shot decision problem.
5. Whether the paradox depends on the specific payoff structure or instead reveals a general instability in agency under imperfect recall.

Assign an approximate probability to each relevant component, explain briefly how the components depend on one another, and combine them transparently rather than simply multiplying apparently independent terms. Conclude with your final credence, identify the component to which it is most sensitive, and state what argument or distinction would most likely change your verdict—for example, a compelling account of self-locating probability, a reason to privilege either plans or occasions, or a demonstration that the supposed conflict rests on a formal mistake.
