I am a novelist at work on a book whose narrator was raised in isolation by a father who taught her English from a deliberately doctored dictionary, so that as an adult she uses a number of perfectly common words with systematically shifted meanings -- not nonsense, not a private language, just stable idiosyncratic usage that everyone around her must figure out on the fly. Much of the plot turns on a second character slowly learning to understand her. My problem is practical: my editor keeps flagging her dialogue as inconsistent or as simple error, and early readers split between thinking she means something different by a word and thinking she believes something false about the world. I have come to understand that philosophers have a worked-out treatment of exactly this situation -- Davidson's radical interpretation and the principle of charity, the question of when an interpreter should read a strange utterance as deviant meaning and when as mistaken belief -- but the versions I have encountered are stated for the general project of interpretation, not for someone in my position, who must make the call repeatedly, scene by scene, and who, unlike a real interpreter, controls all the evidence and can plant whatever clues readers will need.

What I want is a procedure I can actually run, in under half an hour, on any given utterance of hers: the questions to ask in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure concludes this is a difference in what she means, the point at which it concludes she is simply wrong about something, and -- since she is a fictional construction with no fact of the matter beyond what I write -- a rule for what to do when the evidence I have planted genuinely underdetermines the reading, where I must simply choose and then revise the surrounding text to match. Please be explicit about which distinctions in the underlying theory have to survive the simplification for the procedure to work and which I can safely drop, and mark honestly the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as steps. I would also like the procedure run in reverse as a design tool: given the reading I intend, what evidence must the surrounding scenes contain -- which circumstances of utterance, which patterns of assent and dissent, which successful and failed interactions -- for a charitable reader to land there rather than on the rival reading?

Then apply the procedure to three cases from my draft. First, she calls the midday meal dinner and the evening meal supper, consistently, in all contexts. Second, she says her father is alive, years after his death, and organizes her life accordingly; I cannot decide whether she means something defensible by the word or is simply deluded, and the book is ambiguous on purpose, so tell me what each disambiguation would cost. Third, she calls casual acquaintances friends but refuses the word for the two people she loves most -- a case I have already written as a meaning-shift, though I suspect the procedure may tell me I have written a belief or an attitude instead, and I want the uncomfortable answer if that is what it gives. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: where the theory itself runs out, and what no amount of planted evidence can settle for a reader.
