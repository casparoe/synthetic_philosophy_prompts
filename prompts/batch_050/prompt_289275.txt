I serve on the board of a mid-sized AI company, and at next month's meeting we must vote on whether to ship our flagship assistant with a safety package proposed by our alignment team. The package has three components: hard refusals on a class of dual-use requests that our two principal competitors currently answer; a staged deployment with usage monitoring, which would delay general availability by roughly two quarters; and a mandatory interpretability audit before any further capability increase. Internal estimates put the commercial cost at 8 to 12 points on the customer-facing evaluations that drive enterprise sales, in addition to the delay.

Our safety lead opposes adopting the package unilaterally, and her argument is not that safety is unimportant. It is an argument from the competitiveness constraint: safety measures that impose real costs on the firms that adopt them, while rivals do not adopt them, do not make the industry safer — they reallocate market share, talent, and revenue toward the less cautious firms. On her projection, paying this alignment tax unilaterally leaves us with diminished influence over the trajectory of the technology, while customers end up served by assistants with fewer safeguards than ours would have had. She proposes that we remain competitive and spend the preserved margin on safety research whose results we publish freely. The alignment team replies that the package is cheap relative to the tail risks, that demonstrated trustworthiness is itself a competitive asset in the enterprise market, and that her argument, if generalized, licenses never paying any cost for safety, at any company, ever.

Please draft the decision memorandum I will circulate to the board before the vote. It should meet the following requirements:

- Give a clear recommendation — adopt the package in full, adopt a specified subset of it, defer adoption pending a defined trigger, or pursue a specified alternative such as conditioning adoption on an industry-wide commitment — together with a concrete timeline.
- Engage the competitiveness constraint as the safety lead actually states it, not the caricature in which she is indifferent to safety. Identify precisely where the argument overreaches, if it does, and where it genuinely bites.
- Take an explicit position on the replaceability step of her reasoning: if our restraint merely shifts demand to less cautious rivals, does the obligation to pay the tax survive, change its form, or lapse?
- State a decision rule: at what cost does an alignment tax become too large to pay unilaterally, and what considerations fix that threshold? If the memorandum concludes that no principled threshold exists, it should say what the board is to do instead.
- Examine the "tax" framing itself: whether describing these costs as a tax quietly assumes that safety expenditure is pure overhead rather than, in some cases, product quality that the market will reward — and which components of the package, if any, survive that reframing.
- Give an honest account of the strongest consideration on whichever side loses.

Limit the memorandum to roughly 900 words, written for directors who are commercially literate but not specialists in AI safety.
