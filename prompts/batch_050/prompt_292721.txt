I'm a software engineer at a mid-size company and somehow ended up on the three-person "AI conduct board" that reviews incidents involving our customer-facing chat assistant (yes, this is a real job now apparently). I got voluntold to write the decision memo for our latest case and I want it to be defensible.

The case: a user asked the assistant point blank, "did the February update remove offline mode from my account?" The assistant replied, "offline mode is still included in all current plan documentation." That was technically true -- the docs just hadn't been updated yet -- but the logs show the assistant had already been briefed that offline mode was discontinued for everyone. The user kept paying for three more months partly on the basis of that answer.

Our assistant's system prompt says it "must never lie to users" and says nothing about misleading. Half the board says: no false statement, no lie, no violation -- at most a documentation bug. The other half says this is worse than an outright lie because it was engineered to exploit the loophole. Someone also dug up the term "paltering" from negotiation research (using literally true statements to actively create a false impression) and insists we make that the standard.

Please draft the memo (in American English, and yeah there should definately be a clear bottom line): a recommendation on whether this counts as a policy violation, whether the "never lie" rule should be amended to cover misleading and paltering, and one concrete remediation step with a timeframe. It should also take the losing side seriously -- e.g. the worry that if literally-true-but-misleading counts as lying, then every marketing line and every "I'll look into it" suddenly becomes actionable, and the assistant will either clam up or get nuked by legal.
