I am a novelist, and my current manuscript in progress follows an aeronautics engineer who authorizes a fleet of delivery drones over a residential district, knowing that roughly one in forty thousand will fall from the sky; none does during the period the novel covers, and the question haunting the book is whether she has done anything wrong at all. In the course of writing it I became preoccupied with the philosophical question underneath the plot, and I have drafted an essay for a literary magazine, aimed at a general but thoughtful readership, defending a thesis I now worry is either obvious or obviously wrong: that imposing a risk of harm on others without their consent is a wrong in itself, of the same family as trespass, and that this wrong is committed in full at the moment the risk is imposed, regardless of whether any harm ever materializes. My thought is that we misclassify such cases when we treat risk imposition as merely a factor in calculating expected harm, because what the drone operator does is not simply to lower the expected welfare of the people below but to press their bodies and homes into service as the unacknowledged scenery of her project, without asking. I lean on an analogy with walking uninvited through someone's garden without damaging anything: we do not excuse the trespasser on the ground that the expected harm was zero or that the walk was socially useful, and I claim the person below the drone is wronged in the same way even on the lucky days. I then draw the implication, which I state rather than shrink from, that a great deal of ordinary life — driving, emitting, congregating while infectious, releasing algorithms whose failure modes one cannot enumerate — involves pro tanto wrongs of this kind, and that these are excused or justified, when they are, not by pointing to net expected benefit but by something more like reciprocity: the fact that each of us stands in roughly the same relation to the others, imposing and bearing comparable risks under a practice from which all derive comparable benefit.

The specific points on which I would value comment are these. First, the trespass analogy: I suspect it carries more argumentative weight in my draft than it can bear, since trespass protects an antecedent property right whereas the person under the drone seems to have a claim of a different and perhaps weaker kind — but I am unsure whether this difference defeats the analogy or merely refines it. Second, the reciprocity clause: a philosopher friend glanced at the draft and said it smuggles in something like a fair-terms-of-cooperation test that does much more work than my trespass framing, and threatens to make the essay two theories welded together rather than one. Third, and most worrying to me as a writer, the conclusion seems to condemn nearly everyone nearly all the time, and while I am prepared to accept that as a feature — the novel's heroine is sympathetic precisely because her guilt is the ordinary kind — I cannot tell whether a view on which ordinary social life is a dense fabric of small, excused wrongs is a deep insight into the moral texture of modernity or a reductio of the premise. There is also a puzzle I raise but do not resolve: if the wrong is complete at the moment of imposition, then compensating victims after a crash seems to leave the original wrong untouched, which would explain, I think, why restitution in such cases feels to the victims' families beside the point even when generous.

What I would like from you is not a verdict on whether my thesis is fashionable but a stress test of the argument as sketched: which steps are doing real work, which are rhetorical scaffolding I have mistaken for argument, and where a careful opponent would plant her feet. If the view collapses into the expected-harm picture I set out against, I would rather know before the essay is typeset; and if it survives but only at the cost of the reciprocity clause absorbing the entire theory, I would like that stated plainly. I am also open to the suggestion that the novelist's instinct I am trusting — that the families' sense of grievance is data, not noise — is precisely where philosophers would say I go wrong, and if so I would like to understand why without the discussion collapsing into a citation duel, since I am writing for readers who have never opened an ethics journal and, honestly, neither have I.
