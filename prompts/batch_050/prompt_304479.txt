I've been reading about Newcomb's problem and I keep running into the same wall. One-boxers and two-boxers have been going at it for fifty-odd years with the same setup, same payoffs, same arguments recycled on both sides, and nobody budges. My working hypothesis is that they aren't really disagreeing about what to do in front of the boxes at all: one side is answering "which act causes the best outcome" and the other is answering "which act is the best evidence that you'll get the good outcome," and since those come apart only in weird predictor cases, the real fight is over what 'rational choice' is even supposed to track. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a genuine substantive disagreement buried under there -- about causation, about what a reliable prediction does to your evidential situation, about whether the predictor scenario is even coherent as stated? Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, what each side would have to concede in order to lose, and whether anything could realistically settle it -- a sharper formalization, a result in decision theory, a distinction nobody's made yet. And if my hypothesis is muddled or the problem as standardly framed is broken, just say so plainly rather than politely working within it.
