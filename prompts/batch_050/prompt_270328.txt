I teach an undergraduate course in philosophy of language (about 16 students, one 75-minute session, mid-semester). Design an in-class activity that makes students feel the pull of semantic externalism before anyone names it: the idea that two speakers who are internally identical — same experiences, same dispositions, same brain states, as far as they can tell — can nonetheless mean different things by the same word, because of facts about their environment or their linguistic community that neither is in a position to state. The name Putnam and the phrase Twin Earth should not be uttered until the debrief; assume roughly a third of the class has already encountered the thought experiment and will try to spoil the reveal, so the design needs a role or rule that neutralizes the knowers without alerting the rest.

The activity should take the form of a staged game with real choices and small stakes (points, not money), in which participants must classify, trade, or coordinate over samples whose status depends on facts hidden from them, and where it later emerges that two groups following identical rules with identical evidence were answering different questions all along. I want the game to generate both components of the view separately, not as a blur: first, the environment-dependence of reference (the same word, fixed by different stuff in different settings); second, the division of linguistic labor (speakers successfully using a term they cannot themselves apply reliably, because their community contains experts or practices that carry the burden for them). Design the timeline so these two moments land in sequence and feel different from the inside.

Please specify: the materials (which must be purchasable or printable for under $20); the exact rules, including what participants are and are not allowed to communicate; the scoring scheme; a minute-by-minute timeline; the mechanics of the reveal; and a debrief that maps particular moments of the game onto the corresponding moves in the externalist argument. Include at least one scripted intervention for a designated skeptic role: a student briefed in advance to argue, at a fixed point, that the game shows only ambiguity or ignorance rather than a difference in meaning, so that the class has to locate what, if anything, blocks that deflationary reading. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and be frank about which feature of the externalist intuition the activity cannot reproduce in the classroom and must instead be supplied by discussion afterwards.
