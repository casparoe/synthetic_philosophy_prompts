In my constitutional theory seminar, the Ulysses analogy does almost all of the justificatory work whenever entrenchment comes up. The constitution, the story goes, is a polity's rope: a people in a cool moment, anticipating that future majorities will face the siren song of demagogues, inflationary finance, or emergency powers, binds itself in advance so that it cannot act on those temptations later. My professor leans on it to argue that entrenched rights, supermajoritarian amendment rules, independent central banks, and constitutional debt brakes are not anti-democratic constraints but instances of rational self-government — the democratically authorized self, master of its own future weakness. Several classmates find this decisive, and I want to know whether they should.

My request is an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on constitutionalism itself. Please give me the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of Ulysses, the crew, the ropes, the sirens, the wax in the ears, and the prior knowledge of what lies ahead. Then lay out the disanalogies and sort them by whether they matter for the intended use. The ones I have noticed so far: Ulysses binds himself, whereas constitutional framers bind later generations who never ordered the ropes; Ulysses wanted to hear the song while being prevented from acting on it, but entrenchment does not let future majorities enjoy their temptation safely — it denies them the object altogether; the crew rows on with plugged ears and no say in the arrangement, which maps awkwardly onto judges, central bankers, and constitutional courts; and Ulysses knew precisely which temptation was coming and when, while framers bind successors against circumstances they cannot foresee, which seems to convert rational self-binding into something more like rule by the dead. Perhaps most fundamentally, precommitment presupposes that the binder remains the same self with stable preferences across the interval of weakness, and it is unclear that a polity across generations has that kind of diachronic identity at all.

The deliverable I want is: which of these disanalogies defeat the conclusion that entrenchment is rational self-government, which are merely cosmetic, and whether the analogy was carrying the whole argument or can be repaired by re-describing the constitution's role. If a better analogy exists for what entrenchment actually does, name it. Please also flag where your own audit is most likely to be wrong — for instance, if you think one of my disanalogies is overstated, say which one and what consideration would reverse your verdict. Keep the whole response under 700 words.
