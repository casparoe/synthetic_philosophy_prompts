Since retiring from thirty-odd years as an experimental physicist, I have been working my way through the philosophy of time, mostly out of irritation at how casually my old colleagues used to talk about the "block universe." I recently read J. M. E. McTaggart's 1908 paper "The Unreality of Time" (Mind, vol. 17), and one passage in particular has lodged itself in my head:

"It would, I suppose, be universally admitted that time involves change. A particular thing, indeed, may exist unchanged through any amount of time. But when we ask what we mean by saying that there were different moments of time, or a certain duration of time, through which the thing was the same, we find that we mean that it remained the same while other things were changing. A universe in which nothing whatever changed (including the thoughts of the conscious beings in it) would be a timeless universe."

Whatever one makes of his conclusion, I find McTaggart's insistence bracing: change is the whole game, and change, for him, lives in the A series of past, present, and future rather than in the mere B-series ordering of earlier and later. To test whether I have actually understood the presentism–eternalism dispute this passage feeds into, I would like you to write me a piece of satire rather than another earnest exposition. Write the internal claims-handling manual of the "Presentist Mutual Assurance Society," a fictional insurance company founded on strict presentist principles: since only the present exists, the company's actuaries and claims adjusters must take seriously the awkward consequences. The manual should include numbered policy provisions, a schedule of optional riders (the Growing Block rider, which extends coverage to the past but never the future; the rival Eternalist comprehensive plan, under which every loss is covered because every loss eternally exists, though the premiums are likewise eternally due), and one specimen denial letter from an adjuster to a policyholder whose home was burgled last Tuesday and whose loss, regrettably, no longer exists.

The humor must come from the metaphysics being done correctly, not from caricature: the adjusters should grapple in deadpan legalese with the truthmaker problem (what presently existing fact makes a claim about last Tuesday true?), with the difficulty of causal responsibility when causes are always past, and with the company's preamble quoting McTaggart's point that a changeless world is a timeless one as justification for reclassifying certain disputes as "outside time" and therefore outside coverage. Keep it around 600 words, fully deadpan, funny to someone who knows the A-series/B-series debate and still legible to someone who doesn't, and make sure a metaphysician reading it feels the satire lands on presentism fairly — the joke being that the company's position is perfectly defensible in the seminar room and perfectly outrageous in a letter to a burglary victim.
