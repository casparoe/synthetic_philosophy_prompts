A colleague in my game theory reading group has an argument that he thinks dissolves the backward induction paradox by showing it is much worse than a paradox, and I have been unable to find the weak joint. I will state it as he gave it to me.

(1) In a finite centipede game, if both players are instrumentally rational and their rationality is common knowledge, then backward induction delivers a unique solution: the first player ends the game at the first node.

(2) The reasoning in (1) makes no use of the particular payoff structure of the centipede; it uses only the finiteness of the game, the existence of a known final node, and common knowledge of rationality.

(3) Therefore, in any finite interaction with a known final round, instrumentally rational players who share common knowledge of their rationality must act myopically at every stage: the logic of the last round unravels every earlier one.

(4) Ordinary life is full of finite interactions with a known final round in which people do not act myopically: a shopkeeper gives honest change to a tourist who is leaving the country tomorrow, colleagues cooperate on a project right up to a retirement date everyone knows, and departing students return borrowed books on their last day of enrollment.

(5) These actions are not mistakes or sentimentality; they are often the very behavior a cool-headed person would choose, and the parties involved typically know this about one another.

(6) So the antecedent conditions of (3) are frequently satisfied in real life while its consequent is false.

(7) Therefore, either backward induction reasoning is unsound as a general method, or common knowledge of rationality between two people is impossible in principle — and since the second disjunct would wreck most of game theory anyway, the centipede "paradox" is actually a reductio of the standard apparatus.

My colleague presents this as a dilemma that the literature refuses to face. I suspect something goes wrong between (2) and (4) — the generalization feels too quick — but I cannot pin down whether the failure is in the move from the centipede's payoff structure to all finite games, in the treatment of what "common knowledge of rationality" requires at nodes that are never reached, or somewhere else entirely. If forced to keep things, I would most like to keep (1) and (4), and I am willing to sacrifice (5) if that is where the cheapest repair lies.

Which step should I reject, and why does it look plausible? Please price the main alternatives — including the options of rejecting (2), rejecting the inference from (4) and (5) to (6), and biting the bullet on one horn of (7) — so I can see what each repair costs. Limit your answer to 400 words.
