So I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, and I keep bouncing off this debate about whether anything besides human minds really has intentionality, i.e. whether stuff like words, maps, or my code is about anything in itself, or only about stuff because we interpret it that way. I've read a bit: Searle's Chinese Room argument says computers only ever have "derived" intentionality and never the "original" kind that brains supposedly have. Dennett fires back with this example he calls the two-bitser, a coin-sorting machine built for US quarters that, when shipped to Panama, ends up sorting a local coin instead, and his point (I think) is that what the machine is "really" detecting is underdetermined and only settled by its history or selection context, same as us. Which sounds like he's saying even our intentionality works like the machine's, and Searle thinks that's absurd. Both of them feel weirdly right and wrong to me at once.

Here's what I actually want. Before I read any more of the primary literature, write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of original vs. derived intentionality has to deliver for it to be worth taking seriously. I'm thinking of things like:

- which cases it has to classify in a principled, non-arbitrary way (a thermostat, a chess engine, a vending machine, a neuron firing, a human belief, and -- the one I actually care about -- a large language model, which neither Dennett's coin sorter nor Searle's room quite captures since it produces novel content at scale)
- which puzzles it must dissolve or at least explain rather than just stipulate away -- e.g. misrepresentation (how can a state be about something it gets wrong?), the underdetermination of content (Dennett's point that no finite set of behaviors pins down what a system "means"), and whatever the symbol-grounding worry is really demanding
- which distinctions it has to respect and which it's allowed to collapse (is the original/derived distinction itself load-bearing or is it doing Searle's argumentative work for free?)

For each desideratum, give the case or intuition behind it, and mark which ones you think are genuinely non-negotiable vs. which are just the hobbyhorses of one camp (e.g. I suspect "a theory must explain why MY thoughts are obviously about things" is doing a lot of unexamined work for Searle). Most importantly: flag any pair or subset of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied, because that's the part that would actually be a result rather than a wishlist.

Then, briefly, score Dennett's stance and Searle's against the list, and if teleosemantics or some other third option belongs in the scoring, mention it in a sentence or two -- but keep the weight on the criteria themselves. The purpose of the theory, for my purposes: good enough to tell me whether "the model means something by its outputs" is a claim with teeth or just a manner of speaking.
