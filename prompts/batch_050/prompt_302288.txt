I recently listened to a podcast about Robert Brandom and the idea that what a word means is a matter of the inferences a speaker is prepared to draw with it, rather than the things in the world it picks out. I have no formal training in philosophy, but the claim struck me as bold enough that I want to see it tested properly, so I would like you to construct a thought experiment for me, built to order rather than lifted from the literature.

The scenario I want is this: two speakers, or two small communities, who use one and the same word in ways that are extensionally identical — in every situation that actually arises, they apply the word to exactly the same things and withhold it from exactly the same things — yet whose inferential commitments involving the word differ, so that an inferentialist account must say the word means something different in the two mouths, whilst a referential or truth-conditional account says it means the same. Some constraints. First, the word should be an ordinary English word or a plausible piece of everyday technical vocabulary, not an invented term. Second, no one in the story may be stupid, misinformed, or simply mistaken: the inferential difference must flow from a principled disagreement or from divergent background commitments, not from error, since otherwise the case proves nothing about meaning as opposed to ignorance. Third, the extensional sameness must be generated by the set-up rather than merely asserted — explain why it is that circumstances never bring the difference to the surface.

After presenting the scenario (a paragraph or so), please do the following, in clear American English: walk through the verdict each of the two accounts gives and why; identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed; and finally address the obvious countermove — namely, that the inferential differences would surely produce divergent applications in merely hypothetical circumstances, so the referentialist can recover a meaning difference after all by considering counterfactual cases. Does that move collapse the thought experiment, or does it concede something important to the inferentialist? I would rather have an honest answer on that last point than a tidy one.
