I chair my hospital's pharmacy and therapeutics committee. Six years ago we approved an anesthetic for off-label use in a narrow class of chronic pain patients, and since then the pain service has come back roughly every year asking to extend approval to a slightly broader patient group. Each time the argument has been honestly pretty good: the proposed group is clinically indistinguishable from the last one we approved -- same monitoring, same risk profile, just one notch further from the original trial population. Nobody cut corners or lied. But compare where we are now to where we started and the drug is being used in patients the trials never touched, at cumulative exposure nobody has studied. A seventh request is now on the table and once again I can't point to any real difference between group six and group seven.

One committee member says this is exactly the self-torturer setup (each step is a small improvement or at least a wash, but the sequence ends somewhere nobody would choose directly), so we're stuck either approving forever or making an arbitrary stop. Another says a committee can only ever judge the request actually in front of it, and anything else is punishing this applicant for our past decisions.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete way to handle this request and future ones, and it has to actually engage the rationality problem rather than dodge it: if every individual approval was reasonable, what makes it legitimate to refuse the next one? Is a committee allowed to act on a pattern-level judgment ("we don't want to end up there") that contradicts every pairwise comparison it would make along the way? And take the second member's objection seriously -- don't just declare we need a bright line, explain where a committee gets the standing to draw one at a point where, by hypothesis, nothing changes.
