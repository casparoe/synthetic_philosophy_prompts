So I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book that's giving me trouble, and I've wandered into a philosophy problem by accident. Here's the setup. There's a thought experiment from cognitive science / philosophy of mind where you imagine an ant colony: no single ant is smart, each one just follows simple rules, but the colony as a whole does things that look uncannily like thinking -- it forages, it fights wars, it "decides" to move the nest, it remembers where food was yesterday even though every individual ant involved yesterday is doing something else today. Some people argue the colony is literally a mind, just implemented in ants instead of neurons, and that the "symbol level" of the colony's behavior can have beliefs and goals the same way the symbol level of your brain does. Other people say no, that's a loose metaphor, the colony doesn't *experience* anything, there's nobody home.

My novel: I'm trying to write a book where the protagonist is a city. Not a book *about* a city -- the city is the character. No single human in it gets a POV chapter. The "scenes" are crowds, traffic flows, market days, a rumor spreading through three neighborhoods. My agent read twenty pages and said "this is just vignettes, there's nobody to care about," which is, I realized, *exactly* the philosopher's objection to the ant colony having a mind. So now I want to know if the philosophy can tell me whether my book can work.

Here's the actual ask. Take the debate over whether a colony-level mind is real -- the functionalist case (if it processes information and has goals at the right level of description, it's a mind) versus the "that's just a metaphor, no subject is home" case -- and translate it into the vocabulary I actually think in: point of view, interiority, free indirect discourse, ensemble casts, the narrator as a character, whether a chorus can carry a novel, reader identification. I live in this vocabulary, please don't make me live in yours. Do both sides of the debate in my terms, as faithfully as you can.

Then the part I care most about: a residue report. Which parts of the philosophy debate translate cleanly into novelist-craft terms, which parts you had to fudge, and which parts just have no expression in craft vocabulary at all. And tell me which side the residue cuts against -- is the untranslatable stuff something the philosophy knows that novelists don't, or is it fog that the craft translation exposes? Because I have a suspicion that "is there somebody home in the city" might secretly just be the question "can a reader get inside this book," and if those are the same question I'll feel very smug.

At the very end, give me one line in exactly this form: \answer{...} where ... is a short phrase saying whether my novel's premise, by the lights of the translation, commits me to the city literally having a mind or not. Something like "yes, committed" or "no, metaphor only" or whatever your honest bottom line is.
