I'm putting together the handout for the medieval unit of my history of philosophy survey, and this year I want to do something different with Avicenna's flying man instead of just rehashing my old lecture notes. For anyone rusty: a man is created fully formed, instantaneously, suspended in midair, limbs spread so nothing touches anything, eyes covered, hearing nothing, in still air so he has no tactile input at all. Avicenna's claim is that this man would nonetheless be certain of his own existence and of his self, and since he affirms the self while affirming nothing bodily, the self (the soul) must be something other than the body. I was arguing with a colleague over coffee about whether the argument deserves its reputation and realized my handout's objections section is basically stale -- it's the same four objections everyone raises, and students can find them anywhere.

So here is my list of standard objections, the ones I explicitly do NOT need: (1) the conceivability-possibility gap -- even granting the scenario is coherently imaginable, imaginability of disembodied self-awareness doesn't establish that the self is actually separable from the body, any more than imagining water without H2O shows water could be something else; (2) the negative-awareness objection -- the flying man doesn't affirm the absence of his body, he just fails to notice it, and failing to notice X while noticing Y doesn't show Y can exist without X; (3) the existence-essence objection -- even if everything works, the argument delivers only certainty that he exists, not any knowledge of what he is, so the leap to the soul's incorporeality outruns what the experience could possibly support; (4) the incoherence objection -- the scenario presupposes a subject with functioning awareness who was never embodied, but if cognition essentially depends on a brain then the stipulation of the thought experiment already assumes the dualist conclusion it's meant to establish, so it begs the question against the materialist.

What I want is a fifth objection, genuinely different in structure from these four -- not a rewording, not a special case, and not a hybrid of two of them. Tell me what it is, reconstruct it charitably enough that I could put it on the handout without embarrassing myself, and then give me your honest verdict on whether it actually damages Avicenna's argument or just looks like it does. Bonus if you can say how Avicenna (or someone sympathetic) would most plausibly respond, since a couple of my students every term are pretty taken with the flying man and I'd like to have the reply ready.
