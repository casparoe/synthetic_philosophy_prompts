My book club picked Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" for next month, and I volunteered to lead the discussion. Everyone always reads it as a straight utilitarianism-vs-deontology story (one kid suffers, everyone else is happy, is the trade worth it), but I want to push the group somewhere weirder: read it through the lens of the torture-vs-dust-specks problem, i.e. whether astronomically many tiny goods or harms can ever aggregate to outweigh one severe one. In Omelas the child's misery is one big concentrated harm, and the town's flourishing is a huge pile of small diffuse goods, so it's basically the dust specks structure with the signs flipped. What I want to know is: does the story actually engage with the aggregation question, or does it rig things by making the child's suffering maximally vivid and concrete while the citizens' happiness stays soft-focus and generic? If Le Guin is winning the argument through salience rather than through the math, is that a flaw in the story or is it a point about how moral intuition actually works? Also take seriously the inversion: if Omelas's happiness required every citizen to get a dust speck in the eye every morning instead of one child locked in the cellar, does anyone walk away? Does the story itself give us the resources to answer that, or does it quietly depend on the harm being concentrated and hidden?

And what about the walkers themselves -- on the aggregation reading, is walking away a coherent response at all? They don't free the child, they don't change the ledger of harms, they just decline to be on the benefiting side of it, which looks more like a deontological flinch than an answer to the aggregation question. I'd like a reading that's faithful in both directions: don't flatten the story into a thought experiment with prose around it, but also don't let it off the hook philosophically. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence (does the story illuminate the aggregation problem or evade it), then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating my question. Point me to specific moments in the story the group should look at.
