I had a long argument last week with a friend who works in machine learning, and it ended in a stalemate I want to convert into a fundable project. Her claim: "factored cognition" idealizations of deliberation — the picture where a question is answered by recursively decomposing it into sub-questions answered by copies of a reasoner, with the limit of arbitrarily deep recursion treated as the idealized verdict — are scientifically empty, because they assume unlimited decomposition depth and perfectly lossless handoffs between copies, whereas real deliberation (human or artificial) is shallow and lossy at every step. My claim: an idealization can be false of every actual system and still do real explanatory and normative work, the way frictionless planes or infinite populations do — but when she pressed me on what work this particular idealization does, and how we'd know if it were misleading rather than merely idealized, I didn't have a good answer. I'm now applying for a six-month early-career research grant and want a two-page proposal that turns that stalemate into a tractable project.

The angle I want the proposal to take: instead of asking whether the idealization is "realistic," ask which properties of a deliberative process are depth-stable and which are depth-fragile — that is, which features of a reasoner's eventual output are invariant as recursion depth increases, and which flip or drift depending on depth, on the quality of the base reasoner, or on the decomposition strategy. A subsidiary question I care about: under what conditions does iterated amplify-and-distill converge to a verdict independent of where you started, and when instead does the starting reasoner act like a basin of attraction that the idealization silently inherits — so that two groups could both claim to be "extrapolating deliberation" while extrapolating different things? The proposal should state these questions precisely enough that a hostile reviewer (say, my friend) couldn't dismiss them as definitional; explain why neither the standard "idealizations are harmless fictions" line nor the "test everything empirically" line settles them, and why this is a conceptual project rather than an empirical benchmark; give a month-by-month plan (formalizing depth-stability for simple deliberation trees, constructing minimal examples where depth changes the verdict, checking whether the fragility results depend on assumptions about how sub-questions are generated); and say concretely what a deliverable looks like for people actually building or evaluating such systems, plus what would count as the project failing — e.g., if the distinction between depth-stable and depth-fragile properties collapses or turns out to track something trivial.

Please write the proposal itself, not advice about how to write one. Begin directly with the proposal's title and text — no preface, no commentary, no restating of my request.
