At a city-council hearing on maintaining a flood barrier, one member argues that harms in 2200 should count for less simply because they are later: temporal distance, she says, works like spatial distance—just as we are often more moved by suffering nearby than by equally severe suffering abroad, we may legitimately care more about suffering in the near future. Audit the analogy. Give an explicit mapping between spatial and temporal distance, identify where it breaks down, and distinguish discounting merely because a harm is later from discounting because of uncertainty, economic growth, opportunity costs, or changes in future people’s needs. Even if some spatial partiality is morally acceptable, does the analogy justify a positive pure time preference? If not, what conclusion about long-term policy can it still support?
