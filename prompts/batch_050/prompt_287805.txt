Suppose a small interdisciplinary institute has funding for one five-year research program — three historians, two social theorists, and one comparativist — dedicated to the question of how old nations are. By that I mean the dispute between the modernist school associated with Hobsbawm, Anderson, and Gellner, for whom nations and national traditions are largely products of the last two or three centuries, and their ethnosymbolist and perennialist critics, for whom modern nations typically grow out of much older ethnic communities and shared memories. The program cannot pursue everything, and the director wants a defended research agenda rather than a survey of the debate.

Which open questions should the program take up, and in what order? Please consider at least the following candidates, though you may add to, merge, or replace them: (1) the premodern consciousness question — whether ordinary people in, say, fourteenth-century France or Song-dynasty China had anything recognizable as national identification, and what evidence could bear on this given that surviving sources are overwhelmingly elite; (2) the mechanism question — whether print capitalism, the administrative state, mass schooling, or conscription was causally primary in producing national consciousness, and whether these can even be disentangled or whether the question needs reframing; (3) the intentionality question — Hobsbawm's account of invented tradition sometimes reads as if traditions are consciously fabricated by identifiable actors, yet many look emergent, so whether the modernist thesis survives if most invention is unintentional; (4) the negative-case question — why some language communities with plausible nation-building material never became nations, and why some multilingual polities did, and which side of the debate better explains the failures rather than the successes; and (5) the downstream normative question — whether anything about the legitimacy of present-day national claims actually depends on how the antiquity debate is resolved, or whether the debate is normatively inert and the program should say so explicitly.

For the ordering itself, state your criteria — I suggest at minimum how many other questions depend on a given answer, how tractable the question is for this particular team within five years, and how neglected it is relative to its importance — and discuss the trade-offs where the criteria conflict. For each item, indicate what finding or development over the course of the program would move it up or down the ranking. Finally, say what would change your mind about the agenda as a whole: which argument or piece of evidence would lead you to reorder it substantially, and where you take your own reasoning to be most likely to fail — for instance, where the ranking leans on a contested reading of one side of the debate or on an assumption about what historical evidence can in principle show.
