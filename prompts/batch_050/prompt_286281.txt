I came across this line in Simone Weil's essay On the Abolition of All Political Parties (1943) and can't stop chewing on it: "In the Anglo-Saxon world, political parties have an element of game, of sport, which is only conceivable in an institution of aristocratic origin, whereas in institutions that were plebeian from the start, everything must always be serious." I've got no philosophy background, I just watch alot of football and read the news too much, and I've been testing my gut verdicts on group loyalty against that passage. Roughly:

- Roaring that the ref is blind when he clearly got it right, purely because he's against my club: totally fine, part of the fun.
- Ticking my party's box at election time without knowing what they've actually promised: not fine, though I do it.
- Defending an MP on my side who's been caught lying because the other lot are worse: corrosive, I hate catching myself at it.
- Swearing my brother didn't cheat at cards when I watched him do it: wrong, but I wouldn't judge anyone for it.
- Backing my department's project over the other team's at work when theirs is better: a bit bad, but basically human.
- Fans of a band piling onto a critic online til she deletes her account: feels dreadful. But I honestly can't say how it differs from the football case, which I'm fine with. That pair is what's bugging me.

So: is there one principle that sorts these the way I sort them? Weil seems to think the game element in a group changes its moral character — but does the sport framing make tribalism safer, or more dangerous (you do real harm while thinking it's a laugh)? If her thought captures my verdicts, show me how; if some simpler principle captures most of them but convicts the football one, say so straight out and tell me which verdict I'd have to drop. And say what would change your mind — what case or argument would push you to a different principle, or where your reading of the passage is most likely to be off.
