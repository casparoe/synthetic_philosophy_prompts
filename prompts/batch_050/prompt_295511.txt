In the historiography seminar I help run, the debate about objectivity in history has reached a standstill that I find telling. One side defends the Rankean ideal: the historian's task is to recover how things actually were, and the fact that this is difficult does not make it incoherent. The other side follows Carr and Hayden White: the facts of the past only become a history through selection, arrangement, and emplotment by the historian, so the idea of the past as it actually was, independent of any narrative, is a phantom. What strikes me is that neither side seems to deny anything the other asserts: the Rankeans concede that every history is constructed and selective, and the sceptics concede that some narratives are straightforwardly false and ruled out by the evidence. Yet both continue to talk as though something large is at stake. My hypothesis is that the disagreement has migrated, unnoticed, from a question about the past to a question about what histories are for -- that the two sides are really disputing which failures of historiography matter most, and calling that dispute 'objectivity'. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a genuine philosophical disagreement underneath, about truth, about narrative, or about whether correspondence can survive selection? Give me a diagnosis with reasons, grounded in what each side actually claims, and tell me what -- if anything -- would count as settling it: a sharper distinction, a test case, or the admission that the question should be retired.
