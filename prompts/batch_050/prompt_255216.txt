I'm behind on midterm grading for my philosophy of mind course and I'm loosing steam, so tell me what you'd do with this one. The question I set was:

"Set out Block's distinction between phenomenal consciousness and access consciousness. Is global workspace theory best understood as a theory of one of these rather than the other? Explain. Finally, do the partial-report experiments (Sperling) show that phenomenal consciousness overflows access consciousness? Defend a verdict."

And here's the student's answer:

"Block's distinction is between phenomenal consciousness, the experiential what-it's-like aspect of a state, and access consciousness, which is a functional notion: a state is access conscious when its content is poised for use in reasoning, report, and the rational control of action. Importantly, poise is enough; the content doesn't have to be actually used, so an item sitting unattended in working memory can still be access conscious even if it never gets reported. Block argues the two can come apart, and the philosophically interesting direction is phenomenal without access: experiences whose contents never reach the machinery of reasoning and report at all.

Global workspace theory, originally from Baars and developed neurally by Dehaene, says a content is conscious when it's broadcast from a limited-capacity workspace to a wide range of consumer systems (memory, language, planning). Since broadcasting just is widespread availability for report and reasoning, GWT maps naturally onto access consciousness. Read that way it's a good theory, but it leaves phenomenal consciousness and the hard problem untouched, since a theory of how contents become accessible is not thereby a theory of why there's something it's like to have them.

The evidence that phenomenal consciousness overflows access comes from Sperling's experiments. Subjects shown a grid of twelve letters for a fraction of a second can report almost all of them if asked immediately, but the memory fades within about a second, so a delayed request yields almost nothing. Subjects also insist they saw all the letters. Block's conclusion: all twelve were phenomenally conscious, but only a few were ever accessed. This proves the overflow thesis. It also refutes global workspace theory: the unreported letters were conscious but never broadcast, so broadcasting can't be what consciousness is."
