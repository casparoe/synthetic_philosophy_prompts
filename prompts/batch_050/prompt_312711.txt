I am a novelist, and for the past year I have belonged to a small reading circle of fiction writers who work through philosophy of mind texts in the hope of sharpening how we render consciousness on the page. Last month the group reached the philosophical-zombie literature, and I offered what I took to be an innocent remark: that in drafting my current novel I had found it easy to imagine a character exactly like my protagonist in every outward and physical respect — same brain states, same dialogue, same tears at her father's funeral — but with nothing happening on the inside, no felt experience at all, the way a stage prop of a person might be. Another member, who writes thrillers but reads metaphysics, immediately responded with a line she clearly regarded as decisive: conceivability is a guide to possibility. Since I could conceive of my hollow character, she argued, such a being is possible, and since it is possible, consciousness must be something over and above the physical — which she then took to license a rather mystical passage in her own manuscript. The room nodded along, and I felt both that something important had been asserted and that I had no idea what, precisely, it was.

What I would like is a careful disambiguation of that one-liner — conceivability is a guide to possibility — treated as a piece of language rather than as the name of a theory. Please lay out, as a menu I can annotate and bring to the next session, the distinct precise theses it might be expressing. In particular, I want the menu to vary along the dimensions my fellow novelist slid past: what sort of conceiving is doing the work (merely finding a sentence intelligible, vividly picturing a scenario, or having an airtight description with no hidden contradiction), what sort of possibility is claimed in return (logical, metaphysical, or merely consistent with the actual laws of nature), and how strong the claimed connection is (infallible entailment, defeasible evidence, or something weaker still, like shifting the burden of proof). For each precisification, tell me whether it is a platitude no one would dispute, a substantive but defensible position, or something likely false, and illustrate with an example a fiction writer will appreciate — I have already been offered the case of conceiving water without its chemical composition, and the case of conceiving a proof of some mathematical claim that turns out to be false, and I am unsure whether these help or hurt the slogan on its various readings.

Finally, and most importantly for my purposes: for each reading, state plainly whether it would actually support the use my colleague made of it — that is, whether it licenses the inference from my being able to imagine the hollow character, to such a character's being genuinely possible, to consciousness being something more than the physical. I suspect the slogan survives only on a reading too weak to carry her conclusion, or is strong enough only on a reading no one should grant, but I would rather have this shown to me than assert it myself over wine next Thursday. If there is a difference between the slogan as a methodological rule of thumb for storytellers — who must, after all, treat anything vividly writable as fair game — and the slogan as a metaphysical instrument, I would like that drawn out as well.
