I serve as the secretary of a professional society with about 4,000 members, and our recent election has blown up in a way that has made social choice theory abruptly relevant to my life. We elect nine board members and three officers annually. This year, for the presidency, we had three candidates and, for the first time, collected full ranked ballots because a bylaws amendment last year let us experiment. The trouble is that the three natural ways of counting the ballots give three different outcomes: under the old plurality rule, which only counts first preferences, candidate A wins narrowly; under a Borda-style points count, which some members favor, candidate B wins; and the pairwise comparisons among the three form a cycle, with A beating B, B beating C, and C beating A, so there is no Condorcet winner at all. The board must decide two things at its meeting in six weeks: how to certify this election, since the bylaws as amended say only that the election shall be conducted by ranked ballot without specifying the counting rule, and what counting rule to write into the bylaws going forward, since any amendment needs a two-thirds vote of the board and then ratification by the membership. Our members span a huge range of sophistication: some are statisticians who email me impossibility theorems, and many are practitioners who barely vote and will revolt if the counting method is something they cannot explain to a colleague in one sentence. Turnout is around thirty percent, and our election software vendor can implement any standard ranked method.

Draft the memo I will present to the board recommending how to resolve both questions. I have recently learned the rough content of Arrow's impossibility theorem and related results, and I want the memo to take them seriously rather than treating the dispute as a technicality: I want it to explain, in terms a board of non-specialists can absorb, why the search for a counting rule that satisfies every reasonable-sounding fairness condition at once is provably doomed, and therefore why the real question is which desiderata we are willing to give up and why. The memo should lay out the live candidate rules for our situation (plurality on first preferences, instant runoff, Borda, and at least one Condorcet-consistent method with a stated tiebreak for cycles), say plainly which attractive properties each one sacrifices, and make a definite recommendation on the standing rule with reasons tied to our actual circumstances: low turnout, mixed member sophistication, the two-thirds amendment threshold, and the fact that whoever loses under any rule will suspect the rule was chosen to defeat them. On the pending election, recommend a resolution the board can actually defend, given that the bylaws are silent and each of the three candidates wins under some rule already in circulation. Give an honest hearing to the strongest argument against whatever you recommend, which I suspect will be the argument that we should count first preferences exactly as we always have because transparency and member trust outweigh any refinement, and explain why that argument loses or partly wins. Finally, the memo should tell the board candidly what this kind of theorem does not settle: whether there is any sense in which one of A, B, or C is the actual will of the membership, or whether the premise that a group has a coherent preference is itself what the cycle calls into question, and what difference, if any, that makes to how the board should talk to the membership afterward.

Please write the memo as continuous prose, in the manner of a formal letter or opinion: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold text, and no tables, even though it is a memo. Put the recommendation up front in the opening sentences and let the reasoning follow, the way you would in a document the board will actually vote on.
