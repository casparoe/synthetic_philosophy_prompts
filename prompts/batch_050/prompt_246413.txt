After forty years as an experimental physicist, I have retired and, in the manner of my kind, drifted into philosophy by way of a reading group. Last week we were discussing whether a sufficiently advanced computer could in principle replicate human mathematical reasoning, and a member of the group closed the discussion by declaring that Gödel proved the mind is not a machine. The remark was received as decisive, and we moved on to the wine.

I have since gone back to careful second-hand statements of the incompleteness theorems, and I cannot see that they deliver anything so grand. My suspicion, formed long ago in the laboratory whenever a single dramatic sentence was made to carry a whole interpretation, is that this one-liner is doing duty for several distinct claims of very different strengths, and that its conversation-ending force came from sliding among them. I would like a disambiguation: take the slogan 'Gödel proved that the mind is not a machine' and lay out the distinct precise theses it might be expressing, from the near-trivial to the highly contentious. I understand there is an argument in this neighbourhood associated with J. R. Lucas and later with Roger Penrose, so one reading should presumably be that argument stated carefully; but I also want the weaker and stronger readings, including any that are simply misstatements of the theorems. For instance, I have heard it said that Gödel showed there are mathematical truths no machine can ever prove, which I gather is wrong, since the undecidable sentence varies with the machine.

Please structure the answer as follows:

1. **The setting** (roughly 100 words): what position the slogan is usually deployed against, and what would have to be true for it to defeat that position.
2. **The menu of readings** (roughly 500 words): a numbered list of the distinct claims, ordered from the most anodyne to the most radical, each stated precisely enough that I could tell what evidence or argument would bear on it, and for each its actual relation to the incompleteness theorems — consequence of them, merely consistent with them, or misstatement of them.
3. **Verdicts** (roughly 250 words): for each reading, whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or false; and identify the weakest joint of the strongest defensible reading — I believe this is the step where the mind is said to 'see' the truth of the Gödel sentence, and I want to know what that seeing has to amount to for the argument to go through.
4. **Back to the reading group** (roughly 200 words): which reading my colleague's use of the slogan most plausibly required, whether any defensible reading supplies it, and the most charitable reconstruction of his remark.
5. **Suggested reformulation** (roughly 75 words): a sentence or two I can offer at the next meeting stating what the theorems do and do not show, without polemic.

Two constraints. First, do not rest the taxonomy on any one commentator's terminology; I want the claims themselves, not a survey of the secondary literature. Second, where a reading fails, exhibit the failure on its own terms rather than by appeal to the general implausibility of its conclusion. I am after the mechanics of the mistake, as one would want the failure mode of a flawed instrument, not merely the assurance that its readings are not trusted.
