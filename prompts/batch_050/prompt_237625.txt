I've been reading Hume's Natural History of Religion (1757) and got stuck on this bit from section III:

"We hang in perpetual suspence between life and death, health and sickness, plenty and want; which are distributed amongst the human species by secret and unknown causes, whose operation is oft unexpected, and always unaccountable. These unknown causes, then, become the constant object of our hope and fear; and while the passions are kept in perpetual alarm by an anxious expectation of the events, the imagination is equally employed in forming ideas of those powers, on which we have so entire a dependance."

And then, a couple of paragraphs later in the same section:

"In proportion as any man's course of life is governed by accident, we always find, that he encreases in superstition; as may particularly be observed of gamesters and sailors, who, though, of all mankind, the least capable of serious reflection, abound most in frivolous and superstitious apprehensions."

Here's the argument I'm having with a friend. If you're explaining religion as a natural phenomenon, what's actually in the category? My gut verdicts:

1. A Roman soldier sacrificing to Mars before a battle: religion, and Hume's mechanism fits perfectly.
2. My flatmate tapping the doorframe three times before a job interview: superstition, not religion. Except on Hume's account it's running on exactly the same engine as the soldier. This pair is my first problem.
3. A devout Theravada Buddhist — no creator god, no pleading with invisible powers, mostly meditation and precepts: still religion.
4. The John Frum ceremonies on Tanna: religion, even though they're about aeroplanes and we can roughly date the founding.
5. A striker who wears the same socks all season: not religion. But I can't see what separates it from (2), so any principle has to put those two together.
6. Neopagans doing solstice rituals who openly say they believe none of it, that it's for community and aesthetics: not religion, just theatre with extra steps.
7. A Vietnamese family keeping an ancestor altar, who'd describe it as respect and tradition rather than belief: religion. And yes, I can see that (6) and (7) look awfully alike. That's the other problem.
8. A mate who's deep into astrology, plans her week by it, dumped a bloke over star signs: honestly I keep going back and fourth on this one. Not sure I even have a verdict.

So: is there one principle that sorts these the way I want them sorted? State it precisely, tell me which clause of it is doing the work in the tricky pairs, and if the simplest version forces me to drop a verdict, say which one should go and why. And the bit I most want: does Hume's passage already contain the answer — hope, fear, ignorance of causes, anthropomorphism — and I'm just refusing to follow it where it leads? Notice he calls the gamesters and sailors superstitious rather than religious, so he plainly thinks there's a line somewhere, but I can't find where he draws it. What is the line, and what does it do with the astrology case?
