I am preparing a seminar handout on hinge epistemology and Wittgenstein’s On Certainty, and I keep encountering a difficulty that the usual examples do not quite resolve. Hinges are often described as commitments that stand fast for us without being known, justified, or ordinarily open to doubt: for instance, that there is an external world, that one’s name has not been secretly changed overnight, or that the rules of a familiar practice are not merely hallucinated. Yet it is unclear how this category works once a hinge has been articulated as a sentence. If someone can state “there is an external world”, entertain its negation, and construct a sceptical scenario in which it is false, does its becoming an object of reflection turn it into an ordinary proposition? Or can the very same sentence function as a proposition in one context and as a hinge in another?

Please explain, at an intuitive but philosophically careful level, how a hinge theorist should distinguish between (i) a belief supported by evidence, (ii) a certainty that is practically enacted rather than explicitly believed, and (iii) a contingent proposition temporarily treated as beyond doubt within a particular enquiry. In doing so, consider the following case: a hospital introduces an AI triage system whose designers and users never seriously question whether its output screen belongs to the same shared world as the patients it describes, although they can of course imagine a Cartesian demon scenario. Is that unquestioned assumption a hinge, and would anything philosophically important change if a philosopher persuaded the staff to discuss it explicitly? I am especially interested in whether hinges can migrate between the background and foreground of enquiry, and whether Wittgenstein’s river-bed image helps or obscures that process.
