Lately I've settled on a position about machine sentience that I'll call attention-schema functionalism: a system is sentient just in case it maintains a compressed, constantly updated model of its own internal processing -- a self-model it uses to predict and control its own behavior -- and it doesn't matter whether the hardware is neurons, silicon, or something else. No self-model, no sentience; right kind of self-model, full sentience, whatever the substrate. What would count against this? Not the usual "but it doesn't feel like anything to be a server rack" gut reaction, which I've priced in. I want the more serious defeater map: what empirical finding about actual conscious brains (e.g. about what happens in cases of minimal consciousness, locked-in states, or split-brain patients) would sink it, what formal or theoretical result would show the self-model criterion is doing no real work, and what cases could show it counts too much as sentient rather than too little -- e.g. does a thermostat-with-a-self-model style construction actually exist, or does any system rich enough to have the right kind of self-model necessarily clear a higher bar than I'm assuming? Also: is the view even risky enough to be wrong, or has it been defined so loosely that any case of apparent sentience can be redescribed as involving a self-model after the fact? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether a committed fan of the view would concede it counts, or would have a principled way of waving it off.
