I'm in an informal reading group on philosophy of history and we just spent two whole sessions arguing about the Great Man question — Carlyle-style "history is the biography of great men" versus structural explanation, and specifically whether something like Nazi Germany happens without Hitler, or whether Versailles, the Depression, mass antisemitism, and the collapse of Weimar institutions make some version of it nearly inevitable anyway. One guy in the group keeps insisting that history is basically a list of biographies and everything else is footnotes, and I want to gently destroy him via a writing exercise.

So: write me a satirical piece in the form of a deadpan, Swiftian mock-pamphlet authored by an invented enthusiast (give him a name and a fictional institute, something like a Society for the Cultivation of Indispensable Persons). His modest proposal, taking Great Man theory completely seriously: since singular individuals make history and material conditions are mere stage dressing, the state should stop wasting money on sociology, economics, and cliometrics and instead identify, cultivate, stockpile, and deploy Great Men — talent scouts posted at art academies and military schools, a strategic reserve of potential Churchills and Napoleons kept in readiness for national emergencies, and insurance schemes against the accidental upbringing of catastrophic Great Men, whose talents must be detected early and gently redirected into harmless hobbies. Where he confronts the obvious objections (mass movements, economic forces, the fact that the same "great man" flopped in a dozen contexts before succeeding), he should wave them away with total confidence, and that hand-waving is itself part of the joke. The satire should function as a reductio — follow the logic to conclusions the reader recoils from — while the author never once winks or concedes that anything is amiss.

The humor needs to come from the actual content of the debate: someone who knows the Carlyle-versus-structure literature should recognize that you understand what's being satirized, e.g. the unfalsifiable counterfactuals ("remove the man, remove the event"), the circularity of defining greatness by historical impact and then citing impact as proof of greatness, and the awkward fact that structuralists never deny individuals exist, just that they're replaceable at the level of outcomes. Aim for about 600 to 800 words. Format-wise, write it as continuous prose the way an open letter or pamphlet essay would read — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no numbered sections, just paragraphs.
