Last weekend I had a long and rather heated argument with a friend about sweatshops, and it has left me wanting to set the dispute properly for my students. I tutor an upper-level undergraduate course in political philosophy, and I am writing this year's exam on exploitation. My friend's position was the standard one my students arrive with: if a transaction leaves both parties better off than their next-best alternative, calling it exploitation is confused moralising. I tried to shift him with a case I made up on the spot, and I would like that case to anchor the exam question. The case: a garment firm opens a factory in an impoverished region and pays wages well above what workers could earn otherwise, so employment there is genuinely life-improving for everyone hired; but the firm's very presence, because it bids up the price of the one skilled input the region has (say, literate supervisors), makes the neighbouring village's existing workshop unviable, and that village's workers end up worse off. So the transaction is mutually beneficial for the transacting parties, yet the arrangement as a whole leaves identifiable third parties worse off than before the firm arrived.

Draft one exam question built around this case, answerable in 90 minutes, that forces candidates beyond reciting the mutual-benefit defence. The crux I want tested is whether third-party harm changes the verdict on the transaction itself: does the exploitation charge attach to the wage bargain between firm and workers (in which case the neighbours' losses are a separate injustice), or does it attach to the arrangement as a whole (in which case the mutual benefit to the transacting parties may not be exculpatory at all)? A strong answer should have to take a position on what the morally relevant baseline is for judging whether the workers are exploited, and on whether the firm's responsibility for the neighbours differs depending on whether the harm flows through an ordinary market mechanism rather than through, say, the firm lobbying to keep competing employers out. I want candidates who merely know the labels — wrongful exploitation, mutually beneficial exploitation, the non-worseness claim — to be unable to pass.

Present the whole response as exactly seven numbered items, with no preamble before the first and nothing after the last: (1) the exam question itself, phrased as it would appear on the paper; (2) through (5) the model answer, broken into the four moves a top answer must make, each a short paragraph; (6) a marking scheme allocating 20 marks across five must-hit points, with a one-line note on the characteristic way weaker candidates fumble each; (7) a statement of the single distinction that most reliably separates a first-class answer from an upper-second. One further request: if the case I have constructed is subtly incoherent — for instance, if the third-party harm dissolves on inspection, or if no defensible view treats the two parties' bargain as separately assessable — say so plainly within the relevant item rather than quietly patching the question to make it work.
