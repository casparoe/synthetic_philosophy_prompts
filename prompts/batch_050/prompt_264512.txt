I am a novelist at work on a book whose plot turns on a prophecy. In a port city, an oracle tells an admiral that there will be a sea battle tomorrow. One character devotes the night to preventing the battle; another, believing the oracle infallible, sees no point in trying. I have realised that I cannot write the later chapters consistently until I fix the internal logic of the fictional world: what the oracle's sentence does at the moment it is spoken, whether it is then true, false, or neither, and what it would mean for a character to act so as to falsify it.

You are a philosophical logician with expertise in tense, modality, and the problem of future contingents, acting as a consultant on the metaphysics of my fictional world. Please think carefully, and begin with a short brainstorming paragraph setting out the logically available options before you commit to a structure.

Your main deliverable is a decision tree that takes me from my one fixed starting commitment — the oracle's utterance is meaningful, grammatically ordinary, and spoken in good faith — to a named position on the truth of statements about the contingent future. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the actual positions located within that space. Each branch node must be a single question I can answer as a novelist without knowing which theory it leads to, phrased concretely in terms of the story's events — for instance, whether, if the admiral keeps the fleet in harbour, the prophecy was false all along or instead changed its status — rather than in technical vocabulary. Where technical terms are unavoidable, gloss them in a clause.

The following positions must appear as leaves: classical bivalence holding even for contingent futures; the Aristotelian view on which such statements are neither true nor false when uttered; a three-valued treatment with a distinct value of indeterminacy; a supervaluational treatment on which truth requires holding on every open future; branching time with one future singled out as the actual one; branching time with no future singled out; and the fatalist reading on which the prophecy's present truth settles everything. You may add further leaves if the logical space demands them. For each leaf, give the position's name (or 'unnamed'), one sentence on what it licences me to do in plotting — whether self-defeating prophecies, fulfilled-against-effort prophecies, or stable time loops are available — and one sentence on its most awkward commitment for a storyteller. Note any coherent-looking combination of answers that no named position occupies, and diagnose briefly whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and say what further question, if any, would divide them.

Aim for six to nine leaves, and keep each branch question answerable by a reader who knows the novel's plot but no philosophy. Write your answer in British English.
