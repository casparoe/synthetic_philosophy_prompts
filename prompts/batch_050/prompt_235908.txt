I am reading a popular book on the philosophy of religion, and its chapter on Hume summarizes the argument of Section X of the Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding as follows:

"Hume's essay Of Miracles offers a decisive, fully general refutation of miracles. A miracle, for Hume, is simply a violation of a law of nature. But a law of nature is nothing more than a completely uniform experience, so the evidence standing against any alleged miracle is automatically the strongest that experience can possibly supply. Testimony, by contrast, is something we know from experience to be fallible: witnesses lie, exaggerate, and deceive themselves. The rational person therefore weighs the two against each other and must always conclude that it is more likely the witnesses are deceived or deceiving than that the event actually happened. From this it follows that no testimony, however numerous or however respectable the witnesses, could ever make it reasonable to believe that a miracle occurred. Hume illustrates the underlying point with the case of an Indian prince who refused to believe travellers' reports that in cold countries water turns solid; the prince was wrong, Hume says, and his mistake shows how a parochial experience can lead us to reject testimony we ought to accept. The argument culminates in Hume's celebrated maxim that 'no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous than the fact which it endeavours to establish.' Since this condition is one that no actual body of testimony could ever satisfy, belief in a miracle is always irrational; and the fact that the miracle stories of rival religions cancel one another out provides, so to speak, an experimental confirmation that no religion can be securely founded upon them."
