I'm a software engineer who's been going down a rabbit hole on acausal trade lately -- the idea that two agents who can't communicate can still end up cooperating if each can model the other's reasoning well enough that each one's behavior factors into the other's simulation. Take the Cold War as a case study. The US and USSR had almost no real communication and no enforcement mechanism, yet all these tacit norms held for decades: no targeting each other's leadership, keeping conflicts proxy-only, the taboo on nukes even in Korea and Vietnam where using them was militarily tempting. Analyze this through the acausal trade lens. Does "each superpower reasoning about how the other models it" actually explain the stability of these norms, or is ordinary iterated-game tit-for-tat doing all the work and acausal trade adds nothing? Where does the mapping break -- the Cuban missile crisis ended via secret back-channel negotiation, which is like the opposite of acausal coordination, so what does that do to the analysis? And then run it the other direction: what does the episode show about the theory, e.g. does acausal-style coordination only work when both sides' models of each other stay stable, and does it fall apart the moment one side misreads the other (Cuban missiles again)? I'd rather you say where the concept just doesn't apply than force it to fit.
