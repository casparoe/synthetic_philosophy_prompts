For a decision theory reading group I have to write down where I stand on Allais before we meet, and I want to know if my position actually hangs together or if I'm papering over a crack. Here's roughly what I think:

(1) In the classic Allais setup -- the version where you pick between a guaranteed million versus a gamble that's 89% a million, 10% five million, 1% nothing, and then the version where the 89% chunk becomes nothing in both options -- I'd take the sure million in the first pair and the gamble in the second. And I don't think this is me being irrational or falling for a framing trick. The certainty effect is a legitimate preference, not a bug.

(2) The independence axiom (or the sure-thing principle, however you want to cash it out) strikes me as close to non-negotiable for rational choice over lotteries. If two options share an identical consequence in some state of the world, your choice between them shouldn't turn on that shared part.

(3) Risk aversion is perfectly rational -- preferring a sure thing to a fair gamble with the same expected value isn't automatically a bias or an inconsistency.

(4) Money-pump arguments are decisive: if your preferences let someone cycle you through trades until you're worse off and back where you started, that's a reductio of those preferences.

(5) Rational choice shouldn't depend on how the options happen to be described -- relabeling or redescribing the same outcomes and probabilities can't change which choice is correct.

Which of these are actually in tension with each other, and which only look like they are until you think it through? Rank the genuine tensions by how severe they are, say for each one what would have to give and at what cost, and then tell me which single revision cleans up the most damage. I'd rather not say in advance which commitment I'd drop, so don't guess at that -- just audit.
