I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rational choice theory, and I am writing the take-home final. I want one essay question on money-pump arguments and diachronic rationality that cannot be answered by reciting the textbook line that intransitive preferences are irrational because they make you exploitable. To that end, please build the question around the following three agents, whom I will describe to students verbatim in the exam paper:

Agent A has stable, cyclic preferences over three goods (prefers the first to the second, the second to the third, and the third to the first), and willingly pays a small fee at each step of a sequence of trades that returns her to her starting point with less money.

Agent B has perfectly transitive, stable preferences, but knows that her future self will, at a predictable later point, come to prefer an outcome she now ranks lowest. Faced with a sequence of choices designed around that known preference shift, she can follow either of two policies: at each choice point, pick whichever option her current self prefers given what her future self will do (call this the sophisticated policy); or settle now on a complete plan and stick to it even when a later step looks worse to her then-current self (call this the resolute policy). Under the sophisticated policy she ends up with an outcome every one of her selves, present and future, ranks below an outcome that was available to her under the resolute policy.

Agent C has preferences that are transitive at every moment but depend on which menu she was offered at an earlier stage; a clever trader who chooses the sequence of menus can extract a fee from her even though no single choice she makes is one her at-that-moment self ranks below an available alternative.

The question should ask students, in roughly 90 minutes of writing: which of these agents, if any, are irrational, and what exactly the money-pump argument establishes. The point I want tested is whether students can see that the classic argument has a gap between two conclusions -- "this agent can be predictably exploited" and "this agent is irrational" -- and that closing the gap requires a substantive bridge premise about whether rationality governs individual choices at a time or patterns of choices across time. Students should have to take a stand on whether the sophisticated chooser's outcome-dominated result convicts her of irrationality or instead convicts the resolute chooser of a kind of rigidity, and on whether exploitability by a sufficiently informed trader is even the right test when every stepwise choice is locally impeccable.
