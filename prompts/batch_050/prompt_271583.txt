I coordinate an interdisciplinary graduate reading group — students from cognitive science, clinical psychology, and comparative literature — and this semester I am leading our session on Merleau-Ponty's account of the lived body and motor intentionality in Phenomenology of Perception. We have ninety minutes. Only one member has read any Husserl; the cognitive science contingent speaks fluent information-processing and will reach for it reflexively; the literature students know phenomenology mostly at second hand, through reception theory, and tend to treat all such texts as elaborate metaphors about embodiment. I have read the book carefully but never taught it, and I do not want to spend the session summarizing chapters.

What I am asking for is a plan for the explanation rather than the explanation itself. Specifically: the order in which the pieces must be introduced, with the dependency structure argued rather than assumed — my guess is that they need the critique of treating the body as one object among others before the notion of the lived body can even be posed, and the lived body before habit and the practical I-can make sense, but I want to know whether that order is right and what breaks if it is violated. Then the carrying example: I had planned to build the session on the Schneider case, the patient who can grasp objects in the flow of a practical task but cannot move to command or point on request, but I want to know whether to open with an everyday case like touch-typing or catching a ball first, so that pathology functions as contrast rather than spectacle, and why the phantom limb should or should not carry the session instead. Then the misconception map: the cognitive scientists will translate everything into subpersonal computation and hear motor intentionality as the brain's motor-control module; someone will collapse body schema into body image; someone will read Schneider's deficit as a visual or intellectual one, since his eyesight and reasoning are largely preserved; the humanists may take Merleau-Ponty to be offering one more layer of inner representation between perception and action, or else to be simply anti-science. Tell me at what point in the sequence each misreading predictably forms, and exactly where to head it off. Then one closing check for understanding: a question or short exercise that would show they have grasped motor intentionality as a mode of directedness toward the world, rather than as a mechanism inside the head. And finally, what I should openly leave out at this level — the late ontology of the flesh, the Husserlian lineage, the later empirical disputes about how accurately the Schneider case was reported — and say that I am leaving it out.

Please structure the response under the following headings, in this order: The Dependency Order (roughly two paragraphs, arguing the sequence), The Carrying Example (one or two paragraphs, including the case for the everyday example first and the verdict on the phantom limb), The Misconception Map (a list pairing each predictable misreading with the point in the sequence where it should be defused), The Comprehension Check (one paragraph), and What to Omit at This Level (a short paragraph). Conclude with a section titled Where This Plan Could Fail, in which you say what would change your mind about the ordering — for instance, which feature of this particular audience, if different, would reorder the sequence — and where your reading of Merleau-Ponty is most contestable, such that an alternative reading would force a genuinely different teaching plan rather than a cosmetic one.
