I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm writing a profile of a quantum-foundations theorist I've interviewed several times over the years. Across our conversations she has been strikingly consistent about when she will call a quantum state "real" and when she won't. Her verdicts, as I have them in my notes:

- A single electron in a double-slit experiment, described by a pure superposition: the wavefunction corresponds to something physically out there, really traversing both paths.
- A neutron in an interferometer, again in a pure state: real.
- A spin-1/2 atom prepared by selecting one output port of a Stern-Gerlach device, so that its state is pure: real.
- The same kind of atom when the experimenter simply failed to record which port it emerged from, so that it must be described by a mixed state: "just bookkeeping of our ignorance" — not real.
- The reduced state of one member of an entangled pair, considered on its own: not real; it encodes our incomplete information about the pair.
- When one half of a Bell pair is measured and the distant partner's state "collapses": nothing physical happens at the distant site; only the local agent's description changes — collapse is epistemic.
- The thermal state of a gas: epistemic.
- On the PBR theorem, she says it confirms that one cannot consistently treat pure states as mere information about reality.

The article requires me to state her position on a case we have never discussed: the wavefunction of the universe as a whole — the single pure state a quantum cosmologist writes down for everything there is. Is it, on her view, physically real?

Write me a memo, in American English, that does the following. First, predict her verdict on the universal wavefunction. Second, identify the rule her track record suggests she is running, plus at least one alternative rule that fits every one of her recorded verdicts just as well but delivers the opposite verdict on the cosmological case; say what each rule predicts there and why. Third, commit to a single prediction — I do not want a menu of options for me to choose from — and defend it as the most defensible reading of her record, while noting briefly what in that record, if anything, cuts against it. Fourth, name one further thought experiment whose verdict would discriminate between the candidate rules, and explain what each rule would say about it. End with a final line in exactly this form:

Verdict: [her likely ruling on the universal wavefunction — real or not real — and the rule it follows from]
