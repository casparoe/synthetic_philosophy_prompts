I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on the mind–body problem, and we've just made the jump from Descartes to the contemporary literature on philosophical zombies. Reading the opening of the Sixth Meditation from the Meditations on First Philosophy (in the John Veitch translation), I was struck by a principle Descartes states before he ever gets to mind and body:

"For there can be no doubt that God possesses the power of producing all the objects I am able distinctly to conceive, and I never considered anything impossible to him, unless when I experienced a contradiction in the attempt to conceive it aright."

As I read it, this is a bridge principle: the absence of contradiction in distinct conception suffices for possibility (in Descartes' setting, possibility-for-God). The modern conceivability argument against physicalism seems to run on a nearly identical bridge — if a microphysical duplicate of our world lacking phenomenal consciousness is conceivable without contradiction, then it is metaphysically possible, and physicalism is false. But two things have changed in transit: omnipotence has been swapped out for metaphysical possibility, and the relata are different. Descartes applies the bridge to two thing-concepts (a thinking thing and an extended thing), whereas the zombie argument applies it across levels of description — a complete physical-functional specification of a world on one side, phenomenal character on the other.

Here is what I would like from you:

1. Your actual credence that the conceivability argument against physicalism is sound — that is, that there is a metaphysically possible world physically identical to ours but lacking phenomenal consciousness, and that this refutes physicalism. I want a number, not "it depends on your theory of consciousness."

2. A decomposition of that number. I would expect roughly the following sub-questions, but restructure or add to them if you think the argument leans on more: (a) the probability that the zombie scenario is ideally conceivable — coherent under ideal rational reflection, not merely apparently coherent at first glance; (b) the probability that Descartes' bridge principle holds in its modern form, i.e., that ideal conceivability is a reliable guide to metaphysical possibility; (c) the probability that, conditional on zombies being metaphysically possible, physicalism is actually refuted rather than saved by an a posteriori necessity or by the phenomenal concept strategy; (d) anything else load-bearing that I have missed. Assign each a rough probability with one or two lines of justification, and show how they combine into your final figure.

3. The historical question the passage raises for me: does your credence in the bridge principle — term (b) — differ between the two applications? Is the conceivability–possibility inference more or less trustworthy when the relata are two thing-concepts, as in Descartes, than when they are descriptions at different levels of description, as in the zombie argument? If you think the modern application is weaker, identify which feature of the relata does the damage.

4. Finally, my own credence that the argument is sound is about 0.3, mostly because I suspect term (b) is where the action is. Tell me which sub-question your own final number is most sensitive to, and what argument, result, or conceptual development would move it most.
