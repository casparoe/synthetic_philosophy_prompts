I'm in my final year of an undergraduate philosophy degree, currently enrolled in a seminar on Wittgenstein's later philosophy, and for the creative component of my assessment I want to write fiction rather than a standard essay. I'd like you to write a short story of roughly 2,000–2,500 words that works through, entirely by dramatization, a tension I find in Wittgenstein's treatment of religious belief.

The tension is this. On the reading we've been developing, a sentence of faith — say, the conviction that one will face a judgment after death — does not function for the believer as an empirical hypothesis does: it is not held tentatively, is not revised as evidence comes in, and is not the sort of thing that could be settled by better data. Instead it operates as a picture that organizes an entire life, so that the believer and the unbeliever are arguably not even contradicting one another, since each is doing something different with the words. That account is attractive as a way of making sense of how religious people actually talk. But it has an uncomfortable edge: if the two sides never genuinely contradict each other, it becomes unclear what their disagreement is about, unclear how such a conviction could ever be criticized from outside or lost from within, and unclear whether the account dignifies the believer or quietly demotes her faith to something that can't be wrong because it can't be right either.

Some constraints on the story:

- Include at least one character whose religious conviction genuinely does not behave like an empirical hypothesis, and let the reader see this through what the character does and declines to do, not through any explanation of it.
- Include at least one moment where that conviction produces concrete consequences for another person who does not share it, so that the question of whether calling it a different language game is a defense or merely a diagnosis becomes pressing rather than abstract.
- Set the story somewhere such a disagreement would surface naturally and with stakes: a hospital ethics consultation, a contested will, a planning dispute in a small community, a parole hearing — wherever you think the pressure is highest.
- No philosophical jargon in the dialogue unless the character would plausibly use it, and no character should serve as a mouthpiece for the seminar reading. The concept has to be carried by plot, behavior, and what goes unsaid.
- End in a way that leaves genuinely open whether the Wittgensteinian reading honors the believer or patronizes her — the story should not resolve its own question.

If it helps, the title I have in mind gestures at the idea of a picture that holds a life captive, but feel free to find your own.
