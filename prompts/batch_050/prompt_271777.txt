i help run a grad-student philosophy of mind colloquium and our next guest is a philosopher who defends the old Wittgensteinian line that dreams aren't experiences you undergo while asleep — on her view a dream just is the narrative you find yourself with on waking, and asking what you "really" experienced at 3am is a confused question, since the report is the criterion rather than a description of some inner movie. the audience will have read both Malcolm and Dennett, so draft 8-10 interview questions that go past having her restate the position. I want at least one question on each of these:

- where her view parts company with Dennett's cassette theory, since both deny the received view that dreams are conscious episodes during sleep but for different reasons — press her on whether she's making a conceptual point about what "dream" means or an empirical bet about how memory works, and whether the two can come apart
- the old case, popular with Freud and Dennett alike, of the sleeper who dreams a long revolutionary trial ending at the guillotine and wakes to find the bedboard fallen across his neck — the received view says the stimulus got woven into an ongoing dream, so what does she say instead, and when she says the whole thing was composed at the moment of waking, does that mean the trial was never dreamt at all?
- the experiments where lucid dreamers agree beforehand to signal with a pre-arranged pattern of eye movements once they become lucid, and then do — this looks awfully like reporting an experience in real time, so ask whether she's forced to say the signaling subject isn't conscious in any sense during those minutes
- what a false dream report is even false *to*: if I misremember my dream, the received view says there's a fact I get wrong, but on her view what exactly am I getting wrong?
- falsifiability — what finding would actually change her mind, or is the view a stipulation immune to whatever sleep science turns up?

for each question, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give (she knows the literature cold, so assume the standard reply and push one step past it). no softballs about how she got into the field.

format: first a section called The questions — the 8-10 numbered questions, each 2-4 sentences with its follow-up underneath it. then a short section called Where I'd start — a few sentences on which question youd open the hour with and why.
