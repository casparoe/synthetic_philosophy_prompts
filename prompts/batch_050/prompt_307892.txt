I am a graduate student in moral philosophy, and I have been working through a family of axiological views sometimes called suffering-focused or craving-based theories of wellbeing. The version that interests me holds, roughly, that nothing is intrinsically good except states of consciousness, and that a state of consciousness is good only insofar as it is free of craving and suffering: pleasure matters, on this view, not as a positive addition to a life, but as the relief or absence of some frustrated desire or bother. A perfectly tranquil mind, free of all longing, is optimal; nothing further could improve it.

Out of this I have assembled the following argument, and its conclusion is one I cannot accept. (1) A state of consciousness has value only insofar as it lacks craving and suffering; satisfied pleasure contributes nothing beyond the removal of a pre-existing lack. (2) Any being that does not yet exist has no cravings and no needs; there is no one who is deprived by the absence of the pleasures that being would have enjoyed. (3) Creating a being and then satisfying its cravings therefore produces no net value that would not have obtained had the being never been created: at best, the being is returned to the untroubled baseline that non-existence already secured. (4) Every actual finite life contains at least some suffering or frustrated craving, however small. (5) A world containing no sentient beings contains no suffering and no frustrated craving, and nothing counts against it, since there is no one for whom its emptiness is a loss. (6) The value of a world is fully determined by what it contains of value and disvalue for conscious beings. (7) Therefore the empty world is at least as good as any populated world and strictly better than any world containing suffering; and if one could painlessly bring about the extinction of all sentient life, one would have decisive reason to do so.

I take (7) to be a reductio of the argument, not a discovery. My question is where the reasoning goes wrong, if it does. Please identify the single premise or inferential step you would reject; explain why that step looked compelling given the surrounding premises (I suspect something slides between what is good *for* a being and what is good *simpliciter*, or between relief of a lack and the absence of any subject to be lacked, but I cannot pin it down); and then set out what rejecting that step costs: in particular, whether one can resist (7) while retaining the core tranquilist claim in (1) that pleasure is valuable only as the removal of craving, or whether resisting (7) forces one to concede that some pleasures are good independently of any prior lack, and so to abandon the view's original motivation. If you think the argument is in fact sound and (7) should be accepted, say so and defend that verdict against the obvious charge that it proves too much.
