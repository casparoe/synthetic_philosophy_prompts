So I'm a few weeks into a grad seminar on modality and I've basically talked myself into the view that conceivability is a guide to possibility -- roughly, that if you can genuinely conceive of a situation clearly and distinctly, without contradiction showing up under reflection, then that situation is metaphysically possible, and conceiving of it is decent evidence for its possibility. This is what makes zombie arguments and the like get off the ground, and honestly it seems like the only way we could know about non-actual possibilities at all, since we obviously can't run experiments on them. But I'm aware I'm maybe just attached to it because it's the load-bearing premise in a lot of philosophy I find fun, so I want to stress-test it properly.

What would actually count against this view? I know the standard Kripke stuff -- water might have turned out to be XYZ, Hesperus/Phosphorus, the seeming conceivability of gold not being an element -- but I want it sorted properly: which of those cases show conceivability is unreliable, and which just show people were conceiving of the wrong thing or sloppily, as defenders like Chalmers would say. If the defender's move is always "you didn't really conceive it, you only thought you did," does that save the view or immunize it against every counterexample, and is that rescue itself a problem? Also: is there a principled way to tell genuine conceiving from mere seeming-to-conceive that doesn't presuppose you already know what's possible, because if not, the view looks circular as a *guide* even if it's true. I'd also like to know what the view predicts that its rivals (say, taking possibility as primitive, or grounding modal knowledge in actual causes and counterfactuals instead) don't predict, so there's some way the dispute could in principle be settled rather than just dug in on. What would a committed defender of conceivability-possibility themselves concede as a defeater, versus what would only the critics treat as one? And rank the threats from fatal to merely annoying, because right now I genuinely can't tell whether the view is risky in the right way or so hedged that nothing could touch it.
