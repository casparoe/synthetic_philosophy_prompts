**Question (worth 25 marks).** Davidson's project of radical interpretation asks how one could understand a speaker from scratch, without any prior knowledge of what the speaker's words mean or what the speaker believes. (a) Explain why Davidson insists that an interpreter cannot first learn what a speaker believes and then use that to fix the meanings of the speaker's sentences, or vice versa. (b) Explain the role of the principle of charity in breaking this interdependence, being careful to state what charity actually directs the interpreter to do. (c) Briefly state how Davidson's project differs from Quine's radical translation, focusing on what each takes as given.

A student has submitted the following answer:

**Student's answer.** Davidson's radical interpretation begins from the same problem as Quine's radical translation: imagine confronting a speaker of a wholly unknown language, with nothing to go on but their utterances and the circumstances in which they are made. Davidson's point is that an interpreter cannot fix meaning first and beliefs second, nor beliefs first and meaning second, because each can only be inferred via the other. If I notice that the speaker says "Gavagai" whenever a rabbit runs by, I can take this to mean that "Gavagai" means rabbit — but only on the assumption that the speaker believes a rabbit is present. Conversely, I can attribute the belief that a rabbit is present only if I already know what the speaker's words mean. Meaning and belief are thus interdependent, and any evidence that bears on one bears equally on the other.

The principle of charity is Davidson's way out of this impasse. Charity instructs the interpreter to assume that the speaker is mostly rational, i.e., that the speaker's beliefs mostly cohere with one another and that the speaker rarely holds contradictions. By assuming the speaker is rational in this sense, the interpreter can take their utterances at face value and assign meanings so as to maximize the logical consistency of the attributed beliefs. Charity is therefore a psychological assumption about speakers: we interpret charitably because we have reason to think most people are in fact consistent.

As for Quine, Davidson's radical interpretation is essentially Quine's radical translation carried out for a speaker's own home language. Quine showed that translation is indeterminate; Davidson extends the same argument to show that meaning within a single language is equally indeterminate, since interpreting one's neighbor poses the same problem as interpreting a foreigner. Both projects take the evidence available to the interpreter — assent and dissent to sentences in observable circumstances — as given, and both conclude that this evidence underdetermines the assignment of content.

Because belief and meaning are interdependent, Davidson concludes that interpretation is ultimately circular: the interpreter must simply begin somewhere and hope the resulting interpretation is coherent. There is, on Davidson's view, no non-circular guarantee that radical interpretation must succeed, and where the evidence underdetermines interpretation, there is no fact of the matter about what the speaker means.
