I run a small interdisciplinary reading group on the philosophy and psychology of humour, and two of our members have been arguing past each other for nearly a year. One defends a Hobbesian superiority account of laughter: taking her cue from the discussion of laughter in chapter VI of Leviathan, she holds that laughter always expresses a sudden sense of one's own eminence, triggered either by some success of one's own or by apprehending a defect or deformity in someone else — or, as Hobbes himself adds, in one's own former self — by comparison with which one applauds oneself. The other defends an incongruity account: laughter is the response to perceiving a mismatch between how things are and how they ought or are expected to be, and needs no self-congratulatory comparison at all. Each has a stable of cases the other allegedly cannot explain. She cites derisive laughter, mockery, and the awkward fact that laughter is so much easier in company; he cites puns, nonsense wordplay, absurdism, and tickling, none of which seem to involve any triumph over anyone. They have now agreed, under some duress from me, to attempt an adversarial collaboration, and I have volunteered you — a philosopher of emotion with experience moderating adversarial collaborations and no stake in either camp — to draft the joint statement they should produce.

Please think carefully and work in two passes: first brainstorm, privately, the candidate claims both camps might sign, the formulations each camp would reject as slanted, and the cases each side treats as fatal to the other; then draft the document. Do not adjudicate or declare a winner. Where Hobbes's own account is at issue, treat it as a text to be interpreted, not a verdict to be applied, and note that the superiority theorist reads his self-comparison clause as friendly to her view while the incongruity theorist reads laughing at one's former self as showing that no other-regarding comparison is needed at all. You may mention the relief and benign violation camps only in passing, and only to mark where the two parties agree these views are doing different work rather than to adjudicate them. Please do not include citations or reading lists; the document should stand on its arguments alone.

Structure the joint statement with the following sections, in this order: "Shared Ground" — a numbered list of claims both camps accept, about the phenomenology of laughter, its social character, and what any adequate theory must explain (roughly 250 words); "Residual Disagreements" — the exact points of dispute, each stated in neutral wording that both parties could sign as a fair statement of their own position (roughly 300 words); "Cruxes and Registered Predictions" — for each crux, a concrete case, observation, or experiment both sides agree bears on it, with each party's prediction recorded in advance, including their divergent readings of laughing at one's own past self and of hard cases like tickling and derisive laughter (roughly 400 words, a table is welcome); "The Hobbes Clause" — the minimal interpretation of the superiority passage both can accept, and a precise statement of where each locates their disagreement with it (roughly 200 words); and finally "Verbal or Genuine?" — an honest identification of which parts of the year-long dispute were merely terminological and which are substantive, with reasons (roughly 150 words).
