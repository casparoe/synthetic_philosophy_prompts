Here is an argument I have been unable to refute, though its conclusion seems to license everything. (1) An act is wrong only if it makes things go worse than they otherwise would have gone. (2) When I buy a chicken at the supermarket, no chicken is harmed because of my purchase in particular: the store adjusts its orders in response to aggregate demand, and a single purchase is far below the granularity at which ordering decisions change. (3) So buying the chicken is not wrong. (4) The same holds wherever an individual's contribution to some large-scale practice is below the threshold at which anything would change: taking a job at a firm that would do its harm with another employee instead, paying taxes to a government that would fight its war regardless, joining a crowd whose conduct would be identical in one's absence. (5) So no one is ever complicit in collective wrongdoing, and whenever a harm requires many participants, each of whom is individually dispensable, nobody acts wrongly in contributing. (6) That cannot be right. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would especially like to keep (1), since I do not know how else to assess conduct, and I would rather not conclude that nothing is wrong with purchases like the one in (2) -- so tell me what I have to give up to avoid both.
