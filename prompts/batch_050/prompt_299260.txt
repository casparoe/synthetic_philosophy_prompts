Construct a thought experiment that shows Carroll's regress (the Achilles and the Tortoise thing) can't be fixed by adding premises, but with a twist: I want the character to be maximally cooperative. So no stubborn tortoise who just refuses to infer out of perversity. Instead design a case where the character sincerely accepts p, sincerely accepts "if p then q", sincerely accepts and even *states out loud* the rule "from p and if-p-then-q, infer q" -- and yet the deduction still stalls, without the character being irrational, forgetful, lying, or secretly confused about the words. The stalling has to fall out of the structure of the situation, not from a character flaw.

Constraints: no sci-fi devices (no evil demons, no brains in vats), no infinite regress made literal (don't just have them writing down premise after premise forever -- that misses the point), and it should be set in an ordinary context, like a student, a lawyer, or a computer program someone is writing.

After the scenario, walk through it: say exactly where the deduction fails to get going and why adding one more premise (the conditional, then the conditional-about-the-conditional, etc.) never helps. Then the honest part: the usual objection to Carroll-style cases is that they just smuggle in a behavioral gap -- the character accepts everything but doesn't *do* anything, so it's about psychology not logic. Say whether your case dodges that objection or falls to it, and if it falls, admit it. Finally, state what the case shows the real difference is between a rule of inference and a premise, in one or two sentences, and whether a machine implementing modus ponens as code escapes the regress or just hides it.
