Is there an established name for the following tension? I have been thinking about how we evaluate forecasters, and I keep running into a configuration I cannot find discussed anywhere.

Suppose two weather forecasters are equally skilled, and both hold genuinely well-calibrated beliefs. Forecaster A always reports her honest probabilities: when she says 70 percent chance of rain, it rains about 70 percent of the time. Forecaster B holds the same underlying beliefs but systematically rounds his reports toward 0 or 100. Under any reasonable scoring system, A's strategy maximizes expected score, and B's does not. But in any given season, A's realized score is reliably middling, while B's realized score is either excellent or disastrous.

Now place them in an environment where realized performance, not expected performance, determines survival: a television station that renews only the contract of the forecaster with the best annual score, or an investment firm that promotes whichever analyst made the year's most accurate calls. Honest reporters almost never finish first, because some exaggerator always gets lucky, so over time the profession fills up with systematic overconfiders. Worse, the survivors' track records are genuine -- they really did score best -- so an outside observer cannot easily detect the distortion by inspecting past performance.

I want to be clear about what I am not asking. This is not miscalibration, since each individual's underlying beliefs can be perfectly calibrated. It is not the observation that scoring rules reward honest reporting, because that observation concerns expected score, which is precisely what selection does not track. And it seems distinct from both survivorship bias, which is an error an observer makes in drawing conclusions from winners, and the winner's curse, which concerns estimation error by the winner of an auction -- here the distortion is in the population of forecasters itself, produced by the incentive structure, before any observer reasons about anything.

Does this phenomenon -- selection on realized accuracy generating incentives that run directly against what proper scoring rules guarantee in expectation -- have an established name, either in the forecasting literature or elsewhere? If it does, how does the standard treatment handle the fact that the surviving forecasters are not deceiving anyone and may even believe their own exaggerated reports? If it does not, how would you define it precisely, and what boundary cases would separate it from survivorship bias on one side and ordinary incentive-driven dishonesty on the other?
