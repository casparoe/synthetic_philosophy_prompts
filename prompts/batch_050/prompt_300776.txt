Last night I had a long and unresolved argument with a friend about the Zhuangzi, and I would like a referee. We were reading Book XX, "Shan Mû, or 'The Tree on the Mountain'" (in James Legge's translation, where the author is called Kwang-dze). The chapter opens with two cases running in opposite directions: a great tree is spared by the wood-cutter because its timber is useless, and that same evening a host orders his goose killed -- and chooses the goose that cannot cackle. The disciples put the obvious question to the master: "Yesterday the tree on the mountain (you said) would live out its years because of the uselessness of its wood, and now our host's goose has died because of its want of power (to cackle);--which of these conditions, Master, would you prefer to be in?" Kwang-dze first offers the position between being useful and being useless, then retracts it -- it "would not put me beyond being involved in trouble" -- and instead praises one who "takes his seat on the Tâo and its Attributes," who "is above the reach both of praise and of detraction; now he (mounts aloft) like a dragon, now he (keeps beneath) like a snake; he is transformed with the (changing) character of the time, and is not willing to addict himself to any one thing." The section ends: "Union brings on separation; success, overthrow; sharp corners, the use of the file; honour, critical remarks; active exertion, failure; wisdom, scheming; inferiority, being despised:--where is the possibility of unchangeableness in any of these conditions?"

My friend argues that this chapter, taken seriously, refutes itself. His reasoning, as I wrote it down afterwards:

(1) The chapter's own cases show that no policy tracks survival: uselessness saves the tree and kills the goose; usefulness would have saved the goose and doomed the tree; and the closing lines extend the point to exertion, honour, and wisdom generally -- each invites its own ruin.

(2) If no policy -- neither effort nor restraint, neither usefulness nor uselessness, nor any fixed intermediate -- reliably improves one's prospects, then deliberating about which policy to adopt cannot improve one's prospects either.

(3) Hence the chapter commits anyone persuaded by it to abandoning practical deliberation altogether.

(4) But Kwang-dze plainly does deliberate: he weighs the disciples' question, proposes an answer, finds it wanting, and replaces it with a better one. A teaching that counsels abandoning deliberation while itself being a piece of deliberation is performatively self-undermining.

(5) Moreover, the conclusion proves too much in a second way: if (2) is true, then there is also no reason to follow the chapter's own positive advice ("Let your abode be here, -- in the Tâo and its Attribute"), since adopting that abode is itself one policy among others, with no more reliable connection to flourishing than any other.

(6) Therefore the chapter, read as practical teaching, either says nothing action-guiding at all or refutes itself; the celebrated ideal of effortless action it is taken to express is not a practicable ideal but a confession that nothing can be practiced.

I told him the conclusion must be wrong -- I want to keep both that the text is coherent and that wu-wei names something a person can genuinely cultivate rather than a counsel of despair -- but I could not produce a clean refutation on the spot. Every premise looked defensible when we examined it separately, and his inference from (1) to (2) especially seemed to me to smuggle something in without my being able to say what.

So: which step of his argument should I reject, and why does that step look so secure when stated on its own? In answering, please do three things. First, identify the exact point at which the reasoning goes wrong -- or, if you judge the argument valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender and what it costs to surrender it. Second, say whether the fault lies in my friend's reading of the passage or in the passage itself: does the disciples' question, as Kwang-dze handles it, really frame the problem as choosing the policy with the best track record, or has my friend imported that framing? Third, apply your diagnosis to the practical residue: on your reading, what would it actually be to follow the advice about the dragon and the snake, and does deliberately setting out to do so fall prey to the paradox of trying not to try -- or show, on reflection, that the paradox was never generated by this text in the first place?
