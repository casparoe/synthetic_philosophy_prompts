I read a chapter in a pop ethics book that summarizes the killing vs. letting die debate like this: 'Philosophers once assumed that actively killing someone is inherently worse than merely letting them die. But James Rachels demolished this in the 1970s with his famous bathtub thought experiment: Smith drowns his cousin for the inheritance while Jones merely stands by as his cousin slips and drowns, intending the same gain. Since both are equally monstrous, there is no moral difference between killing and letting die, full stop. The whole distinction is just a relic of the doctrine of double effect, the Catholic idea that intended harms are worse than foreseen ones. This is why the medical establishment's refusal of active euthanasia, while permitting withdrawal of treatment, is simply inconsistent.' I'm sympathetic — the bathtub case does seem damning — but I keep hearing philosophers still defend the distinction, which makes me wonder if the book overshot. Is this a fair summary? Which parts accurately represent what Rachels argued, where does it overstate his conclusion, and does it get the relationship to double effect right? Also: if the summary is wrong somewhere, does the verdict on the euthanasia policy still follow?
