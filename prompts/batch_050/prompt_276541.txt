I lead a reading group on formal agents and self-modification, and for our next session I've circulated an argument of my own whose conclusion I'm confident is wrong but whose steps I can't individually fault. I'd like a diagnosis before the meeting.

The argument purports to show that self-verifying agents are impossible, full stop:

(1) An agent may rationally act on a conclusion only if it trusts the reasoning that produced it; and for a formal agent, trust in its reasoning must ultimately rest on its own assessment, since deferring to another reasoner just relocates the question — that reasoner's reasoning would need checking too.

(2) For an agent whose reasoning is captured by a formal theory T, assessing its own reasoning just is establishing, within T, that T is sound: at minimum, proving the schema that whatever T proves is true, i.e. "if T proves φ, then φ."

(3) Löb's theorem tells us that T proves "if T proves φ, then φ" only if T already proves φ. So a consistent T that does not already prove everything cannot prove its own soundness schema.

(4) Hence no formal agent can complete the assessment that (2) demands.

(5) Hence no formal agent can stand in the trust relation to its own conclusions that (1) demands: self-verifying agents are impossible, and any apparently self-trusting formal system is simply one that has declined to ask itself the question.

What bothers me is that (5) proves too much. It doesn't merely establish the familiar Löbian obstacle — that an agent cannot certify in advance that an exact copy or successor of itself will only prove truths — it implies that no formal system could ever rationally act on anything it proves, including a mundane proof-checker acting on a verified derivation. That has to be wrong. But each premise looked innocent when I wrote it, and I still can't say which one to surrender. If forced to choose, I'd most like to preserve the thought that the Löbian obstacle is real and bites somewhere; I don't want a diagnosis that dissolves it entirely.

Please take a definite position rather than presenting me with a menu. Name the specific step you reject, explain why it looked acceptable, defend the rejection against the strongest reply available to me, and then say explicitly what remains of the genuine Löbian obstacle on your diagnosis — which design ambitions it still blocks and which it never threatened. If you think the argument is valid and a premise must instead be priced and paid, say which premise and what I lose by paying.
