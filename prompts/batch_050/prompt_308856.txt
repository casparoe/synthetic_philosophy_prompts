I'm a phd student in philosophy of mind (cog-sci adjacent) and I brought this argument to our intentionality reading group last week. It was supposed to be a reductio of Dennett, but now I think it reduces basically everything, and I cant see where it slips. Background: we were doing the two-bitser case -- the vending machine built to sort US quarters that gets shipped abroad and starts accepting some local look-alike coin, so there's no fact of the matter whether it "really" sorts quarters or sorts the other coin -- next to the frog's-eye stuff, where the frog's visual signal was selected for firing at small dark moving things, so whether it means fly or means small dark moving thing is fixed only by selection history. Dennett's moral, as I read him, is that our intentionality is derived too -- derived from evolution instead of from a designer -- and that derived intentionality is the only kind there is. My friend in the group pushed back with this, and it seems to prove far too much:

(1) If a physical token is about something, its aboutness is either original (underived) or inherited from something that already has aboutness.
(2) The two-bitser is about quarters only relative to us: delete the designers and users and it just responds to metal discs of a certain size and mass. So artifact aboutness is inherited.
(3) Whatever fixes what a frog's-eye signal, or a human brain state, is about is the same kind of thing that fixes the two-bitser's content: a history of being selected for a function. (We granted this for the reductio; it's Dennett's whole point.)
(4) Inheritance is transitive and needs a terminus: any chain of inherited aboutness has to bottom out in something whose aboutness is original. Natural selection cant be that terminus -- selection doesn't represent flies, or quarters, or fitness; it isn't about anything at all.
(5) There is no other candidate terminus.
(6) So no brain state is about anything, and since utterances and texts get whatever aboutness they have from brains, nothing anyone has ever said or written is about anything either -- including premise (1).

My friend insists this is just Dennett's own conclusion (no original intentionality) wearing a fake moustache, but it seems strictly stronger: not "our aboutness is derived rather than original" but "there's no aboutness anywhere, full stop." I also tried running it on our lab's language model: if the model's outputs inherit aboutness from training text, which inherits from us, and we bottom out nowhere, then the chain never grounds -- which either makes the argument self-defeating or shows that model tokens and frog signals are in exactly the same boat. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want you to name the step, explain why it looked innocent, and then price the standard escape routes -- Searle's original intentionality, teleosemantics of the Millikan proper-function variety, and Dennett's own response -- so I can see what each one pays. If I'm forced to choose I'd rather give up the idea that meaning-chains need an intender at the bottom than accept that the frog's signal isn't about flies, but tell me the price tag before I commit.
