I am preparing a take-home assessment for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of mind module on higher-order theories of consciousness. Design one essay question, together with a model answer and a detailed marking scheme.

The essay question should be based on the following case:

An artificial system contains a first-order visual representation with the content that there is a red triangle before it. The system can operate in four configurations.

1. It has the first-order representation but no representation of itself as being in that visual state.
2. The first-order representation causes a separate higher-order state, but because of a malfunction the higher-order state represents the system as seeing a green circle.
3. The system has a correct higher-order state representing itself as seeing a red triangle, but this higher-order state is produced by a general statistical routine that is not causally sensitive to the particular first-order representation.
4. The first-order and higher-order contents belong to a single self-representing state, which both represents the red triangle and represents that very state as occurring.

The question should require students to determine, under higher-order thought theories, higher-order perception theories and self-representational theories, whether each configuration involves a conscious visual experience; if so, what its phenomenal character would be; and if the theories give unclear or divergent answers, where the unclarity lies. It should also require students to consider whether consciousness consists in a relation between distinct mental states, in the manner in which one state is monitored by another, or in an intrinsic self-representational structure. Students should be asked whether empirical evidence about the system’s internal operations could settle the issue, or whether the disagreement partly concerns what should count as a conscious state in the first place.

The model answer should:

- distinguish phenomenal consciousness, access consciousness, state consciousness and creature consciousness without treating these distinctions as a substitute for argument;
- explain why the higher-order representation need not itself be conscious, and why the standard theories need not generate an infinite regress;
- analyse configuration 2 as a case of higher-order misrepresentation, considering whether the resulting experience is of a red triangle, a green circle, both, or neither;
- analyse configuration 3 by distinguishing the mere co-occurrence of first-order and higher-order representations from an appropriate non-inferential causal or constitutive connection;
- explain why configuration 4 is congenial to self-representational theories but may be regarded as re-describing rather than explaining consciousness;
- compare the three theoretical families by their treatment of the four configurations rather than by reciting their standard motivations;
- reach a defended verdict about which configuration poses the most serious difficulty for higher-order approaches and why.

The marking scheme should be out of 100 marks. Allocate marks to the principal components of the answer and specify what would merit first-class, upper-second, lower-second and failing work. It should also identify at least six specific mistakes that should lose marks, such as treating higher-order consciousness as a little observer inside the system, assuming that every higher-order state must itself be conscious, equating consciousness with verbal reportability, ignoring the causal defect in configuration 3, confusing self-representation with merely having information about oneself, or presenting an accurate taxonomy of theories without applying it to the case.

The assessment should be suitable for students who have studied the main higher-order approaches but should not depend on close knowledge of any particular theorist’s terminology, article or historical formulation. The essay question must be answerable in approximately 1,500 words. The model answer should be approximately 1,000 words. Write the entire response in formal British English.
