So my local LW-adjacent reading group has two people who have been going at it for months about lethal autonomous weapons. One thinks we need a treaty banning weapons that select and engage targets without what campaigners call "meaningful human control." The other thinks "meaningful human control" is a motte-and-bailey slogan, and that a well-tested autonomous system could easily be *more* discriminate than a sleep-deprived 19-year-old with a rifle, so banning them on control grounds is confused (or even immoral, since it keeps worse options in use). They've clearly talked past each other a lot and I want to force a double-crux situation.

You're a professional philosopher who works on ethics of war but has also actually read some engineering literature on automation, human factors, etc. Think carefully here. Before drafting anything, brainstorm: (1) claims both sides could sign as-is, (2) places where their disagreement is actually verbal, (3) places where its a real factual crux vs a real values crux. Then write the joint statement they'd produce after a proper adversarial collaboration.

One thing I want you to actually engage with rather than just nod at: I suspect "meaningful human control" is three concepts wearing a trenchcoat. There's control-as-authorization (a human decided this *category* of engagement may happen, like a commander approving a strike package, or the way shipboard defensive gun systems have been left on auto-fire mode for decades). There's control-as-veto (a human is watching and can abort, subject to automation bias and the fact that a 20-second abort window on a loitering munition might be veto in name only). And there's control-as-predictability (the humans understand the system's targeting logic well enough that outcomes are attributable to their decisions, which arguably fails even for *supervised* systems if the targeting model is an opaque neural net). Your document has to say which of these the two camps actually disagree about, and whether the "ban" side can state its requirement without smuggling in whichever of the three is most convenient at the moment.

Also make them deal with the awkward comparison-class point: humans go "out of the loop" all the time (a submarine that loses comms, standing orders, artillery fired at coordinates, mines, which the ban side mostly accepts in legacy form). What principled line, if any, separates those from an AWS?

Format: a numbered list of agreed claims first (phrased tightly enough that both would actually sign, no diplomatic mush), then a disagreements table where each row is one residual disagreement stated in words each side would accept for its own position, then a cruxes section. For each crux, name a concrete case, experiment, or observation both agree would bear on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. If you think some piece of their dispute dissolves on inspection, say so and say why, dont paper over it.
