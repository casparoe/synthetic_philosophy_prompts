I'm a PhD student in philosophy of history, and I'm writing a dissertation chapter on the outbreak of the First World War in July 1914 as a test case for hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy in historical explanation. My advisor keeps pushing me on whether this is anything more than a terminological redressing of points historians already make about determinism and contingency, so I want the analysis done properly. Take the July Crisis — the assassination at Sarajevo, the blank cheque, the ultimatum to Serbia, the mobilization schedules, the domino-like entries of the great powers — and analyze it through the lens of hindsight bias (roughly, the tendency, once an outcome is known, to see that outcome as having been more foreseeable than it was) and the narrative fallacy (roughly, the tendency to impose a coherent causal story on events that were driven in large part by contingency, miscalculation, and noise).

Here is what I want the analysis to deliver. First, identify the specific features of the standard historiography of 1914 — the search for deep structural causes (alliance systems, militarism, imperial rivalry), the assigning of war guilt, the retrospective reading of statesmen's diaries and letters for signs of what they "really" expected — where hindsight bias and narrative pressure plausibly distort the explanation, and say for each feature what an explanation uncontaminated by that distortion would look like instead. Second, do the harder directional work: say what the episode shows about the two concepts. For instance, does the fact that historians armed with every archive still disagree about inevitability suggest hindsight bias is correctable by expertise, or that it isn't? Does the July Crisis vindicate the narrative-fallacy critique (the war really was a contingency that storytellers converted into an inevitability), or does it actually resist the critique (the structural pressures were real, and "narrative" is just what causal explanation looks like)? I don't want a verdict that the concepts "apply" — obviously they apply somewhere. I want to know where their application is doing explanatory work, where it's merely relabeling a methodological disagreement among historians, and whether the two concepts are even independent of each other in a case like this or collapse into one complaint.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the July Crisis is ultimately a confirming case, an anomaly, or a case the two concepts were never equipped to handle — then give the analysis, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
