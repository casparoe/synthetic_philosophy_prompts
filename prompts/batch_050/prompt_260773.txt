I'm putting together the position paper I have to circulate before my graduate seminar on population ethics, and before I commit to it I want a consistency audit of the five commitments the paper is built around. The seminar is on the nonidentity problem, and I've noticed that when I defend these individually everyone nods along, but I've never checked them as a package. Here they are. (1) Person-affecting restriction: an action can be wrong only if there is someone — someone who exists at some point in the world in which the act is performed — for whom that action is worse. (2) Case verdict: a couple who conceive now, knowing that any child conceived this month will be born with a painful congenital condition that still leaves life clearly worth living, act wrongly, given that they could simply wait three months and conceive a healthy child. (3) Incomparability of existence and nonexistence: it can be neither better nor worse for a person that they exist rather than never exist, because a comparison of that kind would require the person to be around in both states to be compared. (4) Wrongful-life verdict: conceiving a child whose life will be unavoidably miserable and not worth living is wrong, and moreover it wrongs that child specifically, rather than merely being a bad thing to do. (5) Rejection of impersonal grounds: an act's wrongness can never rest merely on the fact that one outcome contains more total wellbeing than another; impersonal betterness alone never makes anything wrong.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs (or triples) genuinely pull against each other, ordered by severity, and which only appear to be in tension but can be reconciled with a clarification I would happily accept. For each genuine tension, I want to know precisely what would have to give: which commitment could be revised or abandoned at the lowest cost to the rest of the set, and what that revision would forfeit in terms of the cases I care about. In particular, check whether (1) can actually deliver the verdict in (2), and whether (3) and (4) sit comfortably together given the way I've phrased the wronging claim. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for suggested readings; I am asking you to trace what each commitment commits me to and to find the cheapest coherent package in the neighbourhood. Please also distinguish, in your assessment, between points on which the philosophical terrain is genuinely settled and points where competent people disagree, and don't present a contested resolution as though it were the consensus. And since this is for a British department, please write your response in British English.
