I work on the epistemology of comparative psychology, and I want a diagnosis of a debate that seems to me to have stopped generating new arguments: the dispute over Morgan's canon and the attribution of cognitive capacities to nonhuman animals. The two sides are, roughly, the parsimonists, who hold that behavior explainable by associative learning, behavioral rules, or other relatively undemanding mechanisms should not be attributed to richer representational or metacognitive states, and the critics, who hold that the canon, in its classic formulation, presupposes an outdated scala naturae of faculties and that its routine application amounts to a systematic bias against animal minds -- what de Waal called anthropodenial, and what others have sharpened into the complaint that parsimony is invoked asymmetrically, with demanding mechanisms for humans being the default and for animals requiring heroic proof.

What strikes me is that both sides now grant each other's best cases. The parsimonists concede that null-hypothesis associative explanations of, say, corvid or primate behavior have repeatedly failed to fit the data; the critics concede that associative models are far more powerful than the canon's early defenders supposed, and that clever experimental designs have indeed unmasked apparent insight as learned cueing. And yet the standoff persists at exactly the same altitude: every new experimental result (mirror self-recognition, episodic-like memory in scrub-jays, metacognition paradigms) is absorbed by both camps as confirming their prior position.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement about what animals can do but a disguised disagreement about what parsimony is: one side understands simplicity as counting the number of mechanisms postulated, the other as counting the number of ad hoc stipulations a theory needs, and these orderings diverge precisely on the contested cases. If that is right, the debate is partly verbal and partly methodological, and no amount of new animal data will settle it. Test my hypothesis against what the two camps actually say. If you find it wrong, give the better diagnosis: is the impasse an empirical disagreement in disguise, a conflict over which defaults are epistemically justified, or a bedrock clash of starting intuitions about mind? And given your diagnosis, state concretely what would count as progress -- a formal measure of model complexity, a new experimental design, a conceptual distinction -- or argue that none should be expected.
