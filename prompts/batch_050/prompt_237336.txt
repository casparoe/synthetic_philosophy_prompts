Write a deadpan corporate white paper of 900–1,100 words from the fictional consulting firm CerebroMetric Partners, advising a multinational company to reorganize itself so as to maximize its Φ under Integrated Information Theory. Use sections such as Executive Summary, Current-State Causal Audit, Integration Roadmap, Governance Risks, and Frequently Asked Questions. The satirical target should be the managerial impulse to convert every theoretical concept into a measurable KPI—not IIT itself.

The humor must arise from a faithful treatment of the theory. Distinguish consciousness from intelligence and task performance; explain why merely increasing communication, collecting more data, or connecting every employee to every dashboard need not increase integrated information; apply the exclusion postulate to the question of which organization, department, or spatiotemporal scale counts as a single conscious system; and address why a highly efficient feed-forward workflow might be functionally impressive but phenomenally negligible. Acknowledge, without overplaying it, the unsettling possibility that certain simple recurrent architectures could score better than systems that appear far more useful or intelligent.

Include recommendations that sound plausible in consulting language but become increasingly disturbing on inspection: mandatory reciprocal causal loops, the elimination of one-way reporting structures, a committee charged with preventing departments from becoming rival maximal complexes, and procurement standards requiring “grain-compatible” clocks. In the FAQ, answer with complete seriousness whether the CEO remains conscious if the corporation achieves the higher Φ.

Maintain a sober white-paper voice throughout: no overt punchlines, invented quotations, citations, or bibliography. Do not misrepresent Φ as information storage, complexity, intelligence, or workplace synergy, and do not reduce the theory to “everything is conscious.” By the end, an IIT specialist should recognize that the satire understands the theory, while an ordinary reader should recognize the critique of corporate quantification.
