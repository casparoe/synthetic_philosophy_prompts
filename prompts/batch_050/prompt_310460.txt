Okay so here's something that's been bugging me for a while, and I'd like your help sorting it out. I'm a philosophy professor, and I'd like you to take on the role of one too -- specifically someone who works on the philosophy of preference and AI ethics. Think carefully about this one, and feel free to do some open brainstorming before you commit to an answer; I'd rather see you explore than produce a tidy textbook summary.

Consider two practices that are both pretty common these days:

1. Deliberative polling. You take a random sample of citizens and poll them on some issue -- say, carbon taxes. Then you bring them together for a weekend, hand them balanced briefing materials, let them question experts and hash things out in moderated small groups, and poll them again. The organizers then present the *second* poll as the more meaningful one: what the public "really" thinks, or would think if it had the time and information to think properly.

2. Alignment ambitions for AI assistants. At least in some corners of AI research, the goal isn't a system that simply executes whatever you literally request, but one that does what you *would* want it to do if you were more informed, more reflective, less impulsive -- sometimes framed as acting on your "coherent extrapolated volition." In milder form this already shows up when an assistant pushes back on a request it judges short-sighted or self-destructive.

My question: what do these two practices presuppose about the nature and authority of preferences? A few subquestions to structure your answer, though you needn't follow them rigidly:

- Both practices clearly assume that raw, unreflective, on-the-spot preferences are not fully authoritative. But do they presuppose the *same* thing about what confers authority? More information? More reflection? Freedom from specific biases? A coherent internal structure? Are those the same presupposition, and if not, which practice leans on which?
- Does the CEV-style picture presuppose something stronger than the deliberative poll -- namely, that there is a determinate fact of the matter about what someone would want under idealized conditions, and that an extrapolation procedure could in principle converge on it? Could the deliberative poll get away with a much weaker presupposition, like "informed preferences are simply better, whatever better amounts to"?
- Both practices seem to be in tension with the economist's revealed-preference orthodoxy, on which preferences just are whatever people choose. What exactly would a revealed-preference theorist have to say is going wrong in a deliberative poll -- that the post-weekend answers are contaminated, or coerced, or something else?
- Suppose the stronger presupposition fails: suppose extrapolated or idealized preferences turn out to be unstable, or underdetermined by the extrapolation procedure, so that different idealizations yield different "true" preferences. What would actually have to change in each practice? Does one of them survive that discovery better than the other?

Finally, please be explicit about where these practices are philosophically *underdetermined* -- points where several rival theories of preference would license exactly the same weekend-poll procedure or the same pushy assistant, so that observing the practice tells us nothing about which theory its designers tacitly hold. I find people skip that part, and it's often the most instructive bit.
