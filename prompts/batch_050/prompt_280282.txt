I am a senior at a secondary school founded in 1912, and I have been selected to give the student address at this year's Founders' Day assembly: roughly six minutes, in front of about six hundred people — current students aged eleven to eighteen, the faculty, the principal, and alumni, including several in their eighties who attended in the 1960s and sit in the front row.

Here is the problem. While preparing, I spent two afternoons in the school archive and discovered that our most cherished traditions are invented in Hobsbawm's sense. The Lantern Ceremony, which the school magazine describes as held every winter since the school's first year, and the Founder's Charge, a short text each graduating class recites, supposedly unchanged since 1912, were in fact created from scratch in 1986 by a headmaster named Aldous Venn. His memo to the board of governors survives: "A school without ritual has no memory of itself. I propose we give it one, and let time do the rest." The elderly alumni genuinely remember these traditions as ancient — they were simply never told otherwise — and most current students assume the same. The principal now knows that I know, and has asked me not to, in her words, "make a scene," because "a school needs its myths."

I have read enough Hobsbawm and Anderson to suspect the situation is more interesting than "the traditions are fake." As I understand it, Hobsbawm's point is that invented traditions are deliberate attempts to establish continuity with a suitable past, usually because a community needs cohesion; Anderson's is that every community larger than a face-to-face group is imagined, and that being imagined is not the same as being imaginary — communities differ in how they imagine themselves, not in whether they are genuine or fraudulent. So a truthful speech cannot claim these traditions are a century old, and a decent speech cannot dismiss them as a con, because they have genuinely held people together for forty years and the shared imagining seems to be the substance of the community rather than a defect in it.

Please act as a professional speechwriter who is also an expert on the sociology of nationalism and collective memory. Think carefully about what a speaker in my position may honestly say, and to whom they owe what. Specifically:

1. First, brainstorm three distinct framings for the speech (for example: full disclosure, tactful silence, or some third option), with one or two sentences each on what each framing risks and for whom.
2. Then choose the framing you think best and justify it in a short note of about 150 words addressed to me.
3. Then write the full address, 750–850 words.

Constraints on the speech itself: no sentence may assert or imply that the traditions date from 1912 or are timeless; it must land for the eighty-year-old alumni, for whom these rituals are lived memory rather than myth, without losing the eleven-year-olds; it may not name Hobsbawm, Anderson, or any other academic — the ideas must do their work structurally, in plain language; it must contain one moment of genuine humor that does not mock the traditions or the people who love them; and it should end with something the graduating class can actually do, treating a tradition as a promise a community makes to itself and must keep remaking, rather than an inheritance it merely finds. Please do not reach for the easy line that it does not matter whether a tradition is invented because it feels real to us, unless the speech has earned it.
