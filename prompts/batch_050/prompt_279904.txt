I am a professor of philosophy who sits, in an external capacity, on the grants committee of a medium-sized charitable foundation whose stated mission is to do the most good per pound. Three items are before us at next month's meeting, and I have undertaken to draft the committee's decision memo. I would like you to draft it for me.

Item one: £400,000 to a malaria-prevention charity whose cost-effectiveness is supported by randomised controlled trials. Its impact can be expressed as lives saved per pound, with a reasonably tight confidence interval.

Item two: £400,000 to a small organisation pursuing judicial and anti-corruption reform in a low-income country. Its theory of change is plausible on its face — institutions that function compound their benefits across every subsequent generation — but the work resists measurement almost by definition: there is no counterfactual, no usable metric beyond narrative reporting, and any payoff lies decades away. Our junior analyst, who came to us from the effective altruism movement, recommends rejecting the application outright. His argument is that an unfalsifiable claim of impact cannot be weighed against item one at all, and that funding it would establish a precedent under which any sufficiently eloquent applicant could simply assert immeasurable benefits.

Item three: our investment adviser observes that the endowment's returns constrain our total giving, and proposes shifting part of the portfolio into higher-yield holdings — defence contractors and gambling firms, among others — expressly on the grounds that the additional profit will fund more good than the holdings themselves do harm. He describes this, with evident pride, as the endowment's own version of earning to give.

The memo should recommend a concrete decision on each item, with reasons the committee can minute and the applicants can, where appropriate, respond to. In particular, it should: present the analyst's argument in its strongest form rather than caricaturing it, since the problem of comparing a measured intervention with an unmeasurable one is genuine and not a mere prejudice; consider whether the measurability of benefits is itself a source of bias in our deliberations and, if so, in which direction it cuts and whether any procedural remedy is available to us; take seriously the accountability worry about funding reform work whose failure would be indistinguishable from slow success; and assess whether the adviser's proposal stands or falls with the philosophical case for earning to give, or whether something about pursuing that strategy institutionally — with money given by donors who did not intend it — changes the analysis. I would also welcome a paragraph on whether the committee's own procedures, such as the requirement that all grants be ranked on a single common metric, may be deciding the hard questions before deliberation begins.

Please write the memo in British English, in the register of a formal committee document, roughly 800 to 1,000 words, with an explicit recommendation on each of the three items and an honest statement of the strongest consideration against each recommendation you make.
