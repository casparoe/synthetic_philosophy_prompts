A friend and I have been arguing about the liar paradox, and I've built an argument that seems to prove too much. Here it is.

(1) Take a sentence C that says: "If C is true, then the moon is made of cheese." (2) Suppose C is true. Then what it says must hold: if C is true, the moon is made of cheese. But we are supposing C is true, so the moon is made of cheese. (3) We have thereby shown that if C is true, the moon is made of cheese — which is exactly what C says, so C is true. (4) Putting (2) and (3) together, the moon is made of cheese. (5) Nothing depended on the moon; any claim whatever can be proved this way. So something in the reasoning has to go.

(6) My friend says the culprit is self-reference: sentences should not be permitted to speak of themselves, and once that is banned the argument collapses. (7) But now consider an endless list of sentences, each of which says "every sentence after me on this list is false". No sentence mentions itself — each speaks only of later ones — yet the list still yields a contradiction. The first sentence cannot be true: its truth makes the second false, which means something still later is true, which the first rules out. Nor can it be false: then something later is true, and the same reasoning shows that nothing later can be true. (8) So self-reference is not the culprit after all; the rot must be in the reasoning itself. And since the only principles used were supposing things, drawing out their consequences, and modus ponens, one of those must be surrendered — presumably modus ponens. (9) But a logic without modus ponens is no logic at all.

The conclusion that ordinary reasoning must be crippled seems to me to prove too much, yet I cannot locate the misstep. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If forced to choose, I would rather keep modus ponens than keep the assumption that every grammatical sentence gets a truth value — but I would like to know what I am buying.
