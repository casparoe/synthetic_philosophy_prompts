Cost-effectiveness analysis in philanthropy ranks interventions by their measurable near-term effects — lives saved or income gained per dollar within a few years — while setting aside each intervention's long-run ripple effects on economic growth, political institutions, migration, and the composition of future generations, on the grounds that such effects are too uncertain to estimate. Yet the same analysts would refuse to fund a program with excellent measured near-term results if it were known to cause an equal harm half a century later; distant harms are not treated as negligible when they can be traced. What does this asymmetry presuppose about the relationship between knowledge and obligation? Reconstruct the implicit principle — that unpredictable effects are zero in expectation, that moral reasons extend only as far as our evidence, that the foreseeable/unforeseeable distinction tracks something morally real, or something else — and assess whether it is coherent given the consequentialist logic these analyses otherwise assume. Finally, say what the practice would have to look like if we instead took seriously the possibility that an intervention's largest effects are precisely the ones we cannot trace.
