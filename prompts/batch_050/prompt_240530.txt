Two regulars at my local rationality meetup got into a dispute over the Ross–Littlewood urn supertask, and I'd like an outside verdict. The setup: at each step n (compressed into the minute before noon, Zeno-style), balls numbered 10n−9 through 10n are added to an urn and the lowest-numbered ball present is removed. Their exchange went as follows:

1. She argued the urn is empty at noon: for every ball k, there is a specific finite step at which k is removed, so no ball survives the supertask. What remains must be nothing.

2. He replied that this can't be right: the number of balls in the urn grows by nine at every step and diverges to infinity, and a quantity that is strictly increasing without bound at every stage cannot drop to zero at the limit. He accused her of sliding between "no particular ball survives" and "no balls remain," which he called a quantifier-switching fallacy.

3. She countered that his argument proves too much: by the same logic, Thomson's lamp would have to have a definite state at the end of its supertask, since it has a definite state at every earlier moment -- yet the standard verdict is that the lamp's final state is genuinely underdetermined by the prior history. Global properties of a system, she said, need not be continuous in the limit.

4. He answered that the lamp case cuts against her, not for her: the lamp's state has no limit at all, which is exactly why it's underdetermined, whereas the ball count has a perfectly good limit (infinity). If limits are to be respected uniformly, the urn should contain infinitely many balls at noon, and her "each ball is eventually removed" reasoning conflates pointwise convergence with convergence of the cardinality.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it is correct, incorrect, or partially correct, and justify briefly. Then state what, if anything, both parties are missing about which mathematical facts settle the question and which intuitions are unreliable here.
