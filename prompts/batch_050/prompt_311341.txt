I am a doctoral student in philosophy of biology, and for the past two years I have been following the dispute over whether holobionts — hosts together with their symbiotic microbiota — are themselves organisms or biological individuals, and I have come to suspect that the debate has stalled in a structurally interesting way. As far as I can tell, both camps agree on essentially all the empirical facts: that many symbionts are horizontally acquired, genetically heterogeneous, and capable of evolving at cross purposes with the host; that hosts and microbes nonetheless depend on each other developmentally, metabolically, and immunologically; and that textbook cases such as the squid–vibrio light organ and the aphid–Buchnera symbiosis sit at very different points on a continuum of integration. Yet one camp, reasoning from evolutionary criteria — fitness alignment, reproductive bottlenecks, the suppression of internal conflict — concludes that the holobiont is an ecological community rather than an individual, while the other, reasoning from physiological integration and developmental interdependence, concludes that it is a unified whole whose boundaries can be drawn by metabolic and immunological continuity. Nobody moves, and the arguments on both sides have been stable for years.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about holobionts at all, but about which explanatory project the concept of an organism is for: an evolutionary project that needs units of selection and adaptation, versus a physiological project that needs bearers of health, function, and self-maintenance — and that each camp quietly treats its own project as the one the concept must serve. A related suspicion I would like tested: organism functions as an honorific, a status that licenses functional and normative talk (health, interests, adaptedness), so the real dispute is about which entities deserve that explanatory treatment rather than about where any boundaries lie.

Please diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicate it. Is it verbal, substantive, a continuum problem wrongly treated as binary, or two questions pursued under one heading? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually argues, and stress-test it against neighboring cases where the same criteria seem to be applied without controversy — eusocial insect colonies, colonial siphonophores such as the Portuguese man o' war, volvocine algae, lichens — asking whether the camps are even internally consistent across these. Finally, given your diagnosis, say what progress would look like: a formal criterion, an agreed empirical discriminator, a principled pluralism about individuality, or instead an argument that the impasse shows the question itself is ill-posed.
