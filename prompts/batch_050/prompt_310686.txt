So I've been hanging around LessWrong and the ACX comments for years and one habit I picked up from that lot is treating dead authors like forecasting systems: log their calls, reverse-engineer the algorithm they seem to be running, then test it on a holdout case they never saw. I've been doing this all summer with Machiavelli — rereading The Prince (the old public-domain Marriott translation) alongside the Discourses, and keeping a little spreadsheet of his verdicts on founders and rulers. It's going badly, and I want help.

His calls, as I read them:

- Moses, Cyrus, Romulus, Theseus (Prince ch. 6): top marks, full virtù. Fortune gave them the occasion and nothing more; everything else they made themselves.
- Savonarola (same chapter): doomed from the start, because unarmed prophets always come to ruin — the crowd is easy to persuade and impossible to keep persuaded, and he'd no way to make belief stick by force.
- Cesare Borgia (ch. 7): Machiavelli says he can find nothing to reproach him for, offers him as a model for new princes, and when the whole project collapses anyway he puts it entirely down to the extraordinary and extreme malignity of fortune — not one mistake of Cesare's.
- Agathocles of Syracuse (ch. 8): potter's son, murders the entire assembled senate in one morning, wins, holds Syracuse for life, fights off Carthage, dies in his bed — and Machiavelli point-blank refuses to call what he had virtù. This is teh passage doing my head in (Marriott's translation; the words he renders as "talent" and "genius" are, as I understand it, both virtù in the Italian):

"Yet it cannot be called talent to slay fellow-citizens, to deceive friends, to be without faith, without mercy, without religion; such methods may gain empire, but not glory. ... What he achieved cannot be attributed either to fortune or to genius."

Empire but not glory — and achieved, apparently, by neither fortune nor virtù, which leaves a mysterious third thing he never names. Meanwhile Romulus, who murdered his own brother, gets a full pass: in the Discourses (I.9) Machiavelli argues the deed accuses Romulus but the outcome excuses him, because he used sole power to lay Rome's foundations rather than for private gain.

Now the holdout case. A mate and I cooked up a fictional ruler (it started as an argument about whether any real founder is clean, long story): a general in a collapsing republic who seizes power by massacring the assembled senate in a single night, rules through fear and exemplary punishment for fifteen years whilst exterminating the old oligarch families, then builds a civil service recruited by open examination, codifies the laws, and hands a stable, prosperous state to an adopted heir, under whom it lasts two centuries. Crimes at least as bad as Agathocles's; outcomes at least as good as Romulus's.

What I want:

1. Predict Machiavelli's verdict — virtù or not — and give me an actual probability. I'm sat at about 55/45 and my mate says I'm being mealy-mouthed.
2. Lay out the candidate rules that fit the whole track record above. I can see at least two: a moral-filter rule, where certain means bar you from virtù regardless of results, and a founding-excuse rule, where crimes committed to build durable orders get laundered by the outcome. Maybe there's a third I'm missing. Say what each predicts about our general.
3. Tell me whether The Prince and the Discourses genuinely disagree about this case, or whether there's a reconciliation — I've been staring at the later bit of ch. 8 about cruelties being well committed (done once, out of necessity, then converted into security) versus badly committed, and I'm not convinced it does the work people claim for it.
4. Name the case, real or hypothetical, whose verdict would best tell your candidate rules apart. And if his corpus genuinely underdetermines the answer, I'd rather hear that than a forced verdict.

Few hundred words is plenty, no citations needed beyond what's above.
