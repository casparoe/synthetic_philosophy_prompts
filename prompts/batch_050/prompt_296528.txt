In a discussion about whether large language models have refuted Chomsky-style nativism, someone told me that children face a 'poverty of the stimulus' and so the fact that an LLM can learn grammar from data is irrelevant, since children learn from a tiny fraction of that data. I suspect 'poverty of the stimulus' is doing several different jobs in arguments like this and I want to pull them apart. What are the distinct theses the phrase could be expressing? For instance: that finite input can't fully determine an infinite capacity; that the input lacks specific kinds of evidence (e.g., children never hear the ungrammatical sentences they nevertheless know to be ungrammatical, or lack direct evidence for structure-dependence in question formation); that children receive no systematic correction or negative evidence; that the input is too noisy or degenerate; that some specific grammatical principles must therefore be innate; or merely the weak claim that learning requires prior constraints of some kind, which nobody denies. For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive empirical claim, or something close to false, and then tell me which readings would actually be needed to support my interlocutor's move that LLM success leaves nativism untouched -- and whether those are the readings people who invoke the phrase typically have in mind.
