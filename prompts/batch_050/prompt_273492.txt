I am a philosophy professor designing the capstone exercise for my graduate seminar on normative ethics under uncertainty, and I need you to produce the model answer. You are an expert in decision theory and metaethics with a reputation for drafting documents that partisans on both sides of a dispute will actually sign. Please work carefully: begin by brainstorming — separately, and before writing anything polished — the points of agreement, the genuine fault lines, and the merely verbal tangles between the two camps described below. Only after that brainstorming should you draft the deliverable.

The situation. Two members of my seminar have been deadlocked all term. The first defends *expected choiceworthiness maximization* (ECM): an agent facing moral uncertainty should assign credences to first-order normative theories and choose the option that maximizes credence-weighted choiceworthiness, where each theory contributes its own verdicts on its own scale. The second defends a *parliamentary* or *buck-passing* view: moral uncertainty should be modeled as negotiation among the theories one takes seriously, with no cardinal intertheoretic scale — theories bargain, compromise, or hold vetoes, and there is no fact of the matter about how much choiceworthiness a utilitarian gains from a given act relative to what a deontologist loses.

Draft the joint statement they should have produced by now, with the following sections:

1. **Agreed claims.** The propositions both parties can sign, stated non-vacuously — for instance, claims about what any adequate approach to moral uncertainty must explain, about which toy cases give clear verdicts, and about what the opponent's view is *not* (each side has been caricaturing the other, and part of your job is to state what each camp actually holds, in words that camp would accept).

2. **Disagreements, stated neutrally.** A numbered list (or table) of the remaining points of dispute, each formulated so that both parties would endorse the wording of their own position. Avoid terms of art that smuggle in one side's framing; where the two camps use the same word differently, note this explicitly rather than picking a usage.

3. **Cruxes.** The sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. I suspect, but you may confirm or correct me, that these include: whether choiceworthiness differences are intertheoretically comparable in principle (not merely in practice); whether what I call *Pascalian capture* — a low-credence theory assigning astronomical stakes to some option swamping the expected-choiceworthiness calculus — is a reductio of ECM or a bullet it can bite; and whether any parliamentary procedure can avoid being an unprincipled gerrymander whose output depends on arbitrary weights assigned to the delegates.

4. **Tests.** For each crux, a concrete case, argument, or proposed formalism that *both* parties agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case recorded in advance. In particular, I want both parties on record about the following device of my own devising: the **canonical-gap benchmark**, which proposes to render scales comparable by fixing, for every theory, the choiceworthiness difference between two agreed canonical options (say, saving a stranger's life versus keeping a minor convenience for oneself) as the common unit, and normalizing all scales so that this gap equals one. Both parties must state whether they accept this benchmark as theory-neutral, and if not, exactly which step of the normalization begs the question against them. Do not let either party escape with the bare claim that the benchmark is "arbitrary" or "obviously fine."

5. **Verbal residue.** If you conclude that some portion of the year-long dispute was about words rather than substance, identify which portion and explain why; if you conclude that none of it was, explain what that shows about the depth of the comparability problem.

The tone throughout should be that of a document both parties could put their names to — neither a verdict on who is right nor a transcript of their arguments, but the constructive residue of an adversarial collaboration, with the residual disagreement made exact.
