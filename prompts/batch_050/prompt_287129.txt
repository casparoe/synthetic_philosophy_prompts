I would like to know whether the following pattern has an established name in the philosophy of technology or in some neighboring field, because I keep needing a word for it and cannot find one that fits exactly.

The pattern is a ratchet in the status of a technology rather than in its design. A technology is introduced as a convenience: no one has to use it, and it genuinely serves the people who do. Adoption spreads, and at some point institutions quietly reorganize around the assumption of adoption. Forms move online only; the staffed ticket office closes; colleagues assume you saw the message. Nothing about the artifact itself has changed, and no single decision was ever taken to make it compulsory. Yet by the end of the process, declining to use it is no longer a neutral option. It carries real costs, and -- this is the part I find most striking -- those costs are now treated as the non-user's own fault. Early on people say that you can simply opt out; later the same people say that you should simply have used it, without acknowledging that the meaning of opting out has inverted somewhere along the way.

Two instances I have in mind: a restaurant that replaces printed menus with a QR code, where the diner without a smartphone is at first cheerfully accommodated and eventually treated as difficult; and the private car in a city that dismantles its public transit as car ownership becomes normal, until not owning a car is simultaneously expensive and regarded as an eccentric lifestyle choice.

The reason I am asking rather than just calling this lock-in is that the discussions I have encountered of lock-in, path dependence, and network effects seem to concern the mechanism -- why switching away becomes costly -- whereas what I want to name is the normative reversal that rides on top of the mechanism: the migration of responsibility onto whoever is left outside, and the survival of the language of free choice after the choice has effectively disappeared. It also seems distinct from the claim, which I associate with Langdon Winner, that certain artifacts are inherently political because of how they are constructed or what social arrangements they require; here the artifact may be entirely innocuous in itself, and the politics emerge purely from the adoption dynamics.

So, three questions. First, is there an established term for this specific configuration, and which literature, if any, treats it directly? Second, if the nearest concepts are the ones I have mentioned, which fits best, and where exactly does each fit fail? Third, if nothing established covers it, could you propose a name with a definition precise enough to mark its boundaries -- in particular, does the pattern apply to literacy, or to money, and if not, what feature excludes them?
