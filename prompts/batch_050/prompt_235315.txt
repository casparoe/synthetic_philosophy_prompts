I'm a novelist in the early chapters of a book whose narrator's older sister, after a small right-hemisphere stroke, becomes calmly convinced that her husband has been replaced by an impostor -- she recognizes his face, his voice, his habits, and insists none of it matters. In researching Capgras delusion so the character rings true, I've fallen down the rabbit hole of the two-factor theory: roughly, the idea that a delusion needs both something that generates the strange content in the first place (an anomalous experience or neuropsychological deficit, of the sort associated with work by Max Coltheart and colleagues) and a second, separate failure of belief evaluation that explains why the person doesn't simply reject the implausible hypothesis. What I can't get straight is what each factor does on its own, and my novel's plot may hinge on the answer, so I'd like a thought experiment built to order rather than another summary of the literature.

Construct a scenario with two characters, each exhibiting exactly one of the two factors in isolation. Character A has the full anomalous experience underlying a Capgras-type presentation -- the missing or altered affective response to a loved one's face -- but a fully intact belief-evaluation system. Character B has the second-factor deficit (impaired ability to reject hypotheses on grounds of implausibility) but no anomalous experience of any kind -- give B some perfectly ordinary seed for an odd conviction instead, like a neighbor who has recently started behaving slightly strangely. Constraints: both characters must be clinically and psychologically realistic, no fictional technology or stipulation of outcomes, each character sketched in no more than a paragraph, and the verdicts must be generated by applying the theories, not asserted. Then run the demonstration: state what the two-factor theory predicts for each character (in particular, whether either ends up deluded, since the case is designed to test whether both factors are individually necessary), and contrast this with what a one-factor or endorsement-style account predicts, especially for Character A, where the accounts should come apart.

Finally, take up the question my novel actually needs answered: for whichever character ends up with the stranger mental state, is that state a belief? Work this through behaviorally rather than by definition -- does the character act on the conviction, integrate it with the rest of their worldview, and show the emotional responses it would warrant, or do they exhibit something like the double bookkeeping clinicians describe, where the delusion is avowed but rarely lived? Identify the single feature of the scenario doing the most work and say what happens if it's removed, then give the strongest reply a defender of the one-factor view would make to stop Character A's case from counting against them. Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this -- for instance, don't present the doxastic status of delusions, or the empirical standing of the second factor, as resolved if they aren't. Please write the response in American English.
