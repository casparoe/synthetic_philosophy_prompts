For my high-school philosophy seminar, I am trying to infer my teacher’s theory of valid consent from these verdicts: lending a bicycle to someone who falsely claims to need it for errands, but actually uses it for an equally short, safe ride to meet a friend, is valid; lending it after being told the ride will be short and flat, when it will really be long and hazardous, is invalid; donating money after intense family pleading but no threat is valid; donating after a threat to reveal an embarrassing secret is invalid; selling old trading cards cheaply when the buyer merely fails to mention that they might become valuable is valid; and signing a contract while too intoxicated to understand it is invalid. Now suppose someone agrees to complete an unpaid survey after being falsely told that the anonymized answers will improve school lunches, when they will actually be sold to advertisers. The questions, time commitment, and lack of payment are accurately described, but the charitable purpose is the person’s only reason for agreeing. Predict my teacher’s verdict. Identify at least two principles consistent with all six earlier verdicts, state what each predicts here, explain which precedent is closest, and describe one further case that would distinguish your favored principle from its strongest rival.
