Draft the joint statement that a pragmatic-encroachment theorist and an epistemic contextualist would produce if they were made to run an adversarial collaboration on whether what you know depends on what's at stake for you. Format it as: (1) a numbered list of claims both will sign — covering the data, what each view actually says, and what neither is committed to (candidate to chew on: can both sign something like "you should treat p as a reason only if you know p"?); (2) a table of the remaining disagreements, each row stating the dispute in neutral wording and then each side's position in words that side would chose for itself; (3) the cruxes — sub-questions where settling them would force at least one side to give ground — with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

For the data section, both sides must reckon with the usual bank-style cases (people get reluctant to say someone "knows" once the cost of being wrong goes up), but also with these cases I've invented, which I want treated as tests each side must call before any philosophising:

- Crossed stakes. Priya is idly wondering whether the 6:10 to Leeds stops at Wakefield; nothing hangs on it for her and she has decent but ordinary evidence that it does. Nearby, Marcus stands to lose his house if it doesn't, and he's deciding whether to rely on Priya's say-so. He tells his mate: "Priya doesn't know." On each view, is what Marcus said true? Whose stakes count — hers or his? (This looks to me like the cleanest seperating test going, so make both sides commit.)

- Diachronic self-talk. Last week Sam told his flatmate "I know the post office opens Saturdays." Today, with a visa application that must go off by noon, he says "I didn't really know — I should've checked." Was his retraction true? Did he lose knowledge, or just lose the right to claim it, or was he wrong then, or wrong now?

- The eavesdropper. A stranger overhears a low-stakes chat in which Zara says she knows the library's open, and passes the report on. Later the stranger learns Zara's job depended on it and takes the report back. Which view predicts that retraction, and which has to write it off as a mistake?

Finally: state plainly whether these views are genuine rivals or could both be true at once, and if part of the row is verbal, say which part. Each side must also name one result or case that would make it pack up shop — saying "nothing could" doesn't count.
