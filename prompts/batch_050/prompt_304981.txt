A friend who studied philosophy briefly in university recently explained to me why, in his view, being morally responsible for something doesn't require that you could have chosen differently. Picture a neurosurgeon who has implanted a device in a man's brain that would force him to commit a murder the moment he showed any sign of deciding against it. As it happens, the man decides to kill entirely on his own, the device never activates, and so he is responsible for the murder even though he never had an alternative available. This proves that living in a determined universe is no threat to blame or punishment, and it also puts to rest the old principle that you can only be obligated to do what you're actually able to do."

I find the thought experiment striking, but I have a nagging sense that my friend is making it carry more weight than it can bear — particularly the leap from one imagined case to sweeping conclusions about determinism and moral obligation. Still, I can't articulate where, if anywhere, he goes wrong.
