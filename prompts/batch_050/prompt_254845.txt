I teach the discussion sections for a large introductory course on rational choice and politics, and the prisoner's dilemma unit is coming up. The lecture already covers the standard material — the payoff matrix, dominant strategies in the one-shot game, Axelrod's tournaments and why tit-for-tat did well. My problem is that students can recite all of this while remaining completely unmoved by it: they nod along at "cooperation emerges among egoists" without ever feeling the actual pull to defect, and they treat tit-for-tat's success as a magic fact rather than something conditional. I want them to experience the mechanism rather than memorize the conclusion. In particular, I want them to discover three things in their own play before I ever name them: that knowing when the interaction ends poisons the final rounds (and unravels backward), that a strategy which looks generous can be exploited by a strategy which looks merely selfish, and that a single misread of the other player's move can send two "nice" players into a feud neither wanted.

Design a classroom activity for one 80-minute section of 22 students, no computers, small real stakes of your choosing (candy, participation points, whatever you think works). The activity needs to include at least one phase of genuinely anonymous one-shot play so students feel why defection dominates there, and an iterated phase engineered so that the end of the game is not known in advance — and I want you to handle the noise problem somehow, because real interaction involves misperceiving the other side's intentions, and a classroom game with perfect information quietly teaches the wrong lesson. Give me the full rules, the exact payoff matrix with a defense of the numbers, how pairings and rematching work, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief: which moments in the game map onto which concepts, what will predictably go wrong (students colluding to split stakes, revealing the endgame, treating it as a popularity contest), and how I should respond when it does.

Finally, be honest about the limits of the exercise. Axelrod's later work is about evolution — strategies replicating in proportion to their success — and a classroom full of self-conscious humans playing for ninety minutes is not that. Tell me which features of the real theory the game cannot reproduce and what I have to supply in words afterward, rather than pretending the activity teaches everything.
