So I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist who's fallen down the philosophy-of-probability rabbit hole in the last couple years, and I keep circling back to Bertrand's paradox: pick a chord of a circle "at random" and ask if it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle — you get 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4 depending on how you parametrize the chords. Jaynes famously tried to dissolve the thing by demanding invariance of the physical tossing procedure under rotation, translation, and scale, which singles out 1/2.

Here's what I'd like you to argue for me: that Jaynes's fix only works because the chord setup smuggles in a physical randomizer, and that the principle of indifference therefore survives only as a rule about symmetries of well-posed sampling procedures — not as a rule for assigning probabilities from sheer ignorance. I want the argument to run through a wrinkle I haven't seen discussed: Bertrand's cube case (side somewhere in [0, 3]; ask whether it's between 2 and 3 — length, surface, and volume parametrizations give 1/3, 5/9, and 19/27). There's no plausible physical procedure lurking behind the cube, so invariance has nothing to grab onto. Defend the conclusion that this is a feature, not a bug: indifference reasoning is legitimate when it's shorthand for the symmetry of a generative mechanism, and illegitimate when it's about the geometry of the parameter space itself.

If you think the cube case does admit an invariance treatment, push back on my wrinkle — but the final verdict should still be that indifference-from-ignorance is dead while indifference-from-symmetry is alive. Keep it under about 800 words, and no bibliography, I just want the argument itself.
