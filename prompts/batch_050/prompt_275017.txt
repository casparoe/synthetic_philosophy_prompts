Is there an established name for this pattern, and if so, how is it usually resolved? I keep tripping over it when thinking about governance rules for small groups, and I've never seen it discussed on the blogs I read.

Concrete case: two housemates, Priya and Leo, are deciding what to do with the spare room. Priya could use it as a painting studio (P), Leo could use it as a drum practice room (L), or it could stay empty (E). Priya's ranking is E > P > L: she'd most like the room left alone, but if anyone is going to use it, she'd rather paint quietly than live next to drumming. Leo's ranking is P > L > E: he's convinced painting would be wonderful for Priya and loves the idea, would drum there himself otherwise, and hates seeing a room go to waste. Their house charter says two things: (i) each person has the final word on whether they personally take up a hobby in the room, and (ii) wherever the charter is silent, if both housemates prefer one outcome to another, that outcome prevails. But applying the rules: Priya's personal say picks E over P, Leo's personal say picks L over E, and unanimity picks P over L. No outcome survives all three comparisons, and every "reasonable" resolution violates one of the charter's clauses.

What strikes me is that the collision seems to require that people have preferences about other people's private business — if Leo were indifferent to whether Priya paints, nothing goes wrong. Is there a standard concept for exactly this: a clash between protected personal domains and a unanimity rule? I'd like the name, the canonical statement of the result, and a clear sense of how it differs from ordinary majority-tyranny worries and from Condorcet-style preference cycles, as well as which of the two clauses the surrounding discussion tends to treat as the culprit. No citations needed — once I know what it's called I can find the primary sources myself.
