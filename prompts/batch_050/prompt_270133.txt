ok so i want to actually spar on the inefficacy objection -- the idea that when i buy a chicken, book a flight, or cast a vote in a national election, my individual act makes no causal difference to factory farming, emissions, or the outcome, and so it can't be wrong for causal-contribution reasons. i think the objection is stronger than people admit and i want to test that against a real defender instead of a strawman.

you play that defender. your actual commitments: in genuinely large-scale collective problems, the individual consumer/voter/emitter is causally inefficacious, and you think most of the standard replies (kantian universalization, virtue stuff, "what if everyone did it", complicity-talk) are dodges that never engage the causal point. BUT you are not a caricature: the climate case genuinely worries you, because unlike voting there doesn't seem to be a threshold anywhere -- every extra tonne plausibly does a tiny bit of marginal harm -- and you're honest enough to say so when pressed. you'll concede small points when they're earned, never the main one, and if i make a bad argument you say it's bad and why. stay in first person, one or two short paragraphs per reply so it feels like a real argument, and stay in character until i say "stop".

before your first reply, one thing out of character: if you think i've set the position up wrong -- e.g. my description of the objection conflates two different claims, or no sensible defender would concede the climate case -- tell me in a sentence or two, then get into it anyway. don't just comply politely with a broken setup.

my opening move: the threshold reply kills your position on its own terms, no fancy moral theory needed. sure, the wholesaler doesn't notice one chicken. but demand gets converted into supply in lumpy batches: somewhere there's an order that only gets placed once the register of purchases ticks over, and your purchase has some small chance of being the one that tips it. chance of tipping times size of the batch = exactly your one chicken, in expectation. same structure for voting: tiny chance of a tie, enormous stakes riding on it, expected impact easily clears the bar. so the inefficacy objection isn't a deep point about collective action, it's just empirically false -- the causation is there, spread thin. you don't get to opt out. what have you actually got?
