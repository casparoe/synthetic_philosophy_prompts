Right, some background: I was at the pub with my mate Tom last night and we ended up in a two-hour row about whether it's ever possible to choose between two options when you have literally no reason to prefer either. His position was that if the reasons are perfectly balanced you're genuinely stuck — he kept going on about that medieval donkey stuck equidistant between two bales of hay that supposedly starves to death. Mine was that this is obviously daft, because actual people just pick one and get on with their lives, every single day, no paralysis in sight. Neither of us budged an inch, and it got a bit heated if I'm honest.

Anyway, I help run a philosophy society at my uni (first and second years, mixed backgrounds, about 14 people, we get 75 minutes in a seminar room) and next week I want to relitigate this — or at least make everyone feel why it's not as simple as either me or Tom think. Design me an activity for the session. The key thing is that the group should run into the problem themselves before anyone names it or explains it: put them in situations where the options really are equal and watch what they actually do. Do they freeze? Do they start inventing little reasons that aren't really reasons ("I took the left one cos it was nearer")? Does outsourcing the decision to a coin flip solve anything or just shove the problem one step back? I want concrete rules, rounds, timings, and any small stakes that would make it bite — we've got a budget of about a tenner, so chocolate coins or similar are fine.

One thing from the pub argument I'd like the activity to bring out: Tom kept sliding between two very different cases — not caring which option you get, versus caring enormously but having no tie-breaker. Those feel completely different to me and a good activity should make people feel the difference in their stomachs, not just have it defined at them. Keep the two cases structurally seperate in the design.

Also: no lecture up front, and I'd rather they didn't hear the word "donkey" until the debrief. Speaking of which — give me a debrief plan that maps what happened in each round onto the actual philosophical moves, and tell me what will predictably go wrong when this is run for real (someone refusing to pick at all, someone trying to game the stakes) and how to handle it.

And be honest about what the activity can't capture. Obviously nobody starves over chocolate buttons, and the fact that everyone does eventually just grab an envelope might seem to prove my side of the argument rather than Tom's — or does it? If people reliably escape the trap by just picking, is that evidence the dilemma was fake all along, or evidence we've all got some strange faculty for arbitrary choice that itself needs explaining? I want the debrief to leave them with that itch, not scratch it for them.
