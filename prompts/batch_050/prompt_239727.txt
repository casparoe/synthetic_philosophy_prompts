I run a small philosophy-of-science reading group at my university, mostly third-year undergrads, about 14 people, and we meet for 75 minutes. We're doing a unit on formal epistemology and I want to design an activity that makes Bertrand's paradox land as a genuine problem rather than a cute puzzle they nod at. What I have in mind: split them into groups and give each group the same question -- a chord is drawn at random in a circle, what's the probability it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle -- but make each group commit to one specific method of "drawing a chord at random" and compute the answer under their method. I want them to arrive at 1/2, 1/3, and 1/4 depending on their method and each be provably right, and then to have to put (fake) stakes on it: we'll actually generate chords physically in the room (spinning a pointer, dropping straws on a drawn circle, whatever) and each group bets points on what the empirical frequency converges to. The twist I want them to stumble into is that the physical experiment doesn't settle the dispute, it just inherits whichever randomization procedure we used to run it, so the word "random" was doing all the work from the start. Please give me concrete rules, materials, the payoff scheme, and a rough timeline, then a debrief that maps each moment onto the principle of indifference and why it fails for continuous quantities.

One more wrinkle. In the debrief I want to engage Keynes's discussion in A Treatise on Probability, where his diagnosis, roughly, is that the principle of indifference is only ever legitimate when the alternatives are discrete and your evidence really is symmetric across them, and that geometric cases like Bertrand's are exactly where that condition quietly fails -- I'm paraphrasing him, so treat that as my gloss rather than his words. Should the debrief push the groups toward his fix (refuse to assign any probability until the alternatives are carved up in a defensibly symmetric way), or toward the more modern move of saying probability is just relative to a specified sampling procedure and the original question was ill-posed? I'd like you to tell me which framing the activity's structure actually supports, since I suspect the betting element smuggles in one of them. Also: what's predictably going to go wrong the first time I run this (I assume at least one group will have already seen the paradox and will spoil it, and someone will object that "random" obviously means uniform over *something* and we should just say which), and how do I handle it? And be honest about which feature of the paradox the activity can't reproduce -- my worry is that because the physical simulation does produce *some* stable frequency, students may walk away thinking the paradox is just about ambiguity in lab procedures rather than about whether "no relevant information" can ever justify a unique distribution.
