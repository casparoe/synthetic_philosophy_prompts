I sit on the acquisitions committee of a mid-sized regional art museum, and we've got a decision due at next month's meeting that has split us right down the middle. The piece in question is a series of large-format photographs by a fairly established artist — call her M. — documenting the harvest ceremonies of a small indigenous religious community in our region. She spent about two years embedded there, got written permission from the council of elders, paid the community a licensing fee, and by all accounts the elders like the work and want it shown. The photographs themselves are, I think, genuinly good — not poverty porn, not exoticizing; they're restrained, and the community members are named as collaborators in the captions.

The problem: since the series was announced, a group of younger members of that same community has objected, loudly. Their position is that the elders had no standing to license what isn't theirs to license — the ceremonies belong to the tradition, not to any seven people who happen to be alive right now — and that turning sacred rites into wall décor for mostly white museum-goers is exactly the kind of extraction that a licensing fee can't launder. There's also a photographer from within the community whose own work we passed on last year, and she's pointed out, not unreasonably, that the museum would now have the outsider's version of her culture on its walls while the insider's version isn't in the collection at all. One of my colleagues says this is straightforward: consent was sought and given, the artist acted in good faith, and treating cultures as sealed boxes that only insiders may depict is incoherent and would paralyze any art that crosses boundaries, which is most good art. Another colleague says good faith is beside the point; the question is whether the work's value depends on an authority the artist never actually had, no matter how politely she asked for it.

Draft the committee's decision memo: acquire, decline, or acquire with conditions. I need an actual bottom line we can vote on, not a survey of considerations — and if you recommend conditions, make them things a museum could really implement and enforce (display rules, revenue-sharing, advisory roles, whatever), not gestures. The memo should give its reasoning in terms the objecting faction and the artist could both read and feel their position was taken seriously, which means stating the strongest version of the losing side and answering it rather than just outvoting it. If it matters: our charter charges us with representing the artistic life of the region, our funds are limited so acquiring this series probably means acquiring nothing else this cycle, and whatever we decide will set a precedent for how we handle this kind of case going forward.
