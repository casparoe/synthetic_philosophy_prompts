Our assigned textbook companion for a political theory course glosses Hobbes's argument in chapters 13–15 of Leviathan like this: Hobbes argues that life without government is a war of every man against every man, and his evidence is that people lock their doors at night and their chests even inside their own houses, which shows that human beings are by nature selfish and aggressive, so that the state of nature describes what actual human life was like before governments existed, and what it would immediately become again if government collapsed. Because conflict springs from natural human wickedness, the only cure is a sovereign with unlimited power, to whom each person surrenders all of their natural rights by contract in exchange for protection; and since the alternative is civil war, Hobbes's position is that any government, however tyrannical, is better than none, and that resistance is never justified.

The companion is by a reputable scholar, but two things make me suspicious. First, I recall that right after the passage about locking doors, Hobbes explicitly says something to the effect that in describing this diffidence he is not accusing human nature itself — which seems to cut against the textbook's 'naturally wicked' reading. Second, I have heard it said that for Hobbes the right to defend one's own life can never be given up, which doesn't sit well with 'surrender all of their natural rights.'

Please audit this gloss claim by claim, using only the text of Leviathan chapters 13–17 and the distinctions Hobbes himself draws there (for instance, right of nature versus law of nature, and fear of the sovereign versus fear of one another). For each claim, say whether it is accurate, a defensible simplification, or something Hobbes explicitly denies, and in the last case indicate where the denial occurs. Then answer the two questions that matter to me as a reader: does the locking-doors passage support an anthropological claim about innate human selfishness, or a structural claim about what rational agents are driven to when there is no common power over them — and does anything in the argument turn on the state of nature being a real historical period rather than an analysis of what is already present, in civilized societies and between sovereign states? Finally, given the corrected account, does the textbook's concluding claim — that for Hobbes any order is preferable to none and resistance is never justified — follow, or does his treatment of inalienable rights and of the limits of obligation leave more room than the companion admits? I am inclined to think the textbook is flattening the view, but I would rather know where it is right than win the argument.
