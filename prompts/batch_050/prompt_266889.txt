I teach a seminar on the epistemology of history, and every year I run aground on the same question: when does a legend become history, or rather, when are we entitled to extract history from legend? The reading list always includes Section X of Hume's Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, and this year I was struck not by the famous maxim but by a less-quoted passage in Part I, where Hume explains why testimony has authority at all:

"Were not the memory tenacious to a certain degree; had not men commonly an inclination to truth and a principle of probity; were they not sensible to shame, when detected in a falsehood: Were not these, I say, discovered by experience to be qualities, inherent in human nature, we should never repose the least confidence in human testimony. A man delirious, or noted for falsehood and villany, has no manner of authority with us."

Hume's thought, as I read it, is that confidence in testimony rests on contingent features of human beings that experience confirms: memory, honesty, and the policing effect of shame when liars are caught. But notice what happens to all three when a report passes through centuries of oral retelling before anyone writes it down. The memory in question is no longer a witness's memory; probity may survive intact, since the bard transmitting the tale is not lying but performing; and shame-at-detection loses all purchase, because no one remains who could detect anything. On Hume's own principles, then, a legend transmitted across generations may not be degraded testimony but something that was never testimony in the relevant sense at all. I find this both illuminating and, I suspect, too quick, and it is against that suspicion that I want my own case judgments tested.

Here are my verdicts, stated as I actually hold them:

(1) The Trojan War. I believe there was a historical war, or at least a historical destruction of the city at Hisarlik near the end of the Late Bronze Age, and that Homer preserves a memory of it. I do not believe in Achilles, Helen, or the abduction as casus belli. So: historical core, legendary surface.

(2) King Arthur. I do not believe there was a historical Arthur in anything like the legendary form. I am willing to grant a Romano-British commander associated with the Battle of Mount Badon, which the near-contemporary Gildas mentions without naming Arthur. But I would not call the Arthur legend history, even at its core.

(3) The Exodus. I do not believe the biblical narrative as written, and here, unlike Troy, I decline to grant the core event. I am open to a small-scale departure of some group from Egypt being dimly remembered, but I notice I am less confident about what that concession even amounts to, and this is the verdict I most often go back and forth on.

(4) Sargon of Akkad. The birth legend, the basket on the river, is obvious legend, and the man is nonetheless fully historical. His legends do not impugn him at all.

(5) Romulus. Pure legend. Yet I hold that Rome's eighth-century origins are history, and I do not feel these two commitments are in tension.

(6) William Tell. Nothing historical there at all, and the story's late and derivative character makes this the easiest case on the list.

Now, the pair I cannot reconcile: I accept the Trojan War's core on the strength of a text composed four or more centuries after the event, matched to an excavated destruction layer whose connection to the text is contested, while I reject the Exodus's core on the strength of a text arguably composed closer to its alleged events, also matched by some to archaeology, also contested. And in the Arthur case I am unmoved by a near-contemporary source that mentions the battle but not the man, whereas in the Trojan case I am moved by a late source that names the men but whose battle I can only infer. I cannot currently state the difference-maker that licenses both judgments at once, and I do not think "archaeology decides" does the work, since the archaeology is comparably ambiguous in the Trojan and Exodus cases.

So my questions are these. First, is there a single principle of legend-to-history adjudication that captures verdicts (1) through (6) — some combination, presumably, of independent and contemporary attestation, material corroboration, the ordinariness of the core claim relative to the rest of the record, and the distance between event and text — and can you state it precisely enough that each clause is motivated by a named case rather than bolted on? Second, if no such principle survives contact with all six verdicts, tell me which verdict you would overturn and defend the sacrifice; I am genuinely uncertain whether (1) or (3) is the weaker commitment. Third, state what your principle predicts for a case I have not listed: the Chinese Xia dynasty, attested only in texts written many centuries after its alleged existence, and identified by some archaeologists with the Erlitou culture, a correlation as disputed as anything at Hisarlik. Fourth and finally, relate your principle back to the Hume passage. Does the best account of my verdicts vindicate Hume's diagnosis — that multi-generational legend fails the conditions under which testimony earns authority, so that only the non-testimonial evidence (the walls, the king lists, the strata) is doing real work — or does it show that Hume's framework is incomplete, because we plainly do extract history from legend in some cases, and the question is only which?
