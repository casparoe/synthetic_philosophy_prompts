I am an animal welfare scientist with over a decade of experience assessing welfare in farmed and laboratory animals. My working vocabulary consists of things like: behavioral and physiological stress indicators (corticosteroid levels, stereotypies, redirected aggression), preference and motivation tests (how hard an animal will work to obtain or avoid something), the distinction between the Five Freedoms and the Five Domains model, aversion learning, anticipatory behavior, and judgment-bias paradigms as proxy measures for affective state. I take it for granted in my work that affective states are not directly observable and must be triangulated from converging indirect evidence.

I have recently been following the debate about whether reinforcement learning agents could suffer -- specifically the dispute over whether an agent's reward signal, or its reward prediction errors, should be understood as functionally analogous to pleasure and pain (valence), or whether reward is merely a training signal whose role in shaping behavior is easily confused with felt experience. Part of that dispute turns on the wanting/liking distinction drawn in affective neuroscience: the claim that the systems that drive pursuit of rewards and the systems that constitute enjoyment of them are dissociable, so that maximizing reward need not involve anything like positive experience.

Please restate the main positions in that debate using the vocabulary of my field. For each position, render its central claims as claims about welfare indicators, motivational structure, and the evidential standards we use to attribute affective states to non-verbal subjects. Then give me a residue report: which parts of the debate translate cleanly, which parts require distortion (and what gets distorted), and which parts -- if any -- have no counterpart in welfare-science vocabulary at all. Finally, tell me where you think the residue points: does it reveal something my field's inferential framework cannot express (for instance, questions about the substrate or architecture dependence of experience), or does it reveal that parts of the RL debate are trading on an ambiguity my framework happens to dissolve? I am especially interested in whether our standard practice of inferring affect from behavioral and physiological proxies would, if applied consistently, commit us to verdicts about artificial agents that welfare scientists would be reluctant to accept -- and if so, whether that reluctance is principled or merely parochial.
