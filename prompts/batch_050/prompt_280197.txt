I am a trustee of a regional art museum in the north of England, and last month the ground shifted under my feet. Our most-loved painting — a quiet interior of a woman reading, long attributed to a little-documented seventeenth-century Delft painter, and for decades the image on our posters and shop mugs — has been shown, by new pigment analysis and then a paper trail in a forger's estate papers, to be the work of a mid-twentieth-century restorer. He was a painter of real talent who never copied existing works; instead he invented whole 'periods' for obscure masters and painted new canvases to fill them. In our case he conjured a late style for our painter that never existed, and a generation of scholarship about the genuine works was quietly bent around our fake one.

The board meets in six weeks and must decide what to do. The live options, as I see them, are: (i) remove it to storage indefinitely, on the grounds that hanging it asserts a falsehood; (ii) keep it on display with a candid new label giving the full story; (iii) deaccession and sell it — famous forgeries do change hands for real money, and the proceeds could fund conservation of works that are genuine; (iv) destroy it, which one trustee has seriously proposed as the only honest course. A nearby university has also asked to borrow it indefinitely as a teaching object, whatever we decide.

I would like you to draft the written opinion I will circulate to the board in advance: a short, formal document recommending one course of action, with reasons the other trustees can actually weigh. Two things matter to me in the drafting. First, the opinion must take seriously the strongest case against whatever you recommend. If you recommend storage or sale, you must reckon with the fact that visitors loved the painting before the label changed, that the canvas itself has not altered one bit, and that it is arguably now a more interesting object than half our genuine collection. If you recommend keeping it on the wall, you must reckon with the fact that everything it appeared to teach our visitors about the seventeenth century was false, and that our authority with everything else hangs on how we handle this. Second, I want your honest judgement, not a fence-sit: a clear recommendation, the reasoning behind it, and a time frame for acting.

If you think the question as I have framed it rests on a confusion — for instance, if the options are not really exhaustive, or if the real disagreement is about something other than what I have described — please say so plainly at the outset rather than politely working within a faulty frame.
