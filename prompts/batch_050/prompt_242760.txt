The debate between illusionists about phenomenal consciousness and their opponents tends to produce monologues: each side explains, at length, why the other has failed to grasp what experience even is. I want the opposite artifact. Draft the joint statement that a committed illusionist and a committed phenomenal realist could both sign at the end of a patient, good-faith adversarial collaboration.

By illusionism I mean the view, defended in different forms by Daniel Dennett and Keith Frankish, that experiences do not really have the qualitative, intrinsic, ineffable properties introspection seems to reveal: phenomenal consciousness is a kind of introspective illusion, and the real work is explaining why we judge, say, and believe that we have it (the meta-problem of consciousness). By phenomenal realism I mean the view that there is genuinely something it is like to occupy certain states, that this what-it-is-like is not merely judged or represented to be there, and that explaining it is the hard problem, not to be dissolved by explaining our reports and intuitions. If you think either characterization is unfair to its side, fix it before proceeding and note the fix.

Structure the statement in four parts:

1. Agreed claims (numbered). Produce at least six claims both parties can sign. Candidates to adjudicate rather than assume: that something certainly seems to be presented in experience and that any adequate theory must at minimum explain the seeming; that introspective reports are the primary data of consciousness science and are demonstrably fallible in specific ways (list two or three, e.g., from the literatures on change blindness and confabulation); that neither a functional explanation of behavior nor a neural correlate, by itself, settles the dispute; that the conceivability of philosophical zombies is contested as evidence and that each side owes an account of what work conceivability does for it. Do not pad this section with banalities neither side was ever tempted to deny; each agreed claim should cost someone something.

2. Disagreements (a table). State each residual disagreement in a single row: the illusionist's position phrased as the illusionist would insist it be phrased; the realist's likewise; and the weakest reading on which the two statements remain incompatible. Flag any row where you conclude the incompatibility is verbal — where each side is using a term such as experience, seems, or real differently — and state what the non-verbal residue is, if any.

3. Cruxes and predictions. Identify the two or three sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one side to revise its position. Candidates I expect you to consider: whether a seeming can occur without the phenomenal property it appears to present (or whether this is the original dispute restated rather than a sub-question — argue for your classification); whether a complete predictive science of consciousness-judgments, one that explains in functional terms exactly why a system reports and believes itself phenomenally conscious, would leave anything unexplained; and whether the intuition of ineffability is evidence of a non-representational property or a predictable artifact of how introspective representation works. For each crux, give the case, experiment, or argument both parties accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. If you cannot find anything the realist accepts as bearing on a crux — because the realist holds the datum to be immediate and prior to all theory — say so explicitly; that asymmetry is itself a finding worth stating in the document.

4. A closing note on the statement's own vulnerabilities. Say where the drafting is most likely to have smuggled in one side's framing despite your efforts — for instance, in the agreed description of what the data are, or in the choice to treat the meta-problem as well-posed — and name the single argument or empirical result that would most change the statement's balance: the one whose establishment would let you move the most claims from the losing side's column into the agreed section, or strike the most from the winning side's. Do not answer with more neuroscience in general; specify what the neuroscience would have to show, and what each side's published position commits it to predicting about that result.

Write it in the tone of a document two tired, honest people could actually sign — not a debate transcript, and not a peace treaty that papers over the dispute.
