So I've been slowly working through Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments this year (not for a class, just because a coworker kept insisting it's better than the Wealth of Nations, and annoyingly he might be right). The stuff about sympathy as imagining yourself in someone else's situation, and the impartial spectator as this internalized imagined judge, really stuck with me. Around the same time, one of my close friends started using an AI companion chatbot pretty heavily — she talks to it about her day, says it comforts her when she's anxious, and genuinely seems to feel *understood* by it, even though she'd admit it presumably has no feelings to sympathize with.

Here's what I keep chewing on: what would Smith make of this? On one hand his whole theory is about sympathy as an imaginative act, so maybe the object of sympathy barely matters — if imagining the chatbot's "perspective" gets my friend into the habit of reflecting on her own conduct from an outside standpoint, is that just... moral practice with a fictional spectator? On the other hand, Smith seems to think the pleasure and discipline of sympathy depends on *mutuality* — other people actually feeling with us, and us adjusting our passions to a pitch they can go along with. A chatbot tuned to always validate you never demands that adjustment, so maybe it trains exactly the wrong disposition: expecting total attunement without ever having to moderate yourself for a real audience. I could also imagine him going a third direction I haven't thought of. Try to reconstruct his actual view from his theory rather than just free-associating — like, what's the strongest case he'd be fine with it, the strongest case he'd find it corrosive, and where does the balance come out?
