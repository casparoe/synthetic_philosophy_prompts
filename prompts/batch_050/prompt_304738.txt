I write about science for a general-interest magazine, and I have been trying to articulate the rules I actually follow about when I can simply pass along what a source tells me, as opposed to when I have to verify the claim myself before printing it. Here are my gut verdicts on cases from recent assignments:

When the census bureau's lead statistician walked me through a table of figures from her own published dataset, I reported the figures on her word alone. Fine. When a lab director told me his group had replicated a disputed result but couldn't yet show me the data, I printed the claim attributed to him. Fine, I think, though an editor pushed back. When the same lab's press office issued a statement saying the identical thing, I treated it as worthless until I saw the data. When a colleague at my magazine told me that a researcher she'd interviewed had admitted to cherry-picking, I treated it as established and didn't call the researcher back. Fine. When a stranger at a conference reception told me that same researcher had once plagiarized a chapter, I wouldn't dream of printing it without documentary evidence. When a stranger in an unfamiliar city gave me directions to a venue, I followed them without a second thought. Fine. And the case I keep going back and forth on: a source I know to be highly placed and previously reliable, but who must remain anonymous, tells me a trial was stopped early for safety reasons, and I've seen no documents. I printed it, and I still don't know whether I was right to.

What I want is a principle -- one principle, committed to rather than offered as a menu of options -- that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I could apply it at deadline speed. Tell me which clause of the principle is doing the work in each case, including a definite ruling on the anonymous-source case. Then tell me what it implies about two situations I haven't faced yet: quoting a historical claim from a deceased scientist's memoir, and relying on an AI system's summary of a paper I haven't read. And be honest about the cost: if no clean principle saves all my verdicts, tell me which verdict you think has to go, and defend that choice rather than presenting me with alternatives to weigh myself.
