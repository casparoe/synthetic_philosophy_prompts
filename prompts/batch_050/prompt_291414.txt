I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about strange anticipations of modern philosophy of science. Right now I'm stuck on al-Ghazali's occasionalism -- the argument in his Incoherence of the Philosophers that fire doesn't burn cotton through any necessity in the fire's own nature. Observation, he says, only ever shows us the two things happening together, not one making the other happen; the burning is created directly by God at the moment of contact. Which is why Abraham could sit in Nimrod's fire unharmed: that time, God simply didn't create the burning.

Write me a ghazal about this. Not a poem loosely inspired by it -- an actual ghazal, and the form itself has to do the philosophical work:

- Seven to nine couplets, each one semantically self-contained, the way a proper ghazal's shers are. No narrative or argument may carry over from one couplet into the next, because on al-Ghazali's view nothing carries over from one event to the next either. Each couplet is its own occaision, created whole.
- A radif, the repeated refrain closing each couplet -- something like "only if God wills it," or invent your own -- which functions as the sole connecting thread of the poem, the way divine will is the sole connector of events. Both lines of the opening couplet should carry it, as the form demands.
- The cotton and the fire have to appear, and somewhere the Abraham episode, since that's the case that proves conjunction isn't necessitation.
- One couplet should glance forward at Hume, who made almost the same observation about constant conjunction centuries later but left God out of it. The poem should register what gets kept and whats lost in that swap.
- The final couplet, the maqta where ghazal poets traditionally sign off, should turn on the reader or the poem itself: if nothing in the world necessitates anything else, what is a rhyme scheme doing?

Keep it accurate enough that someone who's read the seventeenth discussion of the Incoherence wouldn't flinch, but it should still work as a poem, not a summary with line breaks.
