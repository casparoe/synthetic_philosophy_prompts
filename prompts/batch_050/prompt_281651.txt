I help moderate the policy-discussion section of a long-running rationalist-adjacent community blog, and I want to operationalize the idea of public reason as a comment-moderation procedure. The theory, as I understand it in outline: in political discussion among people who hold irreconcilable comprehensive doctrines, coercive questions should be argued for on grounds every reasonable participant could in principle assess — shared evidence, common inferential norms — and not solely on grounds drawn from one sectarian tradition. Some versions allow religious arguments provided the speaker also offers, in due course, reasons of the shared kind; others exclude purely sectarian justifications outright. I don't want a treatise on which version is right; I want a procedure our moderators can run.

The constraints: we get maybe fifteen flagged comments a week in policy threads; a moderator should reach a decision in under ten minutes per comment; our moderators are volunteers with no philosophy background; and the community's norms prize steelmanning, charitable reading, and letting heated-but-honest argument run, so any filter will sometimes cut things many of us value. Please write the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step — e.g., how to tell whether an argument's operative premise is sectarian or merely religious in vocabulary, what to do when a sectarian premise has a plausible public translation the commenter didn't bother to state, whether the "proviso" style allowance should be modeled as a warning rather than a removal, and where the procedure should stop with a hard removal versus hand the decision to a human judgment call. Mark explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say what the procedure cannot settle at all.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a comment opposing legalized assisted dying solely on the ground that it usurps a divine prerogative; (2) a comment from a natural-law Catholic arguing against the same policy entirely in secular vocabulary about human flourishing and the ethics of killing; (3) a comment quoting a religious civil-rights leader's rhetoric in favor of a redistribution policy, with no other argument attached. Include at least one case where the procedure delivers a verdict you expect me to dislike, and say so. End by stating which criticism of public reason — the asymmetry objection that secular doctrines also rest on contested foundations, or something stronger — would most plausibly break this procedure, and what change to my community's circumstances would make you recommend abandoning it rather than patching it. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: whether public reason can be turned into a usable moderation procedure at all, and if so, in what form. No preamble.
