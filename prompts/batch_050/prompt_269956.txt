I'm a PhD student working on the epistemology of chance, and for my prospectus I've been asked to state my core commitments so my committee can stress-test them. Here they are:

(1) Objective chance is real and not reducible to actual frequencies: the Born rule fixes quantum chances exactly, including irrational values, even though any actual world contains only finitely many trials.

(2) I endorse the Principal Principle: if I know that the chance of A is x, and possess no inadmissible information, then my credence in A ought to be x.

(3) I accept a broadly Humean best-system account: the laws and chances are whatever figures in the best systematization of the total history of occurrent events, balancing simplicity, strength, and fit.

(4) Rational credence is always a matter of conditionalizing on one's total evidence; there is no category of possessed information that one may simply set aside.

(5) Determinism is compatible with non-trivial objective chance, as in statistical mechanics: even if the fundamental dynamics were deterministic, the best system could still assign chances strictly between 0 and 1.

My questions: which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be? Please rank any tensions by severity, and for each one state what exactly has to give and what the cheapest repair costs. In particular, I'm unsure whether (2), (3), and (4) can all survive together once I reflect on what the best-system theory itself tells me about future histories, and whether (1) sits comfortably with (3) given the finitude of actual trials. I don't want a general survey of the literature on chance-credence norms; I want an audit of this specific package, with a verdict on which single revision would clean up the most.
