I'm writing the background chapter of a master's thesis on algorithmic randomness, and my advisor has now twice rejected my working definition of what it means for a finite sequence to be random. My first attempt leaned on incompressibility -- a sequence is random if no shorter program generates it -- and she objected that this makes randomness relative to a choice of universal machine in ways I hadn't owned up to. My second attempt defined a finite sequence as random if it passes a battery of statistical tests, and she pointed out that the output of a pseudorandom generator with a short seed passes every test I listed while being produced by a very short program. We're clearly going in circles because we keep arguing about definitions before agreeing on what a definition here has to do.

So before I adjudicate between the candidate accounts, I want the specification itself. Write out the desiderata that any theory of finite-sequence randomness must satisfy to count as adequate for my purposes -- which is a notion of randomness usable in theoretical computer science, not in statistics practice. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it: for instance, the demand that a sequence of a million alternating zeros and ones not count as random even if it happened to be produced by a fair coin (or should it count then? say what a theory owes us on this), the demand that randomness of a finite string be at least approximately machine-independent given the invariance-theorem style results, and the demand that the theory explain what, if anything, is left of the intuitive idea that a sequence produced by fair coin flips is random even when its digits look suspiciously patterned. Distinguish carefully between desiderata that are genuinely non-negotiable for any account in this space and those that are really the commitments of one camp -- the incompressibility camp, the statistical-test camp, the frequentist camp -- dressed up as common ground.

The part I most want is this: some subsets of the natural desiderata here appear to be jointly unsatisfiable. For example, a theory seemingly cannot simultaneously make randomness a definite, effectively decidable property of every finite string, tie it to minimal-program length, and respect the Church-Turing boundary on what is computable -- something has to give, since Kolmogorov complexity is not computable and the naive attempt to define "the shortest description" runs into Berry-paradox territory. Identify every such tension you can find in your own list and state explicitly which requirement a working theory must weaken, and what it loses by weakening it. Also tell me which single requirement, if enforced strictly, knocks out the largest number of existing contenders. Finally, since my advisor will ask: state what would change your mind about the list itself -- which argument, case, or known result would lead you to drop or demote a requirement you've marked non-negotiable -- and say where you think the specification is most likely to be smuggling in a contested assumption. No bibliography needed; I have the sources, I need the structure.
