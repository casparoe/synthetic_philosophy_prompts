I've been slowly talked into the capability approach over the past year -- the rough view that what justice should equalize (or bring everyone up to a threshold of) is people's substantive freedoms to be and do things they have reason to value, rather than their welfare or their holdings of resources. The cases that did it for me are the usual ones: the disabled person with the same income who plainly isn't equally well off, the person who's adapted to deprivation and reports being perfectly content. Last week I was arguing about it with a friend who's a committed resourcist in Dworkin's mold, and she hit me with a line I couldn't answer: "your view never risks anything, because whenever a case goes badly for capabilities you just redescribe the case as one where the relevant capability was missing all along." That stung, and it's made me want a serious audit of what I actually believe.

So: what would count against the capability approach? I don't want generic grumbling about it being paternalistic -- I want a map of its real exposure. Which cases would a capabilitarian have to describe in a way that even they find embarrassing (I'm thinking of things like: people who have the capability but never convert it and seem fine; capabilities that are in principle unmeasurable, so the metric can't do the work a metric is supposed to do; findings that hedonic states track policy outcomes more reliably than any capability index, making the fancy metric otiose)? Which objections does the view stand or fall with -- e.g., does it collapse into sophisticated welfarism if "reason to value" is cashed out subjectively, or into resourcism if capabilities turn out to be perfectly correlated with resource holdings plus publicly known conversion factors? And here's the part I most want pressed: Sen famously refuses to give a fixed list of capabilities while Nussbaum gives one -- does Sen's refusal buy the view immunity from refutation at the price of never being testable against rivals, and is Nussbaum's list the price of testability at the price of it always being revisable when embarrassed? Rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a defender (Sen-type or Nussbaum-type -- they differ!) would actually concede it as a defeater or dismiss it as a misunderstanding. If your honest verdict is that the view is well insulated, tell me whether that's a virtue or a symptom.
