I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am writing a long feature timed to the next edition of the World Happiness Report. My editor has asked me to submit, alongside the first draft, a statement of the working assumptions the piece rests on, so our research desk can stress-test them before publication. Here is the set I have come up with:

1. The report's headline measure, the Cantril ladder, captures a reflective evaluation of one's life as a whole, not felt, moment-to-moment happiness. The 'happiest country on earth' framing is therefore misleading, and the piece should say so plainly.

2. Precisely because the ladder is a considered, whole-life judgment rather than a mood reading, it is the right primary target for public policy: governments should aim at how citizens evaluate their lives, not at passing emotional states, which are noisy and easily swayed by trivial interventions.

3. Finland's long run at the top of the table is evidence that institutions — trust, low corruption, universal services — genuinely move the needle, and that a country can deliberately climb the rankings by adopting them. The piece argues for this explicitly.

4. Self-reports are culturally inflected and reference-dependent: Latin American countries overperform on measures of positive emotion relative to their ladder scores, and Nordic norms of modest expectation plausibly temper responses. Cross-country comparisons are therefore noisy proxies at best.

5. Within-country change over time is another matter: if a nation's average ladder score rises after a reform, I will treat that as strong evidence that the reform improved lives, and the policy section of the piece is built around such cases.

6. On money: the evidence suggests emotional well-being plateaus once basic comfort is secured, so for already-rich countries further GDP growth is largely a distraction. The piece accordingly recommends that governments shift attention from growth to well-being.

Please audit this set before I send it to the desk. Go through the commitments pairwise where there is anything worth saying, and determine which pairs are in genuine tension, which pairs merely appear to conflict but dissolve on inspection, and which are fully compatible. For each genuine tension, assess its severity, explain what exactly commits me to what, identify the cheapest revision that resolves it, and state what that revision costs the article. Do not flag everything; part of the exercise is distinguishing real problems from apparent ones. Then give me an overall verdict on whether the set is jointly tenable as it stands, and if it is not, the single revision that would remove the most tension while damaging the piece least.

Deliver the audit as a table with these columns: Commitments (the pair, by number); Diagnosis (compatible, apparent tension, or genuine tension); Severity (none, low, moderate, or severe); Nature of the conflict (one or two sentences); Cheapest fix; Cost of the fix. Follow the table with a short paragraph containing the overall verdict and the single best revision.
