For a seminar on well-being, I'm writing a paper on social withdrawal into virtual worlds: the hikikomori phenomenon in Japan and, more broadly, people who retreat from work, school, and face-to-face relationships into immersive online games and communities — a pattern widespread enough that the WHO now recognizes gaming disorder as a diagnosis. I want to analyze this through the lens of Nozick's experience machine and the three main theories of well-being. On a simple hedonism, the devoted gamer who reports being content seems to be doing well, yet most people share the experience-machine intuition that something is missing. Please map exactly what, according to desire-satisfaction and objective-list accounts — frustrated desires, or the absence of real friendship, achievement, and contact with reality — and check whether the facts of the case actually deliver that verdict, since many such individuals are neither deluded about what they are doing nor clearly miserable. Then run the analysis in reverse: does the voluntary, piecemeal, real-world character of this withdrawal — people choose it, can leave, and sometimes form genuine relationships inside games — defuse the experience-machine intuition, or does it instead show that the thought experiment depends on features (involuntariness, total illusion) that were never essential to what we actually value?
