I'm a software engineer by trade, and over the past year I've been working through the literature on the logical paradoxes in my spare time, initially because of the striking resemblance between Curry's paradox and the fixed-point combinators I know from functional programming, and then out of sheer fascination. I've now read a fair amount about both Curry's paradox (the sentence that asserts, of an arbitrary claim, that its own truth implies that claim, yielding any conclusion you like via conditional proof and modus ponens) and Yablo's paradox (the infinite sequence in which each sentence says only that every sentence after it is untrue, allegedly without any sentence referring to itself). What I cannot get past is that the community seems permanently stuck on two questions that look, to an outsider, as though they should be decidable.

The first is whether Yablo's construction really is free of self-reference. One side insists that no sentence in the sequence refers to itself, so circularity cannot be essential to the liar family; the other side replies that the sequence smuggles in a kind of global circularity, either through the uniform predicate doing the same work for every sentence or through some fixed-point structure lurking underneath, and that 'local' non-self-reference is a red herring. The second question is what Curry's paradox forces us to give up. One camp says the naive truth principles are the culprit and must be restricted; the other says any logic that validates the derivation is the culprit, and that we should instead revise the structural rules of reasoning itself. Both debates have been running for decades, every move and counter-move appears to be on the table already, and as far as I can tell nobody has ever been argued out of their position. My working hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis, is that these are really disputes about what a solution to the paradoxes is supposed to preserve, dressed up as disputes about the paradoxes themselves.

I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Are these disagreements verbal, substantive, or a mixture? Are the parties arguing about the meaning of 'self-reference' and 'circularity', or about which cases count as data, or about desiderata for a satisfactory theory that neither side can justify non-question-beggingly? Does the way each side actually deploys its arguments support my hypothesis that the real fault line is over what may be sacrificed? And given your diagnosis, what would progress even look like here: a formal result, an agreed criterion for when a paradox has been 'solved' rather than relocated, a taxonomy that dissolves the disagreement, or is the impasse a sign that the questions themselves are ill-posed? Please write your answer in British English.
