I run the writers' room on a mid-budget sitcom, and our punch-up passes keep stalling out the same way: someone reads a joke that died in the table read, and the room just offers ten random alt lines until something sounds funnier, with no diagnosis of why the original failed. I've been reading up a bit on the philosophy of humour -- superiority, incongruity, relief, that benign violation idea -- and I want to turn that stuff into an actual diagnostic procedure my writers can run on a dead joke before we start spitballing replacements. So: a step-by-step flowchart-in-words where each step asks a question about the joke (who's the target and does the audience feel above them or attacked, is there a setup that builds an expectation the punchline actually violates, is the violation one the audience experiences as safe given who and where they are, is there suppressed tension the joke is releasing or is it just noise), where each question maps to whichever theory it's drawing from, and where the answers point to a specific class of fix rather than generic "make it punchier" advice.

A few requirements. First, the steps have to be in a sensible order with a rule for when two theories diagnose the same failure differently -- e.g., a joke can be both too predictable (incongruity failure) and too mean (superiority/benign-violation failure), and I need to know which to fix first and whether fixing one breaks the other. Second, include the cases where the procedure says the joke is fine and the problem is elsewhere -- delivery, placement in the scene, the audience not having the information the joke assumes -- because I suspect a third of our dead jokes are actually setup problems upstream in the script. Third, run the procedure on three worked examples I made up: (1) a joke where the boss character mocks an intern's regional accent and the test audience went quiet, (2) a running gag that tested great in episode one and has gotten steadily less funny each episode even though nothing about it changed, and (3) a joke about a recent real-world tragedy that half the room thinks is cathartic and half thinks is radioactive. And be straight with me about where the procedure runs out -- the spots where it's just judgment dressed up as method, and what the theories genuinely can't settle. Assume my writers have zero philosophy background and about ninety seconds per joke in a real punch-up session.
