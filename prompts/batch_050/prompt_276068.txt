Two senior engineers at my company got into a surprisingly heated argument last week and I genuinely can't tell who's right, so I'm outsourcing it.

Some background: our payments service has a rare race condition in the code that, on its own, would sometimes charge a customer twice. But there's a separate deduplication layer downstream (run by a different team) that catches every duplicate, so in three years and billions of requests, no customer has ever actually been double-charged.

Ana's position: the payments service is unreliable, full stop. The bug is sitting in the code right now. If the dedup layer ever goes down, gets misconfigured, or someone bypasses it for a new integration, customers get charged twice. A system that only works because something else is constantly saving it isn't reliable, it's lucky.

Ben's reply: "unreliable" means it fails, and it has literally never failed. Safeguards are part of how systems work. By her logic no bridge is reliable either, since they'd all collapse "if you removed the steel."

Ana came back: the steel is the bridge. The dedup layer is a separate service, owned by another team, toggleable by a config change. Her analogy: a patient whose symptoms are fully suppressed by daily medication isn't healthy, he's managed. You wouldn't let him tell his life insurer he's symptom-free.

Ben: but then reliability is just a choice of where you draw the system boundary. Ship "payments service + dedup" as one unit and it's reliable. She's free to draw the boundary at the buggy code, but she can't say his boundary is wrong, just different.

Ana said the boundary matters because the two layers can fail independently, and that's exactly what makes the bug still there in a way the bridge's steel isn't "still about to be removed." Ben said that's just a claim about probability of failure, which is fine to worry about, but doesn't justify calling something unreliable that never fails.

They both asked me to weigh in and I stalled. So: go through it move by move. Who's right about what? Is there something they're both missing -- like is "the service is unreliable" even a well-formed claim here, or are they arguing past each other about two different properties?
