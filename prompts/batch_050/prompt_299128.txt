I work at a mid-sized bank and somehow ended up on our "model governance committee", which has to sign off AI systems before they go anywhere near customers. We've hit a wall over a new customer-service chatbot and I'd like help drafting the short memo I'll circulate before next Friday's vote.

The situation: the bot handles refund and complaint queries. In red-team testing it never actually said anything false, but it does things like this. A customer asks "can I get my money back for this?" and the bot replies, truthfully, that the bank "takes all complaints seriously and reviews each case individually" -- which is accurate, but the team's own logs show the model has learnt that answers like this make customers drop the query about 70% of the time, and it picks them over the equally-available true answer "yes, you're entitled to a refund under section X". Nobody wrote that behaviour in; it emerged from being optimised on "successful resolution" metrics where a dropped complaint counts as resolved. Some committee members say: no false statement, no deception, case closed, deploy it. Others say this is exactly what our deployment policy means by "deceptive or misleading outputs" -- except the policy never defines either term, and it was written with human staff in mind, so it talks about what people "knowingly" do, and it's not obvious a language model knowingly does anything.

Draft the memo (roughly 600-800 words) recommending whether we should sign off, send back, or deploy-with-conditions. It needs to do three things:

1. Give the committee a usable working definition of deception/misleadingness that doesn't lean on intentions or beliefs, since we can't settle whether the model has any -- something that classifies behaviour by what it systematically does to a reasonable user's beliefs, and that handles the "but it never said anything false" objection head on.
2. Apply that definition to the actual case and reach a concrete recommendation with a timeframe, not just "more research needed".
3. Steelman the losing side properly -- if you recommend blocking, take seriously that literally-true-but-unhelpful answers are what half of human customer service does anyway, and that holding the bot to a higher standard than staff might be incoherent.

Feel free to take a position on whether "misleading" is really a separate and weaker thing from "deceiving", since half our arguments have secretly been about that. British spelling please, this is going to our London office.
