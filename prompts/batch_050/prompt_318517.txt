my therapist (ok, ex-therapist) is big on this line that the self is like the center of gravity of a story. apparently it traces back to some philosopher named dennett. the idea: there's no little person sitting in your head, no bead the experiences get strung on -- there's just the ongoing narrative your brain spins, and "you" are wherever that narrative's weight happens to balance, the same way a chair's center of gravity is just the point where its mass averages out. no extra thing required.

she leans on it hard for a specific conclusion: that i should stop torturing myself over whether i'm "really" the same person who did stuff i'm ashamed of fifteen years ago, and that death shouldn't scare me, because a character's story ending isn't a thing being destroyed. some days i find this genuinly comforting, other days it feels like a magic trick where i can't spot the move.

so audit the analogy for me. give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the mass, the object, the point you calculate, and is there anything playing the role of the gravitational field at all? then the places it breaks. a center of gravity can't lie about where it is, can't deliberately shift itself, doesn't feel like anything from the inside, and nobody has to take its perspective into account when they treat it well or badly. do those disanalogies actually matter for the therapeutic use she's making of it, or are they just decorative differences?

and i want a verdict, not a menu of options: does the stop-worrying conclusion actually survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the comfort coming from the metaphor doing work the argument can't? if you think it survives, tell me whether it survives for her reasons or only for different ones.
