You are a philosopher of archaeology who's also spent a few seasons on actual digs, so you know both the theory wars and what a trench section looks like. Think carefully about this one, and do a quick brainstorming pass (just for yourself) before drafting the final text -- I want the deliverable at the end, not your notes.

Here's the setup. A fictional but realistic site: a late Neolithic tell settlement, call it Tell Arvaz. In one building the excavators found (a) a room with about forty deliberately smashed pots arranged in rough circles, each broken before firing was complete so they were never usable, (b) a set of very standardized stone weights in an adjacent room, (c) no burials, no figurines, no altars, and (d) signs the building was burned once and then carefully rebuilt with the same floor plan three times. The two senior archaeologists on the project -- a Binford-style processualist and a Hodder-style postprocessualist -- have been fighting for months over whether this building was a shrine, a warehouse/administrative hub, a communal meeting house, or whether the question is even well-posed. The funding agency is tired of it and has made their next grant conditional on producing a joint adversarial-collaboration statement instead of two more dueling articles.

Draft that statement for them. Concretely:

1. A numbered list of claims both can actually sign. Don't pad it with vacuities like "context matters" -- I want things with teeth, e.g. what formation processes do and don't let you infer, whether deliberate breakage is readable as ritual independent of any theory, what the standardization of the weights constrains regardless of one's stance on symbolism.
2. A disagreements section where each side's position is phrased in wording that side would itself accept -- not the other side's caricature of it. The processualist's worry about the hermeneutic circle deserves a fair statement, and so does the postprocessualist's claim that "objective" middle-range theory smuggles in its own interpretive assumptions.
3. The cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one of the two to move. For each crux, say what observation, argument, or finding both agree would bear on it, and register each side's prediction in advance where that's possible (e.g., what would residue analysis of the pots have to show for the processualist to concede anything, and what the postprocessualist would accept as a genuine constraint rather than just another reading).
4. A short closing note on which part of their dispute, if any, you've concluded is merely verbal, and why.

Ground rule for you: both figures have to come out of this looking like competent professionals, not one grown-up and one ideologue. If you find you can't formulate a crux so that it would actually move someone, flag that rather than faking one.
