I retired from a career in condensed matter physics about two years ago and have been falling down the philosophy rabbit hole ever since, the way you do. Our little reading group (three retired scientists, one very patient former lawyer) just finished Anarchy, State, and Utopia and I've been asked to write up where I stand on Nozick before the others get to pick me apart. I like his style, honestly, it reminds me of how we build theories from axioms, though I suspect the axioms here are shakier than the ones I'm used to. Anyway, here's roughly what I believe, and I'd like an audit before I embarrass myself:

(1) Whether a distribution of holdings is just is purely a matter of history: it depends on how the holdings arose, not on whether the resulting pattern matches equality, need, merit, or any other template. (2) The Wilt Chamberlain case settles this for me: if everyone voluntarily pays a quarter to watch Wilt play and he ends up rich, the outcome is just, full stop, and stopping it would require constant meddling in people's voluntary choices. (3) Taxing people's earnings from voluntary work in order to redistribute is morally on a par with forced labor — the state is claiming partial ownership of your labor. (4) The minimal night-watchman state is legitimate, and it's fine for it to tax people to fund courts, police, and national defense. (5) Initial acquisition works the way Locke said: you can come to own previously unowned stuff by working on it, provided you leave enough and as good for others. (6) I'm aware that most actual property titles, especially land, are historically tainted — conquest, enclosures, slavery money somewhere up the chain — but since we can't unravel centuries of transfers, the distribution we have now is basically just and the state should protect it rather than try to reengineer it. (7) And one more, maybe softer commitment: I find it genuinely troubling when someone's formal freedom isn't worth much because they have no resources or options at all. A right you can't use strikes me as a thin kind of right.

So: does this set hang together? Please don't just tell me libertarians get criticized a lot — I know that, I want to know where my own specific commitments pull against each other. Go through them, flag which pairs or clusters are in real tension and which only look like they are but dissolve once you're careful, and rank the genuine ones by severity. For each real tension, tell me what exactly would have to give and what that revision costs me. And at the end, if you had to pick one single change that would clean up the position the most while keeping its spirit, tell me which one. I'd rather find the weak joint myself than have the lawyer find it first.
