I'm writing a syllabus-independent exam on the problem of divine hiddenness, and before I can set the question I need the marking rubric it will presuppose. Write the desiderata that any adequate philosophical treatment of divine hiddenness must meet: what such a treatment has to explain, which distinctions it has to respect, and which commitments it has to stay consistent with -- with the case or consideration motivating each desideratum, and a clear division between requirements you take to be non-negotiable for any account and requirements that are really the signature preferences of particular camps (theists mounting defenses, proponents of the argument from nonresistant nonbelief, fideists, universalists).

Please make sure the list addresses, at minimum, the following:

- The treatment must take on the strongest version of the argument from nonresistant nonbelief -- the inference from the existence of people who don't believe in God, through no fault of their own, to the nonexistence of a perfectly loving God -- rather than the weaker versions aimed at culpable doubt. Say what makes the strongest version strong and what a response must do to count as engaging it rather than dodging it.
- It must handle the distribution of nonbelief, not just its existence: the fact that nonbelief is concentrated in certain eras, regions, and demographics, and that for much of human history theistic belief in the relevant sense was unavailable to whole populations. Explain why a defense that works for an isolated nonbeliever in a devout society may fail as an account of the pattern.
- It must account for hiddenness within faith, not only outside it -- the experienced absence of God reported by sincere believers, including the documented spiritual desolation of saints and mystics -- since a theory of why God hides from outsiders that makes insider hiddenness unintelligible is at best half a theory. Say whether you think insider and outsider hiddenness admit one explanation or require two, and what hangs on the answer.
- It must distinguish the goods that are commonly offered to justify divine hiddenness -- free and uncoerced love, soul-making, the preservation of moral seriousness, the possibility of a genuine search -- and state, for each, the empirical or conceptual assumptions the defense needs, so that the desiderata list doesn't smuggle in a verdict by treating one camp's favored good as a datum.
- It must respect the constraints coming from elsewhere in theism: what the account implies about religious experience (if God is hidden, what are experiences of God?), about petitionary prayer, and about the afterlife (does postmortem unhiddenness retrospectively fix the problem or make the earthly hiddenness harder to justify?).

The part I care most about: identify any subsets of your desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied -- for instance, where treating divine love as essentially personal and relational pushes toward expecting constant divine presence, while treating divine love as respecting autonomy pushes the other way -- and say which requirement you would sacrifice first and why. If the list itself, once made precise, turns out to rule out every actual position, that is a finding I want stated explicitly rather than smoothed over.

Close with a brief scoring of the main families of response against your list -- free-will and epistemic-distance defenses, soul-making theodicies, skeptical-theist appeals to unknown goods, the move to inculpable ignorance or to eschatological correction, and the atheist conclusion itself -- but keep the scoring to a few sentences each. The desiderata and their tensions are the deliverable; the scorecard is just to show the list has teeth. No citations or reading list needed; I'll supply references myself.
