I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been reading John Locke's Second Treatise of Government on my own this winter, and I keep returning to one passage in Chapter V, "Of Property" (§28):

"Thus the grass my horse has bit; the turfs my servant has cut; and the ore I have digged in any place, where I have a right to them in common with others, become my property, without the assignation or consent of any body. The labour that was mine, removing them out of that common state they were in, hath fixed my property in them."

This seems to me to expose a crack running through the whole chapter, and I would like to know whether I am right about that. Locke's central claim earlier in the chapter is that a thing becomes yours because you mixed your labour with it. But the labour in this passage was not his: it was the servant who cut the turfs. Locke simply declares the labour his own — "the labour that was mine" — and collects the property. As far as I can tell, then, the theory works only because a prior arrangement (the servant's employment) has already settled whose labour counts, which means labour is not generating the title at all; the wage agreement is doing the real work, and the labour-mixing story is decoration laid over an arrangement it was supposed to justify. The alternative seems no better: if labour genuinely creates the title, Locke is committed to the servant owning the turfs — and, by parallel reasoning, to employees owning what they produce and tenants owning the fields they improve, which is not a conclusion anyone attributes to him. So which horn of this dilemma does Locke take? And if the answer is that the servant sold his labour, I want to understand how labour can be the foundation of all property while also being something you can sign away in advance — that still looks like consent, not labour, bearing the weight. I suspect my reasoning is wrong somewhere, but I would like the mistake located precisely, in relation to this passage, rather than being assured that the chapter is subtler than I have made it out to be.
