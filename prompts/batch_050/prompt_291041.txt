I spent forty years as an experimental physicist and have been reading philosophy in retirement, and I've gotten properly stuck on the reference class problem. Every probability that actually matters to me -- my odds of surviving an operation, whether some drug works for someone like me, the chance of a serious earthquake here in the next decade -- seems to depend entirely on which group you count me as a member of, and there are always endlessly many groups on offer, all overlapping. Before I wade into any more proposed solutions (I've seen gestures at narrowest-class, most-informed-class, relevance-weighting, and some formal criteria), I want the spec first. Write the desiderata: what must any adequate solution to the reference class problem actually deliver? For each requirement, give the case or situation that motivates it -- for instance, whatever consideration kills the naive idea of just using the narrowest class you have statistics for deserves a prominent place, since that was my first instinct from lab work and it apparently collapses into a class of one. Distinguish the genuinely non-negotiable requirements from the mere preferences of particular camps, like the frequentists who want objective probabilities versus the subjective Bayesians who seem to think the problem dissolves once you give up on those. And the part I care about most: if some subset of the desiderata looks jointly unsatisfiable -- say, wanting probabilties that are both objective and genuinely informative about single cases -- say so plainly and explain the tension, because then the list itself is a result. A brief closing note on how the main existing approaches fare against the list is fine, but the list is the deliverable.
