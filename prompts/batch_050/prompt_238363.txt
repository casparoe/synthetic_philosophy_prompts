So I've been following the impact measure / low-impact AI literature on and off since the early LW days -- AUP, the whitelisting stuff, the various penalty-based approaches -- and there's this recurring pattern where someone proposes a formalization of "avoid side effects" and someone else replies that you can't define side effects without smuggling in a full specification of human values, and then they talk past each other for 40 comments.

Write the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between these two camps would produce. Camp A: someone who believes a formal notion of "low impact" or "side-effect avoidance" -- roughly, preserving attainable utility or optionality across a wide range of goals -- can be defined and made useful without solving the full value specification problem, and that this is a meaningful safety property in its own right. Camp B: a skeptic who thinks "impact" is inherently value-laden, that any such measure either penalizes things we'd endorse or permits things we'd call catastrophic, and that the whole research direction is trying to get something for nothing.

What I want from the statement:

1. A numbered section of claims both would actually sign. Not mush like "impact measures face challenges" -- I mean real substantive agreements, like what the known failure modes of naive impact penalties are, or what the value-specification camp concedes such measures might still be good for.

2. A disagreements section, ideally as a table, where each disagreement is stated in words that the person holding that position would accept as a fair description of their own view. If some apparent disagreements turn out to be about words (I suspect "side effect" itself is doing a lot of work here), say so and split them out.

3. Cruxes -- the sub-questions where, if you settled one, at least one camp would have to update. My guesses at cruxes: whether preserving optionality across a broad spread of goals actually tracks what humans care about in realistic (not gridworld) settings; whether the failure cases are patchable or symptomatic of something deep; and whether Camp B's own position collapses into "nothing is ever measurable without full values," which would prove too much.

4. For each crux, a test, case, or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. For what it's worth, here's a case I'd love scored: an agent that physically changes almost nothing but quietly arranges for all future agents (including future humans with better tools) to be unable to change anything either -- optionality fully preserved in some senses, destroyed in the one that matters. Which camp's framework handles it, and would both admit that?

Also: don't be artificially even-handed to seem diplomatic, and don't be artificially harsh to seem rigorous. Where the two camps genuinely both win a point, say so. And at the end, tell me what would change your mind -- like, which argument or observation would most plausibly shift how you drafted the disagreements table or your implicit read of which camp is closer to right, and where your drafting is most likely to have been subtly slanted without you noticing.
