I teach a philosophy of mind seminar for advanced undergraduates -- about 18 students, one 80-minute session. Design an in-class activity that makes participants feel the force of the symbol grounding problem from the inside: the difference between manipulating symbols according to rules that relate them only to other symbols, and operating symbols at least some of which are tethered to things in the world. The point I want them to arrive at themselves, before anyone names it, is that a system of purely internal definitions is a merry-go-round -- you can be flawlessly proficient at it and still not know what any of it is about -- and that a small number of grounded symbols can, in the right structural position, change everything.

The activity should involve no lecture up front. I want the students to experience ungrounded competence first and grounding second, ideally as a competition so the stakes are real. Here is the rough shape I have in mind, which you should refine, correct, or replace as you see fit: teams receive a rulebook defining a set of nonsense glyphs purely in terms of one another, then must answer questions whose correctness depends on what the glyphs "refer to" -- except that nothing fixes any reference, so confident answers and random guesses are indistinguishable from the inside. Then some teams receive a limited "grounding budget": permission to tether a small number of glyphs to actual objects or experiences in the room, forcing them to think about which glyphs it is most valuable to ground and why. I'd like the activity to make vivid that grounding is not all-or-nothing -- that where you spend a scarce grounding budget matters -- without tipping the lesson in advance.

Please specify: the materials and how to construct them (including how to prevent students from smuggling in meaning by inventing mnemonic nicknames for the glyphs); the rules and scoring; the question sets for both phases; the timeline; and a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the parts of the idea -- rule-following without understanding, the circularity of dictionary-upon-dictionary definition, and the asymmetry between a system with zero grounded symbols and one with a few. Include an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the symbol grounding problem the activity structurally cannot reproduce -- for instance, that my students already possess a fully grounded language and can never actually occupy the position of the ungrounded system -- and what I as facilitator must supply by hand to compensate.
