Consider the absent-minded driver problem. A driver must travel along a road with two consecutive exits. Taking the first exit yields a payoff of 0; passing the first and taking the second yields 4; passing both yields 1. The catch: at any intersection she reaches, she cannot remember whether she has already passed an exit, so the two intersections are indistinguishable to her, and she must follow a single probabilistic rule -- "continue with probability p" -- applied identically at whichever intersection she finds herself at. Planning before departure, she computes that p = 2/3 maximizes her expected payoff, at 4/3. But a standard line of reasoning holds that once she is sitting at an intersection, she should believe she is at the first intersection with probability 3/5 (given her own rule), and on that belief exiting looks better than her plan recommends. So the planning perspective and the acting perspective diverge, and a literature has grown up around which is authoritative.

A common move in that literature is to dissolve the tension with an analogy: the driver, it is said, is really a two-person team -- one teammate stationed at the first intersection, one at the second, sharing a single payoff but unable to communicate -- and the "paradox" is nothing more than the observation that teammates who cannot coordinate will each do what is locally best, which need not match what a coordinator would have imposed. On this reading, the acting perspective wins by definition, because there never was a single persisting agent to be bound by the plan in the first place.

Audit this analogy. Give the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the teammates, the shared payoff, the impossibility of communication, the equilibrium, and the planning stage. Then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular: does modeling the two decision points as two agents quietly build in the conclusion that local optimization is legitimate, so that the analogy presupposes what it purports to show? What happens to the driver's belief about her location -- a belief about herself that depends on her own rule -- when the locations are occupied by genuinely distinct agents? And if the rival analogy (a principal programming a forgetful robot that will act twice on her behalf) is exactly as natural, does the team analogy carry any argumentative weight at all, or does the choice of analogy simply decide the verdict in advance? If a better comparison exists, say what it is and what it would license.
