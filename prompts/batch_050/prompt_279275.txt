Ive got a cluster of gut verdicts about imposing risk on people who didnt consent, and I want to know what principle, if any, is underneath them. Take Mill's On Liberty as a rough foil: in effect, once conduct creates a definite enough chance of damage to others, it stops being purely self-regarding and becomes a fair target for moral blame or law. Dont worry about exegesis; use that only as a springboard, no bibliography needed.

Cases and verdicts:

1. My neighbor stores a small, legal amount of fireworks in a shed by our shared fence in a dry summer. Almost certainly fine, and if theres an accident he'll pay. Still wrong to do without asking me, even if nothing burns.
2. Same neighbor, same fireworks, but he asks and I say sure after he promises to be careful. Fine, even though the objective risk is unchanged.
3. A hospital discharges a patient one hour earlier than ideal, creating a tiny added risk to future immunocompromised patients, because the bed is needed for someone bleeding out now. Permissible, maybe even required, though nobody consented on behalf of the later patients.
4. A startup releases a very capable open model with a tiny chance of materially helping a lone actor make a bioweapon, because the expected medical and scientific benefits are enormous. Not permissible without serious safeguards; aggregate benefit plus small probability doesnt launder it.
5. A pilot with an engine failure steers away from a stadium toward empty fields, unavoidably increasing risk to three houses. Permissible if the fields genuinely minimize expected deaths, blameworthy if she picks the houses because their owners are richer and can sue.
6. A city mandates a vaccine with a minuscule risk of myocarditis for some young people, mainly to reduce transmission to others. I keep flip-flopping: permissible if the risk is transparent and collectively authorized, creepy if it is just some people being drafted as shields.
7. A climber crosses above a popular ledge despite loose rock, but only when the ledge looks empty. Wrong even if nobody is there, because the permission of possible victims isnt secured by their absence.
8. A driver goes 5 mph over the limit on an empty road at 3am. Mostly fine; 15 mph over past a school at pickup time is obviously not, even if the increment in expected harm is smaller in absolute terms than case 4.

The hard pair for me is 3 versus 4: in both, unconsenting strangers bear a small grave risk for large benefits to others, but I want opposite answers. And 1 versus 8 also bug me: private property consent seems to matter a lot in one, while background norms seem to do the work in the other.

Give me one principle, or two competing principles if one clean one wont work, stated tightly enough that I can see which clause is doing which job. For each clause say which case forces it. Then test it on cases I didnt list: geoengineering that slightly raises regional flood risk, anonymous genetic data donation that later enables re-identification, and mandatory airline crash-position rules that inconvenience all to save a few. Be honest about which of my verdicts your best principle has to overturn, and which verdict youd surrender first if the whole set cant be saved.
