ok so I need you to referee an argument between two of my friends that went in circles last week. It was about immigration and the welfare state, and I kept flip-flopping on who I agreed with, which annoyed everyone.

Quick background: Lena is basically an open borders person, Andrei thinks open borders plus a modern welfare state is a contradiction. Here's roughly how it went.

Lena started: rich countries keeping poor people out is one of the biggest injustices going, and the economic case that almost everyone gains from free movement is about as strong as economics gets. So the presumption should be open borders, and the burden of proof is on the restrictionists.

Andrei: fine in a world without a welfare state, but we don't live in that world. A country that guarantees healthcare, schooling, housing support and a pension floor can't also let anyone show up, because some migrants will cost more than they ever pay in, and once voters see that happening they'll either slash the welfare state or slam the door. So the real choice is welfare state or open borders, and he'd rather keep the welfare state for the people already inside.

Lena: that's a false choice, because you can fix the mismatch directly instead of blocking people. Let them in but make them ineligible for cash transfers for, say, ten years, charge an entry surcharge or make a sponsor post a bond that covers any welfare they use in that window, refunded with interest if unused. She called these "keyhole solutions" -- you target the actual fiscal problem, not the humans.

Andrei: three problems with that. One, you can't exclude kids from school or people from emergency rooms, so the bond doesn't cover the real costs anyway. Two, you'd get a permanent two-tier society, a big class of residents with fewer rights, which is exactly the thing that's bred resentment and bad integration outcomes in Europe. Three, the scheme is politically unstable: in ten years voters will feel sorry for the bonded immigrants and expand their benefits, or they'll feel threatened and use the scheme's failure as a reason to close the border. So the clever fix collapses back into his original dilemma.

Lena: on schools and ERs, immigrant kids grow up into taxpayers and most migrants are young and healthy, so over a lifetime the fiscal math isn't obviously negative -- Andrei is treating a short-term cost as if it's permanent. On the two-tier thing: a person who chooses to come under those terms is made better off, and it's paternalistic to refuse them the deal on the grounds that it's unfair *relative to citizens*, when the actual alternative on offer is staying home in poverty. And the political instability argument cuts against Andrei too, because closed borders are also politically costly to maintain and plenty of restrictionist regimes drift toward expansion anyway. So "your policy won't survive voters" isn't a real argument, it's a prediction, and a lazy one.

Andrei's last move: even if the bond scheme is voluntary and welfare-improving for the migrant, it screens for the moderately well-off -- people who can front a surcharge or find a sponsor -- and filters out the most desperate, who are precisely the ones open borders is supposed to help most. So Lena's scheme is open borders for the almost-rich, which betrays her own starting principle.

At that point Lena said filtering for ability to pay a bond is still infinitely better than filtering by birthplace, which is what we do now, and Andrei said better than a monstrous status quo doesn't mean good, and they called it a draw and asked me. I have no idea.

Go through the whole exchange and tell me who's right at each step -- the fiscal point, the keyhole-solution point, the two-tier society worry, the political stability claim, the paternalism charge, and the screening-for-wealth objection. Where each side is smuggling in an empirical assumption they haven't defended, flag it. And say who you think is more right overall and what the strongest version of the other side would still have going for it.
