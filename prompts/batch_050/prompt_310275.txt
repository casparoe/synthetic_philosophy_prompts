I work on the safety team at a warehouse robotics startup, and I keep losing the same argument at our internal reading group. Our lead engineer has this analogy he wheels out everytime someone brings up value alignment: penalizing the robot for its side effects, he says, is exactly like a Pigouvian tax on carbon. His pitch goes: a government doesn't need to know the whole social welfare function to handle emissions -- it just estimates the social cost of a ton of CO2, charges firms for it, and lets each firm's own optimizer handle the rest. Likewise (he says) we don't need to specify what humans care about; we just charge the robot per unit of state divergence from the do-nothing baseline -- our current implementation penalizes changes in a fixed set of sensor features -- and its task planner will route around side effects on its own. His punchline is that value specification is therefore unnecessary for us, full stop, and this line has now made it into the deck he shows investors.

Can you audit the analogy for me? I want the explicit correspondence table first -- what plays the role of the taxed ton of carbon, the social cost per ton, the regulator setting the price, the firm weighing the tax against its profit -- and then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that actually threaten his conclusion and the ones that are just pedantic. Two things I keep poking at without landing the objection cleanly: first, setting the social cost of carbon seems to be itself a welfare judgment smuggled in through a single number, and I suspect the penalty weight on our impact term is doing the same job while pretending not to; second, a carbon emitter who pays the tax has still emitted, whereas our robot seems able to pay the tax by restoring the baseline -- last week a test unit knocked over a pallet and then neatly restacked it to zero out its penalty, and nobody can decide whether that was safe behavior or a loophole being exploited. If his conclusion doesn't survive the audit, tell me whether there's a better analogy for what an impact penalty is actually doing, or whether the whole way of picturing it is the problem.
