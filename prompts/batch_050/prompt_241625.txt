I am designing a seminar on collective minds, anchored in Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary -- the ant colony in Gödel, Escher, Bach that converses intelligibly at the colony level although no individual ant understands anything -- and I want to know what theory of mentality my own case-intuitions commit me to, if any. Here are my verdicts on a spread of systems. Your task is to find the principle behind them, or to tell me there isn't one and what to retract.

1. An ordinary human brain. Individual neurons are mindless; the whole thinks, feels, and is someone. Mind: yes.
2. Aunt Hillary herself: an ant colony in which signaling among ants realizes coherent, conversation-level states, while the ants are individually mindless. Mind: yes. If neurons can compose a mind, I see no in-principle reason ants cannot.
3. A honeybee swarm choosing a nest site, where scouts' dances compete and interact until the swarm converges on one option, in a process that looks remarkably like deliberation and meets some plausible criteria for rational choice. Mind: no, or at most I withhold judgment. It is decision-making without anyone home.
4. Block's Chinese-nation case: a billion people with radios, each following instructions so that the population as a whole realizes, for an hour, the exact functional organization of a human mind. Mind: no. Nothing is added by the radios; each participant merely obeys rules, and the idea that a subject flickers into existence for the hour strikes me as incredible.
5. A mature corporation: it gathers information through employees, revises policy, pursues long-horizon goals, holds what we naturally call beliefs, and seems to have something like a temperament. Mind: no -- though I concede its information-processing organization is far richer than the bee swarm's.
6. An octopus, whose nervous system is organized radically unlike ours, with most of its neurons in its arms. Mind: yes.
7. A slime mold that solves mazes and anticipates periodic stimuli. Mind: no.
8. A split-brain patient under everyday conditions, integrated and functioning. One mind, not two.

I can already see trouble: 2 and 4 appear structurally identical -- minds made of communicating simple agents -- yet I answer them oppositely, and 3 and 5 sit uneasily on both sides of the line I draw. What I want from you is reflective equilibrium done on demand. State one precise principle (or, if necessary, a small conjunction of conditions) over which systems have minds, with each clause motivated by the case that forces it. Then test it: what does it predict about the global internet taken over a decade, about an old-growth forest ecosystem, and about a termite colony twice the size and connectivity of an ant colony? Finally, and here I want you to be committed rather than even-handed: if the principle cannot save all eight verdicts, name the specific verdict or verdicts I should surrender, give your reasons, and defend the resulting position against the obvious objection that it is ad hoc. Do not present me a menu of theories of collective mentality and leave the choice to me -- I want a defended answer, including an honest statement of which of my intuitions your answer treats as noise.
