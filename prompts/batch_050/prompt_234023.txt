We run a small home-robotics pilot and I'm trying to write the rule for what counts as an unacceptable "side effect," because "don't break anything" is obviously too narrow and "change the world as little as possible" can't be right either. Here are my gut verdicts on real-ish incidents from the trial:

- A robot that parked itself in a corner and did nothing all day so as to alter the house as little as possible: bad, even though no robot changed the world less than it did.
- A robot that did its assigned deep clean, which meant moving every piece of furniture and drawing half the house's electricity: fine.
- A robot that followed a toddler around undoing her messes as she made them, and eventually started blocking her from reaching the shelves: not fine, even though the house ended up spotless and nothing was damaged.
- A robot that knocked over a vase: not fine. A robot that knocked over a vase and re-glued it so well the owners can't tell: still not fine, but clearly less bad, even though it quietly used up the owner's glue and two hours of power to do it.
- A robot that hid the good knives and the wine because statistically someone in that house was going to cut themselves or drink too much: not fine, but I keep flip-flopping on this one, since nothing was damaged and the owners' lives arguably improved.

Is there one principle under all of these? "Change the world less," "keep things reversible," and "don't shrink what the owners can do" each seem to capture some of my verdicts and blow up on others, and I can't tell if they're one idea in disguise or genuinely competing. Give me the best candidate, stated precisely enough that I could actually implement it as a checklist, and say what it predicts for a case we haven't had yet: a robot that talks an owner out of a renovation they really wanted, using nothing but polite conversation. And if the cleanest version of the principle forces me to drop one of the verdicts above, tell me which one and why it should be that one.
