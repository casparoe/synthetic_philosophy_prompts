I'm in a reading group on logical uncertainty and formal models of agents, and this week we reached the result that's been haunting me all semester: Löb's theorem, and with it the so-called Löbian obstacle to an agent fully trusting itself. As I understand it, the situation is this. Any sufficiently expressive formal system that manages to prove, for some particular claim, "if I can prove this, then it's true," could already have proved the claim outright — the trust buys nothing. And if you instead hand the system that trust as an axiom schema covering every claim at once, applied to its own provability, the whole thing detonates: it ends up proving every sentence whatsoever. The one instance of self-trust a consistent system can never afford is the instance that amounts to saying it never proves a contradiction.

I'd like a villanelle — nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain — spoken in the voice of a theorem prover (or an artificial agent built on one) as it tries to write down its own trust in itself. The two refrains should be the two horns of the dilemma: one line stating the trust rule (whatever I prove is true), the other stating what the theorem does to that rule (proving the rule is the same as proving the claim). Across the tercets, the speaker should keep reasserting its trust and keep discovering the trust has already been spent — that vouching for itself was never an additional step it could take. The closing quatrain, where the villanelle finally sets the two refrains side by side, should enact the collapse: with rule and theorem adjacent at last, everything follows, and the speaker ends up having proved the one thing it cannot afford to prove.

A specialist should be able to tell which refrain is which and why their final meeting is fatal; someone who has never heard of the theorem should still get a good poem about the vertigo of vouching for yourself. Don't name the theorem, or Löb, or Gödel in the poem itself — let the recursive, self-returning structure carry that weight. If it helps, the provability predicate can appear concretely as a stamp or seal the speaker presses onto its statements, with the poem's trouble being that the seal keeps showing up on its own certificate.
