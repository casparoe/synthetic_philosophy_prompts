So I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and for the next issue I'm building one of those interactive flowchart things: "Is it one organism or many?" Readers start with a weird biological case and answer yes/no questions until they land on a verdict. My editor wants the flowchart to be philosophically serious underneath, not just vibes, because half the fun is that biologists and philosophers genuinely disagree about where the leaves should be.

Here's what I need from you: the decision tree that powers it, built from the logical space rather than from a list of famous positions. That means: first figure out which questions actually separate the live options, then locate the real positions in that space. The audience is curious non-scientists, so every question has to be answerable by someone who's never heard the words "holobiont" or "superorganism" -- concrete stuff like "can the parts survive and reproduce on their own?" or "is there a single-cell stage each generation has to pass through?" or "does the collective have anything like an immune system that polices freeloaders?" No question should just be "do you think the colony feels like a unity?"

The tree has to handle these cases, and I'd like them worked in as the running examples: a human plus their gut microbiome, a Portuguese man o' war, a honeybee colony, a lichen, a coral head, an aspen clone like Pando, and one of those slime molds where free-living cells aggregate into a slug when food runs out. The slime mold is my favorite becuase whatever your tree says, somebody's going to be mad about it.

Format-wise, give me the response in these sections, in this order:

1. "The diagnostic questions" -- the 6 to 9 questions the tree rests on, each phrased for a lay reader, with one sentence on why it does real work (i.e., which positions it separates). ~250 words.
2. "The flowchart" -- the tree itself in indented text form, each branch labeled by the question that splits it. Compact.
3. "The leaves" -- each endpoint gets the verdict it delivers on our seven cases, the name of the position if it has one (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it), and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. ~300 words.
4. "The empty branches" -- combinations of answers nobody occupies, and for each a quick diagnosis: is it incoherent, just unattractive, or genuinely unexplored territory? ~150 words.
5. "Where the tree gives up" -- flag any positions your questions can't tell apart, rather than papering over it, and say what extra question would be needed. ~100 words.

Two ground rules. First, don't reverse-engineer: if the questions conveniently happen to land every famous view on its own leaf, I will suspect you built the tree backwards from the names. Second, at the end of "The flowchart," actually run the finished tree on two test inputs for me: a person whose gut microbes have been completely removed, and a termite that can't digest wood without the protists in its gut. If the tree gives a clean answer to the first but chokes on the second, that's fine -- that asymmetry is honestly the interesting part.
