I'm in a small reading group working through Book 6 of Polybius' Histories (we're using the old Shuckburgh translation, the free one online) and this passage about how democracy goes bad has us properly stuck:

"So when they begin to be fond of office, and find themselves unable to obtain it by their own unassisted efforts and their own merits, they ruin their estates, while enticing and corrupting the common people in every possible way. By which means when, in their senseless mania for reputation, they have made the populace ready and greedy to receive bribes, the virtue of democracy is destroyed, and it is transformed into a government of violence and the strong hand. For the mob, habituated to feed at the expense of others, and to have its hopes of a livelihood in the property of its neighbours, as soon as it has got a leader sufficiently ambitious and daring, being excluded by poverty from the sweets of civil honours, produces a reign of mere violence. Then come tumultuous assemblies, massacres, banishments, redivisions of land; until, after losing all trace of civilisation, it has once more found a master and a despot."

Three things are bugging me. First, the actual mechanism. The story he tells here is all about habituation -- the mob gets used to living off other people's property, the rich develop a taste for office and start buying it -- which makes degeneration sound like a moral/psychological failure, something that could in principle be headed off by the right civic education. But right after this paragraph he describes the whole sequence of constitutions as a regular natural cycle and even says Rome's eventual decay will be owed to natural causes, which makes it sound automatic, like ageing. So which is doing the work in this passage: character, or some structural law? And if it's structural, what exactly is the structure? He never quite says.

Second, a grammar question that our group split on: in the bit about the leader "being excluded by poverty from the sweets of civil honours," who is the one excluded by poverty -- the mob or the leader? It seems to matter a lot for who Polybius thinks the villain of this story is, the masses or the ambitious rich man.

Third, how does any of this fit with the mixed constitution stuff? Just a few pages later he praises Lycurgus for designing Sparta's system as if he'd foreseen the whole cycle and built against it, but then near the end of the book (around ch. 57) he calmly predicts that Rome, mixed constitution and all, will decay into mob-rule too. So does mixing the constitution break the cycle, pause it, or just slow it down? Half our group thinks Polybius has a consistent theory here and the other half thinks he's trying to have it both ways and nobody noticed.

Put your bottom line in the first sentence, then walk me through the reasoning -- skip the preamble and dont restate my question back at me.
