I am preparing a position statement for a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and before circulating it I would like a consistency audit. My commitments are as follows:

1. Probabilism, on pragmatic grounds. The synchronic Dutch book argument shows that an agent whose credences violate the probability axioms is thereby irrational. Vulnerability to a sure loss is not merely a practical misfortune befalling an unlucky bettor; it reveals a defect in the credal state itself.

2. Conditionalization is not established by parallel means. The diachronic Dutch book argument fails, because it can exploit only an agent who has announced in advance a rule for updating other than conditionalization. An agent who simply comes to have new credences, without having bound herself to any update policy, cannot be Dutch-booked, and so nothing in the argument shows her to be irrational.

3. Imprecise credences are sometimes rationally permissible. Where the evidence is sparse or equivocal, a rational agent's attitude toward a proposition may take the form of an interval, or a set of probability functions, rather than a point value, and she need not be prepared to name a single fair price for a bet on that proposition.

4. Rationality cannot require logical omniscience. A rational agent may assign less than maximal credence to a logical truth she has not recognized as such, and may fail to believe consequences of her beliefs that she has not derived.

Please audit this set rather than defend your own preferred package. For each pair of commitments in genuine tension, identify the tension precisely: show what the standard motivation or proof of one commitment presupposes that the other denies, rank the tensions by severity, and for each one state the cheapest revision that would remove it and what that revision costs. Where a pair merely appears to conflict, explain why the appearance is misleading. I have deliberately not indicated which commitments I would be willing to abandon; treat all four as equally negotiable.
