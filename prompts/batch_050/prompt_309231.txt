So my dad got diagnosed with Alzheimer's about eight months ago, and somewhere in the whole mess of it I've gotten weirdly fixated on time itself — what it even is. A friend lent me Augustine's Confessions and I ended up completely stuck on book 11, the time part. Meanwhile everyone around me keeps reaching for the same analogy: time is a river. It carries you along, you can't step in the same water twice, my therapist literally told me grief is like standing on the bank watching it all go past. And some days that feels exactly right.

But as I read Augustine, he kind of wrecks the river? His whole thing, if I'm getting it: the past doesn't exist anymore, the future doesn't exist yet, and if you squeeze the present down — this year, this hour, this second — there's no length left in it at all. So the only place time actually lives is in the mind, stretched in three directions at once: memory of what's gone, attention to what's here, expectation of what's coming (the latin is distentio somthing, I think). Watching my dad lose first the memory side and now the expectation side, that doesn't read like metaphysics to me anymore, it reads like a clinical description.

What I want is an audit of the river analogy up against book 11. Map it out explicitly — what's the water, what's the current, what are the banks, is the person floating in it or standing on the bank and does that even matter. Then tell me where the mapping breaks: does the river picture quietly assume the upstream and downstream stretches exist alongside where you're standing, which is the very thing Augustine denies? Does a flow rate need a second clock to flow against, and what would that clock be? Which of the disanalogies actually kill the consoling use people make of the river (grief passes, the current carries you) and which ones are harmless? And if you rebuild the analogy on Augustine's terms — time as somthing the mind does rather than somthing we're in — does anything of "time flows" survive, or should I be reaching for a different image entirely? If so, propose one.

If I've misread Augustine, or if this whole comparison is rigged from the start, say so plainly — I'd rather be corrected than humored. Also please answer in American English.
