I host a podcast on moral education, and our last episode left me in need of help. My guest was a philosopher of a situationist bent who argued that character traits are largely a fiction: behaviour, she claimed, tracks situations rather than dispositions, so talk of courageous or honest persons is confabulation after the fact. Next week's guest is a classicist-turned-moral-philosopher whose recent book defends almost the opposite view — that moral education is fundamentally biographical, that we become good by dwelling on well-told lives, and that Plutarch's Parallel Lives is the model: ethical theory, on his account, is at best a commentary on what the lives already teach us. I have been rereading Plutarch in preparation, and I keep noticing things I want to press him on. In the preface to the Life of Alexander, Plutarch announces that he writes lives rather than histories, and that small gestures and remarks can reveal character where battles and sieges cannot — yet he is also happy to smooth over his subjects' vices and shape his material to a moral, which makes the Lives look uncomfortably close to hagiography. And in the Demetrius preface he explains that he includes vicious lives so that readers learn what to shun, which suggests the moral work is done as much by the pairings and the closing comparisons as by any single life.

Draft nine interview questions for this guest. I do not want him simply to restate the view, so each question should turn on the actual content of his position and press where a thoughtful critic would press, and each should come with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. Please include at least one question on each of the following: the hagiography charge — if the biographer selects and polishes, are readers learning character or merely admiring a well-made statue; the circularity worry — choosing the right exemplars seems to require already knowing what virtue is, in which case lives cannot be foundational but only illustrative; the situationist challenge from my previous guest — what exactly is learned from a life if character does not travel across situations, and whether Plutarch's fondness for small revealing gestures helps or hurts him here; the function of the negative exemplars such as Demetrius and Antony — do bad lives teach by the same mechanism as good ones, or is avoidance a different skill from imitation; whether the moral teaching resides in the individual life or in Plutarch's comparative apparatus of pairing and final judgement, and what that implies for how we should read single, unpaired biographies today; and whether what lives impart is better understood as knowledge that, knowledge how, or a training of the emotions and the attention. I would also like at least one question that invites him to say where Plutarch himself went wrong, and one connecting his view to our present culture of curated self-presentation — if modern lives arrive pre-hagiographied, does his method still apply. Assume listeners have read a life or two but no scholarship; avoid anything generic about his career or what drew him to the classics.
