For my Theory of Knowledge class in the IB program, I have to give a ten-minute presentation defending a philosophical thesis of my own choosing, followed by a question period with my teacher and about twenty classmates. My thesis is drawn from Douglas Hofstadter's idea that a self is a pattern rather than a substance: on his view, the "I" is a self-referential loop of symbols and habits that a brain implements, and because patterns are substrate-independent, a coarse, low-resolution copy of that loop can exist inside other people's brains in the form of their memories, mannerisms, and internal models of the person. My claim is the strong one: when someone dies, the versions of them running in the minds of the people who knew them are not merely memories *of* the person but genuine, degraded continuations *of that very self*, so personal identity is distributed rather than located in one skull, and death is a matter of degree. I chose this because my grandmother died last year and I genuinely feel like I can "run" her reactions to things, so I want to defend the view seriously rather than as a comforting metaphor.

My audience: my teacher has a philosophy background and will press on rigor, not sentiment. My classmates have done one unit on personal identity (Locke, the ship of Theseus, teletransporter thought experiments) but nothing on philosophy of mind beyond that, so their objections will be intuitive rather than technical. One classmate is very into neuroscience and will likely object from that angle.

Please prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I should actually expect from this audience, ordered roughly by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do to my thesis if I handle them badly. Phrase each objection the way that specific audience member would phrase it (my teacher will be precise; my classmates will say things like "but that's just remembering her"). For each, give me a reply I can deliver in under a minute, note what I should concede versus what I should hold firm on, and flag where my reply is weakest. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me honestly whether my thesis, as stated, can survive my teacher's strongest line of attack or whether I should downgrade it to a weaker claim before the presentation.
