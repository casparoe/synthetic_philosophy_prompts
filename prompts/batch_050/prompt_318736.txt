Two of my friends got into a proper argument at the pub last week and it's still simmering, so I want a verdict. Context: Dan's doing a PhD that touches on decision theory, Sam's an NHS radiologist.

It kicked off when Sam mentioned she'd declined to run an extra scan that a patient's daughter had pushed for. Dan said that was indefensible: there's a result in decision theory (Good's theorem, he called it) showing that free information before a decision can never hurt you in expectation. Worst case you learn something that doesn't change your choice, so you're exactly where you started; sometimes it changes your choice for the better. So on average you can only win. Sam said that's exactly the kind of thing that sounds clever until you spend a week in a hospital. Extra scans throw up incidental findings constantly, and those kick off cascades -- biopsies, anxiety, more scans -- for things that would never have harmed anyone. Dan said that's not information doing damage, that's people reacting badly to information; the theorem assumes a Bayesian updater and she's describing humans. Sam said fine, but then the theorem tells you nothing about actual decisions, and the slogan 'more information is always worth having' should be retired from medicine entirely.

Dan then dug in: the result is still the right default for a rational agent, and if hospitals systematically acquire less info than it recommends they're leaving value on the table. Sam came back with what I thought was her best move: she said she'd rather not know her own Huntington's status before sorting out life insurance, and she'd rather certain trial results stayed unpublished until after some risk-sharing arrangement was in place, so information can be genuinely value-destroying even for cool-headed reasoners. Dan said that's cheating because the info isn't staying private -- the theorem's about a lone agent choosing whether to peek, not about what happens once something's on your medical record. Sam said that's the whole point: in the real world information is never a purely private peek, it leaks into records and other people's models of you, so the lone-agent case is the special case, not the default.

Neither budged. Who's actually right about what here? I want a point-by-point ruling: is Dan right about the theorem and its assumptions, is Sam right that the real-world slogan fails, and is there something both of them are missing (e.g. what 'free' information has to mean for the argument to go through)? Definately tell me if one side is conflating distinct claims.
