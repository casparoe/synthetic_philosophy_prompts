I'm on my city's Civic Honors Commission (five members) and we have to rule on our biggest case yet next month. Our main avenue is named after Aldous Crane, a 19th-century industrialist who basically built the place -- funded the library, the hospital, the waterworks -- and the avenue was named for him in 1903 explicitly because of that philanthropy. A local historian has now dug up company board minutes showing Crane personally ordered private guards in to break a miners' strike in 1888, knowing those guards had been violent before; two strikers were shot dead. Nobody was ever charged, and it was in the papers at the time, just forgotten.

Three options are on the table: (1) rename the avenue after one of the dead strikers, (2) keep the name and add an interpretive plaque telling the whole story, or (3) a staff proposal for "dual naming" -- the avenue officially keeps Crane's name but also carries a second name commemorating the 1888 strike itself, the idea being that the street should commemorate the episode rather than honor or erase either man.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote. It needs an actual recommendation (I'm genuinely torn, so you have to pick one), and it has to grapple with what makes this hard: Crane was honored for the philanthropy, not the strikebreaking -- does what a commemoration is *for* fix what it *means* now, or does meaning drift? Does renaming erase history or just withdraw honor, and is the plaque a principled middle or a weaselly compromise? Give the dual-naming idea a fair shake before ruling on it, since half the commission loves it and half thinks it's a gimmick that satisfies nobody. And include the strongest argument against whatever you end up recommending.
