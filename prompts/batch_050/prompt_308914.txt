So my reading group just finished Fear and Trembling and our last meeting completely blew up -- half the room thinks Abraham is a hero of faith and the other half thinks Kierkegaard is basically holding up a murderer and daring us to flinch, and we got nowhere because everyone kept just naming scholars at each other. It's gotten weirdly personal for me too, because my cousin recently quit a good job and moved overseas for a religious calling against his whole family's objections, and at dinner he literally invoked Abraham, and I realized I couldn't articulate what I actually think is going on in that story, let alone whether it has any bearing on him.

What I want for next meeting is a proper sorting instrument, not another summary of interpretations. Build me a decision tree (or quiz-plus-scoring-key, whichever you think works better) with about 9 diagnostic questions that sorts people by what they'd actually say about cases rather than by which scholarly label they find flattering. The questions should be counterfactuals and modern analogues that answerable without any Kierkegaard vocabulary -- things like: suppose the ram never appears and Abraham goes through with it; was the act right when he raised the knife, wrong all along, or does the ending retroactively fix it? Suppose a stranger in your town claims the same command; what do you owe him? Does Isaac consenting change anything? Could a "suspension" ever justify a whistleblower or a CEO breaking the law for a genuine calling, or is whatever Abraham had unrepeatable by definition? Each question gets two or three answer options phrased neutrally.

Then the scoring key maps answer patterns onto positions, and I'd like these to at least be distinguishable if the questions are doing their job: (1) strong divine-command -- obeying God is simply right, full stop, and the story is no paradox at all; (2) the suspension is real but exceptional, faith is a higher telos that can't be codified or repeated; (3) the pseudonymous-author reading -- de Silentio is a poet who admits he can't understand Abraham, so the book is a reductio or a trap for people who want to admire faith from the outside, not an endorsement; (4) the Abraham-failed reading -- he should have argued with God or refused, and the contrast with Agamemnon cuts the other way than the text suggests; (5) a "knight of infinite resignation mistaken for faith" bucket for people whose answers show they admire the sacrifice but don't believe in the getting-Isaac-back part, which is arguably not one of the positions on Abraham at all but a misreading the quiz should catch. Please also flag any positions your tree can't actually separate from each other and say why, and if some combination of answers lands nowhere -- i.e., no actual reading occupies it -- note whether that combo seems incoherent or just unexplored. I'd rather the tree be honest about its blind spots than pretend to sort everyone cleanly.
