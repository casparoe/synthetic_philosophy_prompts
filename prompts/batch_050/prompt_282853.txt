I'm putting together a handout for a reading group that's working through big-history books and the backlash against them, and I want a decision tree that sorts readers into their actual position on grand narratives rather than the position they think they hold. Act as a philosopher of history who's also a good teacher, and think through the logical space carefully before you commit to the tree.

The tree should start from something like 'large-scale patterns in human history are worth explaining' and branch on the diagnostic questions that genuinely separate the views: whether the pattern is supposed to come from material circumstances (climate, crops, germs, geography) or from things like shared stories, cognitive shifts, and political choices; whether the story has a direction (progress, degeneration, or no direction at all, just contingency); whether a narrative this big can even be checked against evidence or is basically unfalsifiable; and whether the main problem with these books is that they're wrong or that they're politically loaded no matter their accuracy.

The leaves should end at named positions, roughly: Diamond-style environmental determinism, the Harari-style view where imagined orders and revolutions do the explanatory work, Graeber-and-Wengrow-style anti-teleology where early peoples were self-conscious political experimenters and hierarchy was a choice rather than an inevitability, and the critic positions that say grand synthesis itself is the mistake. Include at least one leaf nobody seems to occupy and say why that slot is empty (incoherent, or just unpopular?). Phrase each branch as a question someone could answer without knowing which camp it lands them in, and flag any pair of leaves your tree can't cleanly separate.

Format-wise: no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables. Just continuous prose like an email, walking through the tree node by node. It's fine if its a bit long as long as every sentence earns its place.
