Last weekend a friend and I argued until two in the morning about whether it is illuminating or merely lazy to describe present-day US–China relations in terms of the Thucydides trap — the idea, popularized by Graham Allison, that a ruling power and a rising power are prone to drift into war. I said the analogy misleads more than it teaches; she accused me of applying a standard to it that I don't apply to historical analogies I happen to like. That charge stung, because I suspect she's right, and I'd like help working out what my standard actually is.

My friend defends a principle she calls the mechanism-match test: a historical analogy earns its keep only when the present case shares the operative causal mechanism of the historical case, not merely its surface furniture (dictators, deadlines, dominoes, encirclement). I can neither accept it nor refute it, because my own verdicts on famous uses of analogy don't line up cleanly with it. Here are the verdicts, as honestly as I can report them.

1. The lessons drawn from Munich in 1938. As a reading of Hitler himself — that an expansionist with unlimited aims should be confronted early rather than bought off — I judge it sound. Invoked, as it was, to support intervention in Korea in 1950, I still judge it broadly legitimate. Invoked in the run-up to the Iraq War in 2003, I judge it illegitimate. Note that the surface structure of the two applications is nearly identical — dictator, provocation, don't appease — so whatever distinguishes them must live elsewhere. My friend says mechanism does the work: Hitler's aims were unlimited, while Saddam's regime was contained. That feels right, but see the next case.

2. The domino theory. Its proponents did have a mechanism story — revolutionary contagion across Southeast Asia — and the mechanism story was simply false. I judge the analogy misleading, which seems to show that mechanism-matching is insufficient unless the mechanism was actually operative in the source case. My friend's test has no clause for that.

3. Vietnam invoked against the Iraq and Afghanistan wars. Here my verdict splits. The analogy struck me as genuinely instructive about occupation, exit, and the domestic politics of protracted war, and misleading wherever it implied conscription-scale casualties or a peasant guerrilla conflict of a kind Iraq was not. So I find myself wanting a principle that can grade an analogy as right about some conclusions and wrong about others within a single invocation, rather than passing or failing it outright.

4. The July crisis of 1914 and the Cuban missile crisis. It is often said that Kennedy had the sleepwalking escalation of 1914 in mind — he had recently been reading Tuchman's account of it — and that this shaped his restraint; I gather the extent of that influence is debated, and I'd want any claim about it flagged accordingly. Setting that aside, I judge this a genuinely good use of history: the analogy generated vigilance about inadvertent escalation rather than a prefabricated verdict. My friend pounced on this: it is structurally the same move as Allison's — a great-power structural analogy — which I had just condemned. I believe there's a real difference, something like using an analogy to pose questions versus using it to deliver a conclusion, but I can't state it crisply, and I'm not sure the distinction doesn't end up rehabilitating the Thucydides trap, which I do not want rehabilitated wholesale.

5. My neighbor describes his dispute with his landlord as "his Munich." Obviously illegitimate. But I want the principle to explain why without leaning on stakes or scale, since a sound principle of analogical reasoning shouldn't require the case to be grand.

The case I keep going back and forth on: the 1991 Gulf War invoked as a template for limited aims — liberate, don't march on the capital. The analogy seems to have disciplined war aims admirably in 1991, yet later invocations appear to have drawn the lesson that wars can be short and cheap if aims are limited, and that lesson proved dangerously portable. I cannot settle whether this was one good analogy later abused, or a flawed analogy from the start.

What I'd like from you:

First, state the principle — or two or three candidate principles — that best captures my verdicts, precisely enough that someone could apply it to a new case without me in the room. If possible, repair my friend's mechanism-match test by adding the missing clauses rather than discarding it, since it has real appeal. For each clause of the principle, name which case above motivates it.

Second, tell me honestly which of my verdicts, if any, no reasonably economical principle can accommodate, and whether in those cases I should abandon the verdict or accept a more complex principle. I am prepared to lose a verdict; I'd rather know which one.

Third, give me the principle's verdict on the Gulf War case I'm torn about, and on one further case of your choosing that I haven't listed — ideally one where a superficially attractive analogy and a less obvious rival analogy compete.

Finally, a request about epistemic calibration. I suspect some of what passes for common knowledge here is thin: for instance, that it is well documented that policymakers lean heavily on a small stock of salient analogies, but that particular claims — how much Munich actually drove the decisions over Korea or Vietnam, how much Kennedy owed to the July crisis, whether the Thucydides pattern has genuine predictive content — remain contested among historians and political scientists. Please separate what is reasonably settled among people who study analogical reasoning in foreign policy from what is genuinely disputed, flag wherever my framing of a case above rests on a contested reading of the history, and don't present any contested historiography as the consensus.
