I'm drafting the literature-review section of a paper on the grounds of promissory obligation, and before I finalize it I want to make sure I haven't missed an entire family of views. The accounts I already have, each with a rough gloss so you can tell what I mean by them: (1) the expectation or assurance theory -- a promise binds because the promisor deliberately invites the promisee to form expectations about the promisor's future conduct, and reneging wrongs the promisee by abusing an assurance that was freely given (I associate this most with Scanlon); (2) the practice or conventionalist theory -- promising is a rule-governed social institution, and the obligation to perform falls out of the practice's constitutive rules together with a general duty of fair play owed to institutions one has benefited from and voluntarily made use of (roughly the Rawlsian principle of fidelity); (3) the rights-transfer theory -- promising effects a transfer of a claim-right from promisor to promisee, analogous to a transfer of property, so the obligation is simply the correlative of a right the promisee now holds and could not previously have had; and (4) the normative-powers theory -- we have a deep and valuable interest in being able to place ourselves under obligations at will, promising is the exercise of a genuine normative power, and the obligation binds because creating obligations is just what the power does, much as willing an estate works because testamentary powers exist.

What I want from you is a fifth family of account, genuinely different from these four -- not a hybrid of two of them, not a special case (for instance, not 'the expectation theory restricted to reliance rather than bare expectation'), and not a restatement in new vocabulary. Describe the proposed ground of obligation clearly enough that I could present it to a graduate seminar, say what it purports to explain that the four on my list explain awkwardly, and then give me your honest assessment of whether it's any good. In particular, test it against the standard pressure points in this literature: promises to the dead or to people who will never learn whether you performed, promises extracted under coercion, promises to do something immoral, and cases where the promisee neither expects performance nor relies on it. If your candidate handles some of these better than my existing four, say so; if on reflection it collapses into one of them wearing different clothes, say that instead -- I'd rather have the honest verdict than a flattering one.
