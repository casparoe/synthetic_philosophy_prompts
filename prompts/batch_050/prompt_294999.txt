You are a philosopher with expertise in ethics, rational choice theory, and the economics of cooperation. Please think carefully about the following dispute, reconstructing the strongest version of each side before issuing any verdicts.

Two colleagues of mine, Dana and Emre, each donate $4,000 a year to charity. Dana gives to a global-health organization; Emre gives to a farmed-animal sanctuary. Each regards the other's cause as dramatically less valuable than their own: Dana thinks the sanctuary spends enormous sums per animal helped, and Emre thinks deworming-style interventions are a rounding error next to industrial animal agriculture. Last week Emre proposed a swap: instead of each donating to their own cause, each will donate their $4,000 to the other's preferred charity. Their argument has run through several rounds, and I'd like you to referee it.

(1) Emre opened by framing the swap as moral gains from trade. Dana values his money going to global health far more than to the sanctuary, and he values hers going to the sanctuary far more than to global health. The swap therefore improves the outcome as measured by each person's own values — a Pareto improvement with no loser, differing from ordinary trade only in that the goods are moral rather than material.

(2) Dana rejected the framing. She doesn't merely *prefer* global health the way one prefers a flavor; she believes it is genuinely, objectively better. Beliefs aim at truth and cannot be traded like tastes. The "gain" Emre describes exists only relative to her subjective values, but her commitment isn't a subjective value in that sense — so there's no well-defined gain to capture.

(3) Emre replied that this proves far too much. Every coalition government, every legislative compromise, every negotiated budget involves supporting something you regard as suboptimal in exchange for something you regard as better. If treating convictions as tradeable is corrupting, then all political compromise is corrupting, and the principled person simply gets less of what they value while the unprincipled get more.

(4) Dana answered that the political analogy cuts the other way: vote-selling and logrolling are widely condemned precisely because some things are degraded by being priced, and the deeper problem is epistemic. Once their donations are locked into a recurring swap, neither of them has any incentive to figure out who is actually right. The trade doesn't resolve their disagreement — it institutionalizes it, paying both parties to stop investigating.

(5) Emre conceded the epistemic cost but denied it's decisive. The realistic alternative to the swap isn't resolution; it's the status quo, in which each donates as before and the disagreement sits untouched anyway. The swap strictly dominates that stalemate by each party's own lights, and the fact that their beliefs are truth-apt changes nothing: acting on one's best current judgment — including one's judgment about the value of cooperation — is all anyone can ever do.

(6) Dana's final move: even on purely internal terms, the gain is partly illusory. Part of what she values is a world in which people direct resources toward what's genuinely best. A general norm of moral trading pushes toward a world in which moral conviction is priced and exchanged, and she values that negatively. So the swap may not be a gain even in her own currency — which means Emre's opening Pareto argument never got off the ground.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the six steps, say whether the move succeeds, fails, or partly succeeds, and why — in particular, whether Dana's belief-versus-preference objection in (2) is coherent, whether Emre's political analogy in (3) survives Dana's reply in (4), and whether Dana's final move in (6) is a genuine self-undermining of the trade or an illegitimate shifting of the goalposts. Then give an overall verdict: who is more nearly right, and what, if anything, are both of them missing?

Finally, two requests about your own answer: attach a rough confidence level to each of your move-by-move rulings, and single out the single ruling you are least confident in, together with the consideration or argument that would most change your mind about it.
