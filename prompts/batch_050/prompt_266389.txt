I’ve spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and the vocabulary I naturally think in is Bayesian: explicit and implicit hypothesis spaces, priors, likelihood ratios, model comparison, compression, held-out evidence, post-hoc parameters, and the distinction between finding a hypothesis and confirming it. I’m interested in inference to the best explanation, but I’m suspicious that philosophers sometimes treat explanatory goodness as a mysterious extra source of evidence that cannot be represented probabilistically. Please restate the following position in my vocabulary as faithfully as possible: IBE is not an alternative to Bayesian reasoning but a disciplined search-and-representation procedure that helps reasoners notice candidate hypotheses, assign priors to them, appreciate which observations those hypotheses actually predict, and avoid privileging whichever model was considered first.

Use a two-stage translation. First map the familiar explanatory virtues separately: simplicity as compression or description-length cost, scope as predictive coverage, conservatism as compatibility with established models, unification as shared latent structure, fruitfulness as anticipated value on future evidence, and non-ad-hocness as having few parameters introduced after seeing the data. Second, use two provisional concepts of your own if the standard Bayesian mapping fails: an explanation has “explanatory debt” when it accounts for the observation only by adding a poorly constrained component, and it receives a “retroactive compression bonus” when it makes already-known but previously unrelated facts cheaper to describe together. Do not assume these are legitimate probabilities merely because they sound technical.

Then give me a residue report: which parts of IBE translate cleanly, which survive only by smuggling psychological judgments into priors or likelihoods, and which have no Bayesian counterpart at all. Pay particular attention to contrastive explanation—why this rather than a specified alternative—the difference between a hypothesis being probable and being illuminating, and cases where the best available explanation still ought not to be believed. Finally, say whether the residue reveals something important that Bayesianism omits, or whether it is mostly the reification of human fluency and conversational context. Open with a one-sentence bottom line, followed by the sections “Translation,” “What survives,” “What is distorted,” and “Residue,” in no more than 1,100 words.
