I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. I recently read Imre Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations, and I'm trying to work out how seriously to take its central claim that mathematics is a "quasi-empirical" discipline rather than a purely deductive one.

Please write an essay that argues this question to a definite conclusion. Specifically, the essay should do the following:

1. Reconstruct Lakatos's picture of how mathematics actually develops, using the dialogue's central case study: the conjecture that for any polyhedron, the number of vertices minus the number of edges plus the number of faces equals two. Explain the roles of proof, counterexample, lemma incorporation, monster-barring, and proof-generated concepts in that dialectic, and why Lakatos thinks the final theorem is in some sense made true by the proof's demands rather than merely discovered.

2. Test the quasi-empirical thesis against the world of software. In my work, a "specification" that turns out to have counterexamples gets patched rather than discarded, code is refactored so that the concepts better fit what the tests actually require, and a program can be formally verified against a spec that nobody is confident is the right spec. Assess how far these practices genuinely parallel Lakatos's dialectic and where the analogy breaks down — in particular, whether a patched program is analogous to a revised theorem or merely to a revised conjecture.

3. Give the strongest opposing view a fair run: once a conjecture is stated in a rigorous axiomatic framework, its proof is deductively valid or it is nothing, and Lakatos has at best described the psychology and history of discovery, not the epistemology of mathematics itself. Consider whether the rise of machine-checked proofs in systems like Lean or Coq strengthens this objection (since the proof becomes incontrovertible) or undermines it (since the formalization itself is a fallible, negotiated artifact that may not capture the informal concept).

4. Reach and defend a conclusion: is "quasi-empirical" an apt description of mathematics, a misleading metaphor, or something in between? The conclusion should say something substantive about where the fallibility of mathematics actually resides if the opposing view is even partly right.

Write the essay in American English, roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, and assume a reader who is intelligent but, like me, has no philosophy training: define technical terms as you use them.
