So my city has two ways of handling monuments to people we now think were awful, and I can't tell whether they're coherent together. Old statues of colonial generals and slave-trading benefactors mostly stay put but get a bronze plaque bolted on at the base explaining the bad stuff — "recontextualizing," they call it. But street names honoring the exact same sort of figures get changed outright, and as far as I know nobody has ever proposed keeping the street name and just adding some kind of footnote to the sign. What theory of what monuments actually do would justify treating the two cases differently? Does the plaque policy presuppose that a statue is something like a claim — it has content, so you can argue with it, correct it, annotate it — while renaming presupposes that a street name is more like an ongoing act of honoring that no disclaimer can cancel? Or is there one theory that covers both, maybe something about how the pedestal and the central square do the honoring no matter what text you add, so the plaque was never going to work anyway? Tell me what the two practices are strictly committed to versus what they just happen to fit well with, where they're underdetermined, and what would have to change in the policy if we decided statues are honors rather than assertions (or vice versa).
