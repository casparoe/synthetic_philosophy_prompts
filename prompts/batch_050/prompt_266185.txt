I host a monthly philosophy reading group for a dozen educated non-specialists -- engineers, a nurse, a retired judge, two novelists -- and at our next meeting I have roughly forty-five minutes to teach them the thesis sometimes summarized as "deliberation crowds out prediction": the claim, associated with a line of work in the theory of rational choice, that an agent who is genuinely deliberating about what to do cannot at the same time assign credences to her own options, since treating an act as open in deliberation is incompatible with treating it as an outcome to be forecast. Do not explain the thesis to me; I know it. Plan how I should teach it.

I want the plan to specify the order in which the pieces must come and why each depends on the last. My current sketch is: first enough informal decision theory that they take acts (not states of the world) to be the objects of choice; second, the phenomenological observation that deliberation treats options as open in a way that forecasting does not; third, the thesis itself; fourth, the obvious counterexamples; fifth, the repair. But I am not wedded to this, and if you think some piece must precede another or that the order hides a circularity, say so.

The plan needs one carrying example, and I want your advice on what it must have and lack. I have been considering a decision with real stakes that stays genuinely open until the end -- say, whether to accept a job abroad -- contrasted with a case where self-prediction seems perfectly natural, like forecasting whether one will keep a resolution one has broken many times. Tell me whether this pair can bear the whole weight of the session, what the deliberating case needs so that asking "what is the probability that I take the job?" feels like it stalls rather than settles the deliberation, and what the contrast case needs so the group does not conclude the thesis is simply false.

Then give me the misconception map: the misreadings you would expect this particular audience to form, in the order they will form them, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact. I anticipate at least three: that the thesis is just the banal point that the future is uncertain; that it is a claim about the limits of introspection or self-knowledge generally; and that it says our own behavior is in principle unpredictable, like quantum events. There are probably others this audience is prone to -- the judge and the engineers will bring different confusions -- and I would like those anticipated too. Note also that someone will invoke Newcomb's problem within ten minutes, since it came up last month; tell me whether to let that in or defer it, and how.

Finally, give me the check for understanding: one question I can pose at the end whose answer would show the group has grasped the thesis as a claim about the structure of deliberation rather than as a puzzle about predicting the future, and tell me what a merely verbal mastery of the material would sound like so I can tell the difference. Say also what I should openly refuse to cover at this level -- the formal literature on ratifiability and evidential decision theory is the obvious candidate -- and how to say so without making the session feel truncated.

Please answer in continuous prose, as if writing me a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
