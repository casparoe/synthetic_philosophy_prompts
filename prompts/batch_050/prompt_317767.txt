So I help run our grad program's philosophy podcast (very small operation, mostly it's an excuse to get free coffee with interesting people) and next month we're interviewing a philosopher who's just published a book arguing that money pump arguments are basically worthless. Her view, as I understand it, is that there are no genuinely *diachronic* requirements of rationality at all — rationality only ever assesses you at a time-slice, so an agent with cyclic preferences (A over B, B over C, C over A) who gets pumped for money isn't thereby irrational; each individual trade she makes can be perfectly rational given her preferences at that moment. The "pump" just shows that rational agents can be predictably exploitable, which she says is a fact about the world being mean, not about rationality.

I want maybe 8-10 interview questions that actually press her where the view is weakest, not just "tell us about your book." Some angles I'd like covered, feel free to restructure:

- The standard escape from a money pump is to foresee the sequence of trades and refuse the first one, or to decide in advance how you'll respond to the whole series and stick to it. But both of those seem to require exactly the kind of cross-temporal constraint (dynamic consistency, sticking to plans) her view says doesn't exist. I want a question that forces her to either accept some diachronic requirement or explain how a purely time-slice-rational agent avoids being pumped.
- Relatedly: does she think the agent who foresees the pump and the agent who doesn't are equally rational? The foresight answer seems to make rationality depend on what you happen to know about the future, which feels weird.
- Money pump arguments have also been run against agents who violate the independence axiom of expected utility theory, and against agents with incomplete preferences who keep trading back and forth between two options they can't rank. I want to know if she's committed to saying all of those agents are fully rational too, and if so whether she's just denying that preference rationality constrains preferences at all.
- The Dutch book parallel: the structure of Dutch book arguments for probabilism looks really similar to money pump arguments. Does she reject those too? If she accepts them but rejects money pumps, what's the principled difference?
- I'd like one question about what her view actually recommends — like, if you find yourself with cyclic preferences and someone offers you the trades, what *should* you do on her account? Is there any guidance at all?
- And end with something about what could change her mind — what result or case would make her give up pure time-slice rationality.

For each question, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give (she's good at the "that just assumes the diachronic requirement I'm denying" move, so help me corner that). Casual tone is fine, it's a podcast not a dissertation defense.
