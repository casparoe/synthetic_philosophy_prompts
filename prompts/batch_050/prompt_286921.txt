I'm in an upper-division bioethics seminar, and the class has spent most of the term split into two camps over abortion. The first camp thinks Thomson's violinist thought experiment shows that abortion is at least often permissible even if we grant, for the sake of argument, that the fetus is a person with a right to life, since no person's right to life automatically includes a claim on another's body. The second camp thinks Marquis's future-like-ours argument shows that abortion is presumptively seriously wrong in roughly the way killing an adult is, precisely because it bypasses the personhood question: what makes killing wrong, on their view, is that it deprives the victim of a future of value, and a fetus standardly has one. After weeks of the two sides talking past each other, our professor has assigned them to produce a single joint statement in the style of an adversarial collaboration, and I've been appointed drafter. I'd like you to produce the draft I should bring to the next meeting.

The document needs four parts. First, a numbered list of claims both camps can actually sign — substantive enough to be worth signing, not vacuous, and not slanted toward either side. I suspect the list includes that the personhood question does not by itself settle the moral question, that a normally developing fetus has a future containing the kinds of experiences and projects whose loss makes killing adults wrong, and that neither side's position licenses indifference to the circumstances or manner of particular abortions — but part of your job is to work out what genuinely belongs and what only looks agreed. Second, a statement of the residual disagreements, each phrased so that each camp would accept the wording of its own position; where a disagreement turns out to be verbal (I have in mind how both sides use terms like right to life and deprivation), dissolve it explicitly and say what the genuine dispute underneath it is, if any. Third, a list of cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one camp to revise its view. I expect these to include whether the violinist analogy extends beyond pregnancies resulting from rape to pregnancies resulting from consensual sex with contraception, whether the future-like-ours account is committed to condemning contraception as well and whether that implication is a reductio or a bullet worth biting, and whether having a future of value grounds a right against being killed or only a weighty reason against killing that bodily claims can override. Fourth, for each crux, a case or argument both camps agree bears on it, with each camp's verdict on that case recorded in advance — please make use, at minimum, of the people-seeds scenario, a case of pregnancy despite responsibly used contraception, and a case in which carrying to term imposes a serious but non-lethal health burden on the pregnant person.

Two ground rules the professor set: no theological premises, and both camps are to treat the relevant developmental biology as settled and shared. Write this as a document both camps could genuinely put their names to, not as a covert verdict on who is right. Finally, end the draft with a short candid note aimed at me rather than at the class: state how confident you are that each of your agreed claims would in fact be signed by a committed defender of each position, and single out the single formulation in the whole document — whether an agreed claim or a wording of one side's view — that you are least confident both camps would accept, explaining why.
