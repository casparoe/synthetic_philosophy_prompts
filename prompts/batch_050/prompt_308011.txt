I recently learned that Johannes Kepler spent years arguing that the planetary orbits were nested around the five Platonic solids, that he later wrote an entire treatise (Harmonices Mundi, 1619) on the musical harmonies supposedly built into the cosmos, and that he never really gave up on the polyhedron idea — yet he abandoned circular orbits because Tycho Brahe's observations of Mars disagreed with them by only eight arcminutes. Please reconstruct, with reasons grounded in his life and work, what Kepler would think of how beauty is used as evidence in today's fundamental physics. Consider in particular: Paul Dirac's view that a theory's mathematical elegance can outweigh a failure to match experiment; string theorists who argue their framework is too mathematically compelling not to describe something real; and critics who reply that the Standard Model's ugliness — dozens of unexplained constants, three seemingly redundant generations of particles — shows the universe does not care about human aesthetics. Would Kepler side more with Dirac or with the critics? Would he see his Platonic-solid episode as vindicating aesthetic theorizing or as a warning against it? And where would he draw the line between a beautiful pattern being a discovery about nature and a projection of the mind? I care less about a final verdict than about which specific episodes from his career he would invoke, and what lesson he took from each.
