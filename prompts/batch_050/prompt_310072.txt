After forty years as an experimental chemist, I have spent my retirement catching up on philosophy, mostly through a university extension program, and I have been rash enough to accept an invitation to give an evening talk to the university's philosophy society. My title is something like "The Happiness League Table: What It Measures and Why a Country Should Not Try to Win It." My thesis has two parts. First, that the annual world happiness rankings do measure something genuine, namely average self-reported life evaluation on the ladder-of-life question, but that this is at most one ingredient of well-being and arguably the ingredient most vulnerable to recalibration: as circumstances improve, people quietly redraw what counts as the best possible life for them, the way an instrument drifts and has to be re-zeroed, so the numbers can stay flat while lives genuinely improve. Second, that treating rank position as a national policy target is a conceptual mistake before it is a practical one, since the measure is relative and ordinal, and targeting it will distort both the measurement and the priorities of government. I support the first part with an extended analogy from laboratory instrumentation, which I suspect is where I am most exposed.

The audience will be mostly advanced undergraduates who have actually done the reading on theories of well-being (hedonism, desire-satisfaction, objective-list accounts), plus a few faculty: a utilitarian who works on measuring welfare for policy, a Kantian ethicist who I gather dislikes the whole project of quantifying the good life, and a political philosopher interested in governance by indicators. None of them, so far as I know, has looked closely at the survey methodology, so some may grant me the science and attack the philosophy, and others the reverse.

I would like a preparation sheet for the question period. Give me the objections this particular audience will raise, roughly in the order in which they are likely to come, each phrased the way that sort of philosopher would actually put it rather than in my chemist's vocabulary. For each, tell me what I should frankly concede, what I should hold firm on, and a reply I can give in about two minutes, with an honest note on where that reply is weakest. I would also like to know which objection would do the most damage if pressed hard, and the question I am quietly hoping nobody thinks to ask.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would write a long letter to a friend before the event: no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold text, no tables. I intend to read it twice the evening before, and I find I absorb arguments better as paragraphs than as apparatus.
