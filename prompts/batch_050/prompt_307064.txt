My reading group has been doing Dharmakīrti and we built what we think is an argument that his system eats itself, and I can't find the weak joint. Here it is.

(1) To be real just is to be able to produce an effect. If a thing makes no causal difference to anything, calling it real adds nothing — this is his own causal-efficacy criterion, as we understand it.
(2) Nothing produces an effect later than it exists. If a thing produces an effect at all, it does so in the very moment it exists. If it supposedly sits around unchanged for a while and *then* produces something, the thing that did the producing was really the thing-plus-whatever-changed, i.e. a new thing. An unchanged persisting thing does nothing in the interim, so by (1) it isn't real in the interim.
(3) So everything real exists only for a moment.
(4) Perception is fine with this, since it just registers the bare particular in front of it. But inference is different: when I infer fire on the hill from smoke, my inference works with smoke-ness and fire-ness, properties that repeat across moments and across cases.
(5) Repeatable properties are not momentary. So by (3) they aren't real, and by (1) they can't do anything.
(6) But inference that latches onto something unreal and causally inert can't be a source of knowledge. A pramāṇa has to connect you to something real.
(7) So either inference isn't a source of knowledge, or things aren't really momentary, or causal efficacy isn't the mark of the real.
(8) But he needs all three: inference is one of his two pramāṇas, momentariness is the signature doctrine, and the causal-efficacy criterion is what the argument for momentariness runs on.

So the system seems to prove too much: it uses the mark of the real to argue that everything real is momentary, and in doing so it makes its own second pramāṇa impossible. Which step do you reject? Personally I want to keep (1), (7) and (8), so I figure something in (2) or in the (4)–(6) stretch has to give, but I can't say which, and I want to know what rejecting it costs — i.e. what you'd have to give up elsewhere in the system to hold that step false. And if you think the argument is actually sound and the whole thing is just incoherant, defend that instead.
