I've been reading and rereading the iterated amplification / factored cognition material for years and have sketched my own position below. Before I post it, I'd like a consistency audit rather than a general introduction. My commitments:

1. The factored cognition hypothesis is basically right: for nearly any cognitively demanding task, there exists a decomposition into subtasks a competent human can perform in well under an hour, such that composing the answers reproduces (or exceeds) unaided expert performance.
2. The infinite limit of HCH — the human consulting arbitrarily many copies of themselves consulting further copies — is a good idealization of that particular human's fully considered judgment: roughly, what I would conclude if I deliberated indefinitely.
3. Distilling HCH into a learned model is alignment-preserving by construction: each training step merely learns to match the human-plus-consultants, so no misaligned objective ever enters the system.
4. Systems built this way can substantially outperform the unaided human at the root — that's the point of the whole scheme, not a hoped-for bonus.
5. No node in the tree, including the root, needs to understand the global problem; local competence at each node plus message-passing is sufficient for globally coherent output.

Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only appear to? I'm especially suspicious of the interplay between (2) and (1) — real deliberation is sequential and path-dependent, and a tree of frozen copies may not be the same idealization — and of whether (3) and (4) can both hold given that per-node oversight doesn't obviously compose across exponentially many nodes. But I may be wrong about where the real problems are. Please rank any tensions by severity, note any pair that dissolves on inspection, and identify the single cheapest revision that would restore consistency along with what it would cost me.
