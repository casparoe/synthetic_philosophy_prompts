I'm a program officer at a mid-sized philanthropic foundation, and I chair the committee that screens grant proposals before they go to the board. We fund mostly conventional work -- malaria prevention, scholarships, a local land trust -- but twice now we've had serious internal fights over proposals whose entire case rests on a very small probability of an astronomically large benefit. One was a research program aimed at a low-probability catastrophic risk; another was a long-shot scientific bet whose proponent argued that even a one-in-ten-thousand chance of success justified the cost because success would be worth so much. In both meetings, someone pulled out a calculator, multiplied a tiny probability by an enormous payoff, and announced that the math compelled us to fund it, while someone else replied that this reasoning would force us to fund almost anything if the claimed stakes got big enough, including obvious nonsense. I don't think either of them was being stupid, and I don't think "use expected value" or "ignore expected value" is an answer.

What I want is a screening procedure my committee can actually run -- forty-five minutes per proposal, members with no philosophy background, decisions needing a two-thirds vote -- that operationalizes whatever is genuinely defensible in the small-probability-huge-stakes reasoning while protecting us from its pathologies. It should tell us what questions to ask and in what order: how to treat a probability estimate that the applicant produced themselves and no one can check; whether there is a probability below which we should simply decline to multiply, and if so how to set that floor without it being arbitrary; how to weigh the difference between a small probability attached to a mechanism we understand and one attached to a chain of speculation; and what to do about the worry that the size of the claimed payoff is doing all the work -- that an applicant can always win the arithmetic by describing the benefit grandly enough. Where the procedure has to hand a question back to human judgment rather than settle it, say so explicitly and say why that step resists being turned into a rule.

Please run the finished procedure on two cases: the low-probability catastrophic-risk research program described above, and a hypothetical proposal to fund a prize for a proof-of-concept in a speculative energy technology, where the applicant's own model assigns success a one-in-a-million chance but claims success would transform global welfare. Assume for the second case that my instinct is to reject it; if your procedure says fund it, don't soften the conclusion -- I want to know when the procedure bites me, because that's the test of whether it constrains anything. Finally, tell me where this procedure is most likely to lead us wrong: what kind of proposal it would wrongly reject or wrongly fund, and what argument or consideration, if someone raised it in committee, should make us abandon or revise the procedure rather than just apply it.
