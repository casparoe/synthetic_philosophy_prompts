Write a dialogue (roughly 500–700 words) between two art-world philosophers debating the following fictional case:

The Corvid Gallery owns *Evening Marsh*, the only surviving painting attributed to the seventeenth-century painter Anselm Verhoeck. Two years ago, as part of a conservation project, a lab made a complete atomic-level scan of the painting and used it to produce a "molecular duplicate": a physically distinct object, printed by layered deposition, that no instrument or expert can distinguish from the scan data. Last month, *Evening Marsh* was destroyed in a fire. The gallery's board now proposes to hang the molecular duplicate in its place, on the same wall, under the original's title and label.

One character, an aesthetic empiricist, argues that the duplicate deserves the full value we accorded the original: everything anyone ever cared about in the painting — its look, its structure, the experience it affords — survives intact, and to treat the duplicate as lesser is superstition. The other character defends the position that the duplicate, however perfect, lacks the original's value because value partly depends on an object's actual causal history — on being the very surface Verhoeck's hand worked.

The dialogue must engage three complications, which the characters should discover rather than recite: (a) the scan was taken two years before the fire, during which time the original acquired new craquelure and a restorer's varnish — so the duplicate matches an earlier temporal stage of the painting, raising the question of which stage, if any, is "the" painting the gallery meant to preserve; (b) the scan data still exists and could print a second identical duplicate tomorrow, which pressures both sides differently; and (c) midway through, one character proposes the hypothesis that what the empiricist actually values is not experience but a *belief about provenance* — and tests this by asking whether the empiricist's view would survive the gallery secretly swapping in the duplicate without telling anyone, which it has the technical ability to do.

Requirements: both characters must be equally sharp and philosophically literate; neither may be a strawman. Each must respond to the other's actual arguments, concede points where concessions are genuinely forced, and press objections where they genuinely bite. The dialogue may end in agreement, stalemate, or a narrowing of the disagreement, but the ending should follow from what was actually argued rather than being imposed.

As the final line, after the dialogue ends, provide a one-sentence summary beginning "Summary:" stating the deepest unresolved point of disagreement between the two characters.
