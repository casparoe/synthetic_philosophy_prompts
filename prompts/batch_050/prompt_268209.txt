I write a column on science and ideas, and I'm drafting a piece about the moral weight of everyday conventions — tipping, queuing, jaywalking, and the like. The piece grew out of a dinner argument I witnessed between two old friends, and I want to get the adjudication right before I write it up, since neither convinced the other and I genuinely cannot tell who came out ahead. The first, a social scientist I'll call Mara, opened with the claim that conventions are merely coordination equilibria: they have no intrinsic moral force, so the only question is whether a particular violation harms anyone, which means jaywalking across an empty German street at three in the morning is perfectly fine, and tipping in a country where menu prices include service is purely optional. The second, a schoolteacher I'll call Leon, replied that this misses how conventions work: they are public goods held up by near-universal compliance, so even costless-seeming violations erode them, and anyone who benefits from living among orderly queues and predictable streets owes the system their compliance in return — a duty he said falls on visitors with special force, since a guest should follow house rules. Mara answered that the erosion argument proves too much, since it would forbid even principled noncompliance aimed at reforming a bad convention, such as refusing to tip in protest of a system that lets employers underpay staff, and that fair-play obligations require voluntarily accepting a scheme's benefits, which a tourist who never asked for local norms has not done. Leon then argued that some benefits cannot be declined short of leaving — you cannot walk the streets without enjoying their orderliness — and that the street really is the locals' house in the relevant sense. Mara countered that house rules derive their force from property rights, which do not extend to public space, and that if mere existence conferred moral authority on a convention, then pernicious conventions, say rigid dress codes or expectations that everyone stand for an anthem, would become obligatory too. Leon conceded the pernicious-convention worry but proposed a line: conventions that solve genuine coordination problems and distribute their benefits broadly carry presumptive force, and tipping is a special case anyway, because servers' wages are set on the assumption of tips, so withholding them does not reform the system but simply stiffens the worker in front of you. He also added the point Germans often make about jaywalking: an adult crossing against the light in view of children models dangerous behavior, so there is almost never a truly empty street.

Please work through this exchange move by move and tell me who is right about which specific point: whether conventions generate obligations only through the costs of violating them or also through fair-play and erosion considerations; whether a visitor's duty to comply differs from a resident's and in virtue of what; whether the distinction between coordination-solving and merely oppressive conventions can be drawn cleanly enough to do the work Leon needs; and whether tipping really is a special case rather than just one convention among others. I would also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing — for instance, whether there is a relevant difference between violating a convention openly and violating it unobserved, or between conventions that allocate burdens and conventions that merely signal group membership. One request about form: because I will be adapting your answer into column prose, please give me your assessment as continuous prose only, in the manner of a long, careful letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
