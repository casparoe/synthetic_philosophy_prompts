In a seminar on Mill’s The Subjection of Women, a classmate defended gendered divisions of domestic labor by saying, “It is only natural for women to be the primary caregivers.” Our instructor replied that Mill had shown why appeals to nature cannot justify the existing relations between men and women. Later, however, the same instructor suggested that if coercive laws, economic dependence, educational pressures, and social penalties were removed, the choices women then made might tell us something about their uncoerced inclinations. Several of us were unsure whether these two claims were compatible: why is “nature” an illegitimate basis for assigning women a social role, but potentially a legitimate object of empirical inquiry once institutions have been reformed?

Please treat “it is only natural” as a slogan and disambiguate the precise claims it might be making: for example, that a pattern is currently widespread; that it would arise without human interference; that it is innate rather than learned; that it expresses a biological tendency; that it represents healthy or proper functioning; that it realizes a divinely or evolutionarily given purpose; or that whatever is natural is therefore morally right. For each interpretation, explain what further premise would be needed to derive the conclusion that women ought to occupy a particular social role, whether that inference is plausible, and whether Mill could consistently accept the underlying descriptive claim while rejecting its normative use. Then explain the difference between saying that existing behavior proves little about women’s nature because it has been shaped by coercive institutions, and saying that nature is altogether irrelevant to social arrangements. Conclude by assessing whether my instructor’s two remarks are genuinely in tension, and if so, what clarification would resolve it.
