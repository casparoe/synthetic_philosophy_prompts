I teach a second-year undergrad module on the philosophy of history, and we're about to spend a couple of weeks on societal collapse. Roughly 18 students, one 90-minute session, and they've read nothing in advance -- that's deliberate.

I want an in-class simulation that makes them feel the force of one particular idea: that "collapse" might be less a thing that happens to societies and more a description applied to them afterwards. That is, whether Easter Island or the Classic Maya "collapsed" seems to depend on what you take the society to *be* -- the population, the elite institutions, the monumental building programme, the language and farming practices -- and on who is doing the counting and over what timescale. I want them to run a little society, watch it go through something genuinely dramatic, and then discover they can't agree amongst themselves whether it collapsed, because they're each quietly using different criteria.

So: design the activity. Rules, materials (paper, tokens, whiteboard -- nothing fancier), a minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief. A few things I'm set on:

- The core game should be a resource-management exercise with a real squeeze in it, so something dramatic actually happens to their societies. I don't want a discussion exercise wearing a game's clothes.
- Build in a second phase where the *same* events get assessed from a different vantage point -- e.g. they reconvene as their own society's descendants a century on, or each group gets handed a different "record" of what happened (population figures, or elite institutional records, or an oral tradition). The moment where one sequence of events flips between "collapse" and "transformation" depending on which record you're holding is the entire point of the session.
- The debrief should map specific moments from the game onto the real historiographical fights -- the old ecocide reading of Rapa Nui versus the revisionist pushback, the Maya "collapse" versus the awkward fact that millions of Maya people and dozens of Mayan languages carried on -- without me lecturing at them.
- The scoring should be perverse in a useful way: the groups that "win" by the game's own metric should be the ones most vulnerable to being redescribed as having collapsed.
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and what to do about it.
- And be honest about what the game *can't* reproduce. The people who lived through these episodes didn't get to pick their vantage point retrospectively, and real collapses involved famine and violence that a token game trivialises -- I want a note on how to acknowledge that in the room without the whole thing turning po-faced.

One hard constraint: it has to work for students who've never heard of Rapa Nui or Tikal, so the history needs to arrive via the debrief, not be assumed by the rules.
