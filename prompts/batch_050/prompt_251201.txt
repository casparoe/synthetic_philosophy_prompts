I'm a second-year PhD student in history, on the intellectual-historiography side, and I keep losing an argument with a freind in the English department and I'd like to settle it properly. The subject is A Tale of Two Cities. Her line is that Dickens is a sentimental liberal whose revolution is basically ideology plus crowd psychology — the Carmagnole dance, the mob sharpening it's weapons at the grindstone, the wood-sawyer treating his saw like a toy guillotine — and that the novel's only real counterweight is individual moral action: Darnay's personal innocence set against the revolution's logic of hereditary guilt, Carton's sacrifice at the end. My line is that Dickens, coming straight off Carlyle, is closer to a social historian: the book spends its whole first half documenting aristocratic cruelty (the Marquis's carriage running down a child and the tossed coin, what the Evremonde brothers did to Madame Defarge's family), so the Terror reads as the foreseeable wages of that oppression rather than an ideological fever dream. We both have scenes to cite, which is exactly the problem.

So: give me a serious reading of the novel through the historiography of the French Revolution — the social interpretation (Lefebvre, Soboul: class struggle, the sans-culottes as historical actors) against the revisionists (Cobban, Furet: ideology, discourse, contingency). Which scenes actually bear on the question, rather than just being available for plunder? I'm especially interested in the circumstances-vs-ideology crux: does the novel treat the Terror as implicit in the movement from the very start — the spilled wine cask in Saint Antoine staining everyone's hands red reads like a claim about inevitability — or as a later degeneration under pressure, which would land closer to the circumstances school? Relatedly, does Dickens stage the revolutions-devour-their-children thesis (Madame Defarge literally killed by her own weapon, Carton's closing vision of the guillotine consuming the people who fed it) as a structural claim about revolutions as such, or merely as tragedy about particular characters? And one more possibility neither of us has a good handle on: the novel's obsessive doubling — two cities, twin brothers, interchangeable look-alikes, repeated trials — could that be read as a formal version of the Tocquevillian continuity thesis, the new regime repeating the old one's structures instead of rupturing them? I'd like you to actually commit to where the book sits in this fight, if anywhere.

Two constraints. First, be honest about where the novel resists the historiographical frame entirely — the coincidence-driven plot, the spy machinery, the sheer melodrama — because my advisor's whole objection is that you can't do historiography with a book that runs on contrivance, and I don't want to hand him an easy target. Second, end with the meta-question: if the novel does take a position, is there anything a novel can show about historical explanation — causation, counterfactuals, the weight of the past on the present, the way Manette's Bastille letter sentences a generation that wasn't born when it was written — that an essay by Soboul or Furet structurally can't? This is going into a chapter prospectus, so I want a verdict more than balance; if your honest conclusion is that Dickens is just incoherent on the question, that's acceptable too, but argue for it.
