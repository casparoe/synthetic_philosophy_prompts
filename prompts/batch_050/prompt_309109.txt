My friend Nisha takes karma seriously, but as a straight-up theory of moral desert, not the instagram "good vibes come back to you" version. She has this passage from the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (she uses the old Max Müller translation, Sacred Books of the East vol. 15) basicly memorized:

"Now as a man is like this or like that, according as he acts and according as he behaves, so will he be:--a man of good acts will become good, a man of bad acts, bad. He becomes pure by pure deeds, bad by bad deeds. And here they say that a person consists of desires. And as is his desire, so is his will; and as is his will, so is his deed; and whatever deed he does, that he will reap."

Over the years I've collected her verdicts on cases:

1. A guy donates a pile of money to a shelter anonymously, but only to impress a woman he knows volunteers there. Her verdict: no merit at all, maybe a little demerit.
2. A woman dives in to save a drowning kid, tries everything, and the kid dies anyway. Full merit, same as if she'd succeeded.
3. A careful driver, totally sober and attentive, kills a pedestrian in a genuine freak accident no one could have forseen. No demerit.
4. A boss who constantly berates his staff, insists he "doesn't mean anything by it," but knows full well it stings and keeps doing it. Demerit.
5. A sleepwalker who knocks over and smashes a neighbor's lawn ornament while fully asleep. No demerit, nothing.

Now the new case I want to hit her with: a cashier who was drilled for years by a strict first boss into scrupulous honesty, and now, twenty years later, hands back a $20 overpayment out of pure reflex before any thought crosses her mind. When a coworker asks about it later she admits that if she'd actually stopped to deliberate, she's honestly not sure she would have given it back. Merit or not?

Predict her verdict, but the part I actually care about is the reasoning: lay out the two (or three) rules that each fit all five of her earlier verdicts, show me where they pull apart on the cashier case, and tell me which reading of the passage each rule is leaning on, since the text itself seems to point in two directions at once (the will-to-deed chain vs. the "as he behaves, so he becomes" bit). Also: which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog to the cashier, and what new case would definitively settle which rule she's really running?

Keep the whole answer under 450 words, American English please, and don't count the quoted passage toward the limit.
