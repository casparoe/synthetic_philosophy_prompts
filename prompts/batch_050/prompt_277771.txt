Two philosophers who have argued about Sen's liberal paradox for a decade have agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and I want you to draft the joint statement they should be able to sign. The background, for precision: Sen's result shows that no collective decision rule can jointly satisfy three conditions -- unrestricted domain (any profile of individual preferences is admissible), the weak Pareto principle (if everyone strictly prefers x to y, society does too), and minimal liberalism (at least two individuals are each decisive over at least one pair of alternatives differing only in some feature of their own personal sphere). The standard illustration involves a prude and a lewd and a single copy of a controversial novel: the prude prefers that nobody read it, then that he read it himself rather than the lewd; the lewd prefers that the prude read it, then that he read it himself; the Pareto principle and the rights assignment together generate a cycle.

The two parties are these. First, a Paretian welfarist who holds that the theorem exposes a genuine and sometimes tragic conflict between respecting rights and respecting unanimous preference, that the right lesson is that liberal rights must sometimes be overridden or traded off when preference satisfaction is at stake, and that the formalization of a right as decisiveness over a pair of alternatives is basically faithful. Second, a rights-as-constraints liberal who holds that the theorem is an artifact of modeling rights as preferences over outcomes, that rights are better understood as constraints on which alternatives or procedures are admissible in the first place, and that once this is done the "paradox" dissolves rather than being resolved.

Draft their joint statement with the following structure: (1) a numbered list of claims both parties accept, including what the theorem itself does and does not show and where each party thinks their own earlier rhetoric overreached; (2) a disagreements table in which each row states a residual disagreement in wording each side would accept for its own position -- take special care here, because the two parties disagree about whether their dispute is substantive or about the right way to model a right; (3) a list of cruxes -- sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party -- and for each crux a concrete case or consideration both agree bears on it, with each party's prediction or verdict recorded in advance. Candidate cruxes you should consider including, though you may replace them with better ones: whether "nosy" or meddlesome preferences should count for Pareto purposes; whether decisiveness-over-a-pair captures what it is to hold a right; whether the paradox's logic survives when the mechanism can use lotteries, sequencing, or side payments rather than picking a single outcome outright.

As a test of the joint framework, have the two parties apply it to a concrete charter question: a twelve-unit co-living building must write rules covering (a) the paint color inside each unit, and (b) amplified music audible across units, where one resident is a night-shift nurse whose daytime sleep is genuinely ruined by daytime noise. State which provisions each party's approach would recommend, where they would converge, and whether the case counts for either party as a crux-bearing instance or merely as an illustration.

Throughout, keep settled matters distinct from contested ones: the theorem and its proof are settled; what it shows about liberalism, welfarism, and the modeling of rights is not, and the statement should not present either party's interpretation as the received view. Where the two parties are using the same word -- "right," "preference," "decisive" -- in different senses, flag the ambiguity explicitly rather than letting the statement paper over it.
