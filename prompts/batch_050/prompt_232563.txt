Here are my verdicts on when something is a reason for an attitude, and I want to know what principle, if any, lies behind them. A demon credibly threatens to destroy the world unless I admire him: this gives me excellent reason to try to become admiring, but no reason at all to admire him — the admiration would still be unfitting. The same demon threatens destruction unless I believe he is generous: that is no reason to believe it; only evidence of his generosity could be. But his threat to hurt me if he catches me does seem to me a genuine reason to fear him, on a par with his cruelty, and not merely a reason to make myself afraid. The fact that laughing at my boss's jokes keeps me employed is no reason to find them funny, though it is a reason to laugh. The fact that the spider is harmless settles, for me, that fearing it is unfitting, whatever the fear's usefulness. Yet the fact that the roller coaster is perfectly safe does not seem to settle anything: the fear still feels answerable to how the ride presents itself, not to the statistics. Is there a single principle that sorts these — one that explains why threats bear on fear but not on admiration, and why evidence alone bears on belief? If my last two verdicts cannot both stand under that principle, tell me which to surrender. And say what the principle delivers for two cases I have not listed: is my rival's success any reason to envy her, and is the poignancy of the occasion a reason to grieve at the funeral of a man I despised?
