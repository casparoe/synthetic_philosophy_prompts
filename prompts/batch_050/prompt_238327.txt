I'm designing a seminar unit on Kavka's toxin puzzle and want to test whether the puzzle survives translation out of its science-fictional packaging. Construct a thought experiment that reproduces its core structure — a reward conditional on sincerely forming, by a stated deadline, the intention to perform some later act which, once the deadline has passed and the reward is secured, the agent will have no reason to perform — subject to these constraints: the setting must be an ordinary institution (a hospital, a firm, a court, a university), the scenario must fit in a single paragraph, and it may contain no billionaire benefactors, no infallible intention-detecting devices, and no threats. After the scenario, do the following. First, show that the reward in your case genuinely attaches to the intention rather than to any behavioral step toward the act, and explain how the institution in question could detect sincerity using only realistic means — or, if any realistic detection method necessarily rewards preparatory behavior rather than the intention itself, say so and explain what that shows about the original puzzle. Second, say whether your agent can rationally form the intention, and if she cannot, identify precisely which feature of the case blocks her. Third, state what your translation has removed from Kavka's original and whether the puzzle is harder or easier to escape in your version. Finally, say which side your case favors in the dispute over whether there can be reasons for intending that are not reasons for performing the intended act, and how a defender of the other side would attack the construction.
