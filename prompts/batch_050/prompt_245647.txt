I do freelance quality control for a couple of small type foundries, and I keep having the same fight with designers. They'll send me a nearly finished font and one glyph will set off alarm bells -- an 'a' that technically has all the parts but feels wrong, or one that's perfectly readable but looks like it wandered in from a completely different typeface. When I push back they say "it's still an a, users will read it fine," and honestly sometimes they're right, and I can't articulate the difference between a legitimate stylistic choice and a glyph that has stopped being the letter it's supposed to be. I was complaining about this to a friend who does cognitive science and she said this is basically the Letter Spirit problem, the Hofstadter thing about what makes every 'a' an 'a' across thousands of wildly different typefaces, and that there's real literature on categories, prototypes vs. exemplars, family resemblance, all of that. Cool, but I need something I can use on a deadline, not a reading list.

So: turn whatever is actually solid in that territory into a checking procedure I can run on a single glyph in a few minutes. It needs to separate the two questions I keep conflating -- (1) is this thing still an instance of its letter at all, and (2) is it an instance *in the spirit of this particular font* -- because I suspect those have genuinely different failure modes. Tell me what tests to run in order, what counts as passing each one, which distinctions from the theory have to survive the simplification and which I can safely drop, and where the procedure has to stop and hand it back to human judgment because it's a taste call rather than a category call. Then run it on three real cases from my inbox: a single-story 'a' (the circle-with-a-stick kind, like in handwriting) dropped into a humanist sans where everything else is double-decker; an 'a' built entirely out of seven-segment-display segments for a retro calculator font; and an ornate blackletter 'a' that a designer snuck into an otherwise geometric sans because she thought it looked cool. At least one of these should be a case where the procedure gives a verdict I won't enjoy defending to the designer.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- the one distinction the whole procedure rests on -- then give the reasoning, no preamble, no restating my situation back at me.
