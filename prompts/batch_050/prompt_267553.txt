For a reading group on philosophy of social science I have to write down where I stand on laws of history, and I'd like an audit before I get torn apart. Roughly, I hold:

1. There are no laws of history: no lawlike sequence of stages every society must pass through, and no inevitable endpoint or trajectory. Historicism in that sense is dead.
2. But there are real lawlike generalizations in social science, e.g. supply and demand, or Duverger's law (single-member-district plurality voting pushes party systems toward two parties).
3. Piecemeal social engineering is the right method: reform institutions incrementally, treat policies as experiments, expect unintended consequences.
4. Long-run prediction of human affairs is impossible in principle: the future depends on the growth of knowledge, and you can't rationally predict the content of future discoveries without already making them.
5. Nonetheless, probabilistic forecasts decades out (AI capabilities, fertility rates, great-power conflict) can be well-calibrated and are worth paying for — good forecasters really do beat chance.
6. Cliodynamics-style work (Turchin and co. claiming agrarian states recurrantly go through multi-generational "secular cycles" of population pressure, elite overproduction, and instability, and that this applies to us right now) is genuine science, not numerology.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Go through the pairs that matter, rank any real tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give. Then tell me the single cheapest revision that makes the set consistent and what it costs. Don't just flag everything — I'd like to keep as much of this as I can.
