I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I keep running into the same fight in the edit queue. A researcher tells me her work shows that the brain predicts the world rather than just reacting to it, or that a cell decides whether to divide, or that the immune system remembers a virus from years ago, and my editors want to know wether that's a discovery or just a cute way of talking. Last week I quoted a cognitive scientist saying a large language model understands grammar, and a philosopher friend told me over too much wine that Gilbert Ryle sorted all this out back in 1949 and half of science journalism is one long category mistake. He couldn't actually tell me how to spot one in the wild, though, which is the part I need.

So: write me a procedure I can run, in maybe fifteen minutes per story and without a philosophy degree, for deciding when a sentence that attributes something mind-ish (knowing, wanting, deciding, remembering, representing) to a brain, a cell, a gene, a market, an algorithm, or evolution itself is a genuine category mistake in something like Ryle's sense, when it's harmless shorthand the reader will unpack correctly, and when it's a substantive empirical hypothesis that just happens to be stated in mental vocabulary and is fine to keep. Ryle's own moves were things like treating the university as one more building after touring all the colleges, and the ghost in the machine as a doubled picture of the person plus an inner little person doing the real thinking. I need that translated into ordered tests -- questions to ask the source, checks against what the reader will take away -- and it should say explicitly at which steps I'm making a judgment call dressed up as procedure.

Then apply the procedure to three cases on my desk this week: a feature on the immune system learning and remembering pathogens; an op-ed claiming that predictive-processing research shows the brain is a prediction machine (I suspect your procedure is going to tell me something I won't like about this one, and it's my best story, so be straight with me); and a short item saying an image-generation model hallucinates details. Finish by saying what the procedure can't settle, where I still just have to decide as an editor, and what I'd need to get a researcher to tell me before the procedure can even start.
