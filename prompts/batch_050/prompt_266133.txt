A colleague of mine in university administration has written a memo arguing that our faculty's appointment committee should stop voting on criteria and instead either delegate hiring decisions to the chair alone or hold a single unreasoned yes/no poll on each candidate. Her argument runs as follows, and I find the conclusion absurd but cannot locate the error. The committee assesses candidates under a standing rule: tenure is granted iff the candidate's research is excellent (p) and the candidate's teaching is excellent (q).

(1) If the committee votes criterion by criterion and by majority, it can collectively endorse p, collectively endorse q, and collectively deny that the candidate merits tenure — as happens with a three-member committee where the first member judges both criteria met, the second judges research excellent and teaching not, and the third judges teaching excellent and research not. Each member is perfectly consistent; the committee's collective position is not.

(2) A body whose collective judgments are inconsistent is not merely fallible: under its own stated rule it can be forced to both grant and deny the same tenure case.

(3) The construction does not depend on this particular profile. For any committee of three or more members deciding via two or more criteria, some distribution of individually consistent judgments produces the same result. So criterion-wise majority voting is unreliable in principle, not just unlucky here.

(4) A committee cannot refuse admission to members on grounds of the pattern their views take — that would punish people for consistency itself. And it cannot merge the criteria into one question without ceasing to be a reasons-based procedure and becoming a gut poll. So the only remaining fix is to give up majority: let one designated member's judgments on the criteria stand in for the committee's.

(5) Therefore every multi-criterion body — appointments panels, parole boards, prize juries — faces a forced choice between dictatorship and a standing risk of self-contradiction, and since self-contradiction in a tenure decision is intolerable, such bodies should be abolished in favor of single-person decisions or bare outcome polls.

Each step looks locally defensible, yet the conclusion would dissolve most of the institutions I work in. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Identify the specific step you would reject, explain why it appears sound, and state what rejecting it costs. I would most like to keep (1) and (3), since they seem to be established results rather than contestable premises. If your diagnosis requires giving one of them up, say so explicitly. Maximum 400 words.
