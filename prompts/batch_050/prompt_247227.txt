Exam question: Using Capgras delusion as your primary example, explain the two-factor theory of delusion formation and how it improves on purely monothematic, one-factor accounts. Then assess what the theory implies, if anything, for the question of whether delusions are beliefs.

Student answer:

In Capgras delusion, patients insist that someone close to them, typically a spouse or parent, has been replaced by an impostor who merely looks identical. The two-factor theory holds that two distinct deficits are needed to produce such a delusion. The first factor is a neuropsychological anomaly that generates the raw material for the delusion: in Capgras, a disconnection between facial recognition areas and the autonomic nervous system means the patient sees a familiar face but experiences none of the normal affective response, producing the strange sense that this person is not who they appear to be. The second factor is a deficit in belief evaluation, associated with damage to the left prefrontal cortex, which prevents the patient from rejecting the bizarre hypothesis the first factor makes available. A one-factor account, such as Maher's, says the delusion is a rational response to the anomalous experience alone; the two-factor theory improves on this because it explains why patients with the same affective anomaly but intact belief evaluation (for example, some patients with damage limited to the recognition-affect pathway) do not become deluded.

As for whether delusions are beliefs, the answer is no. A belief, by definition, is a state that is sensitive to evidence and that guides action across contexts. Capgras patients do neither: they maintain the impostor claim in the face of overwhelming counter-evidence, and they rarely act on it in the ways one would expect if they genuinely believed a spouse had been replaced (they do not, for instance, file police reports or search for the real spouse). Currie's account, which treats delusions as imaginings misidentified as beliefs, is simply the one-factor theory dressed up in new vocabulary, since it too says the anomalous experience exhausts the explanation. So the two-factor theory actually shows that delusions are not beliefs but a different kind of state produced by damaged evaluation machinery.
