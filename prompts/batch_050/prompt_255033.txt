So I work at a regional transport authority and lately I've been bothered by how we cost out safety projects, and I want to ask about what our whole approach is implicitly committed to philosophically.

Here's the setup. Whenever we evaluate a safety measure -- guardrails on a stretch of highway, a pedestrian overpass, better lighting at crossings -- we have to put a dollar figure on the lives it would save. The way that's done is via the "value of a statistical life," and the numbers we inherit come from labor-market studies: economists look at how much extra pay workers demand to take riskier jobs (roofing vs. cashiering, say), treat that wage premium as the price the workers themselves put on the extra risk, and extrapolate. So if workers on average take $500 extra per year to accept a 1-in-10,000 increase in annual fatal risk, a statistical life comes out at $5 million, and that's what a saved life counts as in our cost-benefit math. Nobody here actually thinks this is controversial, it's just standard practice.

My questions:

1. What does this practice presuppose about the relationship between choice and welfare? It seems to assume that what people choose (given a menu of jobs and wages) reveals how much they value their own safety, and that this revealed valuation is the right input for policy. Spell out the strongest version of that assumption. Does it also presuppose that preferences are well-informed, stable, and formed without coercion or desperate circumstances? Because the roofer who's priced his life at a discount because he's broke is presumably doing the same math as everyone else.

2. Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out? E.g., if you thought welfare consists in something besides preference satisfaction (a list of objective goods, say), would you be committed to scrapping the VSL approach, or can you keep it on other grounds?

3. Here's the apparent inconsistency that got me thinking: the same authority that prices risk from people's choices also overrrides those choices all the time -- seatbelt laws, helmet laws, banning riding in truck beds. When we mandate these, we're saying people are wrong about their own risk acceptance, and we don't respect the revealed valuation at all. Can a single coherent view of preference and welfare underwrite both practices? Or do the two halves of what we do presuppose contradictory theories, with paternalism here and deference to choice there?

4. If we concluded that choices don't reliably reveal valuations -- people misperceive small risks, anchor on whatever wages exist, adjust their preferences to what's available -- what would we actually have to change about how we evaluate safety projects? Is there a workable alternative that doesn't just smuggle the same assumptions back in?

Try to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, since I suspect it's underdetermined in places and several different theories might license the same spreadsheet.
