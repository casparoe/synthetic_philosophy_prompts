I teach a second-year undergraduate ethics course, about 24 students, one 80-minute session on the ethics of torture. The readings have covered the ticking time bomb case, and my students uniformly report that they find the hypothetical easy to resolve and therefore find absolutist prohibitions on torture unserious -- which tells me they have not felt the actual force of the debate. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel two things in sequence: first, the genuine pull of the ticking time bomb scenario when a choice has to be made under time pressure rather than discussed; second, the force of the objection that the hypothetical smuggles in a certainty about the facts (that the right person is in custody, that the information exists, that torture will extract it) that real interrogations never possess. The point should emerge from what happens in the room -- hidden information, roles with conflicting incentives, decisions that cannot be revised -- not from my lecturing afterwards, and the second phase should make them re-examine what they actually did in the first phase, not just be told the hypothetical was unrealistic. I need concrete, runnable detail: the setup and materials, the roles and what each role privately knows, the rules and a minute-by-minute timeline, and how to force real choices without real stakes. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical moves (the consequentialist concession, the absolutist reply, the proposal that torture could be legalized via warrants, and the claim that the case for torture in the hypothetical does no work for a practice of torture), plus an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run and which feature of the debate the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in discussion.

Structure your response under these headings, in this order: Overview and intended lesson (about 100 words), Setup and materials (about 150 words), Roles and private information (about 200 words), Rules and timeline (about 250 words), Debrief plan (about 250 words), Predictable failures (about 150 words), and What the activity cannot reproduce (about 100 words).
