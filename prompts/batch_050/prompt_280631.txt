ok so i'm writing a chapter on karma for a pop-philosophy book (dont judge, it pays) and literally every intro source i read explains it with the same analogy: karma is a cosmic bank account. good deeds deposit merit, bad deeds run up a debt, rebirth is when the bill comes due, and you can even transfer merit to dead relatives like wiring money. my editor loves it, and honestly it's doing real work in the chapter: it's how i set up my two big claims, first that good actions can offset or dilute bad ones (that's the whole logic of merit-making), and second that buddhist ethics is basically consequentialism in robes -- the point of practice is to produce the best karmic outcomes, and moral value is fungible enough to be tallied and moved around.

then, in a fit of diligence, i read an actual sutta instead of books about suttas. the Buddha, in the Loṇaphala Sutta -- the salt crystal sutta, AN 3.99 in Thanissaro Bhikkhu's translation:

"Monks, for anyone who says, 'In whatever way a person makes kamma, that is how it is experienced,' there is no living of the holy life, there is no opportunity for the right ending of stress. But for anyone who says, 'When a person makes kamma to be felt in such & such a way, that is how its result is experienced,' there is the living of the holy life, there is the opportunity for the right ending of stress.

"There is the case where a trifling evil deed done by a certain individual takes him to hell. There is the case where the very same sort of trifling deed done by another individual is experienced in the here & now, and for the most part barely appears for a moment.

"Now, a trifling evil deed done by what sort of individual takes him to hell? There is the case where a certain individual is undeveloped in [contemplating] the body, undeveloped in virtue, undeveloped in mind, undeveloped in discernment: restricted, small-hearted, dwelling with suffering. A trifling evil deed done by this sort of individual takes him to hell."

...which seems to wreck my analogy? a bank debt is a bank debt no matter how rich you are, but here the same deed has wildly different consequences depending on the state of the person who did it. so before i build a whole chapter on the ledger picture, audit it for me. give me the explicit mapping first -- what plays the role of deposits, debts, interest, transfers, the final reckoning -- then where the mapping breaks. what is the ledger quietly importing that the doctrine lacks: that karmic debts are fixed quantities independent of the debtor, that deeds are commensurable, that merit is fungible, that some bookkeeper keeps the accounts? the sutta's own salt-in-water image suggests something more like dilution or conditioning than accounting, so what's the better analogy here -- a seed whose growth depends on the soil? seasoning in a dish? training effects? or is the ledger actually fine and i'm overreading one passage?

and the part i actually care about: my chapter's two conclusions were (1) good deeds can offset bad ones, hence merit-making and merit transfer make sense, and (2) buddhist ethics is consequentialist at bottom. does either of those survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the bank account carrying them the whole time? note the sutta cuts weirdly on (1) -- it seems to say development dilutes bad results, which sort of supports offsetting but by totally the wrong mechanism, and i can't tell if that's a rescue or a worse problem.
