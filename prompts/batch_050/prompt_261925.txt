At a dinner last week, two friends of mine spent the better part of an hour arguing about a third friend's exercise arrangement, and I've been unable to settle it. Some background: our friend Mara pays into a commitment service that automatically donates fifty dollars to a political organization she despises whenever she misses a scheduled gym session. She can cancel any session without penalty, but only up to 48 hours in advance.

Daniel argued the arrangement is straightforwardly irrational. If Mara freely chooses, on the day, to skip the gym, that choice reflects her judgment at the time, made with all the information then available; overriding it with a financial penalty amounts to letting her past self coerce her better-informed present self. Either her future judgment is trustworthy, in which case the device is unnecessary, or it isn't, in which case she has a deeper problem that a fine cannot fix.

Priya countered that this ignores what is known about motivation over time. Mara's long-run preferences are stable -- she genuinely wants to be someone who exercises -- but her weighting of costs and rewards is distorted near the present, so that on the morning of a session the immediate comfort looms far larger than distant health. Her discounting is, roughly, hyperbolic: a preference reversal is built in rather than being a response to new information. Precommitment therefore does not suppress better judgment; it corrects a predictable bias, as when Odysseus had himself bound to the mast before the sirens were in earshot.

Daniel replied that a psychological finding about discounting cannot by itself justify coercing one's future self: from the fact that people discount hyperbolically it does not follow that the far-sighted self's values are the authoritative ones. He also pressed a practical point. The device cannot distinguish weakness of will from good reasons -- a fever, a family emergency, a well-earned rest day all trigger the same donation. A mechanism that punishes rational responsiveness along with genuine weakness is a blunt instrument, and Mara would be better served by cultivating judgment than by paying to bypass it.

Priya answered that the 48-hour cancellation clause meets this objection exactly. Genuine reasons almost always announce themselves more than two days out, whereas temptation characteristically strikes at the last moment. The delay is calibrated so that the very asymmetry between near and far rewards that causes the problem is turned against the temptation. The device does not bypass judgment; it filters judgment by its timing.

Daniel's last move was that this concedes his point. If the cancellation clause works as advertised, then the device does not truly bind -- Mara retains full rational override -- and all the motivational work is done by her standing policy of cancelling only with notice. The fifty dollars is window dressing on a resolution she could simply adopt. Priya responded that this is like calling a lock superfluous because anyone could choose not to open the door: the entire question is what sustains the choice at the moment it wavers.

Please adjudicate. Go through each move in order and say whether it succeeds, fails, or succeeds only in part, with your reasons. Then give a definite verdict on who is more right overall about the rationality of Mara's arrangement, and identify anything both parties are missing. I would rather you commit to a position and defend it than survey the possibilities and hand the decision back to me.
