In my epistemology seminar this term we've been discussing whether knowledge requires a margin for error — roughly, whether you can know something in a situation where you could very easily have been wrong about it, differing from your actual situation in some respect too small for you to detect. I assumed this was a technical sideshow until it became oddly personal. I've been getting migraines lately, and my neurologist asked me to keep a log rating the pain every half hour as it fades after I take my medication. Sitting there at noon trying to decide whether I was still in a little pain or merely remembering the morning's pain, it occurred to me that the margin-for-error question was the only thing standing between me and an answer. I mentioned this to a friend from the seminar, and he presented me with an argument that I now can't stop thinking about, because it seems to prove something I find literally incredible: that I can be in pain without being in any position to know that I am.

Here is his argument, as best I can reconstruct it. (1) If at some moment I know that I am in pain, then my belief that I am in pain could not easily have been mistaken; so at any moment close enough to the present one that I cannot discriminate the difference in how things feel, I must still actually be in pain — otherwise my belief would have been wrong in a case I couldn't tell apart from this one, and it wouldn't count as knowledge. (2) Whenever I am in pain, I am in a position to know that I am in pain. (3) A fading migraine subsides by imperceptible degrees: for any two moments one second apart, the felt intensity is indiscriminable to me. (4) From (2), whenever I'm in pain at a moment, I know I'm in pain then; and from (1) and (3), knowing at that moment requires still being in pain one second later; so whenever I'm in pain at a moment, I'm in pain a second later. (5) Chaining (4) across the morning: if I was in pain at 9 a.m., I was in pain at noon. (6) But at noon I was plainly not in pain. Contradiction. My friend concludes that (2) has to go, since (1) and (3) look non-negotiable. Worse, he says, the construction generalizes: it works for any mental condition that can fade gradually, and applied to knowledge itself — using the principle that if I know something, I know that I know it — it threatens to show that even my own knowledge is never transparent to me, so that nothing about my own mind is luminous at all.

I refuse to accept that I can be in pain while being in no position to know it; if anything is epistemically privileged, my own suffering is. But (1) also seems to me independently motivated, since without some margin requirement it seems I could "know" my lottery ticket will lose just from the odds, or know the barn is real when the countryside is full of façades. And (3) is just a fact about how migraines fade. So: which step should I reject, and what makes that step look so innocent when it isn't — or, if the argument is valid after all, which premise is the cheapest to abandon? If forced, I would rather give up the margin-for-error condition on knowledge than the accessibility of my own pain, but I suspect that choice costs more than I realize, and I want the price itemized.
