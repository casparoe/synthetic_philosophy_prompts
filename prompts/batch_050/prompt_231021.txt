Two friends of mine, both musicians, fell out over something that started as small talk, and I've been asked to settle it. We were listening to a much-praised new album, and Maya remarked that hearing a record is simply hearing a performance across a distance in time — like reading a letter rather than having a conversation, she said, but still the same act of communication. Callum objected that this can't be right for modern records: the lead vocal on the track we were playing was comped together from dozens of takes, the drums had been replaced with samples after the fact, and the pitch had been corrected, so the performance Maya claimed to be hearing never actually occurred; what she was hearing was an artefact assembled in a studio, and calling it a performance was a mistake about what recordings are. Maya replied that whether any such performance occurred is beside the point: the musical work is a repeatable structure, not an event, and anything that faithfully instantiates that structure — human throat or studio assembly line — delivers the work just as well, so comping is no different in principle from an orchestra whose first violins happened to record their part on a different day from the brass. Callum said this proved too much: on her view a track generated entirely by a computer executing a score no human ever played would count as a performance too, which he took to be a reductio, since a performance surely requires a performer doing something on some occasion. At that point they turned to me. I'd like a move-by-move adjudication: for each of the four moves, say whether it succeeds, fails, or trades on an equivocation (I suspect work, performance, and recording are being run together somewhere), and finish by saying what both of them are missing — if anything — and who is closer to the truth overall.
