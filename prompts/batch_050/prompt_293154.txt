Next term I'm running a one-off ninety-minute session for our university's philosophy of physics reading group — a mix of third-year undergraduates, roughly half philosophers and half physicists. Most of them know the second law of thermodynamics only as the slogan that entropy never decreases, and none of them have seen it seriously questioned from within physics itself. As the set text I've assigned the closing chapter of Maxwell's Theory of Heat (first published in 1871): not the famous description of the creature sorting fast and slow molecules through a trapdoor, which several of them will already have met in popular books, but the less-discussed passage immediately following it, where Maxwell draws his moral. There he says, in effect, that the second law is continually being violated, and to a considerable extent, within any sufficiently small group of molecules in a real body, and that it becomes ever more reliable only as the number of molecules under consideration grows — in other words, that the law is statistical through and through rather than a hard exceptionless constraint.

I don't want you to explain Maxwell's demon to me; I want a plan for how I should run the session. Specifically: what ideas have to be in place before Maxwell's claim about continual small-scale violation can even be heard correctly — I'm fairly sure most of the group will read it as the second law being refuted, or as a quaint Victorian doubt we've since put right, and I want the sequence designed so that neither reading is available to them by the time we reach the passage — and in what order those pieces should go, with a note on what depends on what. I'd also like a decision on the one concrete setup to build the whole discussion around: my instinct is Maxwell's original two-chamber trapdoor rather than Szilard's later one-molecule engine, since the trapdoor keeps the statistical point in view, but push back if you think the Szilard version carries the modern resolution better.

Please also give me the misconception map: the errors this particular audience will predictably bring or form, and the exact point in the session where each should be headed off. The ones I'm anticipating are that entropy means disorder; that the demon's intelligence or aliveness is the crux (the philosophers especially will want this to be about mind); that the modern answer is that observing or measuring the molecules is what costs energy, which I understand to be at best contested; and on the physicists' side, a tendency to treat the whole thing as settled by computation and thereby miss what Maxwell himself had already seen. Then suggest one question I can pose in the last ten minutes whose good answers would show that the group has understood the second law as statistical — rather than merely learned a refutation of the demon or a slogan about information being physical — and tell me what I should openly bracket at this level (I assume quantum versions of the demon and fluctuation theorems, but tell me what else, and whether the formal statement of Landauer's principle belongs on the list).
