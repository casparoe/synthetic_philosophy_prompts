In my ethics seminar, the debate over Singer's drowning child argument has been stuck all term in a way I find genuinely puzzling. Everyone accepts that you must rescue the child at the cost of your shoes; everyone also concedes the standard disanalogies with global poverty (the rescue is one-off rather than recurring, the aid's effectiveness is uncertain rather than immediate, the needy party is a stranger in no special relationship to you rather than someone in front of you). Yet the defenders and the critics of the demandingness conclusion haven't moved at all: defenders keep insisting the disanalogies are morally irrelevant, critics keep treating the conclusion as a reductio. My hypothesis is that the sides aren't really disagreeing about the argument but about method -- one side treats intuitions about the pond case as data to be generalized, the other treats the counterintuitive conclusion as evidence that the principle generating it must be wrong. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a substantive disagreement underneath -- about what triggers a duty of rescue, or about whether the cost to the agent is doing moral work the argument officially disavows? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a sharper analysis of which features of rescue cases are morally load-bearing, experimental work on how people actually respond to the analogies, or something else -- and is there a real chance the impasse reflects a bedrock clash neither side can argue past?
