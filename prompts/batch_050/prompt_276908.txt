I co-host a small podcast where we interview philosophers and bioethicists, mostly listened to by grad students and a few professors. Next month we're recording with a bioethicist who defends moral bioenhancement in the Persson and Savulescu tradition -- roughly, the view that we've become capable of causing catastrophic harm (climate change, engineered pathogens, WMDs falling into the wrong hands) faster than our moral psychology has evolved to handle it, and that since traditional moral education and institutions are too slow, we should seriously research and possibly even pursue biomedical means of improving our moral dispositions, like our sense of justice or our willingness to cooperate with strangers and future generations. She's also signed on to the companion claim that funding cognitive enhancement alone is actively dangerous, because it gives people more power without better motivation to use it well.

I don't want to spend the whole hour letting her restate the elevator pitch, which she can do in her sleep. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that actually press on the view? I'd like at least one question each on: (a) the empirical thinness of the actual candidate interventions -- oxytocin, SSRIs, whatever -- and in particular the finding people always bring up that oxytocin seems to increase in-group favoritism, which might make intergroup conflict worse not better; (b) the freedom worry, i.e. that a state-administered moral enhancement program is coercive and bypasses the very autonomy morality is supposed to respect; (c) the circularity problem of who gets to decide which dispositions count as "moral" enough to enhance, given that people disagree deeply about morality itself; and (d) whether pharmacologically induced niceness even counts as moral improvement, or whether being moral requires responding to reasons rather than chemicals.

For each question, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not caught flat-footed when she deploys her standard move. I've been preping by reading some of the critical responses (Harris's stuff on freedom especially) and I want the interview to feel like a real engagement, not a promo spot. Maybe end the sequence with something concrete -- like what she'd actually say to the first parents offered a licensed empathy-boosting drug for their kid -- rather than another abstract objection.
