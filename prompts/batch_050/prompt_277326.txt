A friend of mine doing a philosophy masters leans hard on Wittgenstein whenever we argue about stuff like alternative medicine or religious claims. His move: language works like chess. Each practice is a game with its own internal rules, and you can't call a chess move illegal using poker's rulebook -- so there's no neutral standpoint from which to criticize what another community says. The homeopath's talk of "energy" is fine within the homeopathy game, the theologian's talk of grace is fine within that game, and scientists complaining are just playing a different game and confusing the scoreboards. He uses this to conclude that external criticism of any discourse is basically a category error.

Can you audit the analogy? Give me the correspondence table first -- what plays the role of the pieces, the legal moves, the rulebook, winning, the players, the arbiter. Then the places it breaks. Some things I'm suspicious about but can't fully articulate: chess rules are explicit, written down, and agreed before play, whereas grammatical norms seem implicit, contested, and porous -- does that matter? Games seem insulated from each other but discourses interpenetrate and borrow vocabularly all the time. Chess moves don't claim anything about the world, but assertions do -- does the analogy just dissolve once truth enters? And is there a tension in the analogy's own source, since Wittgenstein also used games as his stock example of a family resemblance concept with no single defining feature, which seems to sit oddly with the "every game has a fixed rulebook" reading? Finally: say whether my friend's no-external-criticism conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the whole argument -- and if the relativism fails, what weaker claim about language-games it might still legitimately support.
