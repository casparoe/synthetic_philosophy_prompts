I help run philosophy club at my high school, and next week I somehow volunteered to do a 25-minute mini-lesson on the identity of indiscernibles and Black’s two spheres. Most people there are smart but haven’t done any formal philosophy, and their only exposure to Leibniz is probably the vague slogan that no two things are exactly alike. I definately don’t want you to just explain the argument to me; I want a plan for how I should teach it. What ideas need to be in place first, especially numerical vs. qualitative identity and what counts as a property, before the two-sphere world even makes sense? Please choose one carrying example to introduce those pieces with and say what it must include and avoid, because I suspect identical twins or two same-model phones will make everyone think this is just about differences that are really hard to notice.

Then map out the order of the lesson: when to introduce the perfectly symmetrical universe containing only the two spheres, when to bring up distance and relational properties, and how to keep students from cheating by secretly giving one sphere a location relative to us or saying this one is on the left. What misconceptions should I expect, in roughly the order they’ll occur, and where exactly should I head each one off? I’m guessing people will mix up being unable to tell the spheres apart with there actually being only one sphere, assume naming them A and B already makes them different, or treat the whole thing as a physics claim about whether real atoms can be identical. Include a final question or mini-scenario I can use to check whether they understand the challenge to the principle rather than just remembering the weird sphere picture, plus what I should deliberately leave out at this level and openly admit we’re leaving out. Keep it usable for actual teenagers, not a university seminar, and don’t give me readings or citations.
