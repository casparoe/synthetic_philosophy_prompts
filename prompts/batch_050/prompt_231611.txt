I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm writing a column about a phrase that resurfaced during a recent political controversy. A public figure, pressed on his continued support for his country's conduct in an unpopular military campaign, answered that for him it was a matter of my country, right or wrong. Readers wrote in both to cheer and to object, and the striking thing is that the two sides plainly heard different sentences.

I'd like you to take that slogan apart for me. Lay out the distinct, precise claims it could be making. Some candidates that occur to me: that loyalty to one's country is unconditional in the way family loyalty is sometimes taken to be; that allegiance is compatible with — or even requires — working to correct the country when it goes wrong, so that the slogan expresses steadfastness rather than endorsement; that the partiality we owe compatriots permits or obliges us to back national policies we privately judge to be wrong; that the word country picks out the people or the nation rather than the state or the government of the day, with different moral consequences depending on which; that the speaker is claiming standing to remain engaged rather than approving what is done in his name. Add whatever further readings you think the sentence genuinely supports, and discard any of mine that you think collapse into one another.

For each reading, I'd like a verdict: is it a platitude that no one serious denies, a substantive but defensible position, or something straightforwardly objectionable — and what does it imply for the standing philosophical question of whether patriotism is a virtue, a permissible form of partiality, or a vice? Then tell me which readings could actually bear the weight the speaker placed on the slogan, namely continuing to support a policy he concedes may be wrong, and treating dissent from that policy as a lapse of loyalty. If none of the defensible readings gets him there, say so plainly, and say which defensible reading his sympathetic listeners were probably hearing instead — because my suspicion is that the phrase survives in public life precisely by trading between a strong reading that does the argumentative work and a mild reading that does the reputational cover.

Structure the answer as a numbered list of readings, each followed by a short verdict and its bearing on the virtue-or-vice question, since I plan to adapt it directly for the column.
