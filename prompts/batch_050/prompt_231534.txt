I'm a literary novelist, and I've been invited to contribute an essay to a magazine symposium on storytelling across media. The prompt the editors gave the contributors is the now-familiar claim that games are distinctive among art forms because the player is a co-author of the story. A friend of mine who designs narrative games says this constantly, and the more I hear it, the more I suspect it's a lazy compliment that flatters players while obscuring where the artistry actually is. My own instinct, from years of controlling every word a reader encounters, is that what a player does inside a game is much closer to what a musician does with a score than to what I do at my desk: the player performs the work, sometimes brilliantly, sometimes badly, but the composer wrote the piece. The composer, in this case, being the designer who built the possibility space, decided which actions are available, and set the costs and consequences of each.

Please write the essay's argumentative core for me: a sustained case that the player's role in a game is best understood as performance or interpretation within constraints, not co-authorship, and that the aesthetics of agency are therefore performance aesthetics rather than authorship aesthetics. I'd like the argument to do real work, not just assert the analogy. It should explain what would have to be true for genuine co-authorship (does the player's contribution need to survive the playthrough as an artifact? does it need to be visible to an audience? does branching-path design, where the player selects among prewritten outcomes, count as authorship at all?), and it should say where authorial credit actually goes on this view: I want the conclusion that the designer's art lies in shaping the space of possible performances so that even a clumsy player produces something aesthetically structured, which strikes me as a harder and more interesting achievement than writing fixed scenes. It should also face the strongest counterexample head-on: so-called emergent narrative, where the interesting story arises from systems interacting rather than from anything scripted, and where the co-authorship claim looks most plausible.

Two requests about how the argument is conducted. First, write it in American English and in a register suitable for a general-interest magazine: positions and distinctions may be named and used, but don't cite specific theorists or works, since I'll be adding any references myself. Second, and this matters to me: if you think my premise is wrong -- if the co-authorship picture is actually more defensible than the performance picture, or if the dichotomy itself is confused and both sides are talking past a third thing -- say so plainly and argue for that instead, rather than politely building a case on a foundation you consider rotten. I'd rather have my instinct corrected than flattered.
