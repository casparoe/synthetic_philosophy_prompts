I'm taking a philosophy of language course, and before I commit to a term paper comparing accounts of how slurs mean what they mean, I want the specification first: what any adequate theory of slur meaning must deliver, independent of which camp it belongs to.

Write the desiderata list. For each requirement, give the phenomenon or case that motivates it, and mark whether it's genuinely non-negotiable or a preference of a particular approach. Please make sure the list speaks to at least the following, and add anything I've missed:

- Derogation under embedding: "there are no [slur] here" still derogates, so the offense doesn't behave like ordinary at-issue content -- but it also doesn't project under conditionals and questions quite the way presuppositions do. Whatever a theory says slur-content is has to predict this profile, not just gesture at it.
- On-the-spot coinage: a speaker can invent a novel slur for a group in a single utterance and it derogates immediately, with no history or convention behind it. Accounts that ground offensiveness in institutional history have to explain this.
- Reclamation and appropriation: the same word, with the same grammar, can be felicitous and non-derogating in in-group mouths while remaining derogating in out-group mouths. A theory has to say where this variation lives -- in the semantics, the pragmatics, the register, or the speech act -- without making the derogating uses mysterious.
- Empty targets: a slur coined for a group that doesn't exist (invent one) still functions as a slur. What does that do to extension-based accounts?
- Indirect report: repeating a slur inside a "she said that..." report can itself offend, even when the reporter disavows it. How does that constrain use-mention distinctions?
- Gradability: slurs vary in strength, and taboo seems to come apart from derogation (a word can be taboo without targeting anyone). The theory must not collapse these.

Then: flag any pair of desiderata you think cannot be jointly satisfied and say why -- if the list itself is incoherent, that's a result I want. Identify the single requirement that excludes the most existing contenders. Finally, one short paragraph scoring expressivist, presuppositional, prohibitionist, and truth-conditional-content accounts against the list, and a note on whether the adequacy standard shifts if the goal is explaining competent speakers' judgments versus grounding policy on institutional speech.
