I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm applying for a year-long reporting fellowship that requires a two-page research proposal. The project I have in mind concerns the so-called gender-equality paradox: the finding that countries ranking highest on standard measures of gender equality often show more, not less, gender-typical patterns of career choice — fewer women in engineering, more in care work — than less egalitarian countries do. My editors and I keep circling the same impasse. One camp reads the data as the lifting of constraints: in wealthy, secure, egalitarian societies, people are finally free to follow their inclinations, so the divergence reveals something about underlying dispositions. The other camp reads it as norms mutating rather than dissolving: where overt hierarchy becomes illegitimate, gender gets re-expressed through the language of personal choice and self-discovery, so the divergence reveals something about culture's adaptability. Both readings fit the same tables, and the popular coverage (including, I'm embarrassed to say, some of mine) just picks one.

My half-formed idea for getting past this: both camps assume occupational choice is a single measurement that either nature or culture shows up in, but the statistics may be lumping together at least two things that could come apart — the gender composition of the careers people end up in, and the considerations people cite in choosing them (intrinsic interest, pay, security, caregiving compatibility, identity expression). It's conceivable, for instance, that egalitarian countries show more gender convergence in what people do while showing more gendered language in why they say they do it, or the reverse; and if so, the 'paradox' might be partly an artifact of which layer a given metric touches. Turn this into a proposal: a precisely stated question that has a determinate answer; an account of why the two standard readings, as usually framed, cannot settle it without this kind of decomposition; a month-by-month plan of what I would actually do as a journalist rather than a lab scientist (which existing cross-national comparisons to re-examine, whom to interview, what a decisive interview or dataset would look like); and a clear statement of what would count as the project failing — including the possibility that the decomposition dissolves the paradox and leaves me with no story.

Please also address one thing I expect the fellowship committee to press on: what follows for justice, not just for explanation. If the 'freed preferences' reading survives, does anything follow about whether remaining gender gaps are a policy problem at all, or does the inference from 'chosen' to 'just' smuggle in a premise? And if the 'mutating norms' reading survives, does it actually support the interventions its proponents favor, or does it predict that those interventions will only push differentiation into subtler channels? I don't want a bibliography — I'll assemble citations myself from my notes — but do flag any place where the proposal rests on a contested empirical premise I should double-check. Write in American English, and keep the tone pitched at an intelligent funder who is not a social scientist.
