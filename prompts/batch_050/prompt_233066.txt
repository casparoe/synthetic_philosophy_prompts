I have been drafting a position statement for a workshop on comparative political ethics, and before I circulate it I would like a consistency audit rather than more commentary on whether my views are right. The workshop keeps circling around the same argument: some participants treat international anti-corruption norms as the straightforward application of universal morality, others treat them as Western bureaucratic standards being imposed on societies whose gift economies, kinship obligations, and patronage networks work differently and, in some places, work reasonably well. I have tried to stake out a middle position, and here is roughly where I have landed: (1) Bribery is morally wrong, not merely illegal — it corrupts the impartial exercise of entrusted power — and this wrongness is universal, holding everywhere regardless of local custom. (2) Much of what Western observers classify as corruption — gifts to officials, favors to kin, patronage dispensed to loyal clients — functions within local gift economies as an expression of obligation and solidarity, and it is ethnocentric to condemn these practices using standards developed for impersonal bureaucratic states. (3) What makes bribery wrong is not the exchange of money or favors as such, but the betrayal of an impartial role: a gift that does not purchase the misuse of an entrusted office is not corruption. (4) Large campaign contributions and disclosed, lawful lobbying in countries like the United States are legitimate forms of democratic participation, not corruption — the fact that they are legal and transparent makes them different in kind from a bribe. (5) Corruption is best defined as the abuse of entrusted power for private gain, where "private" includes family and clan, so nepotistic hiring driven by kin loyalty counts as corrupt even in societies where loyalty to kin is the dominant moral norm. (6) Anti-corruption standards should ultimately be assessed by their effects: if a society's gift and patronage networks reliably deliver predictability, services, and dispute resolution, while formally clean rules exist only on paper, then the informal system is doing better and the formal one is the real failure.

Which of these commitments are actually in tension with one another, and which only appear to be? In particular, I would like to know whether my principle in (3) can actually deliver the verdict I want in (4), whether my definition in (5) sits well with my functional standard in (6), and whether the universalism of (1) and (5) really conflicts with the cultural sensitivity of (2) or whether that apparent conflict dissolves once the principle and its local realizations are properly distinguished. Please rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious; for each, state precisely which commitments collide and why; and for each, identify the cheapest revision that would resolve it and what that revision costs me in terms of positions I would have to abandon. Finally, tell me which single change to the set would clean up the most inconsistencies at once — I am open to revising anything, but I would like to know where the leverage is before the workshop.
