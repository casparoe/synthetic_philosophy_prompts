Jack London's The Call of the Wild is narrated almost entirely from within the experience of a dog: Buck is shown learning, scheming, grieving, remembering, and finally answering an ancestral pull toward the wild. Read the novel against Descartes' doctrine of the animal-machine. In Part V of the Discourse on Method, Descartes argues that animals are automata whose behavior, however intricate, can in principle be explained by the disposition of their organs, and he proposes two marks of genuine thought that animals fail: the use of language to express ideas rather than mere impulses, and the capacity to act from reason flexibly in novel circumstances rather than from fixed mechanisms. I am writing an essay and want a reading that does justice to both the text and the philosophy.

On one side, locate where London simply commits the inference Descartes forbids, moving from Buck's outward behavior to beliefs, desires, and a rich inner life that a Cartesian would refuse to grant. On the other, more interestingly, examine where the novel cooperates with Descartes: London repeatedly explains Buck's interior through instinct, heredity, and atavistic visions of a primitive past by a human fire, which might be read as precisely the organ-disposition explanation rendered from the inside. Consider in particular Buck's calculated deception and defeat of Spitz, his devotion to Thornton and his grief and vengeance after Thornton's death, and his final integration into the wolf pack: are these episodes presented as reasoning, as feeling, or as mechanism running its course? Then press the asymmetry in Descartes: he infers minds in other humans from their speech, yet dogs plainly communicate, so ask whether Buck's howling, signaling, and responsiveness to human words come anywhere near the linguistic test, and whether Descartes' second test, flexible rational action, is failed or passed by a dog who learns to steal food covertly and to adapt his conduct to radically new demands.

Finally, address the deeper tension the pairing exposes. Descartes' tests are epistemic criteria for detecting thought in a being whose interior we cannot occupy; a novel narrated from a dog's point of view claims to occupy exactly that interior. Can fiction show what behavioral evidence cannot establish, and if so, what is the epistemic status of that showing? Or is London's narration an elaborate case of the very projection Descartes diagnoses, in which we attribute our own souls to bodies that resemble ours in motion but not in mind? Where the novel resists a Cartesian reading, say so plainly, and conclude with what, if anything, London's dramatization contributes to the problem of animal minds that Descartes' arguments alone leave unsettled.
