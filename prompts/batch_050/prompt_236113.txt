I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I am trying to settle a running dispute with two sources: a cosmologist who thinks anthropic reasoning is mostly disciplined statistics, and a philosopher who thinks it easily becomes a license for intellectual vanity. Please compare the Self-Sampling Assumption and the Self-Indication Assumption as answers to one question: when the bare indexical fact that I exist, here, as this observer-moment, is itself evidence. Use the standard contrast that SSA tells me to reason as if I were randomly sampled from the observers in some reference class, whereas SIA treats my existence as a likelihood boost for worlds containing more observers; but do not leave it at the textbook level. I want the comparison organized around what each assumption counts as the evidential unit, how badly each depends on reference-class choice, how each handles observer-moments rather than whole persons, and why the presumptuous philosopher is supposed to embarrass one side rather than merely sounding rude.

For the column I am using a homemade case, so please test both principles against it rather than reaching for familiar examples. Two hypotheses predict the same astronomy before anyone looks inside the sealed Lunar Archive. In Sparse, exactly one civilization ever exists and it builds one archive; in Crowded, a million civilizations exist and each builds one archive. Every archive contains a black box whose light will be green if that civilization is typical in its reference class and red otherwise; the catch is that a reliability daemon randomly erases ninety percent of all observer-moments after measurement but before memory consolidation, while leaving the physical lights untouched. Call this the Epistemic Shutter. Say where SSA and SIA agree, where they diverge, whether the Shutter changes anything if the sampling unit is observers, observer-moments, or only memories that survive consolidation, and whether one assumption becomes presumptuous precisely because it treats a low number of actual observers as evidence against a theory that otherwise predicted our data.

End with a verdict I can print without jargon: which assumption better deserves to be called a rule about evidence, which better deserves to be called a metaphysical preference in statistical clothing, and what minimal extra principle would have to be added to the winner to handle Doomsday-style and Adam-and-Eve-style discomforts. If the two assumptions turn out to be not rivals but answers to different questions, say exactly what those questions are, and give me one sentence for readers that preserves the disagreement without making either side sound silly.
