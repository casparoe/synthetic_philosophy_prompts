I teach a second-year undergraduate ethics seminar of 18 students, meeting for 80 minutes, and I want a runnable in-class exercise that makes Bernard Williams’s integrity objection to impartial moral theory, especially the complaint that an acceptable motive can require one thought too many, felt before it is named. Do not open with a lecture, do not use Williams’s name or vocabulary for the first 50 minutes, and do not reuse the familiar drowning-relative or coerced-executioner cases. Invent a mundane institution with plausible forms, queues, deadlines, and audit trails: participants occupy roles in which acting from a constitutive attachment to a person, promise, craft, or long project can be done immediately or can be routed through an impersonal certification step. Build the game around a token economy with no real money, a deliberative toll that makes the extra justificatory thought costly enough that participants resent it but sometimes cannot responsibly skip it, a certification queue whose rules are public, and an alienation ledger in which players record, anonymously at first, whether an outcome they produced felt like theirs. Include at least one round in which the impartially best outcome is achieved only by an action many players later describe as estranged, and one round in which refusing the impersonal step is clearly disastrous so that partiality cannot become a cheap hero pose. Specify rules, materials, room setup, facilitator script, timeline, scoring, and a debrief that maps concrete moments onto the distinction between having a sufficient reason and needing that reason to be ratified by the theory; explain how the debrief should separate integrity from selfishness, integrity from mere squeamishness, and a reason’s weight from its mode of entry into deliberation. State what will predictably go wrong the first time, including token gaming, confusion of delay with alienation, prestige effects around articulate justification, and students collapsing the point into bureaucracy is bad, and how to correct each. End with an honest account of what the activity cannot reproduce, such as the depth of lifelong projects, the difference between first-order partiality and second-order theoretical alienation, and any feature the facilitator must simply state, without relying on citations or a bibliography.
