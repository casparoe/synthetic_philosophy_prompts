I teach an upper-level philosophy of biology seminar and am writing the final exam. I want one essay question on the so-called tautology objection to natural selection, built around the following passage from Chapter III of Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859), which students will have in front of them:

"I should premise that I use the term Struggle for Existence in a large and metaphorical sense, including dependence of one being on another, and including (which is more important) not only the life of the individual, but success in leaving progeny."

The question should make students argue, not recite. Specifically, it should force them to confront the worry that Darwin's parenthetical -- "success in leaving progeny" -- builds reproductive output into the very meaning of the struggle, so that "the fitter survive" risks collapsing into "those who leave more offspring leave more offspring." A strong answer must then assess whether the propensity interpretation of fitness (fitness as a probabilistic disposition to reproductive success, not its actual achievement) dissolves the objection or merely relocates it, and must take a position on whether a theory whose central predicate is defined dispositionally can still issue falsifiable predictions. It must not be answerable by rehearsing the slogan "survival of the fittest is a tautology" plus a textbook rebuttal. Please write: (1) the exam question itself, answerable in 75 minutes; (2) a model answer of roughly 600 words; (3) a marking scheme out of 20, identifying five must-hit points, the marks each carries, and the specific mistake students most commonly make on each.
