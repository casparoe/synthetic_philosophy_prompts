I have defended, in conversation and now in a draft paper, the epistemicist view of vagueness: vague predicates draw sharp but unknowable boundaries, borderline cases are cases of ignorance rather than of semantic or worldly indeterminacy, and the sorites paradox is blocked by rejecting one of its conditionals even though we cannot in principle say which. My version of the view runs roughly along Williamson's lines: bivalence holds at every step of a sorites series, the boundary's location is fixed by speakers' use and dispositions together with the world, and our ignorance of it is explained by a margin-for-error principle governing knowledge rather than by any gap in reality.

Before the paper goes further I want a map of the view's exposure -- what would actually count against it, as opposed to what critics merely find incredible about it. Address at least the following.

First, the formal and semantic front. Would the success of a rival semantics -- a supervaluationist account that tames higher-order vagueness without ad hoc machinery, or a degree-theoretic semantics that better predicts speakers' graded judgments -- constitute evidence against epistemicism, or only an alternative alongside it? Is there any possible formal result, say about the logic of definiteness operators or about knowability principles, that would refute the view outright rather than merely raise its price?

Second, the empirical front. Suppose psycholinguistic work showed that competent speakers systematically treat borderline utterances as neither true nor false rather than as unknown, withholding assent in patterns better modeled by truth-value gaps than by uncertainty. Would that be a defeater, given that epistemicism makes claims about the extensions of predicates rather than about speakers' phenomenology? Where exactly does the view make contact with possible evidence about language use, and where is it deliberately screened off?

Third, the internal front. The margin-for-error explanation of our ignorance is the load-bearing component. What would have to be true of the metaphysics of semantic facts for that explanation to collapse -- for instance, if semantic facts turned out to be constitutively dependent on what speakers are in a position to know, or if margin-for-error principles could be shown to generate sorites-style paradoxes of their own?

Finally, assess honestly whether epistemicism has been qualified into a position nothing could dislodge: if every apparent defeater is absorbed by re-describing gap-intuitions as ignorance and the sharp boundary as in principle undetectable, is the view thereby unfalsifiable, and would that be a defect for a view of this kind or a feature any adequate account of vagueness will share? Rank the defeaters you identify from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed epistemicist would concede it as counting against the view or dismiss it as beside the point.
