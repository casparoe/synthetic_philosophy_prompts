The two-envelope problem has been stuck in my head for months, ever since someone put it to me at a dinner party: two envelopes, one containing twice as much money as the other, you pick one, and before opening it you reason that the other envelope holds either half or double what yours does, so switching seems to have positive expected value — and yet, since the same argument applies to whichever envelope you happened to pick, something has clearly gone wrong. I have no academic training, but I run a small recreational-mathematics group at my local library, and I want to present a genuinely fresh diagnosis at our next meeting rather than recycling the usual material. Please take on the role of a philosopher specializing in probability and decision theory, think carefully, and spend a moment brainstorming candidate angles before settling on the one you present.

Here are the resolutions I already know and do not need repeated: (1) the symmetry reply, according to which switching can never help because the argument works identically for either envelope, so the expected-value calculation must be mistaken somewhere even if it is hard to say where; (2) the improper-prior reply, according to which the naive calculation implicitly assumes that, conditional on seeing any amount x, the other envelope is equally likely to contain 2x or x/2, and no proper probability distribution over unbounded amounts can make that true for every x; (3) the observation that if the total sum of money available is bounded, the paradox disappears; (4) the claim that the paradox only arises for distributions with infinite expected value, making it a close relative of the St. Petersburg paradox; and (5) the complaint that the puzzle is simply underspecified because no distribution over the amounts was ever stated.

What I want is a diagnosis or resolution that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or combination of items on it. In particular, it should not work merely by bounding the amounts, by insisting on a proper prior, by appealing to symmetry, or by reducing the puzzle to infinite expectations. Present your new resolution, explain precisely which step of the naive switching argument it locates the error in (or whether it instead denies that the puzzle is a paradox at all), and then tell me honestly whether you think it actually succeeds or whether it collapses into one of the five replies above under pressure. Conclude with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a one-sentence assessment of whether your proposed resolution stands up as genuinely distinct and defensible.
