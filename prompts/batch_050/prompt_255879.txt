I'm a graduate student TAing an undergraduate introduction to decision theory this term, about 25 students, and I have a single 75-minute session for the week on expected utility and its discontents. Last year the instructor put the standard two Allais choice pairs on a slide -- the sure million versus the near-sure million with a jackpot sliver, then the two low-probability versions -- and polled the class, and it fell completely flat. Students could see that the modal answers were inconsistent with the axiom, shrugged, and moved on, because nothing was at stake and the numbers were abstract. I want to design a classroom activity that makes them actually feel the pull in both directions: first the pull of the sure thing (the regret-flavored feeling of walking away from a guaranteed prize and then watching the 1% disaster ticket come up), and then, in the second pair, the pull of the independence logic that says their earlier choice was irrational. The axiom should break in their own hands before anyone names it, and ideally the common-consequence structure should be physically staged -- I'm picturing drawing numbered balls from a bag in front of them so they can see that one chunk of the outcome space is identical across their options, and still find themselves choosing differently anyway.

Please give me a runnable activity: the concrete lottery pairs (they can be scaled-down versions with small real stakes -- I'm willing to bring candy bars and allocate a handful of participation points, so maybe 100 balls rather than abstract percentages), the exact staging order, what gets revealed when, how to handle the fact that only a few students can actually win something without the rest disengaging, and a rough timeline for the 75 minutes. Then the debrief: how to get the class to articulate the independence principle themselves from the shared-outcome structure before I put a name on it, how to map the moments of the activity onto the axiom and its violation, and -- this matters to me -- how to leave it genuinely open whether their choices were a bias to be trained away or evidence that the axiom is too strong, rather than the activity implicitly settling that question for them. I'd also like a warning about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (e.g., students reverse-engineering the point because they've heard of the paradox, probability comprehension problems, grumbling about unequal winnings) and how to handle each.

Finally, two honest notes. First, tell me which feature of the Allais situation this format can't reproduce and I'll have to supply in discussion -- my own worry is that candy-bar stakes won't elicit the real certainty-effect phenomenology, and I don't know whether that gap is fatal or just a caveat. Second, since the literature disagrees about whether Allais-type choices are rationally defensible, please flag how confident you are in each design choice and single out the one recommendation in your answer you are least sure about, so I know where to improvise if it goes sideways.
