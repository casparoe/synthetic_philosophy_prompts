Since my father's dementia diagnosis last year, our family has been circling the same question in different words: who, exactly, are we still caring for? My brother, who read philosophy at university, tried to settle it at Sunday lunch with an argument he said follows from a view he'd always found attractive -- roughly, the idea that the self is not an inner thing but a kind of fictional centre, the character around whom the story of a life is spun, sometimes called a centre of narrative gravity. I could not accept where he ended up, but when I pressed him I could not find a clean place to break in, and I'd like a proper diagnosis. His argument, as best I can reconstruct it: (1) Who a person is -- their self -- is constituted by the narrative in which they figure as the central character; there is no self over and above that story. (2) A story exists wherever it is being told; nothing about a story's existence depends on who happens to be telling it. (3) So a person's self is constituted by whatever narrative of them is currently being constructed, whether by the person themselves or by others. (4) My father can no longer construct, sustain or follow a narrative of his own life. (5) So his self is now constituted entirely by the stories the rest of us tell about him. (6) But those stories conflict, shift with the teller, and at best pick out a character in our family folklore rather than the man at the table. (7) So the self my father had no longer exists; what remains is a living body plus our stories about it. (8) Therefore there is no one left to whom our duties of care could be owed -- what we do for him is a kindness performed towards a character, not an obligation owed to a person.

Every step looked reasonable as he laid it out, and yet (8) seems to me not just unwelcome but the kind of conclusion that discredits the reasoning that produced it: if the argument is sound, then presumably anyone we stop narrating accurately -- the sleeping, the comatose, the dead we misremember -- loses their claim on us in the same way, which proves too much. I should say that I would rather keep (1) than accept (8); the narrative picture of the self strikes me as independently plausible and I do not want to abandon it just to escape my brother's conclusion. So: identify precisely which premise or transition the argument fails at, explain why that step appeared innocent -- I suspect something shifts in what "a narrative of him" means as the argument proceeds, but I cannot pin it down -- and then price the alternatives. In particular, if the defect lies in the move from (1) and (2) to (3), tell me what a defender of the narrative-self view must say about the difference between a self-told story and an other-told one, and whether that distinction survives cases like my father's; if instead (4) is the weak point, say what residual capacities would have to count as still spinning a tale. Finally, tell me whether anything in the repaired argument still threatens my father's claim on us, or whether (8) was never in the offing at all. Please answer in British English.
