After a long argument with a friend about how evidence supports scientific theories, I tried to settle the matter with the following argument, and I now suspect it proves too much. (1) Evidence E confirms hypothesis H for an agent only if the agent's coming to accept E raises her credence in H, i.e., P(H|E) > P(H). (2) Conditionalizing on E cannot raise credence in anything if the agent already assigns E probability 1, since in that case P(H|E) = P(H). (3) By the time scientists assess whether a body of evidence supports a theory, they always already know the evidence: the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion, for instance, was old data when general relativity was formulated, and in general an observation enters an assessment only after it has been recorded and accepted. (4) So for every actual assessment of a theory against evidence, the assessor's credence in the evidence is 1. (5) Therefore old evidence cannot confirm new theories; and since all evidence is old by the time anyone assesses it, no evidence ever confirms any theory. (6) Yet the perihelion data plainly did confirm general relativity, and empirical confirmation plainly happens, so the argument must go wrong somewhere. Which premise or step should I reject, and why did it look plausible in the first place? If I must choose, I would rather weaken (1) than accept (5), but I want to know what that costs — and what the main alternative repairs (for instance, relativizing confirmation to the credences I would have had without the evidence, or treating my discovery that the theory entails the evidence as itself new information) each have to give up.
