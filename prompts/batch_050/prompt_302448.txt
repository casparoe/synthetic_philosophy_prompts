A few years back I got properly sold on the extended mind thesis — the Otto-and-the-notebook thought experiment did it for me. If a notebook can literally be part of someone's memory in virtue of playing the right functional role, then the bar for what counts as part of my mind is way lower then I'd assumed. And I've kind of started living it. I keep a sprawling notes system I consult more reliably than my own recall, my phone holds most of my intentions for the day, and lately I do a big chunk of my actual deliberating in conversation with an LLM — I'll type in a half-formed worry, react to what comes back, push back, and by the end there's a view I hold that neither of us had at the start. I've caught myself saying "we figured out" about me and the model, and at this point I sort of mean it literally. My position, stated boldly: the extended mind thesis is true, and not just for quaint notebook cases — a good chunk of my day-to-day thinking is genuinely constituted by these external processes, not merely caused or scaffolded by them.

What would count against that? I know the greatest hits already: the coupling-constitution fallacy (being causally coupled to my thinking doesn't make something constitutive of it), the various Otto/Inga disanalogies (the notebook is always consulted and automatically endorsed in ways bio-memory isn't — though defenders reply that the relevant functional criteria are the coarse-grained ones), the cognitive bloat worry, and the derived-content line that the marks in my notes only mean anything courtesy of a mind that's already safely internal. So I'm more interested in what I'd find persuasive beyond those. A few specific things: what would defenders of the view themselves concede as a genuine problem, as opposed to what critics say is a problem? What does the extended view actually predict that the leaner "embedded mind" rival doesn't — is there any observation or experiment that would discriminate, or does each camp just redescribe every case in its own vocabulary? And has the thesis been qualified into unfalsifiability? Every time someone finds a disanalogy between notebook-memory and brain-memory, the defender can slide to a level of functional abstraction where the analogy holds again, and I'm no longer sure there's any finding that would count as discovering the view was wrong. If the interesting version turns out empirically empty, is that fatal, or is it fine on the grounds that the claim was always partly about how best to carve up systems for explanatory purposes rather than a bet on how the world is?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about which ones only touch the strong constitution reading while leaving some weaker causal-scaffolding reading standing (and whether that weaker reading is even worth the name "extended mind"). One more thing: if you think I've misstated the thesis, or the Otto case, or the parity principle, or that the debate is really terminological in a way that makes "what would count against it" a confused question to begin with — say that plainly instead of politely playing along. I'd rather be told the question dissolves than get a confident answer to a bad question.
