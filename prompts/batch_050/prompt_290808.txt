I have no formal training in philosophy or economics, but I run a small business and got into the habit of making uncertain decisions by weighing probabilities, and some late-night thinking about that method has led me somewhere alarming. I'll lay out my reasoning step by step, because I can't tell whether I've discovered something or walked into a trap.

Suppose you're choosing between two possible worlds to be born into, except you don't get to know which person in the world you'll be. You might be anyone, and -- this is the part that feels fairest -- you should count yourself as equally likely to end up as each person. It seemed obvious to me how to choose:

(1) If you must pick between worlds without knowing which person you'll be, and you count yourself as equally likely to be anyone, the rational pick is the world where the average person's life goes best, since that's your expected outcome.

(2) This standpoint -- everyone weighted equally, no one knowing their position -- is exactly what impartiality between people consists in; it's the one perspective that doesn't privilege anyone.

(3) So what it's rational to pick from that standpoint just is which world is genuinely better, morally speaking: the world with the higher average quality of life.

(4) But now compare two worlds. In the first, ten billion people live rich, satisfying lives. In the second, a single person lives a life of almost unimaginable bliss, and no one else ever exists. The second world has the higher average by far.

(5) So by (3), the second world is better, and if I could somehow choose which to bring about, I should choose the one blissful person over the ten billion happy ones.

(6) Worse: on the same reasoning, if some arrangement makes one person sufficiently ecstatic while everyone else suffers mildly, that can still beat a world where everyone is merely content -- the average can go up while nearly every actual life gets worse.

I find (5) and (6) absurd, yet each of (1) through (3) felt like plain common sense when I wrote it down. My suspicion is that something went wrong around (2) or (3) -- that "the fair way for me to gamble" and "the way to rank worlds morally" are quietly being treated as the same thing -- but when I try to pin down the exact slippage, it escapes me, and I've also worried that the equal-likelihood assumption in (1) is doing hidden work, since I don't actually understand what it would mean for me to have had a one-in-ten-billion chance of being someone else.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If forced to keep one thing, I'd most like to keep (1) -- expected-value reasoning about uncertain prospects still strikes me as obviously right -- but I want to know what I'm giving up if I hold onto it, and whether there's a way to keep the impartial standpoint idea in (2) while blocking the absurd conclusions, or whether the whole lottery-of-births picture has to go.
