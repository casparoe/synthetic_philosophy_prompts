My reading of the Buridan's ass thought experiment is that it establishes a constraint on rational procedure: an agent confronted with two options between which it has no preference whatsoever is rationally paralyzed, and therefore any defensible decision procedure must guarantee that a strict preference is always available. Acting on this, I have redesigned our hiring process so that ties are impossible: whenever two finalists come out exactly equal on our scoring rubric, we append further criteria until the ranking is strict. My rationale is that a selection made with no reason favoring the selected candidate would be arbitrary in the pejorative sense — unjustifiable to the rejected candidate and normatively indistinguishable from a coin flip.

A colleague tells me I have the thought experiment backwards twice over: once about what the starving animal is actually an argument for, and once about what rationality requires of an agent whose options are genuinely equal — or, harder still, neither equal nor ranked but merely "on a par," where a slight improvement to one option still fails to make it the better choice. She also claims that my tiebreaker apparatus, whatever its practical merits, does not do the philosophical work I think it does.

Who is right? If my colleague is, please explain: (i) what the thought experiment is actually a reductio of, and why the donkey's absurd fate is supposed to count against the view it targets rather than against arbitrary action; (ii) the difference between choosing on the basis of a reason and merely picking, and why the capacity to pick is a mark of rational agency rather than a defect in it; (iii) whether a pick between equals can be justified to the losing party, and in what sense such a justification differs from justifying a reasoned choice; and (iv) whether a tiebreaker policy like mine might still be defensible on grounds other than the one I offered, and what those grounds would be.
