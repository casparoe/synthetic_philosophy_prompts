I'm putting together the final exam for my upper-level undergrad seminar on rational choice theory (mix of philosophy and econ majors, they've done revealed preference, expected utility, and a unit on bounded rationality). I need one essay question on satisficing that they get 75 minutes and about 30 marks' worth of space for.

Here's the thing I want the question to probe. Every year half the class walks out thinking satisficing = "picking the first option that's good enough" and that the debate with maximizing is just about how picky you are. But they've seen enough revealed-preference machinery that a good student should be able to handle the harder point: pretty much any stop-at-good-enough behavior can be redescribed as maximizing once you fold search costs or deliberation costs into the utility function. So is satisficing actually a rival theory of rational choice, or is it a claim about something else entirely (the procedure, not the choice)? I want a question that forces them to take a stand on what the maximizing framework can and cannot absorb -- e.g., can it absorb aspiration levels that shift based on what you've seen so far? choice patterns that come out intransitive because of a stop rule? -- and what would have to be true for satisficing to be genuinely non-maximizing rather than just maximizing-with-extra-steps.

Deliverables:
1. The question itself, phrased the way it would appear on the exam paper, answerable in 75 minutes.
2. A model answer, maybe 650-750 words, pitched at what my very best student could realistically produce under time pressure (not a polished essay).
3. A marking scheme out of 30 built on five must-hit points, with the marks for each and a one-line note on the classic way students fumble each one (e.g., treating the search-cost move as a knockout blow for maximization without noticing it's basically unfalsifiable as stated).
4. A short note on what seperates an A answer (27+) from a B answer (21-24) in terms of specific moves present or absent.

One more thing: please be careful to flag which parts of this are genuinely contested among people who work on bounded rationality versus which are settled. For instance, I don't want the model answer presenting "satisficing collapses into constrained maximization" as the consensus view, because as I understand it that's exactly what's disputed. If the model answer takes a side on the disputed stuff, it should do so openly and say what the other side says. And write everything in American English please.
