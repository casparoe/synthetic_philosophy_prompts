I'm a PhD student in political theory, and my reading group just did Jaspers on German guilt, which has derailed my actual dissertation plans because I can't stop thinking about it. What got me is his four-way split between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, and how almost everyone in the contemporary debate about collective responsibility -- reparations for slavery, colonial wrongs, climate debt owed by industrialized nations, all of it -- seems to slide between things like these without noticing. My flatmate (a lawyer) insists a nation can't be guilty of anything, only people can, and that assigning guilt to people born decades after the wrong is just punishing the innocent by another name. I suspect he's conflating several things, but when I tried to push back I realized I don't actually know what a theory of collective guilt would even need to accomplish to be adequate. Everyone argues from their favorite case and calls it a day.

So before I read any more of the literature, write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of collective guilt across generations has to deliver. Which cases it must get right -- and I'd want at least: perpetrators still alive, bystanders and beneficiaries, people born after the wrong ended, and collectives that have dissolved or transformed since (does West Germany inheriting something from the Reich generalize, or is that special?). Which distinctions it can't collapse -- guilt vs. responsibility vs. liability vs. shame vs. mere benefit, and the individual/collective line itself. Which facts it has to stay consistent with -- e.g. that only agents capable of something like intention can be guilty in the criminal sense, that membership in a nation isn't chosen, that institutions persist while members turn over. Give me the consideration behind each requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable versus which are really just the prejudices of one camp (methodological individualists demanding everything reduce to persons, communitarians demanding irreducible group agency, etc.). If some subset of the desiderata looks jointly unsatisfiable -- I'm guessing something like "guilt requires agency" + "guilt can descend across generations" + "no one is guilty for what they couldn't affect" is in tension -- say so explicitly, because that result would be worth more to me than any verdict.

Also tell me which single requirement knocks out the most existing positions, and briefly -- only briefly -- how the main contenders do against the list: strict individualism, group-agent/corporate-responsibility accounts, and whatever the "shared inheritance of obligation" people are doing. Don't bother with citations or further reading; I'll dig up the sources myself. The criteria are what I actually need.
