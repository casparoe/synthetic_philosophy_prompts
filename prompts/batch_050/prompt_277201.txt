I'm preparing a short statement of my views for a reading group on transformative experience, and before I present it I want a consistency audit rather than a summary of the literature. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Rational choice just is maximizing expected value under one's current utility function; any decision procedure that recommends acting against your present values is either confused or quietly smuggling in different values.

(2) First-time parenthood is, at least approximately, a transformative experience in L. A. Paul's sense: you cannot know in advance what it will be like, and it predictably restructures your core values and priorities.

(3) Whether to have a child is among the most consequential choices a person faces, and it ought to be made rationally and autonomously -- not left to drift, social pressure, or whichever way one's gut happens to lean at thirty-five.

(4) It is irrational to choose a transformation of one's own values for its own sake: one should not take a pill that makes one love heroin, even knowing that one's post-pill self would wholeheartedly endorse the decision.

(5) The standard move of deferring to base rates still applies here: survey evidence on parental life satisfaction, regret rates, and the comparison with childfree adults is genuinely decision-relevant, whatever the first-person epistemic gaps are.

Which of these pairs are in real tension, which only appear to be, and in the real cases, which commitment has to give and at what cost? Please rank the tensions by severity and identify the single revision that restores the most consistency for the least philosophical damage -- I genuinely don't know where the problem is, so don't assume I've flagged it.
