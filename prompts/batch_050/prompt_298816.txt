I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am designing an interactive feature on the comeback of lottery-based government. In the Politics Aristotle treats selection by lot as the democratic device and election as the more aristocratic or oligarchic one, not because chance picks better rulers but because it equalizes each citizen’s chance to rule and be ruled. Use that contrast as the hinge, but do not quote or cite; build the piece from the logic of institutions.

Write a decision tree, for politically curious readers, that sorts them into one of eight institutional temperaments: pure legislative sortition; stratified citizens’ assemblies with binding power; advisory mini-publics attached to elected legislatures; elected representatives under hard term limits; rotation in office with recall; a mixed regime in which lot chooses the agenda body and election chooses executives; merit-selected professional administrators supervised by randomly drawn juries; and an epistocratic council constrained by sunset review. Begin from the logical space, not from labels: each node must be a yes-or-no or three-way question about authority, duration, recurrence, accountability, competence, constituency, and scope, phrased so readers can answer without knowing the jargon. Include concrete cases such as a city whose budget is set by 150 residents chosen by lot for eighteen months, a two-term mayor followed by mandatory service on a lottery oversight panel, and expert agencies audited by lay juries.

After the tree, name each leaf, state its most awkward commitment in one sentence, and mark any empty combinations with a brief diagnosis: incoherent, unattractive, or merely untried. Flag any leaves your questions cannot really separate, especially advisory versus binding mini-publics and term limits versus rotation. The aim is classification by institutional instinct, not a quiz on famous names. Keep the instrument to twelve questions or fewer, plus a short scoring key.
