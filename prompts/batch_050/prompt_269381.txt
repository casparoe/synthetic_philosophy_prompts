For several years a colleague of mine, a moral philosopher who works on consent, has been collecting cases and recording her verdicts on whether the consent given in each was valid. I have her verdicts on the seven cases below. A new case has now come up in our reading group, and before she rules on it I would like to predict her verdict — not because I care about the verdict itself so much as because I want to know whether I understand the rule she is running.

Her track record:

1. A surgeon does not mention a one-in-ten-thousand risk of a minor, temporary side effect. The patient consents to the operation. Valid.
2. A man tells a prospective sexual partner that he is unmarried; he is in fact married. The partner consents to sex. Valid.
3. A man assures his partner that he will use a condom and then deliberately does not. The partner consents to sex on that understanding. Invalid.
4. A mugger threatens to kill a pedestrian unless he hands over his wallet; the pedestrian hands it over. Invalid — she says there is no valid consent to the transfer at all.
5. A destitute mother sells a kidney to a broker, at a poor price and in full knowledge of the risks, because it is the only way to pay for her child's operation. Valid — the arrangement is exploitative and the situation unjust, she says, but the consent itself is valid.
6. A woman consents to a breast examination by a man who falsely claims to be a doctor; the physical contact is exactly what she expected. Invalid.
7. A patient with mild dementia, who can accurately explain the risks of a routine operation and her reasons for declining it, refuses the operation. Her refusal is valid and must be respected, even though her family considers it unwise.

The new case: a woman enrols in a university psychology study. The consent documents describe the procedure fully and truthfully — a forty-minute memory task involving no concealed risks. When she asks, the researcher tells her the study is funded by the university; in fact it is funded by a firearms trade association, a fact the researchers have been instructed not to volunteer. The participant is a committed gun-control campaigner, and she says afterwards that the funding source was the one thing that would have mattered to her and that she would unhesitatingly have refused had she known. The task itself she found mildly interesting and would not have minded performing.

Please do the following:

- Set out the general rules about what invalidates consent that are consistent with all seven verdicts. I expect there to be at least two plausible candidates; if you find more, say so, and say which verdicts do the most to narrow the field.
- State what each candidate rule predicts about the new case, and make clear exactly where and why the predictions diverge.
- Give your best guess at her verdict, with reasons drawn from the shape of her track record rather than from your own view of the case. If you think the track record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so plainly rather than forcing a prediction.
- Describe a further case she has not yet considered whose verdict would settle which of the leading candidate rules she is actually running, and explain why the new case does not already settle it.

Please write your answer in British English.
