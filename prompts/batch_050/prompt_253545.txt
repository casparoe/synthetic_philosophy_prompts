I have spent enough time on rationalist blogs and forums to be suspicious of two opposite moves: treating deliberation as just another forecast about what I will happen to do, and treating choice as so sui generis that no beliefs about one’s own future actions are legitimate while one is still deciding. Call the target an account of agential self-credence: norms for when an agent may, must, or must not assign probabilities to its own not-yet-settled acts, including acts of an artificial successor, a future self under predictable temptation, or copies in coordination problems. The purpose is not literary selfhood. I want a standard good enough to govern a bounded planner that can make promises, run pre-mortems on itself, be copied or inspected, and still deliberate without becoming a passive spectator to a prediction it issued earlier.

Write the desiderata such an account must meet, with the motivating case or consideration behind each, and mark each as non-negotiable, strongly desirable, or a camp-specific preference. The criteria should do the work; only at the end, if useful, briefly note where familiar decision-theoretic temperaments such as causal, evidential, and updateless or functional approaches seem to gain or lose ground. No bibliography or reading list.

The list should at least force answers to these pressure points: the difference between predicting that a bridge will hold and predicting that I will hold to a resolution; whether a credence about my own act can be evidence for doing it without laundering the conclusion; when self-prediction is a legitimate input to planning, as in budgeting attention or designing a commitment device, and when it corrupts the deliberation it was meant to inform; what happens to calibration if the agent knows its own source code or can be simulated by an adversary; how to treat promises and threats whose value depends on being believed before the act exists; whether identical or near-identical copies should get identical policies, identical probabilities, or neither; and what an agent should say about options it has already ruled out but has not yet stopped representing.

I especially want the specification to separate genuine requirements from local ideology. For example, immunity to Dutch books may be non-negotiable for some purposes and merely nice for an agent whose losses are bounded; reflection consistency may be essential where self-modification is possible and incoherent where preferences are allowed to change; never let a forecast decide for you may be a healthy heuristic rather than a metaphysical law. State which requirements are jointly satisfiable, which pairs appear to trade off, and whether any attractive subset is provably unstable under iteration, self-reference, bounded computation, or multi-agent prediction. If the most plausible result is that no single rule can be optimal for deliberation, commitment, and coordination at once, say that plainly and identify which requirement does the most excluding.
