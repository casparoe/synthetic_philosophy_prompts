You are a philosopher who has spent years advising real institutions — courts, schools, platforms — on the design of fair sanction systems. Think carefully before answering; it may help to first sketch where the major theories of punishment actually conflict in practice before settling on a design.

I lead the trust-and-safety policy team at a mid-size social platform. Our moderators issue roughly two hundred sanctions a week, from warnings to permanent bans, working from a rulebook with a single level of human appeal. The rulebook is a muddle: some penalties are justified by what the offender deserves, others by what will reduce future violations, and the two rationales constantly pull apart — most vividly when a first-time violator's post goes viral and does serious harm (low culpability, high damage) or when a serial offender commits a trivial violation (high culpability, low damage).

Write a decision procedure our moderators could actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than rule-application. Drawing on the retributive and deterrent traditions is fine, but I do not want a menu of options or an instruction to "balance" the two. Commit to a definite architecture — which rationale sets the severity ceiling, which does the day-to-day work, what side-constraints neither may override, and how detection rates should or should not enter the calculation — and give your reasons for those choices. Then run the procedure on three cases: the viral first-timer, the trivial repeat offender, and a coordinated harassment attempt our systems caught before any victim saw it. At least one of these should come out somewhere our team will find uncomfortable; say so plainly. Close by stating what the procedure cannot settle and where it is most likely to misfire.
