So a bunch of companies now offer "griefbots" -- chatbots trained on a dead person's texts, emails, voicemails, and social media, so that the bereaved can keep messaging them after the funeral. The details of how these services actually work matter for my question, so let me lay them out: the bot talks in the first person as the dead person ("I miss you too"), not in the third person as an archive. Signing up the deceased is done by a living relative, usually without any prior consent from the person themselves, who in most cases died before knowing such a thing would exist. The companies' marketing leans hard on the idea that you're staying connected with your loved one and that this helps with grief. But their terms of service, when you read them, insist the bot is just software, a simulation, not your loved one in any sense. And some of the bots are designed to message you first -- the dead person "reaches out" on your birthday.

What I want to know: what philosophical commitments does this whole setup presuppose? A few things I'm specifically stuck on:

- Does having the bot talk as "I" presuppose some view about what a person fundamentally is -- like, that a person is a behavioral/dispositional pattern that could in principle be copied? Or can you defend first-person griefbots while completely denying that the bot is or continues the dead person in any sense?
- The consent situation seems to presuppose that the dead have no interests that could be violated -- or at least that whatever interests they have are properly managed by their family. Is that the only way to make sense of it, or is there a view where the dead matter morally and the practice is still fine?
- The marketing and the terms of service seem to presuppose incompatible things about what the customer is interacting with. Is that an actual inconsistency in the practice, or is there a coherent single view that makes both the marketing and the legal boilerplate true?

Reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) as charitably as you can, tell me which parts of the practice are philosophically underdetermined -- i.e., consistent with several rival views and so not really presupposing any of them -- and then tell me what would concretely have to change in how these services are built and sold if we instead assumed that the dead retain interests that can be harmed. Casual is fine, I just want the reasoning to be tight.
