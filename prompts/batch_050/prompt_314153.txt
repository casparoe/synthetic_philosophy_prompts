I serve as one of two graduate-student members on my university's honor council, and this term we've been handed a pair of cases that have split the council down the middle. In the first, a student purchased an essay from a ghostwriting service, edited it lightly, and intended to submit it — but through a file mix-up on deadline night, actually uploaded a draft he had written himself, which was graded normally. The purchase receipt and chat logs surfaced later, so there is no doubt about what he meant to do; the only question is what he did. In the second case, a student's submitted paper contains three unattributed paragraphs lifted from a published article, but the evidence (version histories and a credible account corroborated by her lab partner) indicates she used an AI paraphrasing tool that inserted the passages without her knowledge, and that she neither noticed nor sought this. Our academic integrity code defines plagiarism as "the submission of work presented as one's own that is not one's own" — language that is entirely act-focused and says nothing about attempts, intentions, or consent.

Here's where I'd like your help, and I'll lay my cards on the table. A reading group I'm in recently worked through some medieval ethics, and I've been turning over Abelard's position that sin resides wholly in the consent of the will — not in the desire, and crucially not in the deed itself, since the same outward act can proceed from blameworthy or blameless interior states, and the fully consenting will is no less corrupt for being thwarted by circumstance. On this view the first student, who consented completely and was saved only by accident, is the guilty one, and the second, who performed the prohibited act without anything like consent, is not — a verdict pair that reverses what our code's plain text and several of my colleagues' instincts deliver. I recognize this is close to the philosophical literature on moral luck, which I've mostly encountered secondhand through years of reading rationalist blogs, where the folk wisdom also runs in both directions: "intent isn't magic" sits uneasily next to the conviction that punishing outcomes rather than decisions produces perverse incentive gradients and makes the council a weathervane for chance.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend a concrete disposition for each student (including whether any sanction, and of what severity, is warranted), and it should be consistent: whatever principle it articulates about where culpability lives must yield both verdicts rather than being patched together case by case. It should also engage seriously with the strongest consideration on whichever side loses — I expect that to be the deterrence-and-verifiability argument, namely that consent is internal and hard to prove while submitted texts are public facts, so an intention-based code invites perjury and a purely act-based code at least administers cleanly. Finally, since our code's wording will outlast this case, please indicate whether the opinion should also recommend amending the code's definition, and if so, what the amended language should track.
