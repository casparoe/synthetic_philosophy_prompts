I keep going back and forth on whether the state should licence parents, so I’d like you to draft the joint statement that could emerge from an adversarial collaboration between these two camps:

1. The Gatekeepers think parenting is a potentially dangerous activity performed on a non-consenting person. They therefore favour a prospective licence for anyone intending to raise a child, provided the criteria concern only minimum adequate care: safety, health, basic emotional availability, and a willingness not to close off the child’s central future options. Licences would be free, appealable, periodically reviewable, and unavailable only where there is strong evidence of serious risk, not merely because officials dislike a family’s values or lifestyle.

2. The Stewards think intimate family life and parental judgement deserve a strong presumption against state screening. They fear that any real licensing system would reproduce class, racial, disability, and cultural biases; confuse unfamiliar parenting with harmful parenting; and encourage officials to treat children as projects to be standardised. They favour voluntary preparation, universal support, and intervention after demonstrable harm or a concrete imminent danger.

Both camps accept something like a child’s right to an open future, which you should define for them as follows: a child should reach adulthood with a reasonable range of central life options still genuinely available. This does not mean preserving every possible option, which would be impossible. It means preventing avoidable and hard-to-reverse narrowing of the capacities, relationships, knowledge, and opportunities needed for many decent ways of life.

Their disagreement should be organised around this passage from chapter 3 of John Stuart Mill’s On Liberty:

“As it is useful that while mankind are imperfect there should be different opinions, so is it that there should be different experiments of living; that free scope should be given to varieties of character, short of injury to others; and that the worth of different modes of life should be proved practically, when any one thinks fit to try them. It is desirable, in short, that in things which do not primarily concern others, individuality should assert itself.”

Please don’t treat Mill as an authority whose verdict settles the issue. Use the passage becuase each side can plausibly claim it. The Stewards can invoke “different experiments of living” and “varieties of character”; the Gatekeepers can reply that a child is plainly an “other”, so parenting is not purely self-regarding experimentation.

The joint statement should contain:

A. A short jointly signed reading of the passage. State three propositions both camps can extract from it, plus one popular interpretation they jointly reject. Say exactly why the phrase “experiments of living” cannot straightforwardly justify every child-rearing practice, while also explaining why the state should not demand one ideal childhood.

B. A numbered section of at least ten agreed claims. These should cover the non-consensual position of children, parental love and discretion, the fallibility of officials, the difference between eccentricity and neglect, the danger of licensing criteria encoding majority values, and the fact that refusing to create a licensing system is itself a policy choice with foreseeable victims.

C. A disagreements table with columns for: residual issue; Gatekeepers’ wording; Stewards’ wording; the crux; evidence or argument both sides accept as relevant; and each side’s advance prediction. Formulate each camp’s position so that its members could sign it without feeling caricatured.

D. At least five genuine cruxes. These might include whether risk to children is more like the risks justifying driver licensing or those involved in politically sensitive activities; whether minimum competence can be measured independently of contested ideals; whether licensing would deter bad parents or merely drive them underground; whether children conceived or raised without a licence would be stigmatised; and whether an “open future” is too contested to serve as a legal standard. For each, distinguish evidence about likely effects from disagreement about moral weights.

E. At least two disputes that turn out to be partly verbal. For example, one side might hear “licence” as permission to become a parent while the other means only certification to retain custody, or “open future” might mean maximising options to one side and protecting a floor of options to the other. Explain what remains after the verbal part is cleared away.

F. A jointly designed policy experiment. It should compare voluntary support, certification with benefits, and mandatory licensing in a way both camps regard as fair. Record what result each camp predicts and what outcome would oblige it to revise its position. Don’t quietly rig the experiment by assuming that state officials are either omniscient or uniformly bigoted.

End with a one-paragraph statement each camp could add under its own name, explaining why it signs the joint document despite remaining unconvinced by the other side. Please write the whole thing in British English, around 1,500–2,000 words excluding the Mill passage, and don’t include a bibliography.
