Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two philosophers of probability who have argued for years about Bertrand's paradox. Party A holds that the paradox — the fact that the question "a chord of a circle is chosen at random; what is the probability it is longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle?" yields different answers under equally natural randomization procedures — refutes the classical principle of indifference and shows that "uniform" priors are never privileged; every rescue (transformation groups, Jeffreys priors, maximum entropy) merely hides an unjustified choice of reference structure. Party B holds that the paradox shows only that indifference must be relativized to a problem's symmetries, and that where a unique symmetry-respecting distribution exists, the principle does real work.

The deliverable: (1) a numbered section of claims both can sign — about what the chord problem does and does not establish, about what the classical principle actually said, and about what a prior is supposed to be for; (2) a table of residual disagreements, each formulated so its own side would accept the wording without amendment; (3) for each crux, a case or result both agree bears on it — candidates include the wine/water puzzle, physical applications where symmetries are fixed by the setup, and Jaynes's "well-posed" solution to the chord problem itself — with each side's prediction about that case recorded in advance; and (4) a closing paragraph identifying any part of the dispute that turned out, in the drafting, to be verbal rather than substantive.
