I'm writing a paper for a graduate seminar on comparative federalism, and I've gotten stuck on something my co-supervisor and I keep circling. Across the systems we've read about -- the United States, Germany, Switzerland, and the European Union -- decentralization is defended in what look like three quite different registers. In the American tradition there's the famous experimentalist idea, associated with Justice Brandeis, that states can try out novel policies on a small scale so the rest of the country can learn from the results. In the EU, the subsidiarity principle written into the treaties is framed almost entirely as a comparative-efficiency test: the Union should act only where member states cannot achieve a goal sufficiently well on their own and the Union can do it better, and the associated early-warning mechanism lets national parliaments (roughly a third of them, by votes) force the Commission to reconsider a proposal -- a mechanism that has been triggered only a handful of times in well over a decade. And in the older tradition from which the word "subsidiarity" comes, Catholic social teaching, the claim seems to be something different again: that larger bodies wrong smaller communities when they absorb functions the smaller ones can perform, which sounds less like an empirical bet about efficiency and more like a claim about the standing or dignity of communities at a certain scale.

Here's the puzzle I want help with. Each of these defenses seems to presuppose a different theory of what political authority over a territory is. The efficiency test appears to presuppose that the outputs of governance at different levels are commensurable -- that there's a single scorecard on which "Brussels does it better" and "Bavaria does it better" can be compared -- and that where authority should sit is ultimately an empirical question. The experimentalist defense appears to presuppose that policies are like hypotheses: testable, transferable, and eventually convergent, so that divergence between jurisdictions is a means to shared learning rather than a value in itself. And the older communitarian view seems to presuppose that some collectives have a claim to govern their own affairs that doesn't depend on outcomes at all. Are these presuppositions consistent with each other, or does an institution that invokes all three -- as the EU arguably does, pairing its efficiency test with treaty language protecting member states' national identities -- rest on a confused foundation?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of political authority that actual federal practice in one or two of these systems commits it to, distinguishing carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely sits comfortably with. Two specific wrinkles I'd like addressed. First, American marijuana policy: states have legalized what federal law still prohibits, and the federal response has been non-enforcement rather than either suppression or an organized attempt to learn from the divergence -- does that practice actually fit the experimentalist story, or does it presuppose something closer to a permanent modus vivendi, and what would genuinely experimentalist practice have looked like instead (sunset clauses, mandated data collection, harmonization after results)? Second, the choice of units: the EU's mechanism gives the trigger to national parliaments, not to regional assemblies in Bavaria, Catalonia, or Lombardy, and American doctrine privileges states over cities -- what does that privileging presuppose about which communities count as self-governing, given that neither a pure efficiency test nor a pure experiment-based argument seems capable of explaining why the line is drawn exactly there? If you conclude that the practice is philosophically underdetermined at some points -- that several rival theories would license identical institutions -- say where, and what observable change in practice would reveal which theory a system was really operating on.
