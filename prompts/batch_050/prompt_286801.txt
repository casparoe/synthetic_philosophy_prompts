I'm in a philosophy of mind reading group that has spent the semester on the status of dreams, and our two most stubborn members -- one a convinced follower of Norman Malcolm, the other a committed Dennettian -- have been arguing past each other for weeks. The Malcolmian holds that the notion of an experience occurring, with judgments and all, while one is soundly asleep is conceptually confused: the waking report is the criterion for the dream, not evidence for some hidden nocturnal event, and "I am asleep" could not be truly judged by the person it describes. The Dennettian counters that dream reports are real data about real past events, but that the events needn't match the reports: the "dream" may be a narrative composed in the moments of waking, with memory fragments spliced in, so that asking when in the night the dream-experience happened is already a mistake. They've agreed to settle their differences by writing a joint statement, adversarial-collaboration style, and I've been asked to produce the draft. Write that draft for them.

The document should contain, first, a numbered list of claims both parties can sign: about what a dream report is and what authority it has, about the phenomena both sides must accommodate (lucid dreamers carrying out pre-agreed eye-movement signals during REM, sleep talkers whose utterances match later reports, external stimuli like an alarm getting woven into a dream narrative, the apparent duration of dreams), and about what each side's position does and does not commit them to. Second, a disagreements table in which each residual dispute is stated in wording that each party would accept as a fair statement of its own view -- no slanted formulations like "mysterious unverifiable experiences" or "denying the obvious." Third, a cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one party, and for each crux, an observation, experiment, or argument that both parties agree in advance would bear on it, with each side's prediction about the outcome written down (for instance, what each side expects from more sophisticated lucid-dreaming paradigms, or from cases where dream content can be predicted from brain activity). Finally, if any part of their dispute turns out to be verbal -- a difference in how "experience" or "occurred during sleep" is being used rather than a disagreement about the facts -- the document should say so explicitly and explain which part and why. Aim for the tone of two serious philosophers who have agreed to stop scoring points and produce something both could actually sign.
