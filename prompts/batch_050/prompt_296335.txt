You are a political philosopher with a reputation for careful analysis of arguments by analogy. Think carefully before answering; you may find it useful to brainstorm candidate disanalogies first and then select the ones that matter.

On a public commission where I sit, a colleague blocked repeal of a decades-old occupational licensing rule -- one whose origins nobody on the commission can now reconstruct -- by invoking the familiar picture associated with Chesterton: an established rule is like a fence standing across a road. Somebody built it for a reason, and a person who cannot see the reason has not earned the right to tear it down. My colleague took this to settle the matter in favor of keeping the rule until its original purpose is rediscovered.

Audit this analogy as an argument, not just as a slogan. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the fence-builder, of the road, of the passerby, of understanding why the fence was erected, and of the act of removal -- for an institutional rule rather than a physical fence. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and distinguish the breaks that damage my colleague's use from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Among the candidates I suspect matter, but which you should assess rather than simply accept: a fence usually has an identifiable builder whose purpose is in principle recoverable, whereas the 'reason' behind an old institution is often contested, retrospectively reconstructed, or invented by the people now defending it; the survival of a fence is evidence that someone once wanted it there, which is a weaker claim than that it serves a neutral public function; and fence removal and rule repeal may differ sharply in reversibility and in whether removal itself destroys the evidence needed to evaluate it.

Finally: say whether some deflated version of the conservative conclusion -- for instance, a rebuttable presumption rather than a veto -- survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the fence picture is beyond repair for my colleague's purpose, propose a better analogy that captures what he is actually entitled to claim.
