So my debut novel is being translated into French, and I just got off a slightly tense call with the translator. She's taken some pretty big liberties -- swapped a running joke for a French one, replaced a character's favorite song lyric with a totally different song, flattened a narrator's weird clipped sentence rhythm into normal elegant French. When I pushed back she gave me this little speech: a translator is like a moving company. The meaning is the furniture, the original words are just the boxes and bubble wrap, and her job is to get the furniture safely into the new apartment. Nobody complains that the movers used different boxes. She was clearly using this to settle the argument -- like, once you see translation that way, my complaints are just sentimentality about packaging. I couldn't tell on the spot whether that's deep or whether it's a rhetorical trick.

Some context on why I'm suspicious: a friend in my book club lent me Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot, where he translates this one tiny old French poem dozens of ways and keeps insisting that the form -- the syllable counts, the rhymes, the breeziness -- is part of what's being carried, not wrapping around it. That got under my skin. At the same time, I don't want to be the precious author who demands a word-for-word rendering of everything; for plain expository paragraphs the furniture picture seems... fine? My novel isn't a poem, but it does have some formal stuff I care about: the narrator slips into short, choppy sentences whenever she's lying, there's a rhythm that recurs at the ends of chapters, and a couple of puns that do thematic work. So I'm genuinely torn between "she's right, let the profesional do her job" and "the analogy is smuggling in exactly the assumption that's up for debate."

Can you act as a philosopher of language who also knows the translation-studies literature and audit this analogy properly for me? Take it slow and think carefully -- I'd actually like you to start by brainstorming the correspondence table before drawing any conclusions: what plays the role of the furniture, the boxes, the apartment, the damage-in-transit, the doorways that force you to disassemble things? Then tell me: where does the mapping hold, where does it break, and what does it quietly import -- I'm especially wondering whether it presupposes that meaning is some separable content that exists apart from its wording, which seems like exactly what a novelist should deny. Separate the disanalogies that actually matter for her case from the pedantic ones. And the bottom line: does the analogy really license her domesticating choices, or was it doing all the argumentative work? If there's a better analogy (my drummer friend suggests translation is more like playing a piece on a different instrument -- the score constrains you but every performance differs), say whether that one survives the same audit any better.
