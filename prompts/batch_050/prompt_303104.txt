I serve on the eleven-member charter revision commission of a mid-sized city, the body that meets once every two decades to propose amendments to our municipal charter. This cycle's most contested item is Proposal 7, which would allow any measure backed by signatures from eight percent of registered voters to go to a binding citywide referendum, with the result shielded from council amendment for five years. The commission is split five to five, I am the undecided member, and I have agreed to circulate a written opinion before our final vote in three weeks. I would like you to draft that opinion for me.

Some context. Last weekend I spent a long evening arguing about Proposal 7 with an old friend, and neither of us conceded much. Her view is uncomplicated: on any question where the council and the electorate would diverge, the council is the usurper, because legitimacy flows from the people and the most direct expression of the people is a majority vote in which everyone may participate. Representatives, donors, and party whips can only distort that will, not refine it. I pushed back with a passage I half-remembered from Rousseau's The Social Contract (Book II, Chapter III, in G. D. H. Cole's translation), which I have since looked up:

"If, when the people, being furnished with adequate information, held its deliberations, the citizens had no communication one with another, the grand total of the small differences would always give the general will, and the decision would always be good. But when factions arise, and partial associations are formed at the expense of the great association, the will of each of these associations becomes general in relation to its members, while it remains particular in relation to the State: it may then be said that there are no longer as many votes as there are men, but only as many as there are associations. The differences become less numerous and give a less general result. Lastly, when one of these associations is so great as to prevail over all the rest, the result is no longer a sum of small differences, but a single difference; in this case there is no longer a general will, and the opinion which prevails is purely particular."

She replied that this passage is actually on her side: our council is itself one of Rousseau's "partial associations," a small body whose internal deliberation produces a will general only in relation to its members, whereas a referendum at least puts the question to everyone. I replied that modern referendum campaigns are precisely the world of factions, funded signature drives, and coordinated messaging that Rousseau says destroys the general will — but I had to grant that his closing thought in the chapter is that where partial societies cannot be eliminated, having many of them, kept roughly equal, is the acceptable second best, and a city like ours has no shortage of competing associations.

The opinion should run about 800 to 1,000 words and be written in a formal register suitable for circulation to the commission and entry into the public record. It must do the following:

1. State an unambiguous bottom line the commission could act on: recommend adoption, rejection, or adoption with specified conditions. If you recommend conditions, make them concrete enough to be drafted as amendment language (signature thresholds, scope limits on what may be decided by referendum, mandatory waiting or deliberation periods, supermajority requirements, or whatever your reasoning supports).
2. Take a definite interpretive position on the quoted passage: what exactly does Rousseau think goes wrong when citizens deliberate through associations rather than as informed individuals, and does a referendum amplify the defect or remedy it? Address my friend's reading of the passage directly, since several commissioners share it.
3. Apply Rousseau's distinction between the will of all and the general will to at least one plausible referendum question a city like ours would actually face (you may construct a realistic hypothetical, such as a popular measure to freeze property taxes that would defund the bus network), showing how the distinction does or does not help decide whether that question belongs on the ballot.
4. Give the strongest honest statement of the case for Proposal 7 — including the point that representatives have their own particular interests and that the status quo is not neutral — and explain precisely where, on your reasoning, that case falls short or succeeds.

I want the opinion to be one I am willing to sign, so do not hedge the recommendation into vagueness: the losing side should feel its argument was taken seriously, and the winning side should know exactly what it is being asked to vote for.
