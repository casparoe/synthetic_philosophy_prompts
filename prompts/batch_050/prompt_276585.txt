Lots of US courts now use actuarial risk scores in bail decisions: the defendant gets scored on stuff like age, prior convictions, missed court dates, and the judge sees an output like "high risk of failing to appear." But any given defendant belongs to a huge number of reference classes -- 24-year-old men with two priors, people charged with this offense, people from this neighborhood, people whose last arrest was 3 years ago, people who showed up to their arraingment on time -- and the re-offense or no-show frequencies differ across all of them. The tool has picked one particular way of carving things up (really, one weighted blend of several), and nothing in the courtroom practice ever asks whether it picked the right one.

What does this practice presuppose about the reference class problem? That there's a unique correct reference class for each defendant and the tool approximates it? That the problem is dissolved because out-of-sample predictive accuracy is all that matters, so the class is just whatever maximizes prediction? Or that individual risk isn't really a coherent notion at all, and the number is strictly about the class -- in which case detaining *this* person on its basis starts to look odd? Reconstruct the implicit view, say whether the practice actually needs it or is compatible with several views, and tell me what would have to change in the courtroom if the reference class problem turned out to have no principled solution.
