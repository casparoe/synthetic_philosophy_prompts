Next term I am giving a single ninety-minute guest seminar on theories of concepts — prototype theory, exemplar theory, and the theory theory — to second-year philosophy undergraduates who have encountered Wittgenstein on family resemblance but have no background in cognitive science. I do not need the material explained to me; I need a plan for explaining it. Please set out the following.

First, the order of ideas. Which of the three theories should be introduced first, and what does each subsequent one presuppose that must already be in place? I am inclined to run them chronologically (prototypes, then exemplars, then theory theory) but I suspect the pedagogically right order may differ from the historical one; tell me whether I am wrong. In particular, explain how to use family resemblance as the phenomenon all three theories are competing to explain, without letting the students conclude that prototype theory simply is Wittgenstein's observation restated.

Second, the carrying example. I want one category that can bear the weight of all three theories across the whole session, so the students see the same case redescribed three ways. Say what such an example must have (gradability of membership, at least one borderline case the class will genuinely divide over, some obviously central features and some obviously peripheral ones) and what it must lack (no standard textbook associations, no settled scientific essence that would short-circuit the discussion). Should it be an artefact kind or a natural kind, and why? If not bird, and not chair, then what — propose one and defend it.

Third, the misconception map. Give me the errors these students will predictably bring or form, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than corrected afterwards. My guesses to start you off: that the three theories are accounts of different kinds of concept rather than rivals about the same concepts; that a prototype is a mental image or stereotype; that exemplar theory means memorising every encountered instance and therefore cannot handle novel cases; and that the theory theory amounts to possessing an explicit definition. Correct or extend this list.

Fourth, the check for understanding. I want to finish by handing the class an invented category — a made-up kind with a stipulated handful of instances — and asking them to do something with it that reveals whether they can apply the three theories rather than recite them. Specify how to construct the invented category (what features and instance structure it needs so that the three theories give distinguishable predictions about it) and what exactly the students should be asked to do.

Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level and to flag as omitted — candidates include compositionality objections, conceptual atomism, and hybrid pluralist views — so the students know the map is incomplete without losing the thread.

No reading list, please; the plan itself is the deliverable.
