I just got out of a stupidly long argument with a friend about the Browning–Goldhagen debate over why “ordinary” Germans took part in the Holocaust. My rough understanding is that Browning emphasizes situational pressures: conformity, obedience, peer pressure, gradual brutalization, war, bureaucracy, and the way refusing could feel socially costly without being physically impossible. Goldhagen pushes back and says the perpetrators were often genuinely willing because they were formed by an especially eliminationist antisemitic culture, not merely pressured into going along.

Our argument kept circling because every example seemed to support both stories. Someone volunteers for a killing: Goldhagen says willingness, Browning says willingness can be produced by context. Someone refuses without severe punishment: Browning says choice remained, my friend says the rarity of refusal shows how powerful the pressure was. It started to feel like we were no longer disagreeing about particular facts but about what would even count as evidence for a cause being “inside” versus “outside” the person.

My hypothesis is that the debate is stalled partly because both sides are explaining slightly different things. Browning may be explaining how participation becomes possible for people who do not begin as enthusiastic killers, while Goldhagen is explaining why participation, cruelty, and initiative became culturally intelligible and morally acceptable. If so, ordinary men versus willing executioners is a bad binary. A person can be ordinary before entering the institution, willing once inside it, and still not primarily motivated by a single longstanding ideological desire.

Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a deeper substantive disagreement here? Break down what kind of dispute this really is: factual, methodological, conceptual, moral, or some mixture. Pay attention to what each side treats as the basic explanandum, what evidence would settle it, and whether terms like ordinary, willing, coercion, and antisemitic motivation are doing equivocal work. Finally, say what would count as progress now: better archival evidence, a clearer causal model, comparisons with other genocides, finer categories of motivation, or simply abandoning the original framing. Don’t just summarize the debate; diagnose why it keeps going nowhere. Sorry if this is abit rambling, I’m still annoyed.
