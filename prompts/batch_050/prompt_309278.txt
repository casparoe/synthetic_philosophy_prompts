I'm preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on anthropic reasoning — the question of what, if anything, we may infer from the bare fact that we exist and are the observers we are. I'd like you to build me a decision tree that sorts readers by their verdicts on cases rather than by which position-label they already identify with, so that someone who has never heard the standard names can end up at the right leaf.

Structure the tree as follows. Start from the logical space, not from a list of names: figure out which independent questions generate the space, then locate the actual positions in it. The internal nodes should be no more than seven diagnostic questions, each phrased as a concrete case with two or three answer options, so that readers answer with a verdict rather than a self-description. Please use at least these cases among your questions: the Sleeping Beauty setup with the fair coin and the memory-erasing awakenings; the 'presumptuous philosopher' scenario, in which rival cosmological theories predict very different total numbers of observers and the question is whether armchair reflection on one's own existence can bear on which is true; an incubator case in which a machine creates either one observer or vastly many depending on a coin flip, and the question is what you should believe upon waking as one of the created; the doomsday-style inference about how long humanity should expect to last given your birth rank; and the question of which class you count yourself a random sample from, if any (all humans, all observers, all observer-moments, or no privileged class at all).

The leaves should include at least: the self-sampling assumption in its standard form; the self-indication assumption; a 'halfer' verdict on Sleeping Beauty reached without appeal to self-indication; and a self-sampling view equipped with an explicit rule for choosing the reference class. For each leaf, give the position's name (or 'unnamed' if the combination of answers that reaches it has no actual defenders) plus one sentence identifying the verdict it is committed to that its opponents find most embarrassing — for instance, what the self-indication view must say about the cosmologist case, or what the self-sampling view must say about birth-rank reasoning.

Then add three short appendix items: first, any pair of positions your tree cannot separate, together with the further question or case that would separate them (I expect at least one such pair among the routes to a 'thirder' verdict on Sleeping Beauty); second, each coherent combination of answers that no leaf occupies, with a one- or two-sentence diagnosis of whether that slot is empty because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored; third, a note on any diagnostic question you considered but rejected because it fails to split the positions cleanly.

Keep the whole instrument self-contained: no citations, no names of authors attached to the cases, and no answer that requires knowing the literature's vocabulary in advance.
