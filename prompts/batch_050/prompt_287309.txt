I'm preparing for a graduate reading group in metaphysics, and the immediate occasion is embarrassingly concrete: I took up ceramics last year, and a few weeks ago I dropped a half-finished vase on the studio floor. Watching it shatter, I caught myself thinking "there goes the vase, but the clay is still right here" — and then realized I had no idea what I actually take the metaphysics of that thought to be. Since then I've fallen down the material constitution rabbit hole (statues and lumps, Tibbles the cat, the ship of Theseus), and the group has asked each of us to circulate a one-paragraph statement of our commitments in advance so the others can attack them. Rather than tidy mine up first, I want to hand them to you in their current, possibly inconsistent state and have you audit them.

Here is where I've landed. (1) Where the statue stands on the pedestal, there is exactly one object: positing two numerically distinct, perfectly coincident things strikes me as extravagance, since nothing could ground the difference between them. (2) Nevertheless, the lump of clay would survive being squashed into a ball while the statue would not — these modal facts are real and non-negotiable. (3) I'm a four-dimensionalist about persistence: objects persist by perduring, i.e., by having distinct temporal parts at different times, not by being wholly present at each time. (4) Ordinary objects genuinely exist — statues, tables, and cats belong in my ontology, so eliminativism about the manifest image is off the table. (5) The persistence conditions of ordinary objects are not up to us: it is a fact about the world, not a convention of our sortals or our talk, that a statue cannot survive squashing while a lump can.

Please audit this set rather than lecture me on the literature. Specifically: identify which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, ordered by severity, and for each one say exactly what would have to give and what that concession would cost. Distinguish these from any pair that merely looks tense but dissolves on inspection — if two of my commitments only appear to conflict, show me why they don't. Finally, if the set is not jointly tenable, tell me which single revision would restore the most consistency at the lowest cost, and be willing to say so even if the cheapest fix is one I clearly won't like. Keep the whole audit under 650 words.
