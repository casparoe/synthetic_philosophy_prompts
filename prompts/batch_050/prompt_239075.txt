I am designing a three-week unit for an undergraduate course on the political psychology of evil, pairing Hannah Arendt's Eichmann in Jerusalem with Shirley Jackson's short story The Lottery. Please write the reading I wish I could assign: a sustained interpretation of Jackson's story through the lens of Arendt's thesis of the banality of evil.

Develop the reading along the following lines, though you may restructure and add. First, the villagers as Jackson draws them — chatting about taxes and tractors while assembling for the ritual, children stuffing their pockets with stones, Mr. Summers presiding with the geniality of a civic functionary running a raffle — seem almost engineered to illustrate Arendt's claim that monstrous deeds can be carried out by unmonstrous people who are following routine, deferring to tradition, and never occupying the standpoint of their victim. Work out where the parallel is tightest: the role of stock formulas and half-remembered liturgy in both cases (Arendt made much of Eichmann's reliance on clichés and officialese; the lottery has its own degraded ceremonial language, retained long after its meaning has been forgotten); the splintered black box, never replaced, as an emblem of fidelity to form over content; and Old Man Warner as the story's pure ideologue of tradition, for whom any report of villages abandoning the lottery is evidence of civilizational decay.

Second, take up Tessie Hutchinson, who arrives late and joking about her household chores, participates without visible qualm, and objects to the fairness of the procedure only once her own family and then she herself are selected. Is her protest a confirmation of Arendt or a complication? On one reading, she dramatizes precisely the incapacity Arendt diagnosed — judgment that never activates until the self is at stake. On another, her late-arriving sense that the drawing was conducted unfairly is a foothold of moral thought that Eichmann, on Arendt's telling, never manages to reach, and so the story quietly exceeds its supposed illustration. Argue both sides and then take a position.

Third, be honest about where the lens distorts. Arendt's analysis leaned heavily on bureaucratic mediation — the desk murderer insulated from his victims by paperwork, distance, and the division of labor — whereas Jackson's villagers kill with their own hands, face to face, in a scene structurally closer to ritual scapegoating than to administrative evil. Does this difference sink the reading, or does it merely show that Jackson belongs to a different explanatory genre (Girard's scapegoat mechanism, perhaps, rather than Arendt's)? Note also the chronology: the story predates Eichmann in Jerusalem by fifteen years, so this is an analytic pairing rather than a historical influence — but say whether you think Jackson anticipated Arendt's picture or only happened to converge on it.

End with the critical payoff: identify one thing the story shows about ordinary participation in evil that Arendt's report, precisely because it is organized around a single defendant in a courtroom, cannot easily show — the lottery is everyone's procedure, and the victim is drawn by chance from among the executioners, so that complicity and vulnerability are shared by the same people. Then say whether this is a genuine addition to the philosophical debate or a point that argument alone could have made just as well.
