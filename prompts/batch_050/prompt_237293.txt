In my philosophy of history seminar, I assigned Braudel's Mediterranean and asked students to defend his method. Nearly all of them reached for the same image Braudel himself favors: history is a sea. Political events, battles, and great individuals are the foam on the surface — visible, noisy, gone in an instant — while the real explanatory work is done by the deep, slow-moving waters: geography, climate, trade routes, durable habits of mind. One student pushed the image hard. Because the foam cannot steer the sea, she argued, no individual decision and no single event can meaningfully alter the course of history; biography-centered history is therefore not merely incomplete but mistaken, and figures like Philip II are at best passengers who mistook themselves for pilots.

Audit this analogy as it is actually being used. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the sea's depths, its tides, its foam, and what in the historical domain does the mapping simply leave out? Then locate the breaks: (i) water cannot act back on the forces that move it — can events act back on structures? Consider cases where a surface event reconfigures the deep layer itself (the Columbian exchange rearranging ecology and trade, a revolution redrawing property relations). (ii) The image treats the three layers as independent clocks running at different speeds; is historical time really stratified that way, or is the stratification partly an artifact of the scale at which the historian chooses to look? Finally, tell me what the picture smuggles in — does it import a hydraulic determinism that Braudel's evidence does not warrant? Does my student's strong conclusion (individuals cannot matter) survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the image doing the argumentative work all along? If a better image is available — geological strata, a rope of many strands, something else — propose it and say what it captures that the sea does not. Please write your answer in American English.
