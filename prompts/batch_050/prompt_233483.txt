I hold a view I'll call the division-of-labor defense of classical epistemic logic. On this view, the so-called problem of logical omniscience rests on a mistake about what epistemic logic is for. A Hintikka-style logic of knowledge and belief — modal operators over possible-worlds models, with the agents' attitudes closed under entailment — was never a psychology of belief storage or a prediction about what reasoners will conclude. It is a theory of what an agent's information commits them to: the propositions that are true in every world the agent has not ruled out. Complaints that real agents fail to see consequences, miss trivial equivalences, or cannot decide undecidable statements confuse competence with performance, roughly the way objections that real gases deviate from the ideal gas law confuse a constitutive idealization with an empirical generalization. On the division of labor I defend, departures from deductive closure belong to a separate theory of resource-bounded inference — of proof search, attention, and computational cost — and the familiar remedies offered within epistemic logic itself (awareness filters, impossible worlds, step-indexed reasoning that grants agents one inference per time unit) are category errors: they rebuild the logic to mimic performance failures, and in doing so they sacrifice precisely the clean notion of implicit information that made the logic explanatory in the first place.

What would count against this view? I want a map of its exposure, not a rehash of the standard omniscience puzzle. Concretely:

1. Which arguments would be genuinely decisive — showing not merely that bounded agents violate closure (I concede that freely) but that the commitment-theoretic reading fails on its own terms? For instance: cases where an agent's considered commitments are themselves non-deductive, so that even the idealized "what their information commits them to" comes apart from closure; or a demonstration that the notion of implicit information the defense relies on collapses into a mere relabeling of logical consequence, so that the logic turns out to be a theory of entailment wearing epistemic clothes and the "solution" dissolves the subject matter rather than saving it.

2. Which arguments would be merely awkward — pressures the defense can absorb by further clarifying the competence/performance line, even if each clarification costs something? Please track the cumulative cost: at what point does the defense stop protecting a theory and start protecting a label?

3. A specific worry I want addressed head-on: explanations of action often need to distinguish logically equivalent contents (an agent may act on a theorem under one formulation and fail to act under an equivalent one). If the idealized notion of information cannot draw such distinctions, does that show the idealization is unfit for a purpose epistemic logic is standardly asked to serve — or does it show only that content-individuation is a job for a different theory, which is exactly my division-of-labor claim restated? Please adjudicate rather than split the difference.

4. What would defenders of the idealization defense themselves concede as counting against it — not what critics of epistemic logic say, but what someone sympathetic to the competence/performance distinction would have to give up if certain results obtained? Are there formal results (about the structure of rational commitment, or about the impossibility of cleanly separating logic from inference) that would force a retreat?

5. Finally, an honest verdict on falsifiability. A normative or constitutive idealization cannot be refuted by behavior, so there is a real sense in which my position risks nothing from any psychological finding whatsoever. Sort out whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability, whether unfalsifiability is even the right charge for a view of this kind, and what — if not observations — could still rationally move me.

For the record, the thing I would find most persuasive is a single well-chosen case where the logic delivers the wrong verdict about what an idealized, unbounded agent is committed to — not a case where a bounded agent fails to draw a conclusion. If such cases don't exist, I want to know that too.
