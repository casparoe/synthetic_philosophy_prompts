I'm a graduate student in a philosophy seminar on formal epistemology, and I've spent the term watching the debate over imprecise probabilities go in circles, both in the papers we read and in our own discussions. The basic positions are clear enough. On one side, the precise Bayesian holds that a rational agent's credences must be representable by a single probability function: the classic betting and accuracy arguments purport to show that anything short of precision leaves you exploitable or dominated, and when the evidence is thin or ambiguous the right response is simply to pick a prior, since action requires a determinate answer anyway. On the other side, advocates of imprecise credences hold that evidence sometimes warrants only a range or a set of probability functions, that forcing a precise number onto near-total ignorance misrepresents what the evidence actually supports, and that phenomena like Ellsberg-type choices and the dilation of imprecise credences under seemingly irrelevant information show that precision is doing work the evidence never licensed. What strikes me is that both sides seem to agree on all the cases and all the formal results. Nobody denies that an agent with a set of credences can be Dutch-booked less straightforwardly, or that a precise agent must commit to a prior; the Ellsberg preferences are agreed data, interpreted in opposite directions. Yet nothing seems to move anyone.

My own hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis, is that the two camps are quietly answering different questions under one name: the precise Bayesian is asking what attitudes an agent must have in order to act coherently, while the imprecise camp is asking what attitudes a body of evidence entitles an agent to have, and that these questions can come apart without either side making a mistake on its own terms. But I can't tell whether this is right, or whether the real fracture is somewhere else entirely: a verbal disagreement about what the word credence picks out, a hidden empirical disagreement about whether human beings actually harbor interval-valued attitudes, a disagreement about whether representation theorems justify norms or merely describe dispositions, or a bedrock clash of intuitions about whether ignorance is something a formal model should be able to express.

So what I'm asking for is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on who's right. What kind of disagreement is this, structurally, and what evidence from how the two sides actually argue supports that diagnosis? Is the impasse a sign that one question is ill-posed, or just that two well-posed questions are being traded under a single label? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves part of the dispute, a formal result that would force one side to give ground, an empirical finding that would settle a hidden factual premise, or a frank admission that the two frameworks are answers to different questions and should stop being billed as rivals?
