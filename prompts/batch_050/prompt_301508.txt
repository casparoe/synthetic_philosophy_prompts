I'm a program officer at a mid-sized research funding agency, and our founding statute says we fund work on computers and computational processes, which was clear enough in 1985 and is a total mess now. Every cycle I get maybe 40 proposals where eligibility turns on whether the thing being studied counts as a computer or a computation: a neuromorphic chip, sure, but also DNA strand displacement setups, a slime mold that solves mazes, soap films that settle into shapes matching solutions to network-routing problems, analog optical rigs, and one guy who wants to study weather systems as computations in themselves. My committee isn't philosophers, they're engineers and one lawyer, and we need a consistent way to say yes or no at the eligibility stage.

You're a philosopher of mind who's also spent enough time around engineers to know what they'll do to a vague checklist. I want you to take what the philosophy of computation actually says about when a physical system performs a computation and turn it into a decision procedure we can run on a proposal in under an hour. Before writing it, brainstorm the ways a bad procedure fails here, because that's the part I keep getting wrong.

A few things the procedure has to deal with. There's apparently an old argument that without constraints you can describe literally anything, a rock, a wall, as performing any computation you like, just by clever redescription of its states. If that's right our statute is meaningless, so the procedure needs whatever fix philosophers have landed on, I gather it's something about the causal structure of the state transitions and what the system would have done under other inputs, but I need that translated into questions my committee can actually ask. There's also the question of whether computing requires the states to represent or mean something, or whether it's purely formal, because the slime mold people lean hard on representation-talk. And tell me where the procedure has to stop and hand things to human judgment, I don't want false precision.

Then run the finished procedure on three cases: a trained neural network that nobody can interpret, the soap-film routing setup, and a thunderstorm. One of these is going to give an answer some of my committee won't like and I'd rather know which. Also say plainly what the procedure can't settle.

Write the whole thing as continuous prose, like a long email to a colleage. No headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, because we print everything and the formatting gets mangled anyway.
