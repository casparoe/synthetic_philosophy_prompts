I work in formal semantics and decision theory, and I'm fluent in the standard apparatus: possible-worlds models, similarity orderings and Stalnaker/Lewis-style counterfactuals, conditional probability, and interventionist talk of causal structure. I want to understand the theological debate over Molinism -- the doctrine of middle knowledge, i.e., God's pre-volitional knowledge of so-called counterfactuals of creaturely freedom -- and the grounding objection pressed against it, the objection that such counterfactuals cannot be true because there is nothing that makes them true. Please translate the core claims of the debate into my vocabulary, as faithfully as the translation allows: the distinction between God's natural, middle, and free knowledge (I suspect this maps onto an ordering of explanatory or logical priority rather than temporal stages, and I want to know if that's right); the claim that would-counterfactuals of free action have determinate truth values independent of the divine will; the grounding objection itself, construed as a demand that a true counterfactual requires a truthmaker in the actual world; and the standard Molinist replies, including appeals to indeterministic events whose outcomes are unsettled yet about which counterfactuals arguably remain true. Then give me a residue report, organized in three parts: which claims translate cleanly into the language of worlds, conditionals, and probability; which translate only with distortion, and what exactly gets distorted; and which have no expression at all in that vocabulary -- I expect candidates here include the very notion of metaphysical grounding or truthmaking, since my framework is purely modal and probabilistic, and perhaps the personal/providential character of the divine knowledge the doctrine is meant to secure. The verdict matters to me: does the residue show that modal-and-probabilistic vocabulary is missing a genuine conceptual resource (grounding as something not reducible to modality), or does it show that the theological debate is trading on notions that feel substantive only because they resist formalization? Finally, please keep the epistemic bookkeeping explicit: separate what all parties to the dispute actually concede -- for instance, that the grounding objection goes through if counterfactuals of freedom require truthmakers of a certain kind -- from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on this, such as whether any satisfactory theory of grounding exists at all, and do not present the Molinist, the open-theist, or the Thomist resolution as the settled view when it is not.
