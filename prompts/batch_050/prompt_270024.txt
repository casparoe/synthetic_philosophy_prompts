I'd like you to respond as a philosopher of language with expertise in conceptual ethics and the theory of explication, and to think carefully — brainstorming candidate concepts from the literature before committing to an answer. I've been reading about projects that aim to improve our concepts rather than merely describe how we use them: Carnap-style explication, ameliorative analyses of social categories, precisification in the sciences. I keep noticing a downstream phenomenon I don't have a name for, and I'd like to know whether the literature does. The phenomenon is this: when a community deliberately revises one of its concepts and the revision succeeds, something odd happens to collective memory. The revised concept comes to be treated as though it had been in place all along. People stop saying "we decided to draw the boundary here" and start saying "the boundary was always here; earlier speakers were simply mistaken." The element of choice involved in the revision gets retrospectively converted into a discovery, and the fact that there was ever a decision to be made disappears from view.

A few concrete instances of what I mean. After astronomers voted in 2006 on a definition that excluded Pluto, I heard people say Pluto was never really a planet, as though a fact about the solar system had settled the matter — when in fact the vote was a stipulation about where to draw a category line, and a different stipulation was available and seriously considered. After the legal and colloquial scope of "marriage" broadened in many countries, partisans on both sides began insisting that the word always meant what they preferred, each treating the settled usage as the word's eternal content. And in physics textbooks, the modern distinction between heat and temperature is presented as though pre-modern speakers who used one word for both were just being muddle-headed, rather than working with a genuinely unified everyday concept that was later deliberately split. In each case, a successful piece of conceptual repair erases its own fingerprints, and the engineered boundary gets re-described as a natural one that was merely uncovered.

Is there an established name for this retrospective naturalization of a successful revision? It seems adjacent to several things but identical to none: the familiar charge that revisers merely "change the subject" is a criticism leveled during the revision, whereas my phenomenon is what happens after the revision wins; Whig history of science is about narratives of inevitable progress, not about the semantics of individual terms; and ordinary semantic drift is unplanned, while my cases involve deliberate engineering whose deliberateness is then forgotten. If there is no existing term, please coin one, define it precisely, and mark its boundaries with care: does the phenomenon occur if the engineers themselves forget that a choice was made, or only when later generations do? Does it occur when a revision only half-succeeds, or is forgetting the choice actually constitutive of full success — in which case, is it a failure mode at all, or just what winning looks like from the inside? Please don't give me a bare label; what I want is the carving — what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
