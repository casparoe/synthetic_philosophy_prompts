I co-teach an undergrad applied ethics course, about 24 students, one 75-minute slot. I want an in-class game that makes students feel, in their gut, that imposing a risk on someone without asking can be wrong even when nothing bad actually happens -- the drunk driver who gets home safe, the company whose dodgy chemical never leaks. Students reliably say "no harm, no foul" and I want the game to break that before I ever lecture on it.

Design a game with secret individual choices and dice or cards, where players can boost their own points by quietly adding risk to other players' outcomes, some risks materialize and some don't, and crucially the reveals happen in a way where people get mad at lucky risk-imposers too, not just unlucky ones. Points not money, but make the points matter a bit (extra credit raffle or whatever). Include: full rules, materials list, the payoff/odds structure, how many rounds and how the information should be revealed round by round, and a debrief plan that maps specific moments from the game onto the ex ante / ex post distinction, the idea of a "mere risk" wrong, and why consent changes everything (maybe a final round where risk-selling is allowed, to show the difference). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time -- e.g. students optimizing weirdly, or the dice being kind so nobody feels anything -- and how to fix it.

At the end, give me a table with columns: Game moment | What students typically feel | Concept it maps to | Discussion question to ask about it. One row per key beat of the session.
