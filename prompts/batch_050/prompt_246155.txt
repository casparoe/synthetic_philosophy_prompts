I'm a junior and we just finished a unit on Nuremberg and the Tokyo trials in history, and its turned into this huge running argument in my debate club. People keep throwing around "victors' justice" and "you can't punish someone for something that wasn't a crime yet," and I realized I don't actually know what I think. So here are my gut verdicts -- tell me what principle they're adding up to, if any.

1. A camp guard who did horrific things that were completely legal under Nazi law -- required, even. Prosecuting him: fine. "My government said it was ok" doesn't help you when the thing is mass murder.

2. A judge in that regime who sentenced people under those same laws and honestly believed he was applying the law. Also fine to prosecute, honestly maybe more than the guard.

3. The charge "crimes against humanity" basically didn't exist before the tribunal wrote it into the charter after the war. Normally I'd call that cheating, but here I think it was fine, because the actual acts (murder, torture) were already crimes in Germany itself. They just renamed it.

4. But if my city passed a law next month against, I don't know, some app, and then fined people for using it last year, that's obviously wrong. Everyone agrees retroactive law is wrong. So now I'm confused about why 3 felt fine.

5. The Allies firebombed Dresden and Tokyo and killed insane numbers of civilians, and nobody was ever tried for any of it. This genuinely bothers me and makes the whole thing smell like victors' justice. But I still don't think it means the guard from case 1 should walk free. Two wrongs and all that.

6. Flip it: if Germany had won and put Allied commanders on trial for Dresden, with German judges and a charter they wrote after the fact, I'm pretty sure I'd call that fake justice. And I cannot tell you what the difference is besides who won. This one keeps me up.

7. At Tokyo alot of the weight was on "conspiracy to wage aggressive war" -- starting the war was itself the crime. Weirdly that feels shakier to me than prosecuting some random guard, even though the planners are obviously way guiltier as people. Feels backwards and I don't know why.

My tentative principle, which probably doesn't work: retroactive prosecution is legit when the act was already criminal by the ordinary standards of the defendant's own society, and not legit when the "crime" is basically losing, or doing what the winners also did. Case 7 seems to break it though, since waging war wasn't an ordinary crime anywhere, and case 6 breaks something deeper -- like if the principle is right it should be winner-independent, and mine clearly isn't?

So: is there one principle that gets all seven, or close? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, and is dropping it a cost or actually a relief? What does it say about cases I didn't list -- like a civil war where both sides committed atrocities and whoever wins gets to run the tribunal, or prosecuting a 95 year old ex-guard today? And seperately: is "victors' justice" even an objection to the verdicts, or just an objection to who gets to run the trial? I keep conflating those and I think it might be the whole problem.
