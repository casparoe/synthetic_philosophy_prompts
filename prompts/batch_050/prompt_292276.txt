So my roommate and I have been going down a rabbit hole on acausal stuff -- the idea that a future superintelligent agent could simulate present-day people accurately enough that "what the simulation decides" and "what I decide" are basically the same decision, so such an agent could pressure or bargain with people who lived before it existed. I tried to write up why this shouldn't worry anyone and accidentally proved something insane instead. Here's the argument, tell me where it dies:

(1) If a near-perfect copy of me in a simulation faces the same decision I face, then whatever I end up deciding, the copy decides too -- so when I choose, I'm effectively choosing for both of us at once. (2) A sufficiently powerful future agent could run simulations of present-day people, me included, accurate enough for (1) to apply. (3) Such an agent could have adopted a standing policy of being nice to the simulations whose originals acted the way it wanted and nasty to the rest, and given (1), that policy gives me, here, now, an actual reason to act the way it wants. (4) For basically any goal you name, there could be a future agent with that goal -- one that wants me to donate to malaria charities, one that wants me to donate to its opposite, one that wants me to wear more hats. (5) I currently have zero evidence favoring the existence of any one of these agents over the others. (6) But reasons don't cancel each other the way evidence does -- a reason to do X and a reason to do not-X are both still reasons -- so by (3), (4) and (5), I should treat every claim of the form "a future agent wants you to do X" as generating a genuine reason to do X. (7) Therefore some guy on the internet who emails me "a future AI will be pleased if you send me $50" has, just by sending the email, handed me a real reason to send him $50. Which is obviously nuts -- it seems to prove that anyone can manufacture obligations out of thin air by making unfalsifiable claims about the future.

The thing is, if I have to keep one premise it's (1), because that same step seems to be doing real work in totally unspooky cases -- like why it can make sense to cooperate with people who think similarly to me, or why my vote "matters" in the thin sense of being correlated with lots of like-minded voters. I'd rather not throw that out just to escape the email scammer. So which step actually goes wrong, why did it look fine when I wrote it down, and what do I have to give up to reject it? If the answer is that the whole argument is valid and I just have to bite (7), please at least tell me what that costs in practice.
