I'm a junior taking a seminar on existential risk and collective action, and we've just spent two weeks on the unilateralist's curse: the idea that when many actors can each unilaterally take an irreversible step affecting everyone, and they hold divergent estimates of whether that step is wise, the step tends to get taken even when the majority would oppose it, because the most optimistic (or most reckless) actor is the one who acts, and this gets worse as the number of capable actors grows. For my final paper I want to read Liu Cixin's Remembrance of Earth's Past trilogy through this lens, and I'd like help working out whether the reading holds.

Here's where I think the fit is obvious: Ye Wenjie at Red Coast, answering the transmission even after being explicitly warned that a reply will bring invasion, is a nearly pure case — one person, divergent private judgment, irreversible consequences for all of humanity, no mechanism for anyone else to veto her. And the dark forest itself looks like the curse scaled to cosmic proportions: if launching a strike is cheap, detection is the only trigger, and any one of millions of civilizations can act alone, then the most paranoid actor sets everyone's fate, and silence becomes the only rational defense. In a sense the trilogy's universe is just what the curse predicts when you remove every institutional check.

But I'm more interested in where the novels complicate or invert the framework, and I'd like you to push on three places. First, the deterrence era: humanity's answer to the curse is to concentrate unilateral capacity in a single person, the swordholder. That seems to solve the many-actors problem by fiat, but at the cost of making everything depend on one individual's psychology — as the succession from Luo Ji to Cheng Xin shows. Is that a genuine cure for the curse, or does it just trade the curse for a single point of failure, and does the trilogy realize which trade it's making? Second, Cheng Xin's refusal to act: the curse literature treats restraint as the default good, but the trilogy makes unilateral restraint catastrophic when deterrence requires credible willingness to destroy. Is there a coherent way to state when unilateral action is a curse and when unilateral inaction is, or does the fiction expose an asymmetry the formal argument flattens? Third, the broadcast of Trisolaris's coordinates by the crew of Gravity, which is decided by vote rather than by a lone actor — is Liu suggesting collective deliberation is the antidote, or showing that even deliberation can't escape the logic, since the information and capability still exist?

Finally: what, if anything, does dramatizing all this add that the formal statement of the curse doesn't capture? I'm tempted to say it's something about the distribution of temperaments — the curse is abstract about who the most optimistic actor is and why, while the novels make you live inside Ye Wenjie's despair and Cheng Xin's scruples — but I want that claim tested, not just echoed. Where the reading strains, say so; I'd rather the paper concede the mismatch than force the trilogy into a framework it resists.
