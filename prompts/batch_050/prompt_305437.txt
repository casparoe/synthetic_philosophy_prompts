I've been arguing on and off for months with a friend of mine who's doing a PhD in history, mostly about those big sweeping books that claim to explain everything, and I've noticed she has a pretty consistent pattern of which ones she respects and which she rolls her eyes at. Let me lay out her verdicts as best I remember them. Diamond's thesis in Guns, Germs and Steel, that the shape and orientation of continents plus the suite of domesticable species explains why Eurasia ended up on top: she thinks that's basically right, a genuine explanation. Harari's bit in Sapiens about the Agricultural Revolution being a raw deal for humans and wheat having domesticated us rather than the other way around: she calls that clever storytelling, not history, just-so stuff. Turchin's forecast, made back in 2010, that instability in the US and western Europe would build through the decade and peak around 2020: she says that's genuinely impressive and counts as real evidence for cliodynamics. Graeber and Wengrow arguing in The Dawn of Everything that plenty of early cities and complex societies got along fine without hierarchy: unconvinced, she says they cherry-pick their favorite cases. And Kyle Harper's argument that climate change and pandemics did serious work in bringing down Rome: she buys it.

So here's the new case. A colleague of hers just published a book arguing that the Reformation caused northern Europe's economic takeoff -- not as a vague Weberian vibe, but with city-level data: adoption of printing, literacy rates, and growth figures, with actual econometrics comparing Protestant and Catholic cities that were otherwise similar. It's ideas doing the causal work, but it's quantified up to the eyeballs. Will she accept it?
