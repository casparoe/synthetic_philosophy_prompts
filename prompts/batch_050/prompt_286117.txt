I spent forty years as an organic chemist and now that I'm retired I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole, mostly by accident (my wife got me a book of Epicurus as a joke and I took it seriously). Anyway I tried to reconstruct the Epicurean argument that death is nothing to us as rigorously as I could, and I think I've overshot, because my version seems to prove a bunch of things that even I, sympathetic as I am, can't swallow. Here it is:

(1) Something can be bad for a person only if there is some time at which that person is doing worse than they otherwise would be. Call it the timing condition -- it just seemed like the natural way to say a harm has to "land" somewhere, like a measurement has to have an apparatus. (2) Before you die, death hasn't taken anything from you yet; you're alive and well. (3) After you die there is no longer a subject around at all, so there is no time at which you are doing worse. (4) Therefore there is no time at which death is bad for you, and a harm that is bad at no time is no harm. So far, so Epicurean. But then: (5) the same timing condition applies to anything whose badness would only materialize after you're gone -- your children struggling in old age, your life's work being discredited, your name being dragged through the mud after the funeral. There's no time at which you're around to be worse off for those either. (6) So none of those can be bad for you. (7) And going the other direction: the obvious fix is to say death is bad because of what it deprives you of, comparing how your life actually goes with how it would have gone. But then the years before I was born deprive me of exactly the same way -- I could have been born in 1930 instead of 1947 and had seventeen more years of good life -- so late birth must be just as bad as early death. And worse, if the never-conceived are deprived of whole lives, we're doing something monstrous to countless possible people every time we don't have another child.

I refuse to believe (6) and (7). My reading group says the argument is fine and I should just become a proper Epicurean about posthumous events too, but I think something broke earlier. Which step do you reject? Please tell me why that step looked innocent -- I clearly fell for it -- and what it costs to reject it, i.e. what other ordinary judgments about harm you now have to give up or reinterpret. If the honest answer is that I do have to swallow (6) to save (1), say so, but then tell me what the cheapest package deal is: I'd rather pay with (1) than with my grandchildren.
