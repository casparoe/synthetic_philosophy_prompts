I'm a political theorist and in six weeks I'm giving a talk at a development-policy institute defending a paper that argues a big chunk of what Western anti-corruption standards file under "corruption" -- kin hiring, gift-giving to officials, patron-client networks -- is better understood as morally binding obligation, and that anti-corruption campaigns which ignore this distinction don't just fail, they actively wreck the informal safety nets people rely on where the state is absent or predatory. My constructive proposal is a two-part test: a practice only counts as corrupt if (1) the participants themselves can't avow it openly and have to keep it hidden or euphemized, and (2) participation is extracted under some kind of threat rather than extended as a relationship. On my test a lot of textbook "clientelism" fails one or both clauses and shouldn't be prosecuted as corruption, while plenty of formally legal stuff (revolving doors, some lobbying) arguably passes both.

The room: mostly political scientists and development economists who work on anti-corruption -- people who build or use corruption perception indices, consult for aid agencies, write about rule of law and state capacity. Plus two or three anthropologists who work on gift exchange and patronage and will be broadly on my side but will have their own complaints about my test being too tidy. And probably one legal scholar who works on foreign bribery enforcement, who's there to be a pain.

Prep me for the Q&A. I want the objections I'll actually get from *this* crowd, not the ones a moral philosopher would raise in a seminar -- the economist versions about measurable harm and fungibility, the index people about how you'd ever measure or compare across countries under my definition, the legal scholar about enforceability, the anthropologists about whatever blind spot they'll spot. Order them by likelihood times damage, phrase each the way the person who'd raise it would phrase it, and for each give me a roughly two-minute reply, what I should concede up front, and a note on where the reply is weakest. Then tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks (I suspect it has something to do with who gets left out of these gift networks entirely) and what to do if it comes.
