I'm drafting a column on decision theory for a general readership and want to get Parfit's hitchhiker case right. As I understand it, the stranded traveller is rescued only because the driver can somehow detect that he would pay after reaching safety, and the celebrated lesson is that it can be rational to possess a disposition whose actual exercise would be irrational. But since the whole scenario hinges on the driver being able to read the traveller's mind — something no real rescuer, creditor, or negotiation partner can do — doesn't the argument lose all practical force, reducing to the mundane advice that one should cultivate a convincing reputation for trustworthiness rather than genuinely trustworthy dispositions? If so, is there any real-world decision in which the distinction between seeming and being disposed to pay actually matters?
