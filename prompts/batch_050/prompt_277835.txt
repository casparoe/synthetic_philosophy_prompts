I run the model review group at a mid-sized consumer lender. Roughly a dozen credit-risk and pricing models cross my desk each year, and each needs a fairness sign-off from me before it goes to the compliance committee, which has neither the time nor the training to adjudicate statistical disputes. The recurring problem is that my analysts come back with models that pass one fairness criterion and fail another, and then the review stalls in argument about which criterion matters. I've read enough to grasp the shape of the underlying impossibility: when two groups have different base rates for the outcome being predicted, a risk score generally cannot simultaneously be calibrated within each group, equalize false positive rates across groups, and equalize false negative rates across groups — at least one of the three has to give, except in degenerate cases. So "make the model fair" is not a coherent instruction, and I want my team to stop pretending it is.

What I'd like you to do is turn that impossibility result into a written procedure an analyst can actually run on a model before it reaches me: what to compute and in what order, how to determine which of the incompatible criteria is being satisfied or sacrificed, what questions must be put to the business and legal side because the choice among criteria is a value judgment rather than a technical one, under what conditions the procedure terminates in a rejection of the model outright, and how the whole thing should be documented so the compliance committee can see the trade-off that was made rather than a false claim of fairness. Be explicit about which steps are mechanical and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, since I intend to audit against that distinction. The procedure should also say what it cannot settle — the cases where the honest output is that a person, not a checklist, has to decide.

Then run the procedure once on this hypothetical: a default-prediction score that is well calibrated within two applicant groups whose base default rates are twelve percent and six percent respectively, deployed at a single approval threshold, where the group's data scientists report that the false negative rate is equal across groups but the false positive rate is roughly twice as high in the lower-base-rate group. State which criterion is being sacrificed, what the procedure instructs the analyst to do next, and whether the model survives. Present everything as exactly five numbered items, each no more than two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: items 1 through 3 give the stages of the procedure in the order an analyst executes them, item 4 lists the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and who must exercise it, and item 5 delivers the worked application and its outcome.
