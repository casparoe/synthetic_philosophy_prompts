I occasionally referee for a combinatorics journal, and I keep running into an epistemological classification problem that I suspect already has a name, though I have not been able to find it stated cleanly. The situation: a theorem is announced whose proof consists of a short, humanly readable reduction plus an enormous case analysis that no human being has carried out or ever could — hundreds or thousands of configurations, each disposed of by a program. Appel and Haken on the four-colour theorem, and Hales on the Kepler conjecture, are the obvious exhibits. What interests me is the shape of the justification my colleagues offer for believing such results. They check the reduction by hand. They review the algorithm rather than its output. And then they do things that look borrowed from experimental science rather than from mathematics: they re-run the computation on different hardware, have an independent team re-implement the algorithm in another language, compare outputs. Hales's own response to his referees' fatigue was not to produce a more readable proof but to have the whole argument machine-verified in a formal proof assistant — which shifts the trust again, now onto the verifier's small trusted kernel.

So the resulting epistemic state seems to fall into none of the three categories I was taught. It is not ordinary proof-knowledge: nobody can survey the whole argument, so the justification cannot be "I followed the steps." It is not testimony in the standard sense either: the computer made no assertion and cannot lie or misremember the way a testifier can; what is trusted is a causal process, not a speaker. And it is not straightforwardly empirical knowledge: the conclusion is necessitated by the axioms, and what gets "replicated" is a deterministic computation, not a measurement.

My questions, in order of importance. First, does this epistemic kind — knowledge of a mathematical truth that depends essentially on the reliability of an inanimate process — have an established name? I know the phrase "unsurveyable proof," but I am unsure whether that names the proof-object or the epistemic position of the person who accepts it, and whether the literature keeps the two apart. Second, how is the category standardly distinguished from testimonial knowledge, given that trusting a verifier looks structurally very similar to trusting a colleague who says "I checked the cases" — is the disanalogy (no assertion, no possibility of insincerity) taken to matter, or is the computer just folded into testimony? Third, where is the boundary supposed to lie? In particular: the classification of the finite simple groups, which no individual can survey but every part of which was surveyed by someone; probabilistic primality results, where the logic is humanly checkable but a tiny probability of error remains; and an ordinary textbook proof that I personally have not read but trust on the basis of the refereeing process. Which of these fall inside the category and which outside?

If there is no established name, I would rather you coined one than forced the phenomenon into the nearest pigeonhole: give a definition, mark the boundary cases above, and say what the phenomenon is not. The label matters less to me than the carving.
