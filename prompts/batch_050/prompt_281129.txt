Last year I read about a methodological rule scientists use when interpreting animal behavior: never explain an animal's action by appeal to a higher, more sophisticated mental capacity when it can be adequately explained by a lower one. I like the rule -- I don't want to read grief into my dog's behavior every time I leave the house -- but I turned it into an argument whose conclusion I can't accept, and I can't find the weak point. (1) One should never explain a piece of behavior by positing a more complex mental process when a less complex one would account for it just as well. (2) For any observed animal behavior, a lower-level explanation in terms of instinct, conditioning, or simple association is always available. (3) Therefore we should never attribute reasoning, planning, or genuine emotion to animals. (4) But the principle in (1) mentions no species, and for any human behavior a lower-level explanation in terms of reinforcement history and learned associations is likewise always available. (5) Therefore we should never attribute reasoning or emotion to other humans either. (6) That is absurd. (7) So at least one earlier step must be rejected. I'd like to keep some version of the parsimony idea, but I want to know which premise or inference to give up, why that step seemed plausible in the first place, and what rejecting it costs.
