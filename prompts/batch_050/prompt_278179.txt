I’m a retired behavioural ecologist who has recently started reading philosophy, and I’m drafting a short essay called “Morgan’s Canon Is Not a Simplicity Meter.” Could you comment on the draft plan below? Please start with a one-sentence overall verdict, then give me numbered comments, and keep the whole response under 400 words. I’m mainly interested in whether the argument is philosophically confused, which example is weakest, and what I should cut or add. No need for citations or a bibliography.

My draft notes:

1. Morgan’s canon is usually presented as telling us not to explain animal behaviour through higher psychological faculties when lower faculties can explain it. That sounds sensible, but I think the familiar version hides two problems.

2. First, “lower” and “higher” are not independent measures of simplicity. A complicated learned routine may require many more assumptions than a single flexible inference. Calling the learned routine “lower” does not automatically make it simpler.

3. Second, “can explain it” does too much work. A sufficiently elaborate associative-learning story can probably be made to fit almost any observed behaviour after the fact, just as an anthropomorphic story can. The real question should be which account makes risky predictions about what the animal will do when the situation is changed.

4. I want to replace the usual lower/higher ranking with what I’m tentatively calling “process parsimony”: prefer the account requiring fewer independently supported psychological mechanisms, but count predictive failures against it even if it sounds less anthropomorphic. So neither anthropomorphism nor its avoidance gets a free pass.

5. Example one: a dog looks “guilty” when its owner comes home to a torn-up cushion. One account gives the dog a concept of wrongdoing; another says it has learned to connect the cushion scene and the owner’s behaviour with punishment; a third says the owner is interpreting ordinary appeasement behaviour. I’d argue that experiments manipulating whether the owner can see the mess, and whether the dog caused it, distinguish these better than merely calling one explanation lower.

6. Example two: a bird re-hides food after being watched. We can explain this through imagining another bird’s future knowledge, through a learned rule about observers, or through sensitivity to present cues. I want to say the best test should change who watched, what the observer could see, and when the observer returns, rather than asking whether the behaviour feels sophisticated.

7. My conclusion is that Morgan’s canon is most useful as a warning against buying mental vocabulary without evidence, not as a general preference for unintelligent explanations. But it may also have encouraged the opposite mistake: treating a strained stimulus-response story as scientifically virtuous just because it withholds a mental state from an animal.
