I'm a software engineer on a team building conversational agents, and I've been trying to get a working grip on pragmatics because it keeps coming up when we diagnose why our system misreads users. I have no formal philosophy background, so I've been reading popular treatments. A blog post by a science writer summarized Grice's theory of implicature like this: "Grice's big idea is that what a speaker means is whatever they intend the hearer to come to believe. Conversation works because everyone tacitly obeys a cooperative principle — say what's true, be relevant, be brief, be orderly — and an implicature arises whenever a speaker deliberately breaks one of these rules: the hearer notices the violation and works backward to figure out what the speaker must really have meant. So implicature is essentially a decoding process, and it covers phenomena like metaphor, irony, and sarcasm, which Grice treats as rule-breakings too. Because the maxims derive from general principles of rational cooperation rather than from the conventions of any particular language, Grice showed that implicature works the same way in every language."

The post then draws a conclusion: since even ordinary, literal-sounding exchanges depend on this kind of inference, meaning in natural language is not rule-governed the way syntax is, and so formal semantics — the project of giving compositional rules for meaning — rests on a mistake. The writer presents this as the main lesson linguists took from Grice.

I've seen Grice invoked often enough in engineering-adjacent writing that I suspect parts of this are off, but I can't locate the problems myself. I'd like an accuracy audit with definite verdicts, not a survey of possible readings. Go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher who actually works on Grice would accept, what they'd regard as a subtle misrepresentation, and what is simply wrong. Some specific points where I'd like you to commit: Is speaker meaning really just whatever the speaker intends the hearer to believe, or does the theory demand more structure than that? Do implicatures arise only from deliberate violations, or is that conflating two routes the theory keeps apart? Is "decoding" the right model for how hearers recover implicatures? Is the universality claim one Grice established, hoped for, or that later work has complicated? Finally: does the conclusion about formal semantics follow from whatever survives of the accurate parts, or does it depend on the errors? I'd rather have a committed answer I can check against the primary text later than a menu of options.
