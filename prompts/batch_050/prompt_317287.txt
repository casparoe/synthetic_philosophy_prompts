I am preparing the background notes for a course unit on behavioral genetics and, before I finalize them, I would like an audit of the position I have landed on. Here are my six commitments:

(1) Heritability is a population-relative statistic: it measures the proportion of variance in a trait that is associated with genetic differences within a particular population at a particular time. It says nothing about individuals, and it says nothing about how fixed or malleable the trait is. A trait can be highly heritable and yet drastically altered by an environment outside the range currently observed — glasses for myopia being the standard exhibit.

(2) All the same, heritability estimates are informative for policy. If a trait such as educational attainment is highly heritable within a population, that is good evidence that the environmental factors that currently vary in that population — differences among schools, differences in parenting — are not doing much causal work, and that interventions which merely redistribute or equalize existing environmental variation are likely to disappoint.

(3) Twin studies license these estimates. The equal environments assumption — that identical and fraternal twins experience equally similar trait-relevant environments — is approximately correct, and the plausible violations of it do not materially bias heritability estimates upward.

(4) Gene–environment correlation is pervasive. Because people select, evoke, and modify their environments partly on the basis of their genotypes, a great deal of what gets counted as "environment" in these studies is in fact downstream of genetic differences.

(5) Within-group heritability has no implications whatsoever for the causes of between-group differences: a trait can be highly heritable within each of two populations while the gap between the populations is entirely environmental in origin.

(6) The gap between twin-study heritability and SNP-based heritability — the so-called missing heritability — is mainly a matter of rare and poorly tagged variants and will steadily close as genotyping and methods improve; it is not a sign that the twin estimates were inflated.

Which of these commitments are in genuine tension with one another, and which pairs only appear to be? Please rank any genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and for each one state precisely what would have to be given up — that is, which commitment must be revised or abandoned, and what the revision costs the overall picture. If a tension dissolves once a distinction is drawn, draw the distinction explicitly rather than merely asserting compatibility. Finally, if you had to pick the single revision that would clean up the most of the set, which would it be?
