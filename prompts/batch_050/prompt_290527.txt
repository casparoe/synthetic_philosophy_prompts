I run my high school's philosophy club — about 12 members, one 45-minute meeting after school. We've just started talking about radical skepticism, and most of the group assumes that if you can't give a justification for something (like there being an external world, or other people having minds), the rational thing is to doubt it. I want to push them toward Wittgenstein's idea in On Certainty: that some commitments aren't conclusions we justify but hinges our practices of doubting and justifying already turn on — and that demanding justification for them isn't rigor, it's a confusion about what justification is.

Design an activity that makes them feel this rather than just hear it. I want the rules or roles to put them in a situation where they discover, in the middle of doing something, that they cannot actually operate while treating every commitment as up for grabs — before anyone names the concept. A few specific requests:

- Give concrete rules and any roles or materials. It should work with 12 people in a classroom, no special equipment.
- Include a debrief plan that maps particular moments in the activity onto the hinge idea — for instance, the difference between a proposition someone refuses to doubt and a proposition that never comes up as a candidate for doubt at all, and why Wittgenstein thinks hinges are shown in what we do rather than stated as beliefs we hold.
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (e.g., members treating it as a debate about whether skepticism is true, or one person performing skepticism theatrically), and how to handle each.
- Be honest about what the activity can't reproduce — for example, whether anything in a 45-minute game can capture the way hinges are invisible in real life and only become visible when someone actually tries to doubt them.
