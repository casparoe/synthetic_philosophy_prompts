You are a philosopher of history and historiography with additional expertise in climate history and historical epidemiology. Think carefully before answering: I would like you to begin by brainstorming the full space of demands one might place on such a theory, and only then consolidate your results into a final, ordered list.

Context: over recent decades a body of scholarship has argued that non-human forces — pathogens, climatic shifts, volcanic eruptions — should be treated not as mere backdrop or stage scenery but as historical actors in their own right. Standard exhibits include the dust-veil event of 536 CE and the Justinianic Plague that followed it, the Black Death's reshaping of European labour relations, the Little Ice Age, and the 1815 Tambora eruption with the harvest failures of the following years. Critics reply that such talk either trivially restates the platitude that nature has causal influence on human affairs, or slides into an environmental determinism that writes human agency out of history.

Before I read any further contributions to this debate, I want the specification first. Write the desiderata that a theory of non-human causal agency in history must meet in order to count as adequate — where the purpose I have in mind is a theory good enough to guide actual historiographical practice, not merely one that licenses provocative framing.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Your list should address at least the following pressure points, though I expect you to add requirements of your own:

1. The causal-background problem. Every event has indefinitely many physical preconditions — the oxygen supply at every battle, the gravitational constant throughout the French Revolution. A theory must explain what could make a bacillus or an eruption a salient historical cause without thereby making everything salient.
2. The determinism boundary. It must remain possible to say that plague or climate constrained and reshaped human affairs without entailing that outcomes were fixed by environmental variables. State precisely what distinguishes causal weight from causal sufficiency in this context.
3. Agency without intention. The theory must either explain in what sense an entity lacking intentions, beliefs, and purposes can be an actor, or else replace the metaphor with something more precise — and say candidly what, if anything, is lost by the replacement.
4. The attribution asymmetry. Non-human causes are credited with collapses and catastrophes far more often than with stability, prosperity, or flourishing. A theory should say whether this asymmetry is a bias in our historiographical habits or tracks something real about how causation works.
5. Scale. It must handle the difference between event-like causes (an eruption), slow variables (a multi-century climatic regime shift), and standing background conditions (an endemic disease pool), and say whether a single account of actorhood can cover all three or whether they demand separate treatment.
6. Consistency with the relevant sciences. The theory must not rest on claims that climate science or epidemiology has superseded, and it should state what historians owe the scientists whose findings they borrow — in terms of both accuracy and levels of confidence.
7. Historiographical payoff. Relabelling nature an actor should change what a historian actually does: which questions get asked, which evidence counts, which counterfactuals are worth testing. A theory that leaves practice entirely untouched fails this requirement even if it is metaphysically elegant.

Then do the following, which is the part I most want to see: mark which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of particular camps (for instance, traditional narrative historians, big-history writers, or environmental historians); flag any subset of the desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and argue the point rather than merely asserting it; and identify the single requirement that eliminates the largest share of existing work in this vein, with your reasoning. A table is acceptable for the desiderata list itself, but the unsatisfiability analysis and the knock-out criterion should be developed in prose. Finally, choose one historical case of your own and briefly test-drive the completed list against it, so that I can see the specification in action.
