I work as a research engineer at an ML lab, mostly on agents that monitor themselves: alongside the main perception-and-action stack, we train separate modules that read the system's own internal activations and produce confidence estimates, error flags, and verbal reports about what the system is currently doing. Over coffee last week, a friend who studied philosophy told me that our group has, without knowing it, been taking sides in an old dispute between first-order and higher-order theories of consciousness. She sketched it roughly: first-order views say a mental state is conscious if it represents the world in the right way and is available to the system's downstream consumers, while higher-order views say a state is conscious only when the subject also represents itself as being in that state — either by a thought about it or by something like an inner sense scanning it. I have essentially no philosophy training, but I found this immediately gripping, because it maps suspiciously well onto design decisions we argue about every week: whether the monitor is doing real work or is decorative, whether a state the monitor can't read is "there" for the agent in any sense that matters, and so on.

Here is what I'd like. Restate this dispute, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually work in: a perception module whose outputs feed a shared working memory that other subsystems read from; a distinct metacognitive monitor trained on the agent's own internal states, which can classify them correctly, misclassify them, and occasionally fire when there is no relevant state to classify; the distinction between information merely being present in a weight or activation somewhere and its being broadcast and consumed. Translate the main positions: the first-order claim that consciousness is a matter of a state representing the world and being poised for use; the higher-order-thought claim that a state becomes conscious in virtue of the subject representing itself as in it; and the inner-sense variant where the representation is quasi-perceptual rather than thought-like. Do the same for the pressure points between them: the problem of higher-order misrepresentation (the monitor says red, the first-order state is green — which determines what is experienced?), the case where a higher-order representation occurs with no first-order target at all, and the insistence by some higher-order theorists that the higher representation must be non-inferential and unmediated rather than inferred from behavior. Tell me what in my framework corresponds to each of these, and tell me where you have to fudge.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most. Which pieces of the philosophical dispute translate cleanly into architectural questions my team could in principle settle by experiment — for instance, ablating the monitor and checking what changes — and which pieces have no counterpart in anything my vocabulary can express? I suspect the central one is this: nothing in my framework even purports to address why any of this monitoring should be accompanied by experience rather than proceeding "in the dark," and I want your honest assessment of whether that is a limitation of my engineering vocabulary (something richer could capture it) or evidence that part of the philosophical debate concerns something with no functional correlate at all, in which case I wonder what the two sides are even disagreeing about. Finally: is my standing intuition — that consciousness just is whatever the monitor can read out and the workspace can broadcast — a fair compression of the first-order view, or does it quietly concede the disputed point to the higher-order side? Please keep the translation honest throughout: don't smuggle a winner into the mapping and then report that my framework favors one side.
