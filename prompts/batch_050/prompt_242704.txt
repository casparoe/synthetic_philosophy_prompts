I spent my career in experimental condensed matter physics and took up philosophy in retirement, mostly through reading groups and online lectures. Lately I have been stuck on the money-pump argument for the transitivity of preferences, and I have an idea for a paper I would like to write during a six-month visiting fellowship at our university's philosophy department. I need a one-page research proposal, and I would like you to draft it. Here is the idea, still half-formed. The standard argument says that an agent with cyclic preferences (preferring A to B, B to C, and C to A) can be offered a sequence of small paid swaps that returns her to where she started, minus money. But the argument seems to depend on the agent taking each trade as it comes, without recognizing the pattern. If she sees the whole sequence laid out in advance, she can refuse it or adopt a standing policy, without ever needing to revise her preferences. This suggests to me that money-pump arguments smuggle in an assumption about the agent's perspective: roughly, that she faces each choice locally and blindly. I want to investigate whether there is a principled way to state this assumption -- call it, for now, the locality condition -- and whether any condition of this kind is necessary for the argument to establish transitivity as a norm of individual rationality, rather than merely as a norm governing how one should respond to a known sequence.

The proposal should state this question precisely, explain why the existing debate -- between those who take money pumps to justify transitivity directly and those who argue that rational agents can simply decline pump sequences or adopt forward-looking strategies -- leaves it open, and lay out my approach. The approach I have in mind is analytical rather than formal: I would reconstruct the best-known versions of the argument, identify exactly which premises concern the agent's knowledge and deliberative standpoint, and test candidate formulations of the locality condition against cases (including sequential choices where each option is genuinely new to the agent, and choices made by institutions that deliberate in stages). Please explain in the proposal why this analytical approach rather than, say, a decision-theoretic formalization or an experimental study of how people actually respond to pumps -- I have my own reasons (the question is about the structure of a normative argument, not about actual behavior), but they need to be stated compellingly. The proposal should also say what a result would look like (a precise statement of the condition and a verdict on whether money-pump arguments can do without it), what would count as the project failing, and what is realistic to finish in six months given that I am a competent reader but a newcomer to the formal literature.

A few constraints. Do not include a bibliography or cite specific works by author and year -- I will fill in the references myself; refer to positions and arguments by description instead (for instance, the response that an agent can foresee the pump and refuse the first trade). Keep in mind that I am a physicist by training, so the proposal can assume fluency with probability and expected value but should not lean on technical philosophy I could not pick up in a semester. Finally, the fellowship committee includes non-philosophers, so please include a short passage explaining, without condescension, why a question about trading sequences and consistency matters beyond a technical corner of decision theory -- my own sense is that it bears on whenever we demand consistency from agents, human or artificial, who act over time, but I would like this said better than I can say it.
