So I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, and I got nerd-sniped by a podcast about whether math is invented or discovered, which led me down a rabbit hole to this old book I found for free online because it's public domain: Essays on the Theory of Numbers by Richard Dedekind (English translation from 1901, some guy named Beman translated it). I'm reading the second essay, the one about what numbers are.

There's a passage about two thirds of the way in (I want to say it's numbered §73 but I might be off by a bit) where he defines what the natural numbers actually are. I can't copy-paste because my version is a scanned PDF with weird OCR, so here's my paraphrase: he says take any simply infinite system ordered by a mapping (basically anything with a starting element and a successor-style operation that never loops or merges), and if you entirely neglect the special character of the elements -- you only keep that they're distinguishable from each other and the relations the ordering puts them in -- then those elements ARE the natural numbers. And he says something like the number theorems then follow from this.

I've read this like six times and I keep flip-flopping between thinking it's genius and thinking it's circular. Here's my attempt, in programmer terms: it sounds like he's saying the natural numbers are an interface, not a class. Anything that implements the interface (a first element, a successor function, no collisions, induction works) IS the natural numbers for mathematical purposes, whether that's von Neumann ordinals in set theory, tally marks, whatever. The implementation details are the "special character" you're supposed to neglect.

But then:

1. What is the interface itself? In most languages an interface isn't an object you can pass around, it's just a constraint. But Dedekind seems to think that after you do this neglecting thing, you're left with actual objects that arithmetic is ABOUT. Is the idea that each person performs this abstraction privately? Because then don't we each have our own personal 2, and how do we know yours and mine match? That seems unhinged but I can't tell where the passage blocks it.

2. The one that really bugs me: if you've neglected EVERYTHING about the elements except the relations they stand in, what's left to stand in the relations? Like, a relation has to relate somethings. If 2 is nothing over and above being the successor of the successor of the initial element, then is there any "it" there at all, or is the position just... the thing? I genuinely can't tell if this is a deep insight or word salad and I'd love someone to steelman it.
