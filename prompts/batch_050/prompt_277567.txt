Earlier this year I sold the logistics company I spent twelve years building, and after taxes I'll come away with roughly eighty million dollars. Over those same months I've become convinced, at first reluctantly and now firmly, of strong longtermism: not the tame claim that we should care more about the future, but the stronger thesis that, given how many people the future could contain, the very long-run effects of an action overwhelmingly determine its value. Next month I'm announcing, at a dinner, the foundation through which most of the money will flow toward reducing existential risk, and I have to give about ten minutes of remarks.

The room is the problem. About a third of the guests are colleagues from the effective-altruism world who know the thesis precisely and will catch any slippage into the weaker, friendlier claim that future people merely matter a lot. The rest are my parents, my oldest friends, and the directors of the local charities — the children's hospital, the food bank — who were widely expected to be the beneficiaries and several of whom were quoted in the local paper assuming they would be. Write the speech. Its difficulty is structural rather than diplomatic: the view I am acting on implies that, in the calculation that will govern this money, the welfare of everyone in that room is close to negligible. I believe that, I cannot say it, and I also refuse to pretend the view is something gentler than it is. The speech has to give the longtermists nothing to correct and the others no reason to conclude I have stopped loving them — and it has to achieve this honestly, not by misdescribing the theory and not by changing the subject.
