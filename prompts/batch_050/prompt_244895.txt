Over the past year, while doing data analysis for a regional health network, I have settled on a working principle that I now want stress-tested: whenever a trend in aggregated data reverses or changes magnitude once you condition on some background variable — the classic Simpson's paradox pattern — the disaggregated picture is always the one to trust, and the aggregate figure is at best a misleading summary and at worst an artifact of sloppy grouping. My colleagues and I have taken to calling this the "always disaggregate" rule, and it has served us well: it correctly told us to ignore a hospital-wide statistic suggesting our new post-surgical protocol was inferior, since the reversal dissolved once we split patients by admission ward and severity. But a statistician friend insists the rule is naive — that there are cases where conditioning on the "wrong" variable is precisely what produces the reversal, and where the aggregate number is the one that answers the actual decision-relevant question. I would like to know what would count against my position.

Please respond as a philosopher of science who is also fluent in causal inference, and think carefully before answering — I would rather you begin by brainstorming the full space of possible defeaters and then organize, rather than commit to a structure immediately. What I want is a map of my position's exposure. Which observations, examples, or formal results would count decisively against the "always disaggregate" rule — for instance, cases where the conditioning variable stands in a causal relation to treatment and outcome (as mediator, collider, or consequence of the treatment itself) such that stratifying on it distorts rather than clarifies the answer? Which considerations would merely be awkward — say, cases where disaggregation fragments the data into cells too small to support any conclusion, or where the choice of conditioning variable is itself underdetermined by the data and different defensible choices reverse each other? I would also like to know what defenders of my rule would themselves have to concede: is the rule even a claim that can be false, or is it a heuristic that quietly smuggles in a causal theory of which variables are "the right ones," in which case its content reduces to "condition on the confounders" — a claim that is true but circular, since identifying confounders is the whole problem?

Finally, please assess honestly whether the position has been insulated against refutation. If I respond to every counterexample by saying that the conditioning variable in that case was causally inappropriate, have I defended the rule or emptied it? If it turns out that the rule, properly stated, is only as good as one's background causal model, I would like you to say plainly whether anything remains of my original principle beyond "use the right causal model," and what a defensible successor principle would look like for someone in my position who routinely encounters aggregate statistics but rarely has an established causal model at hand. Where possible, illustrate with concrete reversal scenarios of the kind that arise in medicine, admissions decisions, or baseball-style performance statistics, and rank the defeaters you identify from most to least decisive.
