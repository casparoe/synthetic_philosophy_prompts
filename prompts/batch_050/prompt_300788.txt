I'm a retired chemist (spent my career on industrial safety, funnily enough) who's been reading philosophy for about a year, and I keep tripping over something about risk. Here's the puzzle. In ordinary life we impose risks on strangers all the time without asking anyone's permission -- every time I drive to the shops I slightly raise everyone's chance of being run over, and nobody thinks I need consent for that. But then the law gets picky in ways I can't reconstruct: you generally can't sue someone in tort just for exposing you to a risk (no damages unless the harm actually happens), yet the criminal law will happily convict someone of reckless endangerment even when nobody got hurt at all. So which is it -- is creating a risk a wrong in itself, or only when it crystallizes?

Anyway, I've been reading Mill's On Liberty, and in chapter V ("Applications") he writes: "The preventive function of government, however, is far more liable to be abused, to the prejudice of liberty, than the punitory function; for there is hardly any part of the legitimate freedom of action of a human being which would not admit of being represented, and fairly too, as increasing the facilities for some form or other of delinquency."

Two questions, then. First: what theory of harm and wrongdoing does the patchwork I described actually presuppose -- is it commited to the idea that risk imposition is only objectionable when unreasonable, or only when it causes damage, or is the practice just a mess with no theory underneath? Second: does Mill's remark help? He seems to think punishing after the fact is safer for liberty than preventing risky conduct beforehand, but our practice of criminalizing reckless endangerment looks like prevention by another name. Read his sentence carefully and tell me whether it's consistent with punishing pure risk creation, and what the law would have to look like if we really took him seriously. I'd rather hear what the practice strictly commits us to than a list of views it happens to be compatible with.
