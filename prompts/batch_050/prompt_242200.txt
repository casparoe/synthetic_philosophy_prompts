A friend in my reading group has an argument about evolutionary explanation that I believe proves too much, but I cannot locate the misstep.

(1) A scientific explanation of a trait is justified only if observation could, at least in principle, count against it.
(2) For any trait and any adaptationist story about it, one can always construct a non-selective alternative — the trait is a byproduct of something else that was selected, a side effect of developmental constraint, a fixation by drift, or a feature co-opted from some earlier use — that fits everything we currently observe just as well.
(3) If two accounts fit all the same observations, observation cannot justify preferring one to the other.
(4) So observation can never justify believing that a trait is an adaptation rather than a byproduct, a constraint, or an accident.
(5) Therefore adaptationist explanations are never justified.
(6) But nothing in this argument is specific to evolutionary biology: it would apply to the vertebrate eye, to echolocation, to any trait whatever, and indeed to any historical inference where rival stories can be tailored to fit the data — so it appears to show that no historical science can ever justify its conclusions.
(7) Since that is absurd, the argument proves too much.

My friend bites the bullet and says so much the worse for just-so stories about eyes. I would rather keep (1) and keep the claim that we know eyes are for seeing. Which premise or step should I reject, why did it look compelling, and what does rejecting it cost me — in particular, what room does it leave for the byproduct and constraint alternatives, which surely are sometimes correct?
