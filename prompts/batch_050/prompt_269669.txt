I help run a small recreational puzzle circle, and at our last meeting we spent the evening on Bongard problems -- those visual pattern puzzles attributed to Mikhail Bongard, where you're shown six panels on the left and six on the right and have to find the rule that separates the two sets. One member, Ana, had designed a problem herself: every left panel showed a dot inside a triangle, while the right panels mixed dots outside triangles with dots inside squares and other shapes. Her intended rule was simply that the dot lies inside a triangle. Another member, Ben, studied the twelve panels and announced a different rule that, as far as anyone could check, also fit all twelve panels perfectly: the dot lies inside a closed figure with an odd number of sides. On her panels the two rules coincide, but they would diverge on new cases, like a dot inside a pentagon. What started as good-natured ribbing turned into a genuinely serious argument that swallowed the rest of the session, and I was asked to referee, which I couldn't do to my own satisfaction.

The exchange went roughly like this. (1) Ana said Ben's rule was a gerrymandered, ad hoc construction, and that hers was the answer because it was the simplest rule consistent with the panels. (2) Ben replied that "simplest" is only defined relative to a description language: in a language with a primitive predicate for his conjunction, his rule would be the shorter one, and this is just Goodman's grue point -- any finite set of examples is consistent with infinitely many rules, so there is no fact of the matter about which rule the panels "really" exhibit. (3) Ana conceded the underdetermination in principle but made two counter-moves: first, that simplicity can be made objective via minimum description length or Kolmogorov complexity, and her rule plainly wins on any reasonable encoding; second, that Bongard problems are partly empirical instruments -- everyone else in the room had converged on her rule within minutes, and a problem whose intended answer nobody finds is simply a failed problem, so convergence is evidence of a shared and legitimate inductive prior, not just of convention. (4) Ben answered that an appeal to consensus is sociological, not epistemic -- a shared bias makes her rule more guessable, not more correct -- and that description-length measures inherit language dependence from the choice of encoding or reference machine, so the alleged objectivity is an illusion; he concluded that Bongard problems are really tests of "guess what the designer has in mind," a communication game rather than a discovery procedure. (5) Ana granted the communication-game framing but argued it changes nothing: even a purely cooperative guesser should prefer simple hypotheses, and grue-style predicates are parasitic, since they must be defined in terms of the ordinary ones (odd-sidedness presupposes sidedness, and so on), so there is an asymmetry Ben keeps ignoring. Ben's last word was that Goodman anticipated exactly this: take grue-like predicates as primitive and define the ordinary ones from them, and the alleged parasitism reverses.

At that point we adjourned, unresolved. I'd like a careful adjudication of the exchange as it actually unfolded: go through the five moves and say who is right on each specific point, with brief justification, rather than just declaring an overall winner. In particular, I'd like your verdict on whether there is a defensible distinction between "the rule" of a Bongard problem and "a rule merely consistent with its panels," whether description-length objectivity can survive the encoding-dependence objection, and whether the parasitism argument (move 5) succeeds against Goodman's rejoinder. If there is something both Ana and Ben are missing -- for instance, about what makes a Bongard problem well-posed in the first place, or about whether human convergence on intended answers is evidence about the world or only about us -- please say so explicitly.
