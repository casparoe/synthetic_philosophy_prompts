So I run a little philosophy-of-mind reading group at my local library (we're amateurs, mostly retirees, and we've been chewing on machine consciousness for a few months now), and I keep tripping over a pattern in our arguments that I can't find a word for. Here's how it goes. Someone proposes a case: a giant printout, or a spreadsheet, that records every state of a brain in pain, moment by moment. Everyone agrees the paper doesn't suffer. Then someone hooks the printout up so each row is used to compute the next row, and suddenly half the room says now it suffers and half says nothing changed. Then someone else says: fine, but the states have to actually push each other around, it can't just be us reading them in order. And then someone points out that by that standard a human brain in a vat, fed prerecorded inputs, might not count either, and the person backpedals and adds a new condition. What I notice is that every test anyone proposes -- it has to affect the world, the states have to cause each other, it has to be the sort of thing that would have behaved differently if things had been different -- seems to get picked after the person already knows where they want the line to fall. The intuition comes first, and the criterion is the intuition wearing a lab coat. Somebody in the group mentioned that a philosopher once argued even an ordinary rock computes everything, more or less, and if that's right then "the pain is really in there, not in our description" can't be the whole story, because on some description it's in the rock too.

Is there an established name for this pattern -- not for the rock thing or the lookup-table thing themselves, but for the move where the criterion for when a morally loaded process is "really there" keeps getting revised to protect a prior gut verdict about which systems count? I've seen nearby labels thrown around: circular reasoning, moving the goalposts, intuition mongering, observer-relativity, but none of those seem exactly right, since each individual revision is locally reasonable and the person often sincerely believes the new criterion. I don't want a bare name anyway -- I want you to take a stand. Pick whichever existing concept you think fits best (or say plainly that none does and coin something, with a definition and a couple of cases that fall just outside it), and tell me where the edges are: how is this different from ordinary refinement of a definition in light of counterexamples, which is supposed to be good philosophy? And I'd rather you commit than hedge -- give me your actual verdict on whether this pattern is a genuine failure mode worth having a term for, or whether it's just what honest conceptual work looks like from the outside, and say why.
