I am setting the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and I need one essay question on Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theater and his multiple drafts model. The standard formulation of this question — explain the Cartesian theater, then explain why Dennett rejects it — can be answered competently by anyone who has memorized the lecture notes, so I want the question built around a thought experiment I have devised specifically for this exam. Here is the scenario students will be given:

Neuroengineers identify a previously unknown subcortical structure, which they name the Forum. All major perceptual processing streams — visual, auditory, somatosensory, interoceptive — converge on the Forum before any unified behavioral response or verbal report is generated. Selective lesions to the Forum leave implicit processing intact (patients can still catch objects, navigate obstacles, and show galvanic responses to emotionally charged images) but entirely abolish reportable experience: patients insist, sincerely and consistently, that they perceive nothing. Advocates declare that the Forum is the place where it all comes together, and that Dennett has been empirically refuted.

The exam question should ask students to assess this claim: does the Forum vindicate the Cartesian theater, and what exactly is Dennett's position committed to saying about such a discovery? The crux I want examined is whether Dennett's objection was ever anatomical in the first place — whether his target is the nonexistence of a convergence point, or rather the deeper functional and conceptual assumption that arriving at such a point would constitute presentation to an inner audience, and that a content becomes conscious by crossing a finishing line rather than by its effects on subsequent processes of report, deliberation, and control. A strong answer should connect this to the multiple drafts model's claim that there is no single moment or place at which a content becomes conscious — the model's analogy between consciousness and fame rather than broadcast — and to Dennett's treatment of cases like the color phi phenomenon, where he argues there is no fact of the matter between what he calls Orwellian and Stalinesque accounts of when the experience occurred. Students should be pushed to say whether the Forum forces revision of these claims or merely relocates the mistake one step inward, so that someone must now watch the Forum.

Please deliver the following, in order:

1. The exam question itself, phrased for a 50-minute essay and including the thought experiment stated concisely enough to fit on an exam paper.
2. A model answer of roughly 600 words, written at the level of an excellent undergraduate.
3. A marking scheme out of 20 marks, organized around four or five must-hit points with a mark value for each. For each point, state the specific move that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer — for instance, whether the candidate recognizes that Dennett's critique survives the discovery of a convergence point because it targets the assumption that consciousness requires a terminus of presentation, not merely the claim that no anatomical locus exists. The scheme should also flag specific errors that must cost marks: treating the multiple drafts model as the claim that nothing is ever really conscious; treating the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction as one that could in principle be settled by better instruments; or conceding that the lesioned patients' lack of report settles that they lack experience, without noting that this conflates consciousness with the machinery of reportability in a way Dennett himself is careful about.

The model answer should not require knowledge of the primary texts beyond what a seminar participant would have from having read the assigned chapters, and the marking scheme should be usable by a teaching assistant who has not taken the course.
