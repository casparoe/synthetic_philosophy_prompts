I'm a retired molecular biologist and over the last year or so I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole, mostly via podcasts and library books. One thing that's been bugging me: I spent my whole career doing null-hypothesis significance testing without thinking much about it, and now I've discovered that statisticians and philosophers have been fighting about frequentism vs. Bayesianism for decades, and it doesn't seem to have gone anywhere. Both sides are mathematically competent, the math itself isn't in dispute, and yet the arguments from 40 years ago look almost identical to the ones today. The replication crisis in psychology just added fuel without settling anything -- one camp says p-values caused it, the other says bad incentives and sloppy practice caused it and better Bayes wouldn't have saved us.

So, please act as an expert philosopher of science who also really knows statistics (not just the textbook versions). Think carefully and take your time. Before committing to an answer, brainstorm at least three different diagnoses of what kind of disagreement this actually is -- e.g. is it verbal (the two sides use "probability" differently but could be translated), is it empirical (which methods actually perform better in real science, which in principle we could measure), is it about what questions inference is supposed to answer in the first place, or is it bedrock intuitions about what probability means that neither side can argue for from neutral ground?

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two camps are answering different questions -- one about the long-run behavior of procedures, one about what a rational agent should believe -- and most of the "debate" is people complaining that the other side's answer is a bad answer to *their* question. If that's right the replication-crisis blame game is also partly a confusion.

Diagnose the disagreement, tell me whether my hypothesis holds up, and say what would actually count as progress given your diagnosis: a theorem, a distinction, a bunch of empirical meta-science, or nothing at all. If you think parts of the debate are genuinely substantive, dont let me off the hook with a both-sides-have-a-point answer -- say which parts and why.
