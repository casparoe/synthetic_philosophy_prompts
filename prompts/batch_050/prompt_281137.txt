The topic: imprecise probabilities. Quick setup so we're on the same page -- some philosophers argue that rational credence shouldn't always be a single sharp number. Instead, your belief state can be represented by a *set* of probability functions (or an interval, like "my credence that the coin lands heads is somewhere between .3 and .7"). Motivations include: distinguishing genuine uncertainty from mere risk (think Ellsberg-style cases where you know nothing about the urn's composition), modeling disagreement among evidence, and avoiding the apparent arrogance of pretending to precision you don't have. Critics fire back that imprecise credences can't guide action (which number do you use when you have to bet?), that they license weird behavior like belief dilation (becoming less certain no matter what you learn), and that a sharp agent can capture everything that matters via higher-order uncertainty anyway.

Now here's the case I want your essay to revolve around. Imagine a medical triage AI that outputs a probability that a patient has some condition, and a downstream scheduling system that uses that number to rank patients. Two design teams disagree:

- Team Sharp says: the system must output one number, full stop. Triage is a ranking task; an interval like ".2 to .6" gives you no way to compare this patient against one scored ".4", so imprecision is a luxury the system can't afford. If the model is genuinely uncertain, fold that into the number (average over the uncertainty) and be done with it.

- Team Interval says: outputting ".4" when your evidence only supports "somewhere in [.2, .6]" is *epistemically dishonest* -- it manufactures fake precision that downstream users will trust. Worse, averaging hides the difference between "we've seen ten thousand cases like this" and "we've seen three." They propose the system output an interval, and that ties in ranking be broken by a rule: prioritize the patient with the higher *lower bound* when intervals overlap.
