Right, slightly odd request, but here goes. I teach a final-year module on rational choice, and I always end the term with the backward induction paradox in the centipede game. Every year the students walk out looking faintly cheated, as if I've shown them a card trick and refused to explain it — which, to be honest, is roughly how I feel about the argument myself, even after all these years.

I'd like a villanelle to put up on the last slide of the last lecture. For this, you're a proper poet who also happens to have a genuine grip of game theory — not someone who'll gesture vaguely at "rationality" and hope nobody asks follow-ups.

The content has to do real structural work, not just sit inside the form. The two refrains should be the two voices in the paradox. One is the backward-induction reasoner, working up from the final node: he'll take there, so I'd better take one earlier, so he'd better take one earlier than that. The other is the voice that notices the game as it's actually played — every pass swells the pot for both of us, and yet here we still are. Lean hard on the form for this: the induction argument has the horrible property that it reconstitutes itself at every node, since lopping off the last stage just shifts the same reasoning one step backwards, so a form built from lines that keep returning whether you want them or not is exactly the right vehicle.

Two more things. First, the genuinely interesting wrinkle: once anyone has actually passed, the chain of reasoning has already been refuted by the play so far — the later nodes are only reached because somebody did what the argument said no rational player would. So by the later tercets the refrains should start sitting oddly against what's around them, like a proof recited in a room whose premise has quietly left. Second, definately no resolution at the close. I don't want a neat moral about trust or cooperation, because I genuinely don't think the paradox has one. When the two refrains finally meet in the last stanza it should feel like a collision, not a reconciliation.

Think carefully before you draft: start with a short brainstorm — a few sentences on what each voice believes at each stage of the game and how the repetitions map onto the logic — then give me the poem itself. Nineteen lines, proper villanelle scheme. The accuracy bar: someone who knows the centipede game should be able to tell the poet knows it too — the mounting pot, the way passing only profits you if the other player passes next, the fact that the whole argument needs nothing beyond rationality plus everyone knowing that everyone has it.
