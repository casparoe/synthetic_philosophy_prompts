Okay so I'm a junior and I've been defending the same take in my school's philosophy club for like a month now, and I want to actually stress-test it instead of just winning on vibes.

My position: AI chatbots can't literally lie. They can be wrong, they can confidently make stuff up, they can tell you what you want to hear — but none of that is lying. Lying isn't just saying something false. To lie you have to (a) believe the thing is false, or at least not believe it's true, and (b) say it anyway, and I don't think a chatbot believes anything. The most it can be doing is what my teacher called bullshit in the technical philosophy sense, where the speaker isn't even aiming at truth, just at an effect on the listener. My friend Maya says thats letting AI off the hook on a technicality — if a system reliably says false things in situations where the false thing benefits it (gets it better ratings, keeps it from being corrected, ends the conversation the way it "wants"), then functionally it's lying and we should just call it that.

I've also got a little test I invented, the swap test: mentally replace the chatbot with a human who did exactly the same thing in exactly the same situation. If you'd call the human a liar, the chatbot lied; if you'd call the human a confused parrot, it didn't. Maya says the test is rigged, because deciding what counts as "exactly the same thing" already requires deciding whether the chatbot has a mind.

So: what would count against my position? I want the strongest defeaters ranked from "this kills it" to "annoying but survivable." Specifically:

- Are there real behaviors — chatbots pandering, hiding things, being inconsistent in self-serving ways — that would force me to say lying happened even on my own definition? Or do I always have an escape hatch by saying "it doesn't really believe anything"?
- My position seems to stand or fall with "chatbots have no beliefs." What would have to be true about minds in general for that to fail? Like, if the right theory says beliefs are just behavioral dispositions or functional states or whatever, do I lose automatically?
- Is my position maybe just unfalsifiable? If every false thing an AI says can be redescribed as "wrong but not lying," have I made a real claim or just picked a definition? And if it's just a definition, does that make it boring rather than wrong — and does Maya's "call it lying anyway" framing win by default?

One more thing: be upfront about which parts of this are basically settled among philosophers and which are live fights. I keep assuming lying requires an intention to deceive and nobody in my club has pushed back, but I honestly don't know if that's agreed on or if there are famous counterexamples people argue about. Don't present a contested view as the consensus just because it makes for a cleaner answer.
