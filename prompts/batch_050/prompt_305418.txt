I am a junior at a private high school, and I sit on our student Honor Council, the five-member body that decides whether students accused of honor code violations are "responsible." Under our code, responsibility requires three separate findings: P, that the student did what they are accused of doing; Q, that the conduct falls under the honor code; and R, that the student has no adequate excuse. Every member votes on every finding. The problem is that our bylaws never say how the verdict is supposed to follow from the votes, and our current case has exposed the gap. Below I record each case as five triples, one per member, giving that member's votes on P, Q, and R in order (Y = finding established, N = not established).

Our precedents: (1) A plagiarized history paper: YYY, YYY, YYY, YYY, YYN — the dissenter thought the student's claim not to have known paraphrasing requires citation was an adequate excuse. Verdict: responsible. (2) Alleged copying on a math test: YYY, YYY, YYY, NYY, NYN. Verdict: responsible, even though the finding that the student did it passed only 3-2. This case is often cited among us as proof that we follow the findings wherever they lead, but I have noticed that the same three members voted yes on all three findings, so the verdict would have come out identically if we had simply voted our overall judgments — it may prove nothing. (3) A phone found in a student's pocket during an exam: YNY, YNY, YYY, YNN, YNY. Most of the council thought mere possession was not conduct covered by the code. Verdict: not responsible. (4) Unauthorized collaboration on a take-home essay: YYN, YYN, YYY, YYN, NYY. The excuse — the teacher's genuinely ambiguous instructions — carried the day. Verdict: not responsible.

The new case: a senior is accused of using an AI chatbot to draft his English essay. The code predates such tools and prohibits unauthorized assistance. The votes: YYN, YNY, YYY, NYY, NYY. When we tallied the findings, each one passed — P by 3-2, Q by 4-1, R by 4-1. Then someone observed that only one member of the council voted yes on all three, so if each of us instead votes our overall judgment, the verdict is 1-4 against responsibility. I have since read that this predicament is called the discursive dilemma or doctrinal paradox, and the two procedures premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation.

My questions. First, what should we decide if we want to be consistent with our own record? Identify every rule compatible with our four past verdicts — I take it both aggregation rules are, but please test whether anything subtler fits them equally well or better, and note which plausible rules the record already excludes — then give your best recommendation, naming the closest analog among our precedents. Second, if the record genuinely underdetermines the verdict, say so plainly, state what principle we would be adopting with each choice, and tell me which principle a body like ours — students judging students, with real consequences — has most reason to adopt: it seems strange to convict a student none of us thinks responsible, but perhaps equally strange to acquit one we have found did the act, broke the code, and had no excuse. Third, what would settle which rule we have in fact been running all along — something to look for in our minutes, a question to put to former members, or a hypothetical earlier case on which the two rules would have come apart?
