In April 2010 the eruption of the Icelandic volcano Eyjafjallajökull led European aviation regulators to close most of the continent's airspace for around six days. The closure rested on modelled predictions of volcanic ash concentration together with what was effectively a zero-tolerance rule: if the models suggested ash above trace levels along a flight path, the airspace stayed shut, because ingested ash can stall jet engines. The certain costs were enormous — roughly a hundred thousand flights cancelled and around ten million passengers stranded, plus the freight and economic disruption — while the probability of an actual engine-failure catastrophe, had flying continued, was never established empirically and rested on uncertain modelling. Airlines ran their own test flights (British Airways and KLM among them), found no damage, and lobbied to reopen; within days the regulators abandoned zero tolerance and introduced graded ash-concentration thresholds instead.

Analyse this episode as a case study for the debate over expected value fanaticism — the view that rational decision-making under uncertainty must maximise expected value even when this lets a tiny probability of an enormous outcome dominate large certain costs. Work in both directions. First, map the decision the regulators actually faced: the options available to them, what was genuinely known about the probabilities (as opposed to merely modelled), and where the true payoffs lay — bearing in mind that the certain costs of closure included real harms, not just inconvenience. Ask whether zero tolerance was a disguised expected value calculation, a rejection of one, or a rule of a different kind altogether, and whether the critics who wanted the airspace reopened were implicitly relying on fanatical reasoning, anti-fanatical reasoning, or neither. Second, run the case against the theory: the eventual resolution — graded thresholds chosen so that flying could resume — looks like an implicit truncation of tiny probabilities at some point, but also like a refinement of the probability estimates themselves. Which reading is right matters for what the episode shows. Does it vindicate the anti-fanatic's claim that policy must sometimes refuse to be held hostage by minute probabilities of catastrophe, or does it instead expose that the real difficulty in the fanaticism debate is not the value structure but that the probabilities were too model-dependent for expected value reasoning to get a grip at all? Be disciplined about where the theory is silent, and note anything about the episode that neither position anticipated.
