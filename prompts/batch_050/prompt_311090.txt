Ok so I need an outside opinion on an argument I overheard at a pub after our dept's grad seminar last week. Background: I'm a PhD student in philosophy (mostly formal epistemology stuff), and two postdocs in our cohort got into it over the doomsday argument. I'll try to reconstruct the exchange as faithfully as I can -- I genuinely can't tell who's winning, and I have my own priors but I'd rather not share them because I want an unbiased read.

Round 1. Mara kicked it off. She said the doomsday argument is underappreciated as a general-purpose tool, and gave an example she'd been thinking about: our field, formal epistemology as a distinct recognizable subdiscipline, is maybe 70 years old depending how you count. If you assume you're a randomly positioned observer in the field's lifetime, she said, then with 95% confidence the field has somewhere between ~1.8 years and ~2700 years left before it dissolves or gets absorbed into something else. She said this follows from the same logic as the German tank problem / Copernican reasoning, and that people only recoil from it in the human-extinction case because the conclusion is scary, not because the math is different.

Round 2. Dev said the math might be the same but the epistemology isn't. The German tank estimator works because the serial numbers are literally drawn from a uniform distribution 1 to N and you have no other information. But with fields (or species, or civilizations) you have tons of other information -- you can look at demographic trends, funding, whether the problems are running out, etc. He said the doomsday-style calculation is only your prior, and for long-lived things the prior gets swamped almost immediately by base rates you can actually observe. So Mara's number isn't wrong, it's just useless, and presenting the 95% interval as if it were a live forecast is misleading.

Round 3. Mara said that concedes her actual point. The argument was never supposed to be the final posterior, it's a constraint on priors, and people who dismiss it wholesale are implicitly claiming to have a prior that's independent of their own temporal position, which she said is impossible to justify. She also said Dev's "we have other information" move proves too much, because you could say the same thing about the original human-extinction version -- we have tons of info about existential risks too -- and yet most people in the room, Dev included, take the human version at least somewhat seriously.

Round 4. Dev then switched tactics and raised the reference class problem: Mara picked "formal epistemology as a distinct subdiscipline" but she could equally have picked "philosophy of probability" (older, different window), "the analytic tradition" (way older), or "our department's grad seminar" (way younger), and each gives a wildly different interval. Since the choice of reference class does all the work and there's no principled rule for picking it, the argument doesn't constrain anything -- you can get whatever answer you want by choosing the class after the fact.

Round 5. Mara's reply (this is the part I found most interesting, and where I got lost): she said reference class sensitivity is a feature, not a bug. The different classes give different answers because they're answers to different questions, and each answer is correct conditional on you actually being a random sample from that class. The real question, she said, is which class you should consider yourself a random member of, and that's the self-sampling assumption debate (SSA vs SIA and all that), which is a live philosophical problem but not an objection to the math. She admitted she didn't have a principled answer for which class to pick, but said "no principled answer yet" isn't "no answer exists."

Round 6. Dev's last move: he said even granting all of that, Mara isn't a random sample from the class. She only wondered about the field's lifespan because she's in it, at a moment when the field is mature enough to have postdocs wondering about its lifespan. That's selection bias -- the observation "I'm in a position to ask this question" filters for certain temporal positions, so the randomness assumption fails from the inside, and the whole estimator is built on sand. He said it's like estimating average class size by asking students how big their class is.
