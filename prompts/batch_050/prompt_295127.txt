Write a dialogue between two equally competent climate-policy philosophers, Dr. Anaya Osei and Professor Lukas Brandt, who have been asked to advise a fictional coalition of small island states. The coalition, facing existential sea-level rise and frustrated by decades of inadequate mitigation, is seriously considering unilateral stratospheric aerosol injection — a program cheap enough, in the scenario, for the coalition to fund alone. Osei defends the deployment as a legitimate act of collective self-defense: she argues that the moral hazard objection (that cooling reduces pressure to cut emissions) is empirically speculative and ethically backwards, since it treats the coalition's survival as negotiable in order to preserve the mitigation incentives of the rich states that caused the problem; and she proposes a governance structure — a ratcheted deployment schedule, an escrowed thirty-year termination fund, and an open registry of regional climate impacts — that she claims neutralizes the termination shock problem. Brandt opposes deployment, but not on the obvious ground that it is risky. His objection is procedural and epistemic: he argues that a decision whose costs fall unevenly on billions of non-consenting parties cannot be legitimized by the consent of its beneficiaries, that the escrow fund solves the financial but not the political dimension of termination shock (a future coalition government, or its creditors, can simply refuse to honor it, and no one can compel a sovereign to keep dimming the sun), and that the open-impact registry cannot resolve disputes over attribution, since every regional drought will be contestable and every contest will be settled by whoever controls the models. The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth: Osei should press Brandt on whether his proceduralism amounts to a veto for the status quo, which is itself an unconsented imposition of climate harm on the coalition; Brandt should press Osei on whether her "self-defense" framing quietly assumes that the victims of geoengineering's side effects are permissible collateral in someone else's emergency. Each should concede at least one real point. End the dialogue with the two of them attempting to identify the single empirical or institutional question whose resolution would most change their positions, and disagreeing about what that question is.
