I run a small repair shop and, in my spare time, have been reading about how we come to trust our sources of information. I keep tripping over a pattern that seems clearly defective, yet I cannot articulate exactly what is wrong with it, and I would like to know whether philosophers have a name for it.

Here are three instances. First, a customer insists his fuel gauge works perfectly. Asked how he knows, he says he has tested it many times: he reads the gauge, notes what it says, then reads it again to confirm, and it has never once disagreed with itself. Second, a man accused of habitual lying offers, as his sole character witness, himself, and points out that his testimony has historically proven accurate -- since, by his own account, everything he has ever told you turned out true. Third, a friend of mine reasons that careful reasoning is trustworthy because, whenever she has carefully reasoned her way to the conclusion that careful reasoning is trustworthy, that conclusion has held up under careful reasoning.

In each case something feels illicit, but the illicit thing is hard to locate. No individual step is a lie or an obvious fallacy; the gauge really does agree with itself, and consistency is ordinarily a point in a source's favor. Nor does it seem to be the plain logical sin of assuming your conclusion outright -- the gauge-owner never writes "the gauge is reliable" among his premises; he seems to be gathering evidence. And yet the evidence feels weightless in a way that genuine cross-checking (say, verifying the gauge by dipping a stick in the tank) is not.

My questions: Is there an established name for this specific pattern, where a method or source is used, through its own deliverances, to certify its own trustworthiness? If so, how do philosophers mark it off from nearby things it could be confused with -- ordinary question-begging, mere stubbornness or confirmation bias, and the innocent practice of checking one instrument against an independent second instrument? I am especially interested in the boundary: what exactly makes the second-instrument case legitimate and the self-checking case not, given that both involve "testing"? And if there is no single established term for the pattern as I have described it, please coin one, define it precisely, and say which of my three cases fall inside it and which, if any, fall outside.
