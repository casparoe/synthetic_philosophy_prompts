I've been going back an forth on the whole reductionism vs anti-reductionism fight about testimony, so let me just dump my gut verdicts on some cases and ask what they add up to. (1) I land in a foreign city, ask a stranger where the tram stop is, and take her answer as knowledge immediately -- I never check her track record, it doesn't even occur to me. (2) My five-year-old nephew says the cat's stuck in the tree: knowledge. Same kid solemnly reports that grandma's house cost more than a car: I don't take his word for it, even though he's completely sincere. (3) An office rumor reaches me through four people, every one of them honest and usually accurate, that the CFO is resigning -- and I don't believe it, despite having no evidence against it and no specific reason to doubt any link in the chain. That one sits badly with (1), I know. (4) A teacher privately thinks evolution is false but teaches it carefully and correctly from the textbook for years; her students, I want to say, genuinely come to know it's true. (5) A guy tells me the time by reading a stopped clock that happens to be showing the right time; his belief is true and sincerely held, but I don't think I know the time from him. (4) and (5) together really bug me -- apparently the speaker doesn't have to believe what they say, and maybe doesn't have to know it either, but *something* has to be in place, and I can't say what. Then there's the case I can't settle: an unsigned index card under my door saying the meeting's moved to 3 -- I'd act on it, but I won't call it knowledge. So: is there one principle that captures all these verdicts, whether it's reductionist, anti-reductionist, or some hybrid? If it needs extra clauses, tell me which case forces which clause. If no decent principle saves everything, which verdict do I surrender, and why that one? And as a test case I haven't listed: what does your principle say about getting information from an AI assistant that's just relaying search results -- is that testimony at all, and does it matter?
