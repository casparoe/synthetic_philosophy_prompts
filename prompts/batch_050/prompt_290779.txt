Last night a friend and I argued for two hours about the doomsday argument, and we kept circling the analogy that almost every popular treatment leans on: the two urns. As he tells it, imagine you know a ball has been drawn from one of two urns -- one containing ten balls numbered 1 through 10, the other containing ten million balls numbered 1 through 10,000,000 -- and the ball bears the number 7. You should strongly conclude it came from the small urn, since your drawing such a low number is vastly more likely on that hypothesis. By analogy, he says, finding yourself roughly the hundred-billionth human ever born is evidence that humanity is among the short-lived species (the small urn) rather than one that goes on to produce trillions of descendants (the large urn), and this licenses updating sharply toward near-term extinction even before looking at any empirical risks. My objection was that the urn case smuggles in features that the human case simply lacks, and that once you strip those away the conclusion collapses -- or at least weakens to something a priori risk estimates were already giving us. He insists the disanalogies I named are cosmetic.

Please adjudicate. First, give an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the urns, the balls, the printed numbers, the act of drawing, and the observation of a low number. Second, separate the disanalogies that matter for the inferential use of the analogy from the ones that don't -- in particular, address whether there is anything in the human case corresponding to a genuine random sampling mechanism, and what the right reference class even is. Third, say whether the doomsday conclusion survives once the load-bearing disanalogies are made explicit, or whether the urn picture was doing work the bare reasoning cannot. If you think a better analogy exists for whatever survives, offer it. Keep the whole reply under 600 words.
