I have no philosophy training, but I've been reading a translation of Spinoza's Ethics on my own, and last week I described it to two friends over dinner. They ended up in an argument I couldn't settle, and I'd like it adjudicated.

Some background we all agreed on: Spinoza holds that everything follows from God's nature with absolute necessity, that nothing could have been otherwise, and that the highest human good is what he calls the intellectual love of God, which arises from understanding this necessity. My friends took it from there.

Maya argued that necessitarianism hollows out the very thing Spinoza recommends. If whether I attain the intellectual love of God is as fixed as everything else, then recommending it makes no more sense than recommending that a river flow downhill. Ethics, she said, requires genuine alternatives, so Spinoza's crown jewel sits in a system that leaves no room for crowning anything.

Daniel replied that she was confusing determination with compulsion. For Spinoza, you are free when you act from your own nature with adequate understanding, and the intellectual love just is the state of grasping why things are necessary. It is a real transformation in a person even if it was itself necessitated. Besides, love for Spinoza is simply joy together with the idea of its cause; since God is the cause of everything, understanding anything properly already yields love of God, with no alternatives required.

Maya pushed back on two fronts. First, this love is strangely one-directional: Spinoza himself says God feels no joy or love in return, and even that someone who loves God cannot wish for God to love him back. Calling a necessarily one-way feeling the highest good seems like winning by redefinition. Second, if God causes everything, God causes our suffering too, so loving God means loving the cause of what hurts us.

Daniel answered that the one-directionality is the point rather than a defect: in loving God, the mind participates in God's own eternal self-love, so it isn't really unrequited. And on suffering, understanding that some evil follows necessarily dissolves hatred of it, while the understanding itself brings joy.

Maya's final move was that this proves too much: if understanding necessity dissolves negative emotions, it should dissolve positive attachment as well, and what necessitarianism actually supports is calm equanimity, not love. Daniel responded that joy and sadness are not symmetric for Spinoza, since joy marks an increase in one's power, and understanding increases power, so necessity yields joy specifically.

I stayed out of it and still can't tell who came out ahead. Please go point by point: who is right about each specific claim, where each side concedes too much or too little, and whether there is something both of them are missing about how necessitarianism and the intellectual love of God fit together. I'd also like an overall verdict if one is defensible. Please answer in American English.
