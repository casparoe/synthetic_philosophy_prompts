Over the past couple of years I've become mildly obsessed with a question I can't resolve on my own. It started with arguments about cryptocurrency among my friends -- whether bitcoin is "real money" or a collective delusion -- and spread from there. I kept noticing that people describe money, race, and gender as "socially constructed," often as if that settled something, but when I press on what a satisfactory theory of socially constructed kinds would actually have to accomplish, I get vagueness. Everyone seems to agree that these categories are real in some sense (money can starve you; racial categories structure who gets hired) and yet made-up in some sense (no physics textbook contains them). I don't want a survey of existing theories. I want the specification first: the list of requirements a theory of socially constructed kinds must meet before I'm even willing to consider it adequate.

Concretely, I'd like the desiderata themselves, each with the consideration or test case that motivates it. To give you the flavor of cases I think the list should speak to: money continuing to function during hyperinflation or after the state that issued it collapses (if social kinds exist only because institutions decree them, why do they outlive the institutions?); a currency invented by a private company for a video game that drifts into real-world use; the category "weed," which has no botanical unity whatsoever yet organizes agriculture, law, and dinner-table conversation perfectly well; the way some categories seem to mutate precisely because the people classified learn the theory about them, while gold never changed behavior upon learning it was Au; and the divergences between a category's official definition and its lived operation, where the law says one thing and social practice does another. I'd also want the list to force a theory to mark the boundaries: what separates a socially constructed kind from a mere convention (which side of the road we drive on, which could flip overnight by agreement) and from a natural kind (quarks, electron charge), since a theory that can't draw those two lines hasn't earned the name. Which of these demands are genuine requirements versus the hobbyhorse of one camp -- say, those who treat construction as pure collective agreement versus those who think power and material practice do the real work -- is exactly what I want made explicit.

Please deliver the specification as a markdown table with these columns: Requirement (a short name); What it demands (one or two sentences); Motivating case (the scenario or consideration that makes it seem necessary, drawn from the ones above or better ones you supply); Status (one of: non-negotiable, negotiable, camp-specific preference); Who insists on it (the camp or motivation, if camp-specific); and What it rules out (the kind of theory that fails this requirement). After the table, add a short prose section answering two questions: first, is any subset of your requirements jointly unsatisfiable -- for instance, does demanding that social kinds be fully responsive to collective belief conflict with demanding that they explain why individuals can't simply opt out of them -- because if so, that tension is the part I most want to see; and second, which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates. Do not score actual philosophers' theories against the list; I want to do that part myself, and the thinking is in the criteria anyway.
