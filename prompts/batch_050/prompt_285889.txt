I sit on my hospital's clinical ethics committee and there's a case I need to write our recommendation on. A 71-year-old woman with moderate Alzheimer's came in with pneumonia. Twelve years ago, right after her diagnosis, she signed an advance directive refusing antibiotics and hospital transfer for serious infections, and here's the wrinkle: it includes a clause saying, in effect, that if she later seems happy or asks for treatment, that should be read as the disease talking and the document should control anyway. Which is exactly the situation we're now in. She seems genuinely content day to day, grasps in a fuzzy way that the IV is helping her, and says she wants the medicine that will make her better. Her daughter, who holds the healthcare proxy, wants the directive enforced; her son thinks overriding his mother's present, apparently sincere wishes because her past self anticipated this exact scenario is grotesque. The treating team just wants an answer before the weekend.

Draft the written opinion I'll circulate to the commitee. It should recommend a concrete course of action -- treat, withhold, or some defined time-limited middle option -- and it has to take seriously the possibility that her current contentment is morally relevant in its own right and not just a symptom, rather than only framing the case as past self vs. present self. I also want an honest statement of what the strongest argument on the losing side gets right, since this will probably set a precedent for us. Format it as exactly seven numbered points, each a sentence or two, with the recommendation as point one, and no intro paragraph or sign-off.
