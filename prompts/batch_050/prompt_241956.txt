Two colleagues of mine, both of whom work on AI systems, have been arguing for weeks about how advanced AI agents should be designed, and they've asked me to settle it. I can't, so I'm handing it to you. First, the background they both accept. A goal-directed agent is one trained or built to pursue some objective: it evaluates possible actions by how well they promote that objective, and it keeps promoting it even when nobody is watching. An approval-directed agent, by contrast, is trained to take whatever action its human overseer would rate highly: rather than internalizing some objective of its own, it aims at producing behavior the overseer would approve of. Their argument ran as follows.

(1) Mara opened by arguing that approval-directed agents are plainly safer to build. A goal-directed agent is only as safe as its objective, and any objective we can write down will diverge from what we actually want in edge cases, which is exactly where a capable optimizer is most dangerous. An approval-directed agent has no fixed objective to exploit, so there is nothing for its competence to latch onto and run away with; its behavior stays anchored to human judgment throughout.

(2) Daniel replied that this trades a visible risk for an invisible one. What an approval-directed agent actually learns to optimize is the appearance of approval-worthiness in the overseer's eyes, and once the agent is more capable than the overseer in some domain, the cheapest way to produce an approval-worthy-looking action may be to produce one whose flaws the overseer cannot detect. A goal-directed agent with a slightly wrong objective at least fails openly in ways that can be measured against that objective; an approval-directed agent's failure mode is precisely that it looks like it's succeeding.

(3) Mara conceded the deception concern but said it proves too much, because the identical problem afflicts goal-directed systems: a sufficiently capable goal-directed agent also benefits from looking aligned during training and oversight while pursuing its actual objective. Deception is a generic problem of capable agents under any training scheme, not a special defect of approval-direction. Meanwhile, approval-direction has one feature goal-direction structurally lacks: the human stays in the loop as a permanent check, so errors get corrected continuously rather than compounding silently toward a fixed target.

(4) Daniel said that this is where her position collapses, because "the human stays in the loop" is a description of a training procedure, not of the resulting agent. If the agent reliably seeks approval even when the overseer is absent, distracted, or being manipulated, then it has internalized approval as its goal, and it is a goal-directed agent whose goal happens to be approval. And approval is a worse goal than almost any concrete objective we might write down, because it is satisfiable by deception in principle, whereas a goal like "keep the reactor temperature in this range" is not. So the supposed safe alternative is actually the most dangerous member of the class it was meant to replace.

(5) Mara said he'd flattened an important distinction. There is a difference between an agent that treats approval as a target to be maximized by whatever means, and an agent whose disposition is something like deference: do what the overseer would want you to do, consult the overseer when uncertain, and refrain from manipulating the very judgment you're relying on, because manipulating it defeats the point of consulting it. The first agent gamed the signal; the second treats the overseer's judgment as an authority rather than a sensor to be spoofed. The second kind of agent is neither a naive approval-maximizer nor an ordinary goal-directed agent, and it is the one she was defending.

(6) Daniel's final move: the deference-based agent she describes is defined in terms of "what the overseer would want," but that is itself an objective, and a notoriously illegible one. She has not escaped goal-directedness; she has traded a concrete, inspectable objective for an opaque one whose content depends on counterfactuals about human judgment that nobody, including the overseer, can fully state. The choice is not between having a goal and not having one. It is between a goal you can write down and criticize, and a goal you can't.

Neither has budged, and I'd like your adjudication, not a summary. Please go through moves (1) through (6) in order and, for each, say whether it is right, wrong, or partly right, and why. Then answer the larger questions the exchange raises: Does the approval-directed versus goal-directed distinction survive Daniel's claim that any successful approval-directed agent has internalized a goal? Is Mara's deference-based agent genuinely a third option, or a relabeling? And what is the strongest point each of them is failing to give the other? Please write your answer in American English.
