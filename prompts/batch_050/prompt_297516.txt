I teach undergraduate ethics, and I keep encountering a recurring pattern in student papers -- and, if I am honest, in my own seminar contributions -- for which I lack a settled label. Two instances will fix what I mean. First: a student defends the principle that promises must be kept, encounters the counterexample of a promise extracted by deceit or one whose keeping would facilitate serious harm, and responds that of course that promise does not bind, since the principle was only ever meant to hold under ordinary conditions; yet when asked to state those conditions, every attempt collapses into a restatement of the principle plus the proviso that it holds whenever breaking the promise would in fact be wrong. Second: a student treats the fact that an action causes pain as a consideration that counts against it, then concedes that in contexts of consensual sport, deserved sanction, or certain forms of education the very same fact counts in favor of the action or not at all -- the consideration appears to reverse its polarity depending on its surroundings.

My question is whether this configuration has an established name, and how it should be carved. It seems to me that at least three distinct theses are being run together here: that the moral force of a consideration can vary with context; that moral principles cannot be stated without exception clauses whose content resists independent specification; and that there are no interesting exceptionless moral generalizations at all. If the literature already distinguishes these, I would like the nearest concepts for each and an account of which best fits my students' pattern. If it does not, I would like a name and a definition precise enough to distinguish the pattern from a mere ad hoc rescue of a generalization, and from the ordinary observation that some duties override others.
