I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a column about status: why people seem to need not just success but acknowledgment of it, and why acknowledgment from certain quarters counts for so much less than from others. A popular book on the psychology of prestige that I picked up for background grounds all of this in Hegel's master–slave dialectic, which it summarizes as follows: "Two self-consciousnesses meet, each needing the other to confirm its sense of self, and they fight to the death for it. Whoever is willing to risk everything becomes the master; whoever clings to life submits and becomes the slave. The master thereby wins the recognition he fought for and secures the independent existence he wanted, while the slave, set to work shaping things, slowly discovers in his labor a power to form the world and with it the genuine freedom and self-certainty the master never attains. Hegel's lesson is thus that domination defeats itself: the master's triumph is hollow from the start, and history in the end belongs to the slave." The book then argues, on this basis, that status hierarchies are inherently unstable because those at the top crave recognition from subordinates who are never in a position to confer it.

Something nagged at me, so I looked up the relevant stretch of the Phenomenology of Spirit (in Baillie's old translation, §185), where Hegel introduces the master–slave relation, and I found this: "This pure conception of recognition, of duplication of self-consciousness within its unity, we must now consider in the way its process appears for self-consciousness. It will, in the first place, present the aspect of the disparity of the two, or the break-up of the middle term into the extremes, which, qua extremes, are opposed to one another, and of which one is merely recognized, while the other only recognizes." Read plainly, this seems to say the master–slave relation is defined by a lopsided recognition in which neither party gets what recognition is supposed to provide — which sits awkwardly with the book's claim that the master "wins the recognition he fought for and secures the independent existence he wanted," at least in any unqualified sense. But I'm not a Hegel scholar, and I know the book's author is a competent popularizer, so before I lean on this in the column I want to know whether I'm catching an error or just tripping over Hegel's prose.

So: is the book's summary fair? Please go through it and separate what a careful reader of the lordship and bondage section would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a misrepresentation — and tell me specifically what the quoted passage does and doesn't establish about the master's supposed victory. I'm also suspicious that the closing line about history belonging to the slave smuggles in a later twentieth-century reading of the dialectic and presents it as Hegel's own text, but I can't say how much of it is Hegel and how much is his interpreters; please address that too. Finally, the practical question for my column: once the summary is corrected, does the book's downstream claim about the instability of status hierarchies still stand on anything Hegel actually argued, or does it survive only in a weaker form than the book suggests?
