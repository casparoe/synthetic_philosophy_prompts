I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, currently preparing a profile of a libertarian commentator famous for arguing that taxation is theft. Before our interview, I reviewed her verdicts on a series of cases so I can anticipate her positions. A mugger who donates his takings to famine relief: theft, she says, since worthy ends don't launder the taking. A yacht club that keeps the deposit of a member expelled for breaking rules he accepted on joining: not theft. A homeowners' association fining a resident who knowingly signed its covenants: not theft. A pirate crew seizing a merchant ship's cargo and dividing it scrupulously among themselves: theft, even under their fair internal code. An employer deducting insurance premiums the employee authorized in writing: not theft. I want to ask her about a citizen of a stable democracy who voted against the governing party, explicitly denies ever consenting to taxation, yet drives on public roads, was educated in public schools, and could emigrate but chooses to stay. Please respond as a political philosopher who specializes in property and coercion: think carefully, begin by laying out the distinct principles her prior verdicts are each consistent with (a pure consent principle, a tacit-consent-through-residence principle, and any others that fit), then predict her verdict on my case and say which prior case is the closest analog. If her track record genuinely underdetermines the answer, tell me which further hypothetical — one she hasn't ruled on — would most cleanly separate the candidate principles.
