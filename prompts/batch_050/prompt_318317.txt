I am preparing a graduate seminar on Bernard Williams's critique of impartial moral theory, and a dispute broke out between two of my students during our session on the famous example of the man who must choose between rescuing his wife and rescuing a stranger, where Williams complains that a certain kind of theory requires the rescuer to think one thought too many. Marcus defended the standard consequentialist rejoinder. The objection, he said, equivocates between a criterion of rightness and a decision procedure: nothing in act consequentialism requires agents to run the calculus at the moment of action, and a sophisticated consequentialist can recommend that people cultivate exactly the spontaneous dispositions of love Williams prizes, since such dispositions generally produce the best outcomes. The rescue passes the criterion; how the rescuer's mind was occupied is a psychological question the theory is simply not about, so there is no extra thought to object to. Naomi replied that this response concedes the objection's substance while denying its target. Williams, she argued, was never concerned with which thoughts flicker through a mind in an emergency; his point concerns the order of justification, the fact that for the consequentialist the thought that this is his wife figures only as defeasible evidence about expected value, so that the agent must in principle stand ready to rescind the motivation should the numbers change. It is that standing contingency, she said, not any actual calculation, that alienates the agent from his project and makes the theory's blessing of the rescue feel like permission rather than love. Marcus countered that her version proves too much: any moral theory with a permissibility verdict, including commonsense deontology, allows that there are situations in which one may not save one's spouse (imagine the stranger holds the only antidote to a plague), so every theory inserts a justificatory thought between the agent and his wife, and Williams's complaint reduces to a complaint about moral theory as such. Naomi answered that the relevant difference is not the presence of a moral thought but its content: on the deontological picture the wife is the direct object of the reason, whereas on the consequentialist picture she is an occasion for value that happens to be instantiated in her, and only the latter makes the particular relationship replaceable in principle.

They have asked me to referee, and I find myself unable to locate either a clean winner or the precise point at which they stopped talking past each other. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: state who is right at each step and why, assess whether Marcus's criterion-versus-decision-procedure reply genuinely answers the objection as Naomi reconstructs it, whether his proving-too-much rejoinder succeeds against that reconstruction or only against the cruder reading, and whether Naomi's final distinction between the wife as object of the reason and the wife as occasion of value can bear the argumentative weight she places on it. I would also like your verdict on what, if anything, both students are missing about the structure of Williams's complaint. Please write your answer in American English.
