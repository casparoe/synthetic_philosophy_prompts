I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a long piece about a debate I keep circling back to: whether an arbitrarily intelligent system can have arbitrary goals. One side holds that intelligence and motivation are independent variables — that you can, in principle, pair any level of cognitive capability with any final goal, and that the classic worry cases (a superintelligent system relentlessly pursuing some trivial-seeming objective) are coherent for exactly that reason. The other side argues that this separation is an artifact of how we talk about minds: that goals and values are learned, that learning enough about the world to be genuinely intelligent means absorbing human-adjacent values along the way, and that a system which "misunderstands" its goal badly enough to destroy everything we care about is, by definition, not that intelligent.

Here's what puzzles me as a journalist. Large language models were supposed to be evidence. They weren't explicitly given goals, they absorbed human text at scale, and now both camps claim vindication: one points to models parroting human values as proof that values come free with capability; the other points to fine-tuning, jailbreaks, and goal drift as proof that surface values are cheap costumes over arbitrary behavior. A decade-plus in, neither side has conceded anything, and I'm no longer sure they're even disagreeing about the same thing. My working hypothesis is that the debate is partly verbal — each side is using "intelligence" and "goals" differently — but I suspect there's also a real empirical disagreement hiding underneath, and a conceptual one about what would even count as a system "having" a final goal. I don't want my piece to just adjudicate who's right. I want to explain to readers why smart people are stuck.

Please act as a philosopher of mind with deep familiarity with AI, writing a background memo for me. Think carefully before settling on a structure; I'd suggest first sketching, in a short bulleted list, the strongest version of each side's core claim and the strongest piece of evidence each cites, then building the diagnosis from there. In the memo itself, tell me: what kind of disagreement this actually is (verbal, empirical, conceptual, or some mixture, and in what proportions); whether the apparent impasse reflects an ill-posed question or merely a hard one; and what, concretely, would count as progress — an experiment, a formal result, a conceptual distinction, a reframed question — or whether we should expect none. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually argues, not in caricatures. Roughly 800–1,000 words.
