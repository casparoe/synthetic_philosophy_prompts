So I watched The Prestige again last weekend (the Nolan one with Hugh Jackman and Christian Bale) and I can't stop thinking about the machine. Tesla's device doesn't move Angier, it makes a copy of him somewhere else, and then one of them drowns in the tank every night. The movie makes a huge deal out of him not knowing which one he'll "end up as."

Here's my thing. I've been going down a rabbit hole online about something philosophers apparently call persistence through time, and I found these two views, endurantism and perdurantism. From what I understand: endurantism says I'm the *whole* me right now, fully present, the same thing that was here yesterday. Perdurantism says I'm more like a four-dimensional worm stretched through time, and what's sitting here typing is just a temporal "slice" of me, like how my left hand is a spatial part of me.

So my request: act as a professional philosopher who specializes in metaphysics of time and personal identity, and who also actually likes movies and doesn't talk down to people. Think carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming the different ways the drowning scene can be analyzed before you commit to a reading.

Then read The Prestige through this endurantism vs. perdurantism lens. Specifically:

1. Which view makes better sense of Angier's dread? On the worm view, is there even a fact of the matter about "which one" continues as him, or is the question broken? I feel like perdurantism might dissolve the horror but also might make it *worse* — like every night a person-stage genuinely dies and the show goes on anyway.
2. The first time he uses the machine, he shoots the copy. Does the movie itself treat that as murder or suicide or neither? What does that tell us about which theory of persistence the *film* is implicitly committed to?
3. Borden and Fallon complicate everything. Two people sharing one life is almost the reverse problem — one biography, two worms? Or two enduring things taking turns being "the same" man? Does the movie handle identity the same way for the twins as for the clones, or is it inconsistent?
4. Honest part: where does the endurantism/perdurantism framework just not fit what the movie is doing? I don't want a reading that pretends the theory explains everything.

I'm not an academic, I just read stuff online, so please keep jargon minimal and explain any terms you can't avoid. Also, at the end, tell me whether the movie actually *contributes* anything to this debate that a philosophy paper couldn't, or if it's just a cool illustration. I genuinely can't decide if the film is philosophically deep or just feels deep.
