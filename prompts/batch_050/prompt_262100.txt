I am a retired molecular biologist who has, in the last few years, been reading my way into philosophy of mind, and I have run into a difficulty I would like examined. Among my former scientific colleagues — and, I gather, in much popular writing about neuroscience and artificial intelligence — it is commonplace to say that the mind is the software of the brain: the brain is the hardware, the mind is the program it runs. In my reading group this analogy is being made to do real argumentative work. It is used to license the claims that mental disorders are, at bottom, bugs rather than broken parts; that one mind could in principle be run on different hardware, so that personal survival is a matter of copying rather than of bodily continuity; and that introspection is like consulting a program's log files — authoritative about what the software reports, but not about the machine underneath. Meanwhile I have also been reading Ryle, and I note that his ghost-in-the-machine critique and his notion of a category mistake are often invoked, including by philosophers I respect, against precisely this sort of picture. What I cannot tell is whether the software analogy actually falls to Ryle's charge or merely sits near enough to Cartesian dualism to catch the same shrapnel.

So: how far does the analogy hold? I would like the correspondences made explicit — a table is fine — identifying what, in the case of a mind, is supposed to play the role of the programmer, the programming language, the hardware, a bug, and the same program instantiated on two different machines. Then I would like the disanalogies laid out: the ones I dimly perceive are that brains have no author, that the hardware–software boundary in a computer is clean in a way the physiological–psychological boundary is not, and that brains alter their own wiring in the course of running, which programs generally do not — but I would like to know which of these matter for the three conclusions above and which are idle. Finally, tell me whether each of the three licensed conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if you think a better guiding analogy exists for a scientist coming to philosophy, say what it is and what it buys us.

One request about manner: I have been a scientist long enough to distrust agreeable answers. If any part of this request rests on a confused premise — for instance, if I have misread Ryle's target, or if the question as posed cannot be answered in the form I have asked for — I would rather be told so plainly than have the confusion politely worked around.
