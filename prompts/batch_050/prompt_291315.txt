I help run a casual philosophy reading group (we're all amateurs, a couple of us did undergrad phil years ago) and last week we did the Twin Earth chapter in some anthology. One member couldn't make it and instead read a summary in a popular philosophy book she had at home, and she sent us this paragraph, which she said cleared everything up for her: "Putnam's Twin Earth case is simple. Imagine a planet exactly like ours except that the liquid they call water is a different chemical, XYZ, though it looks, tastes, and behaves identically. When a Twin Earthling says 'water' they mean XYZ, not H2O, even if they are molecule-for-molecule identical to one of us. So Putnam's conclusion is that meaning is fixed entirely by your environment and causal history rather than by anything in your head, and that two people in identical brain states mean different things, which shows psychology is basically irrelevant to semantics. He also argues that since ordinary speakers can't tell gold from fool's gold without chemists, meanings aren't really in individual heads at all but live in the linguistic community as a whole. The upshot is that externalism refutes the Cartesian idea that knowing your own mind is enough to know what you mean: you can be mistaken about what your own words mean and never find out."

Something about this bugs me but I can't pin it down. I remember from the chapter that Putnam made a big deal of setting the case before anyone knew water was H2O, which the summary drops, and I also vaguely recall him saying meaning has more than one ingredient, not just the stuff out there in the world. Is this a fair summary? Which bits would Putnam (or a careful externalist) actually endorse, which are overstatements or conflations of separate claims he kept apart, and does the big conclusion about being wrong about your own meanings actually follow from the accurate parts, or is it smuggled in?
