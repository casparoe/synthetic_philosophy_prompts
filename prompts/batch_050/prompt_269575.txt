In a reading group on comparative constitutional design, we keep circling back to the standard defense of constitutional monarchy in a parliamentary democracy: that a politically neutral head of state, holding reserve powers exercised only in extremis, serves as a kind of constitutional umpire who can break deadlocks that elected politicians cannot, without ever competing with them for democratic legitimacy. I want to stress-test that defense against the episode most often invoked on both sides of it — the Australian constitutional crisis of 1975 — and I'd like the analysis to run in both directions.

Take the salient facts to be these. In late 1975, Prime Minister Gough Whitlam's government held a majority in the House of Representatives, but the opposition-controlled Senate deferred passage of the government's supply bills, creating a deadlock over funding. On 11 November 1975, Governor-General Sir John Kerr dismissed Whitlam — despite his retaining the confidence of the lower house — and commissioned opposition leader Malcolm Fraser as caretaker Prime Minister on the understanding that he would secure supply and immediately call a double-dissolution election, which Fraser's coalition then won decisively in December. Analyze this episode through the theory of constitutional monarchy sketched above. Map carefully: what, on the umpire account, was the deadlock Kerr was supposed to resolve, and did the facts match the theoretical trigger conditions? Where does Bagehot's famous distinction between the dignified and efficient parts of the constitution — under which a ceremonial head of state retains only consultative, advisory, and cautionary functions — place the dismissal, and does the episode expose a gap between that modest picture of the office and the reserve powers actually claimed for it? And note whatever the mapping cannot capture: for instance, that the actor here was an appointed vice-regal officer rather than a hereditary monarch, or that the deadlock was manufactured by an elected Senate rather than arising from any failure of the lower house.

Then reverse the direction and ask what the episode does to the theory. Does it vindicate the safeguard rationale — a genuine impasse between two elected chambers was resolved, and quickly — or does it instead show that a non-elected officeholder lacks the legitimacy to override the confidence of the democratically decisive chamber, with the December election doing the real work of resolution and the dismissal itself being surplus or worse? Consider whether the subsequent popular verdict retroactively legitimizes Kerr's action, and whether it should. Finally, use the comparison class the defense usually rests on: could a Scandinavian- or Japanese-style monarchy, whose sovereigns hold no comparable reserve powers, have produced an equivalent resolution, and if so, does that show the umpire justification was never really load-bearing? Be disciplined about where the theory simply falls silent rather than forcing it to deliver a verdict on every feature of the case.
