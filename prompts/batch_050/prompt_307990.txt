Take the Anglo-French Concorde programme -- the supersonic airliner developed from the 1960s onward by the British and French governments, whose projected costs multiplied severalfold over the course of development and which was continued to completion despite mounting evidence that it would never recoup its costs commercially -- and analyze it through the lens of the sunk cost fallacy. The case is so canonical that the phenomenon is sometimes named after it, and that is precisely why I want it examined rather than invoked.

Begin by reconstructing the charge rigorously. What exactly is the sunk cost fallacy, as a piece of decision theory: which principle of rational choice does it violate, and why do economists regard irrecoverable past expenditure as irrelevant to a forward-looking decision? Then map the model onto the episode with discipline. Who were the decision-makers at each juncture, what options did they actually face at the moments when cancellation was on the table, and what does the sunk cost diagnosis claim was doing the work in their deliberation?

Then do the harder half. Give the strongest available case that continuing the programme was not, or not straightforwardly, a sunk cost error. Consider at least: the binding intergovernmental treaty of 1962, which made unilateral withdrawal costly in ways a simple cost-benefit ledger omits; reputational and credibility effects on future international collaborations; and the distinction between honoring sunk costs and rationally responding to information acquired along the way. For each mechanism, say whether it genuinely rescues the decisions from irrationality or merely redescribes the bias in respectable vocabulary -- escalation of commitment and face-saving are rival explanations here, not absolutions, and I want you to say how one would tell the difference given what the decision-makers could have known at the time.

Conclude by turning the analysis around: what does the episode show about the sunk cost framework itself? Does it vindicate the orthodox view, reveal that real political decisions are rarely the clean single-agent choices the model assumes, or expose that the label 'sunk cost fallacy' is applied retrospectively to projects that failed, in a way that makes the diagnosis unfalsifiable? Where the framework is simply silent about this kind of case, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
