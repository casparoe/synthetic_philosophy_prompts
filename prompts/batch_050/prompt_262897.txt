I am a retired experimental physicist. Since leaving the laboratory I have joined a reading group of fellow scientists working through epistemology and decision theory, and last month we spent three sessions on Aumann's 1976 result in "Agreeing to Disagree": with a common prior, if two people's posterior probabilities for an event are common knowledge between them, those posteriors are equal. I tried to build the theorem into an argument for a thesis I find attractive — that persistent disagreement is a symptom of irrationality somewhere — and the argument came out stronger than I can live with. I would like you to answer as a professional epistemologist comfortable with formal methods. Please work through the argument slowly and carefully before committing yourself, and then give me a committed verdict: name the step you reject, and defend the rejection. Do not hand me a menu of positions with pros and cons and invite me to choose.

Here is the argument as I presented it to the group:

(1) To be rational about a proposition is, at minimum, to hold degrees of belief in it that one updates by conditionalizing on one's total evidence.

(2) Priors are not evidence; they are what one brings to evidence. Where no evidence distinguishes two starting points, rational calibration forbids adopting different ones — two instruments measuring the same quantity should not be zeroed differently on a whim. So two fully rational agents share a prior.

(3) Given (1) and (2), two rational agents' posteriors can differ only because their evidence has differed.

(4) When such agents honestly report their posteriors to each other, each thereby acquires new evidence — namely, that a rational agent with the same prior reached that number — and sustained honest exchange of this sort is precisely the process by which their posteriors become common knowledge between them.

(5) By Aumann's theorem, once their posteriors are common knowledge, they are identical.

(6) Therefore any two rational agents who honestly discuss a proposition for long enough must eventually agree about it; persistent disagreement between honest discussants shows that at least one of them is failing (1) or (2).

(7) Yet I spent thirty years disagreeing with colleagues I consider wholly rational and honest — about how much weight to give a particular anomaly, about which of two experiments to fund — and we did not converge, and I do not think a longer lunch would have fixed it.

(8) So something in (1)–(4) must give, since (5) is a theorem and (6) follows.

Two constraints on your answer. First, if forced to choose I would rather keep (1) than (2) — conditionalization feels to me like the least negotiable part — but I want to know what I am paying either way, so tell me what your rejected step commits me to about the rationality of my former colleagues and about the standing of those old disagreements. Second, if you conclude the argument is actually sound through (6), say so plainly and bite the bullet for me: tell me which of my old disputes I should retrospectively diagnose as someone's irrationality, and on what grounds I should assign the fault.

If locating the flaw requires laying out the theorem's own machinery — what common knowledge demands beyond mere mutual knowledge, what a prior is doing in the proof — please do that in your own terms. I am not asking for citations or a reading list; I want the diagnosis itself, and I want you to stand behind it.
