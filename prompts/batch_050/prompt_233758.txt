I work on a small behavioral insights team inside a city government -- we review proposals from other departments for "nudges": default organ-donor opt-outs in the DMV paperwork flow, putting fruit at eye level in school cafeterias, letters telling residents most of their neighbors have already paid their taxes, that sort of thing. Right now our review process is basically vibes, and last week a proposal got approved that I think was actually pretty manipulative while another one got killed for being "paternalistic" when it seemed fine to me. I've read a bit of the philosophy on paternalism and nudging (the standard distinction between changing people's choices and changing the chooser, the stuff about transparency and reversibility, hard vs soft paternalism) but it doesn't translate into anything I can apply at 4pm on a Friday when a proposal lands on my desk.

So: write me a decision procedure our team can actually run on each proposal, as an ordered sequence of tests -- the questions to ask, in what order, what counts as passing or failing each one, and where the procedure should stop with a firm no versus where it should flag the proposal as "needs a human judgment call" because the remaining issue is a values question no checklist can settle (I suspect whether a default exploits someone's laziness vs just accommodates it is one of those, but tell me where you think the judgment points actually are). One thing I'd like the procedure to incorporate is a distinction I've been toying with between nudges that serve a goal the person already has (like making the healthy lunch the easy one for someone who wants to eat better) and nudges that manufacture or redirect the goal itself -- I have a hunch the permissibility bar should be different for the two, but I can't articulate why, so build that in properly or tell me the distinction doesn't survive scrutiny.

Then run the procedure on three proposals so I can see it working: (1) automatically enrolling employees in a retirement savings plan with opt-out, (2) a "most of your neighbors recycle" letter that uses a slightly cherry-picked statistic, and (3) making the unhealthy cafeteria options require asking a staff member rather than being self-serve. Ideally at least one of these should come out with a verdict that cuts against the intuitive reaction, because that's the whole point of having a procedure instead of vibes. Also be upfront about what the procedure can't do -- where it will give the wrong answer even when applied correctly.
