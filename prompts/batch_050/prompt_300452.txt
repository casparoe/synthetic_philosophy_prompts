So I work on anomaly detection for industrial equipment, and I've been reading a bit of philosophy of science on the side because I keep running into a conceptual problem my engineering colleagues just shrug at. Our system monitors production-line machines and flags when something is "not the same mechanism anymore" -- basically when the normal pattern of operations has changed enough that the old model of the machine no longer applies. But nobody can tell me what actually makes two setups count as the same mechanism rather than a different one. Like, we swapped out a pump for a newer model last month and the system didn't flag it, but then someone rerouted a conveyor and suddenly everything was anomalous, and I can't articulate why those two cases differ.

I've collected the individuation criteria I can find or reconstruct, and I'd like a new one that isn't on my list: (1) the parts criterion -- same mechanism iff it's made of the same component entities (with some tolerance for like-for-like replacement, Ship of Theseus style); (2) the activities criterion -- same mechanism iff the components perform the same operations (grinding, pumping, heating, whatever); (3) the organization criterion -- same mechanism iff the parts and activities stand in the same spatial, temporal, and causal arrangement; (4) the input-output criterion -- same mechanism iff it reliably produces the same phenomenon from the same starting conditions, regardless of internal details; (5) the history criterion -- same mechanism iff there's a continuous causal/maintenance history connecting it to the earlier setup, so gradual total replacement counts as sameness while building an identical copy from scratch doesn't. Each of these has failure modes I know about, so don't just re-derive one of them or combine two of them and present the combination as new.

What I want is either a genuinely sixth criterion for mechanism sameness, or -- maybe more useful -- a single realistic case, factory-flavored or biological, where all five criteria agree on a verdict but the intuitively correct verdict goes the other way, which would show that the list is collectively missing something. If you give a case, tell me which criterion you think the case reveals a need for. And be honest about whether your proposal actually survives its own obvious counterexamples, since I've noticed this is exactly the kind of question where every clean answer has a counterexample waiting one email away.
