Here's the source material I want the exam built around. It's from Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q.110, a.1 ("Whether lying is always opposed to truth?"):

"Accordingly if these three things concur, namely, falsehood of what is said, the will to tell a falsehood, and finally the intention to deceive, then there is falsehood—materially, since what is said is false, formally, on account of the will to tell an untruth, and effectively, on account of the will to impart a falsehood. However, the essential notion of a lie is taken from formal falsehood, from the fact namely, that a person intends to say what is false"

I want ONE essay question (answerable in about 2 hours) that makes students do something with this passage, not just summarize it. The crux I'm after: Aquinas splits off the *intention to deceive* from the *species* of lying, so on his view someone who says something literally true in order to deceive hasn't committed the specific act of lying -- whereas someone who says something false believing it true hasn't lied either, but for a totally different reason. I want students to have to work out, using the formal/material/effective machinery, where Aquinas would actually classify the standard modern "misleading" cases (literally-true-but-implicating-falsehood), and then to say whether that classification supports or undermines the contemporary claim that misleading is morally distinct from -- and usually morally better than -- lying. The trap I want to avoid is students just reciting "Kant says lying is always wrong, Aquinas has a taxonomy, here are three kinds of lie."
