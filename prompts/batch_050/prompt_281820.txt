I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on judgment and forecasting, and I'm writing the final exam. I want one essay question on the reference class problem that cannot be answered by reciting the advice to take the outside view. The question should present a concrete forecasting task — estimating how many more years a particular personalist autocracy that has already ruled for twenty years will survive — together with a short table of stylized base rates (invented but plausible numbers are fine) drawn from four defensible reference classes: all autocracies since 1945, personalist regimes specifically, regimes that have already survived at least twenty years, and regimes in a comparable region and income bracket. The classes should deliver noticeably different median remaining lifespans, so that the candidate cannot dodge the choice. The question should then force candidates to do three things: explain how they would select among or weight the competing reference classes and why that choice is underdetermined by the data alone; say whether conditionalizing on the regime's having survived twenty years is legitimate updating or double counting survival information already baked into the regime's description; and state what role inside-view evidence (an aging ruler with no succession plan, a recently foiled coup) should play relative to the base rates. Write the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 built around six must-hit moves — the marks each is worth, and a one-line note on the specific mistake candidates most often make on each (e.g., picking whichever class best fits the regime's story without noticing that the story was built to fit it).
