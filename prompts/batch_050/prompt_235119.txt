Two colleagues of mine, both philosophy postdocs, got into an argument at a department social that neither would drop, and they've asked me to referee. It started from a familiar case. Aaron holds that any two objects whatsoever compose a further object, so there exists a single thing made of his left shoe and the moon. Beatrice holds that composition is restricted: only certain arrangements of things add up to a genuine whole, and a shoe-plus-moon "object" simply isn't there. The argument then turned on whether their disagreement is even real. (1) Aaron insisted it is substantive: there is a fact of the matter about what exists, and exactly one of them is wrong about it, just as exactly one of two people can be wrong about whether there is a table in the next room. (2) Beatrice replied that the dispute is merely verbal. Each of them can restate the other's claims in their own vocabulary so that those claims come out true: "there is an object composed of a shoe and the moon" is true if you quantify as permissively as Aaron does and false if you quantify as restrictively as she does, and nothing in the world itself favors one way of quantifying over the other. (3) Aaron countered that her test proves too much. By that standard, even the ordinary dispute about whether there is a table in the next room is verbal, since a sceptic about tables can always translate the believer's claim into talk of particles arranged tablewise and agree that, so translated, it is true. If every disagreement that admits of mutual truth-preserving translation is verbal, then virtually no disagreement is substantive. (4) Beatrice answered that the cases differ in a way his objection misses. In the table dispute, the two parties share a common body of facts they both accept in their own mouths — which regions of spacetime contain matter, what would show up in a photograph — and the disagreement concerns exactly that shared residue. In the composition dispute, she claimed, once you fix all the facts about particles and their arrangements, nothing is left to fight over except a word. Aaron's response to this, delivered with some heat, was that she had just refuted herself: both of them do agree on all the facts about particles and arrangements, and they still disagree about whether, given those facts, there is a table, so by her own criterion there is a leftover question after all — it is simply a question about which way of speaking best fits the world's structure, and that question is as substantive as anything in metaphysics. Beatrice said that recasting the dispute as one about which language best fits the world concedes her point, since choosing between languages is a practical or aesthetic matter, not a discovery about what exists.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it succeeds, fails, or succeeds only in part, and briefly justify each rating. Then give an overall verdict on who is more nearly right about whether the composition dispute is substantive, and identify anything important that both parties are overlooking. I want a committed answer: take a definite position on each move and on the dispute as a whole, and defend it with reasons, rather than surveying the options and leaving the adjudication to me.
