Two members of our town's civic society, of which I am an ordinary member rather than an officeholder, fell into a long disagreement at last month's meeting, and it has stayed with me because I genuinely cannot settle who had the better of it. The subject was a bronze statue in our market square of an eighteenth-century merchant, born locally, whose fortune came substantially from the slave trade and who endowed the town's first hospital and its grammar school. The council is deciding whether to remove the statue, and both of them spoke.

(1) Daniel argued for removal on what he called the hospital ward principle: a plaque above a hospital bed naming its benefactor honours the gift, but a full-length figure on a plinth in the centre of town honours the man, and the man is not honourable. He said the form of a memorial fixes its meaning regardless of our intentions — nobody reads a plinth as neutral information, and so long as the statue stands, the town is performing praise every day, whether it means to or not.

(2) Helen replied that this proved too much, because the hospital itself and the school are also 'performed praise' of a far more consequential kind: every patient treated there is a beneficiary of slave-trading money, and the town continues to accept that benefit daily. If the statue must go because its existence praises him, she said, then consistency demands we close the hospital, which nobody proposes; so the real position must be that we keep the benefits while disposing of the visible reminder, which she called commemorative bookkeeping — laundering the history by removing its only public trace.

(3) Daniel answered that there is a principled difference between accepting a benefit and performing an honour: the hospital can be renamed and its origins taught in a museum display, at which point it is merely an inherited asset, like a house purchased with stolen money generations ago, which one may keep without celebrating the theft. A statue, he said, has no such 'quiet' mode — standing on a plinth in the square is inherently loud.

(4) Helen then shifted ground. She proposed what she called the replacement test: ask whether, if the square were empty today, we would commission this statue now. She conceded we would not, but argued the test is worthless because it would condemn nearly every memorial more than a century old, and so it collapses the distinction between commemorating and endorsing — treating the town's whole history as something it must continually re-sign in the present tense. She said the honest alternative was a counter-memorial: leave the merchant where he is and erect, facing him, a monument to the enslaved people whose labour paid for the hospital, so that the square tells the whole story rather than a curated half of it.

(5) Daniel's final move was to reject the counter-memorial on grounds of what he called conversational asymmetry: leaving the statue up means that residents descended from enslaved people must pass their oppressor's honoured likeness daily and carry the burden of explaining it to their children, whereas removal imposes on Helen only the loss of an artefact she occasionally glances at. He said a memorial landscape should be judged by what it costs its worst-placed residents to live with, not by what it teaches the comfortable.

(6) Helen's last point, which she admitted she was unsure of, was that Daniel's asymmetry argument, if sound, would justify removing almost anything any group found painful, and that a principle with no limiting condition cannot adjudicate between this statue and, say, the town's war memorial, which pacifist residents sincerely experience as a glorification of violence. She said both sides therefore needed a limiting principle, and she suspected neither had one.

Could you adjudicate this exchange move by move? For each of the six moves, say whether you think it succeeds, fails, or partly succeeds, and why — treating each move on its merits rather than deciding in advance which speaker you side with. I would also like to know: does the exchange contain a decisive point that settles the question, or does it reduce to a genuine clash of values? And is there a consideration — about statues, memory, or public space — that both of them seem to have missed entirely?
