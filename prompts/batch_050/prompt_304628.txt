I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training, and I keep ending up in the same argument, both at work and in a reading group a colleague dragged me into. The argument is about whether certain categories are "real" or "merely socially constructed" -- money, race, gender, and (when it's my coworkers) things like file formats and HTTP status codes. What strikes me is that nobody actually disagrees about the facts. Everyone concedes that money only works because enough people and institutions treat it as valuable, that racial categories have been drawn differently in different countries and censuses, that sex-based categories track biology in a way racial ones don't, and so on. And yet the argument never moves. One side says "constructed, therefore not real"; the other says "constructed, but still real -- it constrains what you can do, so it's as real as anything." Then both sides restate their positions with more confidence.

My working hypothesis, coming at this like an engineer, is that the two sides are applying different type checks to the word "real": one side's test is something like "would this kind still exist if no one believed in it," the other's is "does it have causal powers -- can it get you arrested, hired, killed, paid." Both tests are perfectly serviceable; the argument stalls because each side treats its own test as obviously the right one. But I could also imagine the disagreement is genuinely substantive -- about whether socially sustained kinds are as stable and as explanatory as physical ones, or about whether anything interesting follows politically from calling a category constructed. Possibly it's several different disputes stacked under one word.

So here's my request: diagnose the structure of this disagreement. Is it verbal, substantive, or a mixture, and if a mixture, which parts are which? What do the two sides actually say that supports each diagnosis? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress -- a conceptual distinction that dissolves the verbal residue, an empirical finding about how such kinds behave, or a frank admission that both tests are legitimate and the interesting work is elsewhere? Finally, I'm curious whether my engineering instinct helps or hurts here: when I define a type in a schema, it exists precisely because the system treats it as existing, and it's no less "real" in its effects for that. Does that analogy illuminate the philosophical dispute or quietly beg its central question?
