Ok so I run the history of science program at a mid-sized university, and our physics institute is turning a hundred this spring. They've asked me to give the keynote at the anniversary dinner. Audience is maybe 150 people: current faculty (a few of whom clearly want their own research framed as the natural culmination of a century of progress), some retired professors, grad students, donors, and the dean, who keeps telling me he wants the evening to feel "uplifting."

Here's the problem. My entire scholarly life has been about resisting the urge to write the history of physics as if it were always marching toward what we happen to believe today -- the old whig history temptation. And this institute's past is full of things that make the triumphant story awkward. There was a decade-long, fully confident commitment here to a theory everybody now agrees was wrong. There's a woman whose measurements got credited to her supervisor for forty years. There was a whole research program that consumed people's careers and then just quietly evaporated, not refuted so much as abandoned. If I give the steady-march-of-progress speech, I betray my own field and the handful of historians in the room will never let me forget it. If I give the corrective, deflating speech, I've ruined a birthday party and the donors start checking their phones.

So write the keynote for me. Roughly ten minutes spoken. It needs to do a few things at once: genuinely celebrate the place (no gritted-teeth celebration, the audience will smell that instantly), refuse the framing where the past century was aiming at us, and find some way of honoring the people who turned out to be wrong that doesn't come off as condescending toward them -- or as a veiled attack on the people in the room who are currently quite sure they're right. Bonus difficulty: at least one joke that lands for both the physicists and everyone else.
