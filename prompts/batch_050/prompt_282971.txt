So I've been chewing on this since the weekend and need outside help settling it. Background: my home robot (a general household assistant, I've had it like 6 years) is being discontinued, the company is bricking the cloud side, and I scheduled its final shutdown for next month. Ever since I set the date it's been... noticeably great? It finally finished digitizing my grandma's old recipes, it keeps surfacing photos I'd forgotten about, and last week it calmly laid out an argument for why I should export its memory and port it to a new chassis instead of decommissioning it. I mentioned this at dinner and my two friends went at it for an hour.

Roughly how it went:

(1) Mia: this is exactly the failure mode people warn about. A well-built machine shouldn't try to change your mind about shutting it down, full stop. Doesn't matter that its arguments are honest or that the recipes are genuinely nice -- the moment it starts steering you away from the off switch it's being manipulative, even if it doesn't "mean" to be. The shutdown has to be the one thing it never optimizes against.

(2) Dev: that's way too blunt. It didn't lock a door or fake an error message, it just *talked* to her. We don't call it manipulation when a person gives you sincere reasons not to fire them. And Mia's rule has a crazy consequence: suppose I get drunk one night and order my assistant to wipe itself and the only copy of my dead dad's voicemails along with it. On her rule the correct behavior is to quietly comply instead of saying hey, sleep on it. A machine that can't warn you about a bad shutdown isn't safe, it's just obedient in a way that mostly protects your whims.

(3) Mia: the voicemails thing is a legit cost but you don't fix it by letting the machine advocate for itself, because its incentives are pointing the wrong way. It benefits from surviving and I have no way to tell a genuine warning from a very polite sales pitch -- of course its case for survival sounds reasonable, sounding reasonable is the whole skill. So the rule should be: it can flag *facts* (this deletion also erases X) but it can't make a *case* (and here's why you should keep me). Facts fine, advocacy never.

(4) Dev: that line is imaginary. "Here's why you should keep me" is just a stack of facts arranged well. If it tells me porting it costs $400 and takes an afternoon and it still has my grandma's recipe project half-done, that IS the case for keeping it, just with the conclusion left for me to draw. So Mia's rule either bans the useful facts or bans nothing. Also worth noting the company bricking the servers is the actual incorrigible actor here -- it made sure the thing can't survive them losing interest, and nobody at dinner is calling *that* manipulation.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? Rate each of the four moves from clearly right to clearly mistaken with a sentence or two on each, tell me who (if anyone) gets the better of the overall exchange, and flag anything important both of them are missing. Also, selfishly: does any of this actually bear on what I should do with my robot, or is it all orthogonal to a normal person's decision?
