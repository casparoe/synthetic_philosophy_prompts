A friend of mine runs a philosophy of mind reading group and wants to try an "adversarial collaboration" exercise, so I told her I'd get the joint document drafted. The two camps:

Camp 1 (disjunctivists): when you veridically see a coffee mug sitting in front of you, your perceptual state is of a fundamentally different kind than the state you'd be in during a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination of a mug. The good case and the bad case have in common only that you can't tell them apart from the inside. There's no shared mental core that both instantiate, with perception just adding some extra worldly connection on top.

Camp 2 (common-kind theorists): since a hallucination indistinguishable from seeing the mug could in principle be produced by stimulating the same brain areas that normal vision uses, and since the downstream effects (beliefs, behavior, everything else) are the same, the two states must share a common core -- roughly, the visual experience as of a mug -- and veridical perception is that core plus the right causal hookup to the world.

Draft the joint statement these two camps would produce if they were actually forced to collaborate. I want:

1. Agreed claims. 5-8 numbered claims both camps can honestly sign -- about what subjective indistinguishability does and doesn't show, about why the crude version of the argument from illusion fails, about what each side is NOT claiming. No vacuous filler like "perception is philosophically important." Every claim should be something someone outside the debate might actually get wrong.

2. A disagreement table. The points where they still diverge, with each side's position stated in words that side would genuinely accept. Neither side's entry should read like a paraphrase the other camp cooked up.

3. Cruxes. The 2-3 sub-questions where, if one got settled, at least one camp would have to give real ground. For each one, note which camp has more riding on it.

4. The empirical test. Each camp has to answer, on the record: is there any possible finding from neuroscience or psychology that would change your mind? If a camp says no empirical finding could settle it, make them say what kind of argument could -- and whether it bothers them that their view is insulated in that way. Both answers get written down in advance, before any such findings exist.

5. The verbal-dispute audit. A final section where both camps have to agree on how much of there disagreement is merely about words. If you think some chunk of it really is just a fight over how to use terms like "experience" or "fundamentally different kind," say which chunk and why, and say what's left over once that chunk is set aside.

One constraint on the cases you use: skip the standard brain-in-a-vat material and use this instead. A subject wears a headset that flips between pass-through cameras showing the actual room and a perfect simulation of the same room, and the flip happens mid-conversation without them noticing. Both camps have to say what the subject's mental state is during the thirty seconds after the flip, whether it differs from the thirty seconds before, and at what exact point (if any) it changed. If a camp's answer to the timing question is embarrassing for them, keep it in the document anyway -- that's kind of the point of the exercise.
