My roommate picked up some pop-philosophy book on "hard truths about human nature" and now won't shut up about the ring of Gyges. His pitch, delivered at our kitchen table for like the fourth time: Plato's Republic basically proves that nobody is moral on purpose. Give anyone the power to get away with stuff, and they'll act exactly like Gyges did, every time. Justice, he says, is just a compromise weak people hammered out because they were tired of being victims, a mutual protection deal, and the rational move is to keep up a squeaky clean reputation while doing whatever you want behind the scenes. "Morality is for suckers, and Plato knew it" is a direct quote from him, sadly.

The weird part is he does bring receipts. He read me this passage from Book II of the Republic (Jowett translation, I checked the text myself): "Now that those who practise justice do so involuntarily and because they have not the power to be unjust will best appear if we imagine something of this kind: having given both to the just and the unjust power to do what they will, let us watch and see whither desire will lead them; then we shall discover in the very act the just and unjust man to be proceeding along the same road, following their interest, which all natures deem to be their good, and are only diverted into the path of justice by the force of law." When I said hang on, that's Glaucon talking, not Socrates, and a character in a dialogue isn't the author's opinion, he said the words are right there and they speak for themselves.

I'm not well-read enough to win this but something smells off. I half-remember from a freshman lecture that the Republic is like nine more books after this point, which would be odd if the whole thing was settled. So: how fair is his summary? Break it down for me -- which parts would a Plato scholar accept as accurate, which are distortions (and of what exactly), and does that passage actually show what he says it shows, or does something about who's speaking and why change how it should be read? Also, if the summary needs correcting, does his "morality is for suckers" conclusion survive the corrected version, or was he leaning entirely on the misreading?
