I'm a graduate student helping to run a seminar on knowledge-first epistemology, and I've been asked to prepare a handout on the KK principle and luminosity. The recurring problem in our sessions is that students treat the debate as a two-party standoff: internalists who think knowing requires being in a position to know that you know, versus externalists who deny this on reliability grounds. Meanwhile the more interesting structure — which conditions are even candidates for luminosity, whether the margin-for-error style of argument generalizes from knowledge to one's own phenomenal states, whether rejecting KK forces one to reject all luminosity or only luminosity for knowledge — gets lost. I want a decision tree that fixes this: it should take a student from the intuitive starting commitment that some of one's own mental conditions are transparent to one (when you have a splitting headache, you are in a position to know that you do) to a named position, purely by answering questions about concrete cases and principles, never by picking a label.

Please build the tree as follows. Every branch node should be a question phrased so a student can answer it without knowing where it leads — for instance, a question about a slowly changing case (a light dimming imperceptibly over an hour, a person becoming cold so gradually that each minute feels just like the last), a question about what follows if you know something but your evidence leaves open a margin of error, or a question about whether the transparency intuition about headaches survives being iterated (if you're in a condition, you know it; if you know it, you know you know it; and so on). Leaves should include at minimum: unqualified KK internalism; the standard externalist rejection of KK via reliabilism; Williamson-style anti-luminosity, on which nothing nontrivial is luminous, not even one's current experiences; a safety-based middle position; and some form of restricted luminosity that grants transparency to current phenomenal states while denying KK for knowledge generally. For each leaf, give the position's name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment (for anti-luminosity, presumably the claim that you can be in excruciating pain and in no position to know it). Then survey the answer-combinations the tree permits but nobody occupies, and for each give a verdict: is the combination incoherent, coherent but unattractive, or genuinely unexplored? Finally, flag any leaves your tree cannot actually separate from each other and say why, rather than forcing a distinction.

One more thing: I don't want a neutral map. The handout should take a stand in two places. First, identify which single branching question you take to be the real fault line of the debate — the one where the deepest disagreement actually lives, as opposed to nodes where the split is verbal or downstream — and defend that choice. Second, commit to one leaf as the correct position and give the strongest two or three reasons for it, including an honest statement of what you think it costs. If any part of the tree rests on a contested reading of a particular philosopher's argument, note that explicitly in passing; I'll fill in citations myself, so no bibliography is needed.
