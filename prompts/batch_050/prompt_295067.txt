I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on collective responsibility and postwar political thought. We recently worked through Karl Jaspers's 1946 lectures, The Question of German Guilt, where he separates criminal guilt (individual violations of law, settled by courts), political guilt (the shared liability of citizens for the deeds of their state), moral guilt (answered before one's own conscience), and metaphysical guilt (answered before something like humanity as such, arising from failures of solidarity). He also insists that speaking of an entire people as collectively guilty in the criminal or moral sense is a category mistake, and he links the acknowledgment of guilt to a process of inner renewal.

For my final essay I want to stress-test the framework against a case it wasn't built for, and I'd like your help thinking it through before I commit to an outline. The case: a 22-year-old citizen of a wealthy industrialized democracy. Her country's cumulative carbon emissions have contributed substantially to harms now falling on poorer nations, whose representatives are demanding payments under the slogan that wealthy states bear historical guilt. Her government continues to license new fossil fuel extraction. She personally benefits from cheap energy but chose none of this, and she resents being told that her generation "owes" for decisions made before she was born — while also feeling that pure innocence is too easy an answer.

Please reconstruct, as rigorously as you can, how Jaspers would likely sort her situation among his four categories, and which category would do the most work. Then press on three specific points where I suspect the framework strains: (1) his notion of political guilt seems to presuppose co-membership in a state whose organs act, but climate harm is an aggregate effect across many states, firms, and consumers — can political liability attach to beneficiaries of a system rather than citizens of a perpetrator state? (2) metaphysical guilt, as he develops it, arises from standing by while fellow humans suffer when one could have acted in solidarity — does it coherently extend to future persons, or does that stretch the concept past breaking? (3) if he would reject blanket "climate guilt" rhetoric as the same kind of mistake he diagnosed in 1946, what vocabulary would he offer instead, and would that vocabulary still be able to justify the demanded payments?

Where the text supports an answer, ground the reconstruction in his actual arguments; where you are extrapolating, say so explicitly and flag the two or three points where his position most plausibly needs revision rather than mere application. No citations needed — I'll supply those myself.
