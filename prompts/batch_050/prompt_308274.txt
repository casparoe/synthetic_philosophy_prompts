I'm a novelist and I'm stuck on a story, so I want you to write me a draft I can steal ideas from. Roughly 1,200 words. It's for a fiction anthology, not a philosophy journal, so it has to work as a story first and the concept second.

The setup: there's a city whose law is written entirely in generics. Not universal rules like "all citizens must pay taxes" but statements like "dockworkers are honest", "cats are ungrateful", "bakers wake before dawn". Here's the thing about this city that outsiders never get: its jurists have always held that a generic can be true law and still have exceptions. "Dockworkers are honest" stands as law even though plenty of dockworkers lie, and nobody thinks that's a contradiction. An exception doesn't void a generic. That's just what kind of statement a generic is.

The story turns on this doctrine suddenly becoming politically live. A faction -- I'm picturing young reformists, call them the purists -- starts arguing that any generic with to many exceptions is void, because a law that tolerates lawbreakers isn't a law at all, just a stereotype with a police force. If they win, the whole legal order unravels, since every law on the books is a generic. Or does it unravel? That's what I want the story to work out.

Constraints:

- The conceptual heart is something like: "mosquitoes carry malaria" is true even though only a small minority of mosquitoes carry it, while a genuinely universal claim dies to a single counterexample -- but never, ever state this outright. No character should explain it. It has to come out through what happens.
- Give me one concrete case, one defendant or petitioner, who IS the exception to a generic and whose life or livelihood depends on whether the generic still holds despite them. My clerk protagonist has to decide, or witness the magistrate decide.
- The ending shouldn't settle the doctrine. The clerk should act on some position, but it should feel costly and maybe wrong.
- No narrator lecturing. The ideas surface through rulings, appeals, what gets said in the tavern after.

One more note on tone: the generics in this city aren't magic. Nothing enforces them. The only strange thing about the place is that it took seriously something ordinary language already does, which is let true generalizations shrug off exceptions. Write it like the city is perfectly ordinary to the people in it.
