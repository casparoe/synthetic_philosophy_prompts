I've been defending the view that, among the standard historical analogies for transformative AI, the printing press does the most analytical work. My claim: nuclear weapons merely added a capability to an otherwise unchanged world, whereas the press transformed the machinery by which society produces, certifies, and distributes belief — and that is the closest structural match to what AI systems do, including their risks. What would count against this position? Treat the analogy as a predictive instrument rather than a rhetorical device: which features of the actual printing-press episode, examined closely rather than as a slogan (its slow diffusion, the way its harms ran through collapsing institutions rather than the devices themselves, the near-total failure of licensing regimes), undermine my claim or merely complicate it? Which observable properties of AI — concentration of control, speed of capability gain, reversibility of deployment, or the possibility that the technology exhibits something like agency rather than only transmitting human content — would force a switch to the nuclear or Industrial Revolution analogy, and for which specific downstream predictions? Please rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing those a careful defender of the press analogy would concede from those they would reframe as supporting evidence. Finally: assess whether a view like mine genuinely risks anything, or whether historical analogies of this kind can always be reinterpreted to fit events — and if the latter, whether that makes the whole enterprise of choosing analogies useless or just weaker than I assumed.
