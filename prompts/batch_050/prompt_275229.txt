Last weekend a close friend and I ended up in a long, increasingly heated argument about rational belief, and I want to know whether either of us was actually right. Her position rested on an analogy she treated as decisive. Degrees of belief, she said, are like the odds a bookmaker posts. If a bookmaker's odds on a set of propositions violate the rules of probability -- say, implying a 60 percent chance that it rains and a 60 percent chance that it doesn't -- then a clever bettor can place a combination of bets such that the bookmaker loses money no matter what happens. Such a bookmaker is plainly doing something wrong. By the same token, she argued, a person whose credences violate the probability axioms can in principle be put in a position where they accept a set of bets that jointly guarantee them a loss, and that shows probabilistic coherence is a genuine requirement of rationality, not just a convention.

I pushed back that this seems to conflate two very different failures: being stupid about money and being unreasonable in one's beliefs. Beliefs are not wagers; nobody is offering me a book on most of what I think; and even if someone were, I'd be free to refuse, which a bookmaker who posts odds is not. She replied that the bets are just a diagnostic device, not the literal point, and we went in circles from there.

So: audit the betting analogy for me. First give the correspondence table -- what in the epistemic situation plays the role of the bookmaker, the posted odds, the cunning bettor, the combination of bets, and the guaranteed loss. Then separate the disanalogies into two groups: those that are merely features of the illustrative story (and so leave the argument untouched) and those that actually threaten its inferential step from guaranteed monetary loss to epistemic irrationality. Candidates I'd like assessed include the diminishing marginal utility of money, the fact that a real agent can decline bets or care about things besides net payoff, the fact that no actual bettor is exploiting most people's incoherent credences, and the question of whether a purely hypothetical loss can bear any normative weight. Finally, say whether the conclusion -- that rational credences must be probabilistically coherent -- survives once the analogy is stripped to what it can legitimately carry, or whether it needs support from a different kind of argument altogether, and if the latter, sketch what a better analogy or a more direct argument would look like.

Please also indicate how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the single point in your answer where you are least sure of yourself, with a sentence on why.
