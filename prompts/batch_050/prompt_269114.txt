I co-host a small econ-and-philosophy podcast (we're talking a few hundred listeners, nothing fancy) and we've booked a guest who's a defender of "ergodicity economics" -- the view, associated mostly with Ole Peters and the Max Planck crowd, that economics went wrong by leaning on ensemble averages / expected values when it should be asking what happens to an individual's wealth over time, so that you should maximize the time-average growth rate rather than the expected payoff. He's sharp and gives good radio, but he's also very practiced, and I don't want an hour of him re-explaining the coin flip game where you gain 50% or lose 40% and why naive expected value says take it while the time average says don't. I already get that bit, and so do most of our listeners by now.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that get past the standard pitch? Here's where I'd like the questions to press, roughly in this order: first, the objection that the whole thing is just logarithmic utility wearing a fake mustache, since maximizing expected log wealth gets you the same betting advice -- his likely answer is that the time-average approach derives the behavior from dynamics rather than assuming a utility function, and I'd like a follow-up that tests whether that distinction actually changes any recommendations or is just philosophical window dressing. Second, one-shot decisions with no repetition: his framework seems to need time to work, so what does it say to someone choosing once, like whether to take a particular job or have a surgery -- and if he retreats to "life is a sequence of decisions," ask what counts as one trial. Third, insurance and risk pooling: insurance companies seem to make genuinely non-ergodic gambles okay for individuals by pooling, which his side actually celebrates as cooperation, so where does that leave the blanket skepticism about expected-value reasoning -- isn't pooling precisely how you turn time averages into something like ensemble averages? Fourth, the empirical bite: does the framework actually predict anything about leverage, savings behavior, or inequality that standard models get wrong, or does it mostly re-interpret results everyone already had? And fifth, an honest-failure question: what observation or result would convince him the whole research program is on the wrong track.

For each question, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, since he has well-worn answers to the obvious stuff. Also keep the wording conversational enough that I can ask them out loud without sounding like I'm reading an exam. American English, please. Oh and if a question only works for a mathematician, flag it -- he's an econophysicist by training but I am very much not.
