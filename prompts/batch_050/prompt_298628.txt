I'm putting together a worksheet for a philosophy of science reading group on verisimilitude -- the idea that one false theory can be closer to the truth than another, and that science might progress by getting truer rather than by being confirmed. Everyone in the group has read a bit of Popper and has heard that his account collapsed under technical objections, but they keep using "closer to the truth" anyway without knowing what they mean by it. So I want a sorting instrument, not an essay.

Please build me a decision tree (questions with yes/no or pick-one answers, leading to named leaves) that sorts a person into a position on verisimilitude based purely on what they think, without them needing to know any of the names in advance. Roughly 6-8 diagnostic questions total.

Constraints on how to build it:

- Build the tree out of the logical space first: what questions actually divide the views, which combinations of answers are coherent, and only then locate the actual positions in it. Note any empty branch and say why it's empty (incoherent, just unattractive, or simply unexplored).
- Each diagnostic question should be anchored in a concrete case, not abstract jargon. Cases I'd like used, though you can adapt them: a thermometer that consistently reads 2°C high; the claim "the number of planets is 7" when the true answer is 8 (and the rival claim "the number of planets is 10,000"); the transition from Newtonian mechanics to general relativity; and a weather forecaster whose daily predictions are accurate on average but wrong about which specific days it rains.
- The leaves must include at least: Popper's original content-comparison account; a post-Popper relevant-content approach; the likeness/similarity approach where verisimilitude is some kind of distance between possible worlds; a problem-relative view where "closer to the truth" only makes sense relative to a class of questions or consequences; and outright skepticism that the notion can be made rigorous (Miller-style). Add any other leaf you think the space forces on us.
- One of the diagnostic questions should turn on the language-dependence worry: whether a measure of closeness to the truth should survive re-describing everything in a weird but logically equivalent vocabulary (the grue-and-bleen style trick Miller used against Popper).
- At least one leaf should carry a one-sentence diagnosis of its most awkward commitment (like the child's play result that on Popper's account no false theory can be improved by tacking on truths).
- If two positions land on the same leaf and your tree can't separate them, say so explicitly rather than faking a question that doesn't really divide them.

Format: the tree as indented text or a flowchart-in-words is fine. End with a short note (a few sentences) on which question in the tree does the most work -- i.e., where reasonable people fork earliest and can't be talked back together.
