I am a graduate student TA, and next week I have to teach the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism in a single sixty-minute section to second-year philosophy undergraduates who are comfortable with propositional logic but have had no metaethics. Do not explain the problem to me; plan how I should explain it.

Specifically, I want:

1. The order in which the components must be introduced, and the dependency structure behind that order. What has to be in place before the embedding phenomenon even registers as a problem rather than a mere observation about grammar? For instance, should students first separate an account of what a moral sentence means from an account of what a moral utterance does? And does the general constraint that a sentence must contribute the same content in embedded and unembedded positions need to be stated for plainly descriptive language before any normative example appears, or does that sequencing backfire?

2. The one example that should carry the whole session. I am reluctant to use the standard moral case — some moral sentence embedded in a conditional and then detached by modus ponens — because students arrive with settled views about the moral content and the normativity distracts from the logical point. I have been considering building the session on expressivism about ascriptions of disgust, or about epistemic uses of "might," instead. Tell me which example to use, what properties it must have and must lack to carry the argument, and whether abandoning the moral domain loses anything essential to the problem itself.

3. The misconception map. I expect at least two: that the problem is just the claim that normative sentences are not truth-apt, and that the equivocation charge shows expressivists simply cannot have valid arguments rather than that they owe an explanation of why the inference is not equivocal. List the misconceptions this audience will predictably form, in the order they will arise, and mark exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact.

4. Where the session should stop. Which refinements — minimalism about truth as a rejoinder, the problem of negation, embeddings under embeddings — should I name but openly decline to treat at this level, and how do I mark them as live issues without making them sound like refutations?

5. One closing question that would reveal whether the idea has landed — ideally one requiring students to reconstruct the problem in a domain the session never touched, rather than recite the version they were shown.
