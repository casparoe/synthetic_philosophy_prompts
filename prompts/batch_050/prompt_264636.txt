I was recently appointed to the regional board that must decide how the costs of a major water-main replacement are distributed, and an argument I drafted to defend one of the options now seems to me to prove far too much. The live choice is between closing one commercial street entirely for fourteen months, which will ruin most of the businesses on it, and instead routing all traffic around the works in a way that adds about ninety seconds to every commute in the metropolitan area for the same period. My reasoning ran as follows. (1) A ninety-second delay is a real cost, and costs of the same kind aggregate: two people delayed is worse than one, and a million is worse than two. (2) There is no point on this scale at which further delays abruptly cease to count, so for any severe burden there exists some number of people such that imposing a trivial burden on each of them is worse than imposing the severe burden on one person. (3) The region's population is enormous, so the aggregate of the small delays exceeds the losses of the street's business owners, and the street closure is therefore the right choice. (4) But the identical reasoning appears to commit me to the claim that one person's being ruined, tortured, or killed can be the best outcome provided sufficiently many others are spared a headache or a speck of dust in the eye. (5) I cannot accept (4). (6) Yet if I deny that trivial harms aggregate across persons, I seem forced to say that a billion people each losing a day of life is, considered as a cost, no worse than one person losing a day, which I also cannot accept. Each premise looks innocent in isolation. Which step should I reject, and what precisely does rejecting it cost me? In particular, I would like to know whether the repair leaves me any principled basis for deciding the actual case before the board, or whether it can adjudicate only the extreme cases and leaves mine to intuition.
