So I've been chewing on this for ages and I can't make it come out consistent. My town does a fireworks show every summer that gets payed for by tickets to the official viewing area. But you can see the entire thing perfectly fine from the supermarket parking lot across the river, and a few hundred people do exactly that, and nobody calls them freeloaders. The organizers even send food trucks over there. But last year a guy in my neighborhood got absolutely roasted online after he admitted he'd been using his neighbor's unsecured wifi for like six months. She had unlimited data and never even noticed, so it cost her literally nothing. And I remember hearing about a baseball team that put up a big screen specifically to block people in the apartment buildings next door from watching games off their own rooftops, and the general reaction was that this was the team's right, like the rooftop people had basically been stealing.

Same basic move in all three cases, no? You benefit from something somebody else paid for, you contribute nothing, and your enjoying it doesn't leave less for anyone else (the wifi wasn't even slowed down). Yet the parking lot people are fine, the rooftop people are thieves, and the wifi guy is a sleaze. What is the actual difference doing the work here?

My half-baked theory was that we care about whether the provider tried to keep you out -- like making an effort to exclude people is what creates the obligation to pay. But that falls apart instantly. Leaving your front door unlocked doesn't mean strangers can walk in, and the wifi was unsecured too, so that theory would acquit the wifi guy. So then I thought maybe it's whether your free use threatens the whole thing continuing to exist. But then the parking lot crowd should feel guilty, and they clearly, clearly don't. Also where does a busker fit in? The hat is right there, you're openly invited to pay, and still nobody thinks you owe anything for stopping to listen.

So here's what I want: figure out what theory of fair play, or of what counts as "taking" something, these gut reactions quietly assume. Reconstruct the implicit theory as best you can. Can all of these judgments be made consistent under one theory, or do different cases secretly assume different theories? And is there one theory the whole pattern strictly requires, or would several different theories (harm-based, reciprocity-based, whatever) all produce the same pattern, so that the everyday practice is just underdetermined and doesn't really tell us what people believe? Finally: if we threw out whatever's underneath and replaced it with a strict "if nobody's worse off, no wrong was done" view, what would actually have to change -- do we have to stop blaming the wifi guy and the rooftop watchers entirely, and is there any version of that position that's even remotely livable?

To be clear I'm not asking about the law, I know wifi piggybacking can be illegal in places. I'm asking about the moral logic underneath the gut reactions, because mine are apparently running on a theory I can't state.
