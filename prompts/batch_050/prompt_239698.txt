I serve on my hospital's ethics committee, and we are about to rule on a transplant proposal that has split us down the middle. I'd like you to act as a bioethicist with long experience advising hospitals on death determination: think carefully through our record before answering. Our precedents are these. (1) We approved organ procurement from a donor declared dead by neurological criteria whose heart was still beating on a ventilator. (2) We approved controlled donation after circulatory death: life support withdrawn from a patient who had declined resuscitation, death declared after a five-minute no-touch period following circulatory arrest, then procurement. (3) We rejected a proposal to procure organs from an anencephalic infant with an intact brainstem who was breathing spontaneously, even though everyone agreed the infant would never have any conscious experience. (4) We rejected a protocol that would have declared death only 75 seconds after circulatory arrest. (5) We approved continuing ventilator and pressor support for several days on a patient already declared dead by neurological criteria, purely to improve organ viability, on the explicit grounds that this was management of a deceased body, not treatment of a patient.

The new proposal is this. A controlled circulatory-death donation would proceed exactly as in case (2), but after the five-minute declaration the surgical team would surgically clamp every vessel supplying the donor's head and then restart circulation to the rest of the body via a mechanical circuit, perfusing the thoracic and abdominal organs in situ to improve their quality before removal. The promoters argue the donor remains dead because brain function can never return; the opponents argue that you cannot declare someone dead because their circulation has permanently ceased and then deliberately restart it in most of their body an hour later.

Please do the following, in order: give the verdict that best coheres with our own precedents; identify which single precedent is the closest analog and why; state the two or three general principles consistent with our record, what each predicts here, and which principle we would be adopting with each possible verdict; and describe one hypothetical future case, different from this one, whose resolution would tell the competing principles apart. If our record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, say so honestly rather than forcing consistency. Begin with your recommended verdict in one sentence, and keep the entire response under 600 words.
