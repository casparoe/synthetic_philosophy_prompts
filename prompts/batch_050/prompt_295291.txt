Me and a friend cooked this up after reading about the separateness of persons objection and it's clearly broken because the conclusion is insane, but we cant find the bad step:

(1) Within one life, a cost now is made okay by a benefit later only because the same person gets both — the later benefit compensates me for the earlier hit.
(2) But people are separate: a benefit to you is not a benefit to me, and there is no subject who experiences the sum of our welfares. So a gain to one person can never compensate a loss to a different person (that's the Rawls-ish point about utilitarianism treating everyone like one big person).
(3) So a loss imposed on someone is only ever justified if that same person is compensated; the sheer size of the gains to others is irrelevant on its own.
(4) But almost every choice leaves someone worse off than some alternative would have: taxing anyone, diverting the trolley, building the hospital here instead of there, even just sitting at home doing nothing (the people you could have saved get nothing). Whatever you pick, somebody bears an uncompensated loss.
(5) So almost every action, and almost every inaction, is morally wrong.
(6) That's absurd, so something above has to go.

Which step do you reject and why did it look fine? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (2) — I don't want to end up a utilitarian — and I also want to keep the ordinary verdict that you should save the five over the one. Tell me what rejecting the culprit costs me on those two fronts, and whether there's a fixed-up version of the objection that still has bite against utilitarianism specifically or whether it was only ever about something narrower.
