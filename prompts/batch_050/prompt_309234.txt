I have a hobbyist's interest in Kant. Translate the Kantian debate about moral worth and acting for the right reasons into that vocabulary as precisely as you can, then give me a residue report.

The debate, as I understand it: Kant holds that an action has genuine moral worth only when done from duty, not merely in conformity with duty -- his shopkeeper who deals honestly with customers out of prudence acts correctly but without moral worth, unlike one who acts honestly because honesty is what duty requires. Beyond this, I want the translation to cover the harder cases philosophers raise: overdetermination (an agent moved by both duty and inclination -- does the inclination contaminate the worth, or does it suffice that duty alone would have moved them?); whether moral worth comes in degrees; the opacity problem (if agents cannot reliably know their own motives, can moral worth ever be ascribed, even by the agent themselves?); and the distinction between motivating reasons and normative reasons.

Specifically: map the dutiful agent and the prudent shopkeeper onto agents in an RL framework. Is "acting from duty" best rendered as optimizing the true reward versus a shaped or instrumental reward, or as having the right objective versus merely the right behavior, or something else entirely? Treat inverse RL carefully here: if an agent's reward function cannot be uniquely inferred from its behavior, does that underdetermination do the same work as the philosophers' point that you cannot read moral worth off outward conduct? And for overdetermination: is there a meaningful RL counterpart to asking which motive was decisive -- for instance, counterfactual queries about what the policy would do with one incentive removed?

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which claims translate cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and which have no expression at all in a framework where an agent is nothing but a policy plus whatever objective or history produced it? In particular: does "for the right reasons" name anything real once you grant that two agents with identical dispositions differ only in their internal history -- or does the moral-worth debate turn out to rest on a distinction that a purely behavioral-cum-causal description cannot locate? Finally, tell me which way the residue cuts: does it expose a genuine lack in my vocabulary, or does it suggest the philosophers are arguing about something with no functional correlate -- and defend your verdict rather than hedging it.
