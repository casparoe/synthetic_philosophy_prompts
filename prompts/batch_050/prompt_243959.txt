I'm a high school student taking a philosophy elective, and I'm writing a short paper on Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert, in which he argues that Geneva should not establish a theater. I'm stuck on what looks to me like a fatal problem for taking the letter seriously, and I'd like help figuring out where my reasoning goes wrong.

As I understand it, Rousseau's case is that a theater would damage the morals of a city like Geneva: it would encourage idleness and luxury, drain citizens' money, give social prominence to actors — a profession he thinks fosters bad character — and, most importantly, stage plays that succeed only by flattering the audience's existing tastes and vices rather than improving them. He even criticizes Molière's Misanthrope on the grounds that it makes the audience laugh at its most honest character. So his position seems to be that the theater is morally corrosive.

Here is my problem. Rousseau himself composed a successful opera and wrote at least one play, all before he wrote the letter, and he stayed involved with music for the rest of his life. Near the end of the letter he also speaks warmly of public festivals and open-air celebrations as things a republic ought to have, so he plainly has no objection to entertainment or spectacle as such. To me this adds up to one of two things. Either he was a hypocrite, trying to deny Geneva an art form he had personally practiced and enjoyed, or he changed his mind sometime after writing his own works for the stage — in which case the letter looks less like principled philosophy and more like the rationalization of a personal reversal. On either reading, I don't see why we should engage with the letter's arguments on their merits rather than treating the whole thing as a biographical curiosity. But my teacher insists that philosophers still read the letter as a serious work of political thought, and that the "hypocrite or changed his mind" diagnosis is too quick.

What am I missing? In particular: is there a way of reading the letter on which writing an opera and opposing a theater in Geneva are consistent positions — and if there isn't, would that actually weaken his arguments, or only his biography?
