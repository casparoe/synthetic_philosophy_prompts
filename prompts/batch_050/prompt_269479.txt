In a graduate seminar on space policy we've spent the term debating METI -- proposals to deliberately beam high-power, structured messages at nearby star systems rather than merely listening. During the most recent session, one participant ended a long exchange by saying: "The universe is a dark forest. The first rule of a dark forest is that you don't shout." It clearly comes from a well-known science fiction treatment of the Fermi paradox, and it landed with real force in the room -- several people who had been pro-broadcasting went quiet. But when I pressed afterward on what exactly the slogan is claiming, I got very different answers from different people: one said it's a game-theoretic dominance argument, another said it's just the precautionary principle dressed up, a third said it's an empirical prediction about what happens to noisy civilizations. I couldn't get anyone to commit to a single precise thesis.

Can you lay out, as an explicit menu, the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making? I can already see some candidate readings: that detection is asymmetric (whoever reveals position first hands others a decisive informational advantage); that intentions can never be credibly verified across civilizations, so trust is impossible; that technological growth rates make current power gaps unreliable, so any detected civilization is a future threat; that first strikes are cheap relative to defense, making offense dominant; and, strongest of all, that firing preemptively is rational regardless of the target's disposition. There are presumably others I'm missing, including weaker expected-value readings.

For each reading, please do three things. First, state the hidden premises it needs (about offense-defense balance, verification, one-shot versus repeated interaction, detection costs, priors over alien motivations). Second, give a verdict: is it a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or actually false as stated? Third, say whether that reading is strong enough to support the specific use it was put to in my seminar -- namely, a near-conclusive objection to deliberate broadcasting. In particular, I'd like to know which reading is doing the real work in the slogan's rhetorical success, and whether that reading survives the obvious rejoinders: that we've already been leaking radio for a century, that would-be strikers face their own coordination problems, and that a policy of silence might itself be detectable or self-defeating.
