I am a novelist, and my professional life sits awkwardly against a puzzle I keep being handed by readers. At a reading last month, a woman told me she had wept over the death of a minor character in my last book and then apologized, as though she had been taken in. When I said there was nothing to apologize for, a man in the audience invoked the old slogan "suspension of disbelief": the suggestion being that she had, for a few chapters, half-forgotten the character wasn't real, and that this explained the tears. That can't be right, and I have two exhibits. First, I invented the man in question — I know better than anyone alive that he never existed — and yet his death affected me more in the writing, draft after draft, than it seems to affect most readers. Second, readers who reread the book, knowing exactly what is coming and when, report crying again at the same page. Neither fact sits well with the idea that emotion here requires some lapse of belief, and I want to understand what the slogan could be getting at instead, because I'm drafting an author's note for the next edition and refuse to write something pious and false.

You are a professional philosopher of mind and aesthetics with particular expertise in the paradox of fiction. Treat the phrase "suspension of disbelief" as your object of analysis. Before composing your answer, briefly brainstorm the full space of possible readings, then present the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making: for instance, a descriptive psychological thesis that audiences temporarily lose, weaken, or quarantine their belief that the fiction is unreal; a normative thesis about what competent readers ought to do with their beliefs; a thesis about a state short of belief (quasi-belief, "alief," or imagining) that generates emotion without conviction; and a deflationary thesis that emotion simply doesn't require belief in the object's existence at all, so there is nothing to suspend. For each reading, say carefully what it would have to mean to be true, whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and — this is the part that matters to me — whether it can actually account for my two exhibits: the author's own grief for his invention, and the undiminished tears of the rereader.

Then commit. I do not want a neutral survey that leaves me to choose; I want a verdict with reasons. Tell me which single reading, precisified properly, captures whatever truth the slogan contains and deserves to survive; tell me which reading does the most damage when people repeat it in bookstores and classrooms, and why; and tell me in practical terms what a novelist should understand himself to be doing when he builds a scene intended to move people who are in full possession of the facts. End your response with a final line of exactly this form: Verdict: [a short phrase naming the precisification of the slogan I should endorse in my author's note].
