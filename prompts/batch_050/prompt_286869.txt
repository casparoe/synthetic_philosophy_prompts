So I hang out on a couple of rationalist-blog spinoff forums and discords, and there's one move I keep losing arguments to and I want it taken apart properly. Most recent case: a thread about those Navy pilot UFO encounters — multiple trained observers, instrument data from more than one sensor, gun-camera footage, the works. I was arguing that whatever you think is going on, this is genuinely strange evidence that should shift our credences at least somewhat away from "business as usual," and someone replied with just "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence," picked up a pile of upvotes, and the thread treated that as checkmate. It felt like a thought-terminating cliche, but I couldn't say exactly why, because the slogan sounds like it's just Bayes' theorem in a trenchcoat.

Answer as a philosopher who's fluent in both Bayesian epistemology and the Hume-on-miracles literature, and start by brainstorming the full menu of distinct precise claims the slogan could be making before you evaluate any of them. The candidates I can gesture at: (a) the modest claim that a low-prior hypothesis needs stronger evidence to reach a given posterior than a high-prior one — which seems to be a platitude, just Bayes; (b) the stronger claim that "extraordinary evidence" is a special category, evidence of a different kind rather than merely a lot of the ordinary kind — but I can't figure out what "kind" would even mean here; (c) a claim specifically about testimony, since Hume's actual argument in "Of Miracles" (section 10 of the Enquiry) is framed around when testimony can establish a violation of a law of nature, and his version reads more like a tug-of-war between two improbabilities — accept the report only if its falsehood would be an even bigger miracle than the thing reported; (d) maybe just a debating norm about which claims you may dismiss without engagement. For each reading: is it trivial, substantive-but-defensible, or actually false? Two things I especially want adjudicated. First, does the slogan double-count the prior — if the strength of evidence needed already scales with prior improbability under Bayes, doesn't demanding evidence that is "extraordinary" in proportion to the claim penalize weird hypotheses twice? Second, is Hume's maxim even a version of the slogan, or something subtler — he seems to allow in principle that testimony could win (his hypothetical about a deep darkness covering the whole earth for days on end, where he appears to say we'd have to accept massive concordant testimony), and his bit about the Indian prince being rational to reject reports of frozen water seems to complicate the picture further.

Then the payoff question: which of these readings, if any, actually licenses ending the UFO thread the way my interlocutor did — and if none of them does, what would the honest, non-slogan version of his move sound like? Give rough confidence levels for your main claims, and explicitly flag the single point you're least sure of. My guess is it'll be the Hume interpretation, since I gather people fight about whether he was erecting an in-principle barrier against testimonial proof of miracles or merely making an evidential point, and about whether the essay's second half takes back what the first half gives — but suprise me if it's something else.
