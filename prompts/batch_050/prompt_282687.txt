Having spent my career as an experimental biologist, I have since retirement been reading philosophy of biology, and I keep returning to the dispute between etiological accounts of biological function (on which a trait's function is roughly the effect for which it was selected) and causal-role accounts (on which a function is simply the contribution a component makes to some capacity of the containing system). I would like you to describe the most important implications of this dispute for a concrete situation I am involved in. I sit on the advisory board of a small research institute that engineers microorganisms for environmental release — for instance, a soil bacterium built from synthetic gene circuits to degrade a pesticide, fitted with a designed kill switch. Our internal reports constantly use function-language: we say a circuit is malfunctioning, that a mutation has destroyed a gene's function, or that some byproduct of the construct is a mere side effect rather than part of what it is for. But these organisms have no selection history of the relevant kind, and several board members disagree about whether our function-talk is even coherent. Please explain which practical consequences for our reporting, failure analysis, and attribution of malfunction actually follow from adopting one account over the other, and which alleged consequences do not follow. Where the two accounts deliver the same verdicts in practice, say so plainly; where they genuinely diverge — as I suspect they do for cases like a kill switch that fails in a way that happens to benefit the organism, or a circuit whose effect drifts after release — spell out what each account would have us write in the report.
