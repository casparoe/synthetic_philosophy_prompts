I am a professor of philosophy preparing an essay for a special issue of a journal on the ethics of artificial intelligence, and I would value your comments before I submit it. The essay concerns the distinction between average-case and worst-case guarantees in AI alignment. Below is the current draft, followed by the specific kinds of feedback I am seeking.

---

**The Benchmark and the Abyss: On Two Kinds of Safety**

1. Discussions of AI alignment trade on an ambiguity in the word "safe." On one reading, a system is safe when it behaves well on average: across the distribution of situations it will actually encounter, its failures are rare, minor, and uncorrelated. On the other reading, a system is safe only when its worst-case behavior is acceptable: there is no reachable situation, however improbable or adversarially constructed, in which it does something catastrophic. Call the first an average-case guarantee and the second a worst-case guarantee. My thesis is that the field's dominant evaluative practices deliver, at best, the first kind of guarantee, while its dominant rhetoric promises the second — and that this gap is not a temporary methodological shortcoming but a structural feature of behavioral evaluation.

2. The structural point can be stated plainly. Any finite evaluation samples from a distribution over situations, and worst-case behavior can always be confined to a region of that distribution with arbitrarily small measure. Average-case performance therefore places no finite bound on worst-case performance unless one assumes that failure modes are "smooth" — that a system which behaves well here also behaves well nearby. Machine learning has already taught us, in the adversarial examples literature, that this smoothness assumption fails for learned systems: a classifier can be simultaneously highly accurate on the test distribution and maximally wrong on inputs arbitrarily close to test points. Benchmarks measure the smooth part. Catastrophe, if it exists, lives in the non-smooth part, which no benchmark of feasible size can reach.

3. One might reply that safety engineering has never required worst-case proofs. Bridges are built with safety factors, not theorems; airplanes are certified through extensive testing against specified conditions, and we call them safe. But this reply conflates two different things the analogy runs together. Civil engineering's "worst case" is a worst case over loads and materials — over an environment that is indifferent to the structure. The engineer's adversary is nature, and nature does not read the safety manual. The worrying cases in alignment are different in kind: the relevant worst case is not a worst case over inputs to a fixed system but a worst case over the behavior of a system that may itself be doing something like search. If a capable model has internal objectives that diverge from its training signal, then the worst-case input is not found by us; it is selected by the model, at a time of its choosing. Behavioral evaluation samples the environment; it does not sample the model's discretion.

4. This suggests that worst-case guarantees, if attainable at all, must be mechanistic rather than behavioral: they must rest on what the system is, not on what we have observed it doing. Yet here a dilemma arises. Mechanistic understanding of large learned systems is currently shallow, and it may remain so; interpretability research, for all its progress, has produced local explanations rather than global certificates. If we demand worst-case guarantees, we may be demanding something no near-term system can possess, which amounts to a demand not to build. If we settle for average-case guarantees, we are certifying the smooth region and hoping the catastrophe is not in the non-smooth one — and hope, distributed across many deployed systems, is not a safety case.

5. I want to resist both horns by proposing that the field needs a third category, which I call the adaptive worst case. The idea is to require guarantees not about what a system will do but about what it can bring about without detection and correction: bounds not on behavior but on the system's capacity to place itself in unmonitored, high-stakes, irreversible situations. Such bounds are weaker than worst-case behavioral guarantees — they permit local misbehavior — but stronger than average-case ones, because they must hold against a system actively trying to violate them, and because they can in principle be engineered architecturally (through containment, tripwires, and limits on unilateral consequential action) rather than established statistically. Whether this category is coherent, and whether it earns the name "guarantee" at all, I leave partly open.

6. I conclude that the alignment debate's recurring stalemates — between those who point to excellent benchmark performance and those who point to unconstrained tail risk — are largely generated by the equivocation diagnosed in section 1. Each side measures one quantity and argues about the other.

---

I would be grateful for comments of the following kinds:

(a) Accuracy. Is the distinction I draw in section 1 the right way to carve up the average-case/worst-case terrain, or does it misdescribe how these notions function in either the machine learning literature or the philosophical literature on risk? In particular, is my claim in section 2 that finite average-case evaluation cannot bound worst-case behavior correct as stated, or does it need qualification (for instance, regarding what distributional assumptions could close the gap)?

(b) Soundness. Section 3 is the argumentative heart of the essay: the claim that the bridge-and-airplane analogy fails because nature is not adversarial while a misaligned model may be. Does this disanalogy do the work I need it to do? One worry I have is that adversarial selection of failures may be a matter of degree rather than kind — earthquakes are not adversaries, but rare correlated failures in complex systems can mimic adversariality. I would like to know whether the argument survives that observation, and if not, how it should be reformulated.

(c) Originality and interest. Is the "adaptive worst case" proposal of section 5 a genuine third option, or is it average-case reasoning about a cleverly redescribed quantity (namely, expected undetected harm rather than expected harm)? If the latter, does the essay still have a point worth making? I would rather hear this now than from a referee.

(d) Structure and presentation. Are there sections that belabor the obvious or skip steps a careful reader will want filled in? Section 4 strikes me as the weakest as currently drafted; suggestions for sharpening the dilemma — or for showing that it is a false one — are especially welcome.

Please be direct and critical; I am not attached to the draft's conclusions and would rather revise substantially than publish something that papers over the hard parts.
