The topic is higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, and here's the thing I keep coming back to: nearly everything in this debate — peer disagreement cases, the level-splitting arguments, the defenses of rational akrasia — seems to treat higher-order evidence as arriving in one lump, after your first-order reasoning is finished. You form a belief, then you learn a trusted peer disagrees, or that you took a pill that impairs reasoning in exactly the cases where you're confident, and then the question is how to revise. But the cases that got me interested in this aren't like that at all. Debugging code at 2am while half-believing I'm too tired to think straight, or reworking a proof while my advisor keeps sending "are you sure about step 4?" emails — in those, the higher-order evidence is arriving continuously and interleaved with the first-order reasoning, and the two interact as you go. My hunch (which I can't yet defend) is that timing changes what rationality requires: maybe steady low-grade self-doubt while you reason is epistemically fine in a way that a single post-hoc verdict of "you were impaired" is not — or maybe it's exactly backwards, and continuous self-monitoring is the really corrosive thing, the self-undermining that critics of akrasia warn about, just in slow motion.
