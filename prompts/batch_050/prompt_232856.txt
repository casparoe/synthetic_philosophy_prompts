The exam question was: "Explain the two standards for determining death recognized in most U.S. jurisdictions. Then identify one philosophical problem raised by organ donation after circulatory death (DCD), and explain how a defender of current DCD practice would respond."

Here's the student's answer:

"Most U.S. states follow the Uniform Determination of Death Act, which settled the matter by law everywhere in the country. Under it, death can be declared in two ways. The first is the traditional cardiopulmonary standard: irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions. The second is the neurological standard, i.e. brain death, which means the irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem. This is different from a persistent vegetative state, where the brainstem keeps working so the person breathes on their own, and only the higher brain is gone. Brain death is stricter than that -- it requires that there is no brain activity of any kind whatsoever, down to the cellular level, which is why doctors run apnea tests and confirmatory scans.

The philosophical problem with DCD is the dead donor rule: you can't kill someone by taking their organs, so the patient has to be dead first. In DCD, doctors wait about 2-5 minutes after the pulse stops and then declare death. The worry is whether the person is really dead that fast. Defenders of DCD respond that after a few minutes of pulselessness the cessation of circulation is irreversible -- the heart won't restart on its own, and since restarting it would require medical intervention, the loss of function counts as irreversible and the patient is legally and morally dead. So procurement can begin."
