A friend and I recently argued late into the night over whether China's four decades of growth, or Singapore's prosperity and order, confer genuine legitimacy on non-democratic rule, or merely purchase acquiescence. The argument ended inconclusively, and it happens that I moderate a campus political-theory colloquium whose next session features a scholar who defends the view my friend took: that sustained delivery of prosperity and public order can ground legitimacy in the absence of consent-based institutions, with input procedures being one instrumental option among several rather than a moral requirement. Draft eight to ten questions for the session, each followed by the answer the guest is most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to that answer. I want questions that press where the view is genuinely vulnerable rather than invitations to restate it, and they should include at least one each on the following: the counterfactual baseline problem (prosperity relative to what -- Taiwan and South Korea grew as they democratized, so the comparison class seems to do all the work); the verification trap (if the regime controls the statistics and the press, what would ever count as evidence that performance had failed, and does legitimacy dependent on unauditable performance collapse into public relations); the downturn problem (whether a legitimacy with no reserve beyond output obliges citizens to withdraw it the moment growth stalls, and whether that makes the theory one in which legitimacy can be lost without any injustice being committed); the charge that order is double-counted, since much of the disorder being suppressed is regime-generated; and the success dilemma (whether a prosperous, educated middle class demanding voice shows the view predicts its own obsolescence). Avoid anything answerable with a summary of the guest's published position.
