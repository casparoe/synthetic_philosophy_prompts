I teach a second-year philosophy of language seminar, 18 students, with one 75-minute session on Quine. Design a game that makes the indeterminacy of translation feel like a genuine discovery rather than a weird claim in a reading. Students should play field linguists investigating an unfamiliar language spoken by a facilitator-controlled informant. Give them a finite set of picture cards, gesture prompts, permitted questions and prediction tasks, but build several incompatible hidden translation schemes that agree on every available response while dividing the world differently over objects, temporal stages, parts and recurrence.

Please provide the full setup: materials, roles, the hidden schemes, round-by-round rules, a scoring system with no real money, and a way for teams to become increasingly confident before the reveal. No lecture beforehand and don’t name Quine or gavagai until the debrief. The debrief should connect particular moves in the game to ostension, analytical hypotheses and radical translation, explain what will predictably go wrong when it’s run, and admit which stronger part of Quine’s thesis a finite classroom game can’t reproduce. Write the answer in British English.
