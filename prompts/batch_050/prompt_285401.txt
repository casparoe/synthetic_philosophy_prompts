I'm taking a philosophy of mathematics seminar this term and we're about to spend several weeks on structuralism -- the idea that mathematics is about structures rather than about objects with intrinsic natures, and that, for instance, what matters about the natural numbers is only that they form a progression with a first element and a successor operation. I keep running into the standard embarrassment: if arithmetic is about the structure shared by all progressions, then it looks like we should be able to say that the number 2 simply is the second position in that structure -- but our set theory gives us many equally legitimate realizations of the structure (on one construction 2 is the set {0, 1} and hence a member of 4, on another it is {{0}} and 4 isn't even a set containing it), and the theory seems forced either to pick one arbitrarily or to declare ordinary identity questions across structures meaningless. Before I read the main structuralist proposals in detail, I want to know what standard they're actually being held to.

Please write out the desiderata for an adequate structuralist account of mathematical objects. Specifically:

1. List the requirements any such theory must meet to count as solving the identity problem rather than dodging it -- for example, handling the multiple-reduction problem, saying something principled about cross-structure identities (is the complex number 0 the same object as the real number 0, or the integer 0?), respecting how working mathematicians actually talk about structures, and dealing with cases where a structure has non-trivial automorphisms so that two positions are structurally indistinguishable (the square roots of -1 being the classic example). Give the case or consideration motivating each requirement.

2. For each requirement, say whether it is genuinely non-negotiable or whether it is a partisan demand of one camp (eliminative versus non-eliminative structuralists, or those who treat positions in structures as genuine objects versus those who don't).

3. Flag any pair or subset of the desiderata that you think cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain why -- I suspect that demanding both a fully general account of identity across structures and fidelity to mathematical practice, where such questions never arise, produces a real tension, and I'd like to know whether that's right.

4. Indicate which single requirement does the most work in ruling out candidate theories.

Then, briefly and secondarily, indicate how the main versions of structuralism you've alluded to fare against your own list. The criteria are what I care about; the scoring is just to check that the list isn't rigged.
