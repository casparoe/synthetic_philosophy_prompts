After teaching a first-year module on thought experiments, here's roughly where I've landed on them, and I'd like to know if the package hangs together:

(1) Gettier-style cases genuinely refute the justified-true-belief analysis of knowledge — intuitions about cases can sink a theory.
(2) Intuitions about properly outlandish scenarios — teleporters, Swampman, brains vatted from scratch — deserve little weight, because our concepts were built for the ordinary run of cases and there's no reason to think they keep working at the edges.
(3) Searle's Chinese Room proves nothing about whether computation could suffice for understanding; it's a rigged set-up that leans on how hard it is to imagine a whole program being run, and on our habit of looking for the understanding in the wrong-sized chunk of the system.
(4) Trolley cases uncover a real moral difference between intending harm and merely foreseeing it.
(5) The experimental findings that trolley judgements shift with question order and wording are serious, and philosophers need to reckon with them rather than brush them off.

Is this consistent? Go through it and tell me which commitments pull against each other and how badly, but rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging every bit of friction — I suspect some apparent conflicts dissolve if you look properly. For each genuine tension, say what the cheapest fix would be, and finish by telling me which single revision would tidy up the most of the set. I'd like an audit, not a lecture on thought experiments generally.
