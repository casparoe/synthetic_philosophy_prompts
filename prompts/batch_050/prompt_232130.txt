I serve as the staff economist for a binational river commission that must recommend an annual water allocation between two member states. When the states negotiate to impasse, the commission's recommendation carries enormous political weight, and I have drafted a proposal that the standing arbitration rule should be Kalai–Smorodinsky rather than Nash: each state receives the same proportion of its maximum feasible gain over the disagreement point, where the disagreement point is the status quo under continued impasse and each state's ideal point is operationalized as its allocation under full priority to its own historical claims. My argument leans on the fact that the feasible frontier shifts substantially year to year with hydrological conditions, so that Nash's willingness to cut one state's allocation when the feasible set expands is, in this setting, not an abstract axiomatic curiosity but a recurring political catastrophe, whereas individual monotonicity is exactly the property a standing rule needs to survive repeated application.

Next month I present the proposal to a university theory seminar that the commission uses as an outside review panel: eight or so microeconomic theorists trained in axiomatic bargaining who regard the Nash solution as the default and the monotonicity-for-IIA trade as a settled question they settled the other way, plus two mechanism designers and one applied economist who works on water markets and will ask why we do not simply price the water.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections I will actually get from this audience, in roughly the order they will come, phrased the way an axiomatic theorist would phrase them rather than the way a textbook summarizes them, and ordered by how much damage each would do to the proposal's adoption. For each objection, tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a reply I can deliver in two minutes, and where that reply is soft. I expect them to press the construction of the ideal point, since historical claims are contested and each side can inflate its entitlement narrative; to press the endogeneity of the disagreement point, since this year's allocation shapes next year's infrastructure investment; and to press what happens when a third downstream state accedes to the compact. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and identify the single reply that has to land for the proposal to survive the seminar.
