I'm part of a small reading group drawn mostly from longtime readers of rationalist blogs and forums, and for several sessions now we have been circling a question that keeps resurfacing in our community in practical guises: when, if ever, is there a duty to remember, and when is forgetting the better policy? It shows up as the right to be forgotten versus the permanent public archive, as requests to delete personal data from model training corpora, as whether a movement should keep its old internal disputes easily retrievable or let them sink, and as the personal question of whether keeping meticulous logs of one's past beliefs and predictions is epistemic hygiene or high-definition rumination. On the theoretical side, our two anchors have been Avishai Margalit's The Ethics of Memory, which grounds duties of memory in the thick relations of family, friendship, and community, and David Rieff's In Praise of Forgetting, which argues that collective memory mostly serves as a reservoir of grievance and that communities often flourish by letting the past lapse.

I want to turn this into a decision tree that sorts a reader by their actual commitments rather than by which label they find flattering. Please construct it to the following specification.

1. Build from the logical space first, names second. Choose the five to seven diagnostic questions on which positions in this debate genuinely come apart. Candidates I expect you to consider (adopt, refine, or replace, but justify any rejection): whether memory has non-instrumental moral weight at all; whether the relevant remembering subject is the individual, the community, or the state, and whether a collective can literally remember or only keep records; whether a duty to remember is grounded in particular relationships or owed to victims as such, whoever you are; whether a faithful archive can discharge the duty to remember or whether record-keeping and remembering are categorically different things; whether memory's track record of fueling vendettas and frozen conflicts is a decisive objection or merely a warning about misuse; and whether reconciliation requires forgetting, remembering first, or neither.

2. Phrase every branch node as a concrete question or case a reader can answer without knowing any of the labels. Mandatory cases to work in somewhere: a village massacre eighty years past with no living survivors, where annual commemoration keeps present-day tensions warm; a person's decade-old embarrassing writing and a deletion request; a movement that settled an internal schism years ago and must decide how prominently to keep the record; a truth commission that records everything and punishes almost nothing; and the removal of a specific person's data from a trained model.

3. The leaves must include at least: Margalit's relation-grounded ethics of memory; Rieff's case for collective forgetting as peace policy; the remembrance-as-duty-to-victims view associated with truth commissions; a Nietzschean active-forgetting position in which forgetting is a precondition of action and health; a functionalist-archivist view that delegates memory to records and treats remembering as an engineering problem; and a flatly consequentialist position on which one remembers or forgets purely as outcomes dictate. Give each leaf a name, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

4. Note any combination of answers that no one seems to occupy, and diagnose briefly whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate rather than papering over the seam.

Aim for eight to twelve leaves in total. End with a final line beginning Summary: containing a single sentence stating which branch question does the most sorting work and why.
