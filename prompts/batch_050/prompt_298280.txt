I help run a small philosophy reading group at my local library — none of us are academics, just enthusiastic amateurs — and we've spent the last month on promising. Everyone arrived assuming the standard story, roughly that a promise binds because it creates expectations in the promisee and breaking it lets them down. But then one member, a retired solicitor, started poking holes with edge cases, and now the group is divided and I want to settle it with something more systematic than vibes. Specifically, three problem cases kept coming up: (a) a promise made to someone who then dies before you can perform — say, promising your gran on her deathbed that you'll scatter her ashes at Loch Lomond — where it feels binding even though there's no living promisee left holding expectations; (b) a "promise" that nobody actually uttered, like when my scheduling app emails colleagues saying I'll be at a meeting and they rely on that, even though I never lifted a finger to create the expectation; and (c) a promise extracted under heavy social pressure, like being cornered at your cousin's wedding into promising to drive the aunties home, where everyone's expectations exist but were sort of manufactured by the crowd's glare.

What I'd like you to do: take five broad accounts of why promises bind — the expectation/assurance account (promising deliberately invites reliance, and dashing it is the wrong), the rights-transfer account (promising hands the promisee a right to your performance), the social-practice account (the obligation flows from a mutually beneficial convention that you'd be free-riding on), the normative-power or self-binding account (promising is an exercise of a power we simply have to bind ourselves), and the trust/harm account (the wrong lies in betraying invested trust). Rank these five into tiers by how well each one handles my three problem cases — the deliverable is a ranking driven by explanatory coverage of the weird cases, not by which account you find plausible overall. Give each tier a sentence or two saying which cases it copes with and which it chokes on, and flag which of my three cases you think is the hardest for the whole field. Assume no fancy footwork is allowed, like quietly redefining what counts as a promise to dodge a case.
