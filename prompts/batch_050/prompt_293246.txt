Two of my friends got into a real argument at trivia night and I need a referee because they both sound right to me.

Background: our team is three people, and we're deciding whether to recruit two more. The candidates are decent but clearly weaker than us — maybe they get a toss-up question right 55-60% of the time, and they also kind of idolize Tom, our best player.

Maya's case for recruiting: even if each person is only a bit better than a coin flip, a majority vote of five people lands on the right answer way more often than 60%. She said there's a famous math result about juries proving this (I looked it up later, the Condorcet jury theorem). So more members = better team, full stop.

Dev's reply: that theorem assumes everyone's guesses are independent. These two will just go with whatever Tom leans toward, so we're functionally still a three-person team — actually worse, because now Tom's off nights get rubber-stamped.

Maya's counter: fine, then everyone writes their answer on a card before we discuss. Independence restored, theorem applies again.

Dev's counter to that: writing it down doesn't really fix it, because we all prep from the same quiz books and the same old question lists, so we have the same blind spots — our errors are correlated even if we never talk. And he added that the theorem cuts both ways: if the new recruits are below 50% on the hard questions, adding them makes the team *less* accurate, not more.

Maya's last move: if someone is reliably below 50%, that's still useful information — you could just do the opposite of what they say. So there's no such thing as a teammate whose vote hurts you.

At that point it devolved into them just repeating themselves. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each step, and whether there's something they're both missing about how group voting actually works?
