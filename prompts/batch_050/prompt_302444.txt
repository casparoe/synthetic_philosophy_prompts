I am a novelist, and I have run into a dispute with a critic over the manuscript of my latest thriller, and I should like the matter examined with some care.

The plot runs as follows. My protagonist, a surgeon, is given an ultimatum: kill a patient on the operating table, or her family dies. Unbeknownst to her — here is the twist — a criminal organisation has implanted a device in her brain that monitors her deliberations and would, were she about to refuse, intervene directly and compel her hand to complete the incision. In the event, she weighs the matter, concludes that her family must come first, and kills the patient entirely of her own accord. The device never fires. She is later arrested, and the book's final act turns on whether she is guilty of anything at all.

My critic, who has some philosophy, tells me the device is simply a Frankfurt case dressed up as fiction, and he deploys the analogy to settle the novel's moral: since she would have been made to do it anyway, yet did it without being made to, she is plainly responsible — and therefore, he says, my closing chapters, in which her barrister argues that she could not have done otherwise and so cannot be blamed, rest on a philosophical confusion that any competent reader will see through.

I am not so sure the comparison can carry that weight. Would you audit it for me? Set out explicitly what in my story maps onto what in Frankfurt's construction — the intervener, the prior sign of what the agent is about to decide, the counterfactual that never becomes actual — and then tell me where the mapping strains. I can already see some candidates, though I cannot judge which matter: my protagonist never learns of the device, whereas Frankfurt's agent, I gather, need know nothing of the counterfactual intervener either; my device works by reading the early stages of her deliberation, which raises the question of whether she had already settled her mind before it could have acted; and my critic slides from "responsible in this case" to "responsibility never requires alternatives", which feels like a further step the analogy itself does not take. Which of these disanalogies damage his use of the comparison and which are idle? Does his conclusion about my barrister's speech survive once they are on the table? And if the Frankfurt case is not the right instrument for what my novel is doing, is there a better way of framing the moral structure of the plot?
