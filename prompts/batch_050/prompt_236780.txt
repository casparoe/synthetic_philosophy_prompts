Next month I'm giving a talk at a workshop on AI and philosophy of mind, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis: Davidson's radical interpretation is the right framework for deciding when we're licensed to attribute beliefs and desires to LLM-based agents, and moreover the principle of charity does real work here -- when a model gives inconsistent answers across paraphrases, we shouldn't jump to "it has no beliefs, just a bag of heuristics," because on Davidson's picture massive inconsistency is exactly what makes the *attribution* fail, not evidence about some inner mechanism. Roughly: there's no fact of the matter about what a system believes beyond what the best charitable interpretation assigns it, and LLM agents pass that test better than the skeptics admit.

The audience: maybe 60% ML interpretability and language-model people (they think in terms of probes, representations, SAE features, next-token prediction), a few formal semanticists, and at least one person I know is a hardcore eliminativist about propositional attitudes generally. No Davidson scholars, which is part of the problem -- half the room will have a vague memory of the principle of charity from undergrad and get it wrong.

Give me: the eight or so objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do to the talk, each phrased the way this audience would phrase it (so "doesn't this just collapse into behaviorism with extra steps" from the ML side, not from the philosophy side), each with a two-minute reply and a note on where my reply is weakest. I'm especially worried about the triangulation problem -- Davidson ties content to causal interaction between speaker, interpreter, and a shared world, and my models were trained on text, so a sharp audience member will ask what the LLM is triangulating *with*. Tell me how to answer that without conceding the whole thesis, and what I'm better off just conceding. Also flag which objections will come from the semanticists specifically, since they'll care about compositionality in a way the ML people won't. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.

One constraint: my co-panelist works on interpretability and will definitely bring up that we can now sometimes find internal features that correlate with "believing that p," and ask whether that makes radical interpretation obsolete -- why interpret from the outside when you can look inside? I need a reply that doesn't sound like I'm dodging empirical work.
