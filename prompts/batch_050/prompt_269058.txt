I'm a junior taking a seminar on paradoxes in decision theory, and this week we're covering the two-envelope paradox. Our professor mentioned it in passing and told us to read up on it ourselves before Thursday's discussion section, where we'll have to defend a position. I don't own the course textbook for this unit, so I picked up a popular book on mathematical paradoxes from the library, and I want to check whether its account is reliable before I lean on it in section.

Here's how the book presents it. Two envelopes are placed in front of you; one contains twice as much money as the other. You pick one, and before opening it you're offered the chance to switch. The book says the paradox is that switching seems provably better: if your envelope contains x dollars, the other contains either x/2 or 2x with equal probability, so its expected value is 1.25x, and you should switch -- but the same argument applies symmetrically after switching, so you should switch back, and so on forever. So far, so standard. But then the book says two further things. First, it says the flaw in the reasoning is straightforward: the expected-value calculation is correct as far as it goes, and the only problem is that the two possibilities are not in fact equally likely for any actual distribution of money, so once you pick a realistic prior the paradox simply vanishes and there is nothing left to explain. Second, it draws a moral: the two-envelope paradox is really just a dressed-up version of the St. Petersburg paradox, and both show that expected-utility maximization is not a coherent rule of rational choice in general -- which is why, the book says, working statisticians have largely abandoned expected value as a decision criterion.

Some of this sits badly with me, but I can't articulate why. The claim that expected-utility reasoning has been refuted seems way too strong given that the rest of my course takes it for granted, and I have a vague memory that the standard diagnosis of the switching argument is about treating x as fixed when it isn't, not just about unequal probabilities. Could you audit this summary for me? Tell me which parts are accurate, which parts are common simplifications that a decision theorist would accept as harmless shorthand, and which parts are genuine misrepresentations or conflations -- in particular whether the puzzle really collapses into St. Petersburg, and whether anything true in the summary supports the conclusion that expected-value reasoning is untrustworthy in general.
