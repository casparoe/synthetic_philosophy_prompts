Two months ago, while clearing out my late grandmother's house, I inherited four cardboard boxes that constitute, as far as I can tell, the entire documentary record of my family: property ledgers kept by my great-great-grandfather, who farmed and served briefly as a county clerk; bundles of letters; a family Bible; and a tin of photographs. Going through them, I have been struck less by what is in them than by who is not. The ledgers record payments to a midwife on six occasions across nine years, but the Bible lists only four children. A hired girl who, by a passing reference in a letter, lived with the family for nearly two decades appears in the ledgers exactly once, as a line item next to a sum of money. My great-great-grandmother signs nothing and is named almost nowhere, though the letters make clear she ran the farm's accounts during her husband's absences. And one photograph in the tin has a figure carefully scratched out of the emulsion.

This has left me unsure how a family history could responsibly be written from such a record, and I would like to see the underlying disagreement argued out properly. Write a dialogue between two historians — call them Elena and Marcus — who are both given access to these boxes and disagree about what can justifiably be inferred from them. Marcus holds that the archive, however patchy, is the only honest basis for history: a silence is an absence of evidence, not a fact, and to write the omitted people back into the record on the basis of a midwife's fee or a scratched photograph is to confuse speculation with knowledge. Elena holds that silences in an archive are rarely accidental — they are produced at each stage by decisions about what was worth recording, keeping, and passing on, and those decisions track power — so that the pattern of omissions is itself evidence about the world the documents came from, and a historian who refuses to reason from it is not neutral but simply ratifies the record-keepers' judgments.

Both characters should be equally rigorous and neither should be a caricature: Marcus should not be made a naïf who thinks archives fall from the sky, and Elena should not be made a sentimentalist who thinks any story told on behalf of the voiceless is thereby warranted. They should argue from the actual contents of the boxes — the ledger entries, the Bible, the letters, the damaged photograph — and each should respond to the other's specific points rather than restate a prepared position. In particular, have them press each other on the hard cases: what distinguishes a principled inference from a silence (the missing children, given the midwife's fees) from an irresponsible one; whether Elena's method can ever be falsified, or whether every counterexample can be absorbed as further evidence of erasure; and whether Marcus's restraint is itself a substantive claim about the past rather than an absence of one. The dialogue should not end in reconciliation; it should end with the disagreement located precisely — with both characters able to state, in a sentence or two each, exactly what would have to be true for the other to be right. Around 1,000 to 1,300 words.
