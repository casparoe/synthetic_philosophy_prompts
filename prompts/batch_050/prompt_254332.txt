I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade, and there's a conversational move I've watched happen many times in threads about reductionism, and it happened again last week. Someone was arguing that "the self" is at best a convenient fiction, and a commenter closed the exchange with the line: there are no chairs, only particles arranged chairwise. The clear implication was that this one observation dissolves the whole category of question -- no selves, only processes arranged selfwise; no public opinion, only people arranged opinion-having-wise; no firm, only contracts arranged firmwise. Everyone reacted as if a point had been scored, and I suspect something was scored, but I genuinely cannot tell what the sentence claims, let alone what it licenses. The same one-liner gets deployed in threads about consciousness, macroeconomics, and group agency, always as a closer, and I want to know what it would have to mean to deserve that role.

Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing? I expect the readings range from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical, and I'd like the full spectrum: a claim about what the fundamental ontology contains versus a claim about how our everyday language manages to be useful anyway; a claim that talk of composite objects is merely a shorthand we could in principle eliminate versus a claim that composition literally never occurs; a claim about the direction of explanation (particles explain chairs, never vice versa) versus a claim about reference (that "chair" fails to pick out anything at all); perhaps also something about vagueness -- that any principle for when some things become a further thing will draw an arbitrary line, so we should deny that any line exists. For each reading, I'd like a verdict: is it a platitude, a live metaphysical position, or actually false, and would it support the move my interlocutor made -- treating any question about whether some composite thing exists or matters as already answered?

Two things I'd especially like addressed. First, which readings actually take a stand on the hard question lurking underneath -- under what conditions, if any, do several things compose one further thing, and is it even coherent to answer "none whatsoever" -- versus which readings quietly change the subject from ontology to linguistics. Second, does the phrase "arranged chairwise" smuggle the chair back in through the service entrance? If the only way to specify the arrangement is by reference to the very thing whose existence is being denied, the paraphrase strategy looks circular, and I'd like to know whether that's a real problem for the strong readings or a confusion on my part. The end goal is a taxonomy I can paste into the next thread where this line gets used as a conversation-stopper.
