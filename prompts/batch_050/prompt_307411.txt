At a family dinner last week, my brother-in-law, who teaches ethics, pressed a well-known analogy on me, and I want to know how much it can actually bear. I had mentioned that my wife and I were renovating our kitchen rather than increasing our charitable giving. He replied with Peter Singer's drowning child case, as he framed it: if I saw a small child drowning in a shallow pond on my way to work, I would wade in and save her even if it ruined an expensive suit; since a comparable donation to an effective charity saves a child's life just as surely, my preference for new countertops over the donation is morally indistinguishable from standing dry-shod at the pond's edge because I didn't want to ruin my clothes. When others at the table objected — that charities might waste the money, that millions of other people could equally well give, that we earned the money and owe something to ourselves — he dismissed all of it as exactly the sort of excuse nobody would accept from a man who had just watched a child drown. He also conceded, in passing, that on his view there is a new drowning child every single day, forever, and that walking past even one pond makes you a monster.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on my kitchen. Please do the following: (1) lay out the mapping explicitly — which features of the pond case correspond to which features of donating to an effective charity — including the features his argument silently depends on but does not state, such as certainty of rescue, being the only available rescuer, a one-off cost, and a face-to-face encounter; (2) identify where the mapping breaks, and distinguish the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the conclusion he drew from the merely cosmetic ones he can safely concede; (3) assess how the analogy fares in the iterated version he conceded — a fresh pond every day, indefinitely — and whether that version strengthens his case or exposes what the original was hiding; and (4) if the analogy survives only in weakened form, state the strongest conclusion it can still carry, and whether some better analogy could carry the stronger conclusion he actually wants.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether his conclusion survives the audit — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
