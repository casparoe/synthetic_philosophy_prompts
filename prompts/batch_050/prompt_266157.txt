Write a villanelle about the socialist calculation debate — Mises and Hayek on one side, Lange on the other — whose two refrains are built around the word given. The first refrain should carry Lange's reply to Mises: a central planning board can issue prices to state factory managers as parameters, watch for shortages and gluts, and adjust by trial and error, raising the price of whatever runs short and lowering the price of whatever piles up, until the ledger clears just as a market's would. The second refrain should carry Hayek's rejoinder: in a market, prices are indeed given to each buyer and seller, who take them as data, but they are given by no one — each condenses dispersed, often inarticulate knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place, knowledge that resides in no single mind and comes into existence only through the very process of exchange that the board replaces. Across the five tercets, let the two refrains return word for word while the surrounding lines bend their sense, so that given gradually splits into given-as-issued and given-as-received; then, in the closing quatrain, where the refrains appear together for the first time, the poem should land the point that the dispute was never really about computational capacity — a bigger calculating machine would not settle it — but about what kind of thing a price is: an instruction that can be dictated, or a summary of knowledge that cannot be gathered because it has no existence apart from its use. Keep the form strict: nineteen lines, five tercets and a quatrain, two rhymes only, the refrains unaltered until they join at the end. The economics should be exact enough that a historian of the debate would find it right rather than merely name-checked, and the poem should also work as a poem — concrete imagery of ledgers, queues, and shop floors is welcome, but no sloganizing.
