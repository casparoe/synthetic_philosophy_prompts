My reading group has been working through the early parts of Being and Nothingness, and one member produced an argument that, if sound, would collapse the book's central notion. I've reconstructed it as fairly as I can. (1) Bad faith, on Sartre's own telling, is a lie that consciousness tells to itself. (2) A lie requires a deceiver who knows the truth and a deceived party who does not. (3) In bad faith, deceiver and deceived are one and the same consciousness. (4) For Sartre, consciousness is entirely translucent to itself: every conscious state carries a non-positional awareness of itself, so nothing can be hidden inside consciousness the way an object can be hidden inside a box. (5) Therefore nothing can be hidden from consciousness by consciousness, and bad faith, understood as a lie to oneself, is impossible. (6) But Sartre presents bad faith not as a rare pathology but as our ordinary, default way of existing — his famous cases (the waiter who plays at being a waiter, the woman on a first date who treats her companion's taking of her hand as if it weren't happening) are portraits of everyday life, not of clinical oddities. (7) So either the book's central case studies describe something that cannot exist, or (4) is false and consciousness is not translucent after all — in which case Sartre's rejection of the Freudian unconscious loses its stated basis, since that rejection rested on translucency.

My friend concludes that the chapter on bad faith refutes itself. I suspect the trouble lies somewhere in the move from (1)–(3) to (5) — perhaps the notion of a lie is doing double duty — but I can't make the suspicion precise, and I can't rule out that (2) is the weak point instead. If forced to choose, I would most like to keep (4) and (6), since those strike me as the load-bearing commitments of the whole phenomenology.

Tell me which premise or step should be rejected, explain why it looked plausible in the first place, and say what Sartre (or a sympathetic reader) must concede, clarify, or give up in order to reject it. If you think the argument is in fact sound, say so and explain what follows for the rest of the book. Lead with the step you would reject, and keep the whole answer under 300 words.
