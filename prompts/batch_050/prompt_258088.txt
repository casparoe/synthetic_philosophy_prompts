I'm a high school junior and the fiction editor of my school's literary magazine. For the spring issue I wrote a short story set in a fantasy kingdom with one unusual rule: breaking a promise is the noblest thing a person can do, and keeping one is disgraceful. I didn't play it for laughs or frame it as a dystopia. The narrator describes a knight who breaks a solemn oath to his closest friend as genuinely admirable — not excused by circumstances, but honorable in itself — and the world of the story agrees with him.

At our workshop meeting, the reaction puzzled me. Nobody objected to the same issue running a poem in which rivers flow uphill, or to another story in which a time traveler meets her younger self, contradictions and all. But half the staff said my story was simply incoherent — that I hadn't managed to describe a possible world at all. One editor argued that an author can make anything true inside a fiction, so the only problem is that I failed to make oath-breaking feel right, and better writing would fix it. Another said no amount of skill could have made it feel right, because some things can't be made true just by writing them down. A third pointed out that readers loved last year's story narrated by a villain who sincerely believes cruelty is kindness — so the magazine clearly can publish a morally inverted viewpoint — and claimed the difference must lie in how I wrote mine, though she couldn't articulate what the difference is.

Two things confuse me. First, who is right in the dispute between "an author can make anything true in a story" and "some things resist being made true in a story"? Second, what actually distinguishes my story from the villain-narrator one, and from the physics-breaking poem, given that all three describe impossible or inverted worlds?

I'd like your advice on whether to revise the story before the issue goes to print and, if so, how — or whether I should defend it to the staff as it stands, and on what grounds. Please explain the reasoning rather than just giving a verdict, since I want to understand the general principle well enough to apply it to future submissions, including ones where a character's distorted values are the entire point.

After your analysis, please summarize it in a table with four columns: "Claim or objection" (each position raised at the meeting, plus any important consideration nobody raised), "Underlying assumption" (what would have to be true about fiction, imagination, or morality for that claim to hold), "Verdict" (sound, partly sound, or unsound, with a one-sentence justification), and "Practical implication" (what the claim, properly assessed, means for whether and how I should revise).
