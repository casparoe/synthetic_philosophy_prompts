I host a long-form interview series in practical ethics, and my next guest is a moral philosopher who has spent her career defending a strong, distance-insensitive duty of rescue: the view, built on the drowning-child-at-the-pond thought experiment, that if you can prevent something very bad at a cost to yourself that is morally insignificant by comparison, you are obligated to do so — and that this principle applies to preventable deaths from global poverty just as it applies to the child in front of you. Draft eight to ten interview questions. I do not want a tour of the thought experiment itself; I want questions that press where the philosophy actually presses, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. In particular, I would like the set to include at least one question on each of the following pressure points:

1. Demandingness: the principle does not stop at one child. There is always another pond, and the cost of rescue, while modest each time, is unbounded in aggregate. How does she keep the view from collapsing into a requirement of near-total self-sacrifice — and when she answers with a threshold or a satiability clause, press her on whether that clause is principled or just the view conceding the objection under another name.

2. The disanalogy critique: the pond case is engineered so that you are the only possible rescuer, the danger is certain, the rescue is immediate, and the cost is known. Global poverty involves millions of equally placed potential rescuers, uncertain aid effectiveness, and costs that are diffuse. Ask whether the intuition the thought experiment pumps survives once these features are relaxed — and when she replies that none of these features is morally relevant, push on the one she has the hardest time dismissing.

3. The epistemic asymmetry: at the pond you see the child drowning; with distant aid you must trust intermediaries, and reasonable people disagree about effectiveness. If she says uncertainty only changes the expected cost-benefit calculation rather than the principle, ask whether the duty then becomes a duty to investigate — and what that investigation costs.

4. Rescue versus rectification: some critics argue that the affluent bear duties of justice, not beneficence, toward the global poor, because they are implicated in the institutions that produce the deprivation. Ask whether she resists this reframing or welcomes it — and if she welcomes it, ask whether her signature thought experiment has become idle, since the pond case is precisely a case where the rescuer bears no responsibility for the child's being in the water.

5. The phenomenology of distance: even granting the principle, the view must explain why proximity and visibility seem to matter so much to moral motivation if they matter not at all to moral obligation. Ask whether she takes the ubiquity of this response as evidence against the principle or as a predictable bias — and when she says bias, ask what independent handle we have for telling debunking explanations from vindicating ones, given that the same debunking story could be told about the pond intuition itself.

For each question, write the follow-up so that it assumes her most probable reply and takes the exchange one move deeper, in the way the example points above indicate. Keep the tone collegial but unsparing: she has answered the lazy versions of these objections a hundred times, and I want the interview to reach the parts of the view she would least like to have to defend on record.
