Next month I'm presenting a working paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on decision theory, and I need you to act as a sparring partner who knows this literature well. Think carefully about who will be in the room and how they actually argue. Before drafting anything, brainstorm a wide list of possible objections, then select and sharpen the ones that matter.

My paper defends a restricted claim about acausal trade. I do not argue that agents should adopt exotic decision theories wholesale. Instead, I argue for what I call the procedural symmetry thesis: when two agents are causally isolated from one another but each has strong evidence that the other is running a structurally similar deliberative procedure over the same abstract problem — similar priors, similar stakes, similar evidence about each other — then it is rational for each to incur a bounded real cost (what I call an acausal premium) to sustain the behavior that constitutes their side of a trade they can never verify. The intended payoff cases are things like a present-day research community accepting a small efficiency loss now on the grounds that causally disconnected counterparts (distant civilizations, future artificial systems reasoning about us, agents in other branches of a physical multiverse if you grant the setup) face a symmetric choice about whether to incur their own small cost in ways that benefit us. My argument runs through algorithm selection: agents choose which deliberative procedure to instantiate, not just which act to perform, and the rationality of selecting a procedure is assessed by its expected output across all agents who instantiate it.

The audience: roughly half mainstream game theorists and economists who treat causal structure as bedrock and think in terms of equilibria, incentives, and verifiability; a quarter formal epistemologists who will press on what evidence about a causally isolated agent's procedure could even be; and a quarter philosophers who know the Newcomb and superrationality debates cold and will suspect I've relabeled one-boxing to sneak it past referees.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me:

1. The objections, ordered by how likely each is to be raised and how damaging it is if I answer badly. Phrase each one the way that specific sub-audience would actually phrase it, in their working vocabulary — an economist's version of the correlation-is-not-causation worry sounds different from a formal epistemologist's.
2. For each objection: a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, plus a candid note on where that reply is weakest and what I should concede immediately rather than fight over.
3. What I should concede up front, unprompted, in the talk itself to take the sting out of the most predictable attacks — and what I must not concede because it guts the thesis.
4. The objection I am hoping nobody asks: the one that most directly threatens the procedural symmetry premise itself, and my best available response given that I haven't solved it.
5. A judgment on the single most dangerous moment of the question period — who raises what, and what the room needs to hear in my first sentence of response.
