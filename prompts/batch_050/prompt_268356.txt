I'm a teaching assistant for a second-year undergraduate course called Ethics and Future Generations, and I'm marking take-home short essays over the break. Students were asked to write about 350 words; the piece is marked out of 20 and counts for a small slice of the final grade. The relevant lectures covered Parfit's non-identity problem in Reasons and Persons and the distinction between person-affecting and impersonal moral principles. I have my own sense of this script, but I'd like an independent mark before I finalize anything, so please grade it fresh rather than trying to guess what I think.

The essay question was: "Explain how Parfit's non-identity problem arises for a present-day policy choice between depleting natural resources and conserving them for the distant future. Does the problem show that choosing depletion cannot wrong future people, and does it show that the choice cannot be wrong? In your answer, state the person-affecting intuition and assess whether it survives this case."

Here is the student's answer: "Parfit's non-identity problem arises for any policy that affects which people will later exist, and the choice between depleting and conserving resources is a clear case. Resource policy shapes everything about the future: where cities grow, what work people do, whom they meet, and when couples conceive. Since a child's identity depends on when it is conceived, after two centuries the population under depletion would consist almost entirely of people who would never have existed under conservation. The choice is therefore, in Parfit's terms, a different people choice. Combined with the person-affecting intuition — that an outcome can be bad only if it is bad for someone, and an act wrong only if it wrongs someone — this generates a puzzle. Consider someone born into the depleted world whose life, though bleak by our standards, is worth living. She cannot complain that our choice harmed her: had we conserved, she would not have existed, so she is not worse off than she would otherwise have been. No one in the depleted future can make this complaint, and since the people who will exist do not exist yet — they are merely possible, and merely possible people cannot be wronged — there is no one against whom depletion is a wrong. Parfit's conclusion is that the person-affecting intuition survives scrutiny and that the choice between depletion and conservation is morally indifferent: an act that wrongs no one cannot be wrong. He reinforces this by rejecting the total view — the principle that we should maximize the total sum of wellbeing — because it implies the Repugnant Conclusion, and his search for a satisfactory impersonal principle, Theory X, was admittedly left unfinished. Until such a principle is found, he holds, morality has nothing to say against depletion."
