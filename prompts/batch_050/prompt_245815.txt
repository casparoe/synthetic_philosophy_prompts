I spent forty years as a systematic biologist, much of that time on species delimitation, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy, lately the philosophy of categories. A question from outside my old field has taken hold of me, and I want it treated with the same discipline I would once have demanded of a species concept: in virtue of what is every instance of the letter 'a' an instance of that letter? Across fonts, hands, and centuries, 'a's share no single shape I can specify, yet literate readers sort them instantly and almost without error, and type designers go on producing 'a's that have never existed before and that nobody mistakes. Before I am offered any theory of letterform identity, I want the specification it would have to satisfy.

Give me the desiderata: the phenomena an adequate theory must explain, the cases it must get right, and the distinctions it may not collapse (letter versus glyph versus allograph versus character; being an 'a' versus being a good 'a'), with the observation motivating each requirement, a verdict on which requirements are genuine and which are merely the inherited prejudices of one school, and a statement of whether any subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable, since in that case the list is itself the result. The cases I would insist the list reckon with include: the two-storey and single-storey 'a' (same letter, different skeletons); 'A' and 'a' (same letter, almost no shared shape, which forces the question of what same letter even means); Latin 'a' beside Greek alpha and the Cyrillic lookalike (identical shapes, different letters, so identity cannot rest on shape alone); the long s of early printing, which modern readers accept as s without instruction; a sorites chain of minute deformations running from an indisputable 'a' to an indisputable 'o'; the same ambiguous mark read as one letter in one word and another letter in another word; the novel 'a' some designer will invent tomorrow; and the observation, easily tested on one's colleagues, that many literate adults cannot draw from memory the printed lower-case g they read every day.

Fix the purpose as follows: a theory adequate to explain how literate minds carve the category, not one adequate to build a machine reader. If it helps anchor the list, you may score the obvious candidates — template matching, prototype and family-resemblance accounts, structural descriptions, and role- or function-based theories — briefly against it, though the criteria are the deliverable, not the scoreboard. Tell me finally which single requirement eliminates the most candidates at a stroke, and whether the taxonomy of theories on offer here recapitulates the taxonomy of species concepts I know — typological, phenetic, biological, phylogenetic — or whether that mapping is merely the bias of an old taxonomist.
