I've been reading about Husserl's phenomenological method, and I keep running into what looks like the same unresolved dispute wearing different clothes. On one reading, the epoché -- the suspension of the natural attitude's automatic commitment to the world's existence -- is a purely methodological move: you bracket existence claims in order to describe how things are given to consciousness, nothing metaphysical follows, and the world is set aside only provisionally so that it can be properly recovered. On another reading, once Husserl makes the transcendental turn, the epoché is inseparable from a form of idealism: the world's being becomes, in some contested sense, constituted by consciousness rather than simply encountered by it. Interpreters have gone back and forth on this for decades, and as far as I can tell nobody has moved anyone.

What puzzles me, coming at this as a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, is that the dispute seems to survive every attempted clarification. My working hypothesis is that it stalls because the two camps lack a shared criterion for what a merely methodological suspension would even look like. There is no agreed test that could distinguish bracketing-existence-then-recovering-the-world from quietly redefining what "the world" means in the first place, so each side applies its own test and finds its own conclusion obvious. The analogy from my own work: if I change how a program is interpreted, I can check whether the change mattered by comparing outputs. Husserl's case seems to have no analogue of the output comparison.

Is that diagnosis right? Is this a substantive disagreement about what consciousness is, a verbal one about words like "constitution" and "idealism," or two groups answering different questions -- one about philosophical method, one about the status of reality -- under a single heading? Please diagnose the disagreement using what the two readings actually claim, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a decisive textual point, a reframing of the question, or an argument that the impasse is exactly what we should expect and why.
