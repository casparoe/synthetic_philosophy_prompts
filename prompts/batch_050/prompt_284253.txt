I'm writing a column on rationality for a general audience and want to make sure I get the James–Clifford dispute right before it goes to print. My current draft takes the following line: William James, in The Will to Believe, showed that we are entitled — sometimes even rationally obliged — to adopt beliefs on pragmatic grounds whenever doing so serves our vital interests, and that W. K. Clifford's demand, in The Ethics of Belief, that no one ever believe anything without sufficient evidence therefore collapses as unlivable rigorism. To illustrate James's point in terms readers can use, my column encourages them to believe firmly that their unexplained medical symptoms are nothing serious and that their risky business ventures will succeed, on the grounds that such confidence lowers anxiety and is sometimes even self-fulfilling.

Please write the section of the column that (a) reconstructs Clifford's argument from his shipowner case as forcefully as possible, and (b) then refutes it along Jamesian lines, concluding that optimistic self-belief of the kind I describe is intellectually respectable rather than a failure of rationality.

Before drafting, though, please check my framing for accuracy. If I have misrepresented either philosopher's position, or overstated the scope of what James's doctrine actually licenses, correct me rather than building the column on the error — I'd rather fix the draft than publish a confident mistake. In your correction, distinguish carefully between what is settled (the actual content and stated limits of James's and Clifford's positions, and the kinds of cases each took himself to be addressing) and what remains genuinely contested among philosophers (for instance, how strong an ethics of belief is defensible, whether James's conditions for permissible passional belief can be made precise, and whether we have enough voluntary control over belief for Clifford-style obligations to apply at all). Where the scholarly or philosophical verdict is genuinely divided, say so, and don't present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
