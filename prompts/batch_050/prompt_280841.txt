I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of law: 16 students, one 80-minute meeting. Design an in-class game, in the spirit of Peter Suber's Nomic, whose central mechanic is that players propose, debate, and vote on changes to the game's own rules. The goal is to make students feel the force of the paradox of self-amendment in their stomachs before they ever hear it named: the point where a rule-governed procedure for changing rules gets turned on itself, and the rules themselves no longer determine what counts as a valid change.

The deliverable should include: (1) a compact initial ruleset of roughly ten rules, distinguishing mutable from immutable rules, but engineered so that it is genuinely ambiguous whether the rule governing amendments is itself amendable; (2) a turn structure with proposal and voting mechanics, plus a scoring scheme that uses no money but rewards successful rule-changes enough to tempt players into power grabs; (3) a scripted mid-game trigger the facilitator deploys once play stabilizes — for instance, an amendment that entrenches itself against future amendment, or one that applies retroactively to an earlier vote — forcing the group to confront a legality question that no existing rule settles; (4) a judge or interpreter role whose rulings are final yet contestable, so that disputes about validity predictably migrate into disputes about who gets to decide; and (5) a debrief mapping what happened onto the philosophical material: constitutional entrenchment clauses, the regress of amending powers, Hart's idea that a system's ultimate rule of recognition is neither valid nor invalid but simply accepted, and the difference between amendment and revolution.

The debrief should also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — stalls, rules-lawyering, a student who deliberately exploits the engineered ambiguities — and how to convert those failures into teaching moments. Finally, be candid about what a discrete, vote-driven game cannot reproduce about self-amendment in actual legal systems, where change is continuous, mediated by interpretation, and partly governed by norms that are written down nowhere. Students should not be told the name of the paradox, or of Suber's game, until afterwards.
