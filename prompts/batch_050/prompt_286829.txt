I work in the policy shop of a state infrastructure agency, and I keep getting into the same fight. Every few months we run a cost-benefit analysis on something with a long tail -- a flood barrier with an 80-year design life, a bridge, once a land remediation thing where the benefits basically run forever -- and the whole conclusion flips depending on what discount rate we plug in. My boss wants to use one rate for everything because it's "defensible," the environmental review people want the lowest rate possible, and last month someone in a meeting said we shouldn't discount future lives at all because that's immoral, which killed the conversation for twenty minutes. I've read enough to know the debate turns on pure time preference -- whether mere earliness counts for anything -- versus discounting because future people will probably be richer and a dollar means less to them. But I can't get from that debate to a number I can defend in front of our board, and I definately can't do it from scratch every quarter.

So: turn whatever is actually solid in the philosophy and welfare economics of discounting into a procedure I can run every time we scope a CBA. I'm picturing a checklist or flowchart-in-words that takes me from the project in front of me to a discount rate (or a small set of rates) plus a paragraph of justification. Some things it needs to handle: the Ramsey-style split between pure time preference and growth-based discounting, and what the procedure should do differently depending on which of those is driving the number; how to think about whether we're discounting dollars or wellbeing and when converting between them actually matters; how to deal with projects where the benefits are mostly avoided deaths or preserved ecosystems rather than money; whether the rate should change across the project's time horizon (someone mentioned uncertainty about future rates dragging the long-run rate down -- say whether that's real and what a non-economist should do with it); and how to handle the "future generations are just us later" vs "they're literally different people" question without it becoming philosophy seminar. For each step, tell me what to do when I can't get the number the step asks for, and mark honestly which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) an 80-year flood barrier protecting a mid-size city, (2) a five-year public health program with benefits in the first decade, and (3) -- the one where I expect it to break -- a nuclear waste storage site with a 10,000-year horizon that our legal team has to sign off on. Include at least one worked case where the procedure gives an answer my boss or the enviro reviewers won't like, and be explicit that they'd have to argue with the philosophy, not the math. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot settle no matter how carefully I run it, so I know which fights in the meeting room are actually unavoidable. We're an agency of about 200 people, I'm usually the only one doing this analysis, and I get maybe two weeks per CBA, so keep that in mind.
