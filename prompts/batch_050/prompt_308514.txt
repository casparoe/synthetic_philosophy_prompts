I TA an intro philosophy of mind course and I'm covering the seminar next week on Dennett, since the prof is away at a conference. The students have read the chapter of Consciousness Explained where he attacks the Cartesian Theater and lays out the Multiple Drafts model, and predictably half of them are lost and the other half are confidently wrong. The bit they keep stumbling over is the passage where Dennett grants that the brain is, in a sense, headquarters, but then says there's no deeper inner sanctum, no place in the brain where arriving counts as the moment something becomes conscious. They read that as him saying consciousness is an illusion, or as him just dodging the question, and honestly I'm not always sure I could explain on the spot why it's neither.

So don't explain the theory to me -- plan how I should explain it, in one 80-minute seminar, to second-years who know a little bit about neurons and have watched The Matrix too many times. Tell me the order the pieces have to go in and why (my instinct is to start with the color phi phenomenon or some other case where the timing of experience seems to come apart from the timing of brain events, but maybe that's too late or too early, you tell me). Tell me the one example the whole session should be built around, what that example needs to have and needs to lack -- I'm wary of using a visual illusion for everything because then they think the point is just that perception is unreliable. And give me the misconception map: I already expect 'Dennett thinks consciousness doesn't exist,' 'the drafts are like rough drafts a final editor cleans up,' and 'so he's just a behaviorist,' but there are surely others, and I want to know exactly where in the sequence each one should be headed off, because correcting them at the end never works, they just nod and keep the wrong picture.

Two more things. First, that passage about the brain being headquarters with no inner sanctum: tell me how to teach it so it lands as a substantive claim about what consciousness would have to be, not as wordplay, and what question I can ask at the end that would show they actually got it rather than memorized the phrase. Second, I want you to commit. Don't give me three possible seminar structures and say it depends on my style -- pick the order, pick the carrying example, pick the misconceptions worth the limited time, and defend those choices, including what you'd deliberately leave out (qualia? the Stalinesque/Orwellian business?) and say you're leaving out. If you think the color phi example is the wrong vehicle, say so and pick better.
