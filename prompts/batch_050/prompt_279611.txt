I'm in a history of economic thought seminar (cross-listed with ancient philosophy, which is why half the readings are Aristotle) and I keep hitting the same wall. This week we read Nicomachean Ethics Book V, chapter 5, where Aristotle argues that exchange presupposes equality between the goods, equality presupposes that the goods are commensurable at all, and that money steps in as the measure that makes a builder's shoes and a farmer's wheat comparable — even though, he says, the things being exchanged are really so different that it's demand or need that actually holds the whole arrangement together, with money existing by convention rather than by nature. Our prof pointed out that Aristotle basically announces the problem (how can unlike things be made equal?) and then sort of... hands it off to convention without solving it. My paper idea is to treat that passage as setting a spec that every later theory of value and exchange is implicitly trying to meet, but before I write anything I want the spec itself worked out properly rather than my half-baked version.

So: what would an adequate theory of what makes goods commensurable in exchange actually have to deliver? Give me the desiderata as a list, with the case or consideration behind each one. I mean things like: it has to explain why a house and a pair of sandals can trade at some definite ratio without requiring some hidden identical substance inside both (or does it?); it has to say what role money plays -- whether it's genuinely the thing that makes goods commensurate or just a numéraire that papers over a comparison that was already possible; it has to handle Aristotle's own suggestion that need is what really binds exchangers together, and what happens to that idea once exchange happens between strangers or across long distances; it presumably has to handle cases where exchange looks exploitative or "unequal" without collapsing the distinction between a fair price and whatever price the market happened to produce; and it should be consistent with money being conventional while still explaining why not just anything can serve as money. Those are my guesses -- push back on any that are really just prejudices of one camp dressed up as requirements, and add ones I've missed.

Then, briefly -- like a paragraph total, this is not the main ask -- score the obvious contenders against the list: embodied-labour theories, subjective-utility/marginalist accounts, and the convention-or-credit view Aristotle seems to gesture at.
