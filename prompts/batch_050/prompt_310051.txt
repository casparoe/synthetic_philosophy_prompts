I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about aging and family obligation -- partly inspired by my own situation, honestly, since my mom just moved into assisted living and my brother and I have been arguing about who's "supposed" to visit how often. While reporting, I learned something that surprised me: a bunch of U.S. states still have "filial responsibility" statutes on the books that can make adult children legally liable for their indigent parents' nursing home or medical bills. Most are rarely enforced, but every so often a case makes the news where a care facility sues an estranged adult kid for a huge sum. Meanwhile the same society that keeps those laws around also broadly treats moving a parent into a care facility as a perfectly respectable choice, and nobody thinks you owe your parents grandkids, or a place in your will, or even a phone call, in any legally enforceable sense. And culturally we seem genuinely split -- plenty of people feel a deep duty to care for aging parents, others feel that duty evaporated after a bad childhood.

Here's my question, which I want to frame philosophically rather than just sociologically. What view of what adult children owe their parents does this patchwork of practices actually presuppose? Does a filial responsibility statute presuppose that the parent-child relationship generates obligations the way a contract does -- you got raised and fed, so now you owe repayment -- and if so, does that sit uncomfortably with the fact that nobody chose to be born or signed anything? Or does it instead presuppose something more like a needs-based or role-based duty, the way we think spouses owe each other support regardless of the history? I'm especially curious how the whole setup looks under the rival idea -- I associate it with Jane English -- that grown children owe their parents roughly what friends owe each other: ongoing care proportionate to the current relationship, not a debt for past sacrifices. Under that model, is the statute just incoherent, or could you reconstruct a version of it that survives? And where is the practice simply underdetermined -- compatible with several theories, so you can't read an implicit philosophy off it at all?

When you answer, please be careful to separate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this stuff -- e.g., don't present the friendship model, or a debt-of-gratitude model, as the obvious consensus if philosophers are in fact divided. Where the practice could be licensed by multiple theories, say so rather than picking a winner. American English, please.
