For most of my working life I was an experimental physicist, and since retiring I have been making my way through the philosophy of language with a small reading group of fellow amateurs. One result has been nagging me for weeks: David Lewis's triviality results concerning indicative conditionals. As I understand it, Ernest Adams proposed that the probability of an indicative conditional equals the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent (what is usually called Adams's Thesis), and Lewis proved that no ordinary proposition can satisfy this equation across a suitable range of probability functions without rendering the probability function trivial, with related results by others generalizing the difficulty. My own instinct, perhaps betraying my physics training, is that the two sides of Adams's equation might involve probabilities relativized to different bodies of information, so that the equation equivocates and the proof never gets going. But I genuinely cannot tell whether this is an escape route philosophers have explored, a confused idea, or something in between, and my reading group was no help.

I would like your assistance in mapping the space of responses to the triviality results. Please act as a professional philosopher of language and logic, think carefully, and construct a decision tree that takes an intelligent amateur from the starting commitment "indicative conditionals seem to express propositions, and Adams's Thesis seems intuitively right" to a named position. Each branch node should be a diagnostic question phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to — questions about whether conditionals have truth values, whether the "if"-clause contributes a proposition or modifies something else in the sentence, what one is willing to say about assertability, and so on. The leaves must include, at minimum: the non-propositionalist family associated with Adams, Gibbard, and Edgington; the restrictor view associated with Kratzer; truth-conditional accounts that keep the equation by restricting which propositions exist, in the manner of Bradley; three-valued semantic approaches; contextualist or dynamic accounts in Stalnaker's tradition; and the blunt option of retaining truth conditions and simply denying the equation, accepting the cost to intuitions about assertability. Please also locate my amateur idea about probabilities relativized to different bodies of information somewhere in the tree, or explain plainly why no coherent branch houses it.

A few constraints on format and method. Aim for five to seven branch nodes and six to eight leaves, sized for a reading group of non-specialists. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first state the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions within that space, and briefly diagnose any unoccupied combination — is nobody there because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? If two of the leaves cannot genuinely be separated by your questions, flag that honestly rather than papering over it. Before presenting the tree, briefly brainstorm the possible escape routes from triviality in a few sentences; then give the tree itself as an indented outline or ASCII diagram, followed by a short gloss on each leaf stating what the position preserves and what it sacrifices.
