So in my political theory reading group last week we got onto populism after someone brought up a recent referendum back home (won't say which country, doesn't matter), and two members, Marcus and Dana, had this exchange that kind of spiralled. I've been chewing on it since and genuinely can't decide who came out on top, so I want an outside referee.

Marcus opened by saying that populist talk about "the will of the people" isn't just manipulative but literally incoherent: whenever there are three or more options on the table, the majority preference can flip depending on the aggregation rule (plurality, runoff, pairwise votes), and social choice theory shows you can get cycling where there's no stable majority position at all. So there often IS no will of the people to express or thwart, and whoever says "the people have spoken" is just retroactively blessing whatever the procedure happened to spit out. Dana pushed back that this is a technocratic dodge -- the cycling stuff is a seminar-room curiosity, real political conflict often collapses to one dominant dimension, and on a binary referendum question voters can have perfectly clear individual preferences. When a government ignores a clear majority on a binary vote, or keeps rerunning the vote until it gets the answer it wants, something real is being trampled, and hiding behind Arrow's theorem to deny that is bad faith. Marcus replied that even binary votes are artifacts: turnout, question wording, timing, and momentum determine the outcome, the "same" electorate would vote differently three months later, and anyway 52% isn't a person -- calling a bare majority "the people" is a rhetorical trick that erases everyone on the losing side. Dana's last move was to say Marcus is attacking a strawman of the phrase: maybe "the will of the people" was never meant as a metaphysical claim about a collective mind, but as shorthand for a procedural principle -- legitimate authority rests on majority decision under fair rules, and elites shouldn't override that for their own convenience. That's defensible even if Rousseau-ish general will is fiction. Marcus closed by saying fine, but then the slogan is doing a bait-and-switch: the defensible thin procedural reading doesn't license what populists actually do with it, which is claim a monopoly on representing the people and treat their opponents as traitors to a real collective will -- so the slogan survives only by smuggling the thick reading in under cover of the thin one.
