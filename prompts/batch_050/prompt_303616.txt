Got into a proper argument about this with a couple of mates after we watched a documentary about the war in Ukraine, and I still can't settle it. As far as I understand, the laws of armed conflict work roughly like this: a soldier fighting in a war his own country had no right to start -- even a flat-out war of aggression -- commits no crime just by fighting. He can kill enemy soldiers, soldiers who are literally defending their own towns, and if captured he gets POW status and cannot be prosecuted for any of the killing, so long as he followed the rules of fighting: wore a uniform, didn't target civilians, no torture, and so on. Meanwhile the people who decided to start the war can be prosecuted for the crime of aggression -- but only them, the leadership, never the rank and file. The bit that bakes my noodle is that the very same legal order clearly does think the war itself was a crime, because that's what the aggression charge against the leaders amounts to. So: the war is criminal, but waging it isn't. Compare ordinary life: if a hitman's victim fights back and the hitman kills him, nobody shrugs and says well, he followed the rules of fair shooting, he's in the clear.

My one friend reckons this whole arrangement only makes sense if you presuppose that soldiers on both sides are somehow morally equal -- equally permitted to kill each other, because neither chose the war and all the blame lives with the politicians who started it. My other friend says the practice presupposes nothing that deep; it's just a workable convention, because you can't expect a nineteen-year-old conscript to run an international law seminar in his head before deciding whether to fight, and both sides will only obey the rules if the rules treat them symmetrically. Can both of those be true at once, or does the practice genuinely stand or fall with the first view? What I'd like is for you to reconstruct the implicit theory of responsibility behind all this, and then be honest about which parts of the practice strictly require that theory and which parts would survive under several rival theories -- I suspect some of it is philosophically underdetermined and my friends are both overclaiming. Also, does the fact that we happily prosecute an ordinary soldier for torture even when he was ordered to do it show the system doesn't really believe soldiers can't judge right from wrong, or is that consistent with the setup?

And then the follow-up: suppose the philosophers who deny the whole symmetry thing are right -- the ones who argue that a soldier defending his country kills permissibly while a soldier waging the aggressive war does something seriously wrong every time he pulls the trigger, even against enemy combatants, full stop. If that view is correct, what would actually have to change in the laws of war? Would we be committed to prosecuting ordinary soldiers merely for participating, which seems monstrous and probably unenforceable, or could we keep the current laws purely as a second-best convention while admitting they license something morally indefensible? And if we did keep them for pragmatic reasons, would the practice then presuppose anything about responsibility at all, or would it just be a truce we've decided not to examine too closely?
