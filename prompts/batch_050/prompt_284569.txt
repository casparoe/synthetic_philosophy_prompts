I'm designing a graduate seminar on epistemic iteration — the question of whether knowing that p requires (or tends to bring along) knowing that one knows that p — and I've realized that my students keep evaluating positions against criteria they've never made explicit. One week they dismiss a view for "making knowledge too easy to iterate," the next they dismiss another for "making our knowledge of our own minds too fragile," and it's clear these demands might not be jointly satisfiable. Before we read another week of material, I want the specification written down. Please write the desiderata for an adequate theory of higher-order knowledge: what any account of whether, when, and why knowledge iterates must deliver. For each requirement, give the case or argument that motivates it — for instance, the theory must explain what goes wrong, step by step, in the sorites-like reasoning that takes someone from feeling cold at dawn to feeling warm at noon while apparently retaining knowledge of how they feel at each moment; it must say whether and why phenomenal states (being in pain, things seeming a certain way to one) are or are not "luminous," i.e., such that being in them guarantees being in a position to know one is in them; and it must reckon with the idea that knowledge requires a margin for error — that one's belief must not be too easily false in nearby situations — and explain how this structural feature of knowledge, if accepted, generates failures of iteration without any irrationality or lack of reflection on the subject's part.

A few further constraints on the list. Include desiderata covering: consistency with a broadly safety- or reliability-friendly picture of knowledge (the theory shouldn't secure iteration by defining knowledge so thinly that lucky true beliefs qualify); a verdict on how many iterations a theory should license (is knowing that one knows that one knows as secure as knowing?); an explanation of why higher-order knowledge feels introspectively obvious even where it allegedly fails (the theory owes an error theory, not just a denial); and compatibility with the plausible thought that in some domains — mathematics, perhaps, or deliberately formed judgments — iteration really does hold, so any argument that kills KK everywhere had better not kill it there. For each desideratum, mark whether it is non-negotiable (any theory violating it is thereby refuted), negotiable (a genuine cost, but one a theory might pay for compensating gains), or camp-specific (a demand only internalists, or only externalists, or only luminosity-defenders would make — say whose prejudice it is). The part I care most about: identify any subset of these desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied given the anti-luminosity reasoning, and say exactly which premise forces the triage — is it the margin-for-error principle, the assumed luminosity of the intermediate states, or the way knowledge is being chained across the series? If the list itself turns out to be inconsistent, that's a result I want displayed, not papered over.

Optionally, close with a brief scorecard — a few sentences each — on how three positions fare against the list: a Williamson-style view that accepts the margin-for-error argument and denies both luminosity and KK; an externalist view on which reliable belief-formation automatically yields iterated knowledge; and an internalist view that tries to preserve privileged self-knowledge by restricting the anti-luminosity argument's scope. Keep the scoring subordinate to the requirements; I mainly want to see which single desideratum excludes the most positions, since that will structure the rest of the seminar.
