I'm a retired biochemist who has fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole in my old age, mostly via podcasts and a reading group at the library. Last week someone brought up psychopathy -- the Hare checklist stuff -- and during the discussion our host kept repeating a line I'd heard a hundred times before on crime documentaries: psychopaths 'know right from wrong, they just don't care.' Everyone nodded like that settled something, and then someone else said that if they really *knew* it was wrong they'd be motivated to act differently, so they can't know, so maybe they're not even blameworthy. Now the group is split down the middle and I got volunteered to prepare the topic for next month.

Here's my problem: I think that slogan is doing at least four different jobs at once and nobody in the room could tell them apart, myself included. When I say of someone that he 'knows it's wrong,' I might mean (a) he can state the rule, the way he could state a traffic law; (b) he can classify new cases correctly, i.e. he'd pass the same moral quiz you or I would; (c) he has the appropriate emotional grip on it -- the wrongness feels like something to him; or (d) the knowledge has its normal motivational force, it actually weighs in his deliberations. Presumably there are other readings I'm not seeing. And 'just don't care' is doing sneaky work too, because if caring is constitutive of the knowing (whatever the moral sentimentalists say) then the slogan contradicts itself, and if it isn't, the slogan is almost trivial.

What I'd like: lay out the distinct precise theses hiding in 'they know right from wrong, they just don't care,' say for each one whether it seems to be true of psychopaths as best we understand them, and flag which reading the blame debate actually needs -- i.e., which sense of 'knowing wrong' someone has to have for holding them responsible to make sense. Also say whether the slogan, on its most defensible reading, supports the conclusion that psychopaths are fully culpable, or whether my reading group member's comeback (no real knowledge without motivation, so reduced culpability) has more going for it than my first instinct said. A menu I can bring to the group, not an essay picking sides upfront.
