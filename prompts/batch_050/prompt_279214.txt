I teach a graduate seminar on medieval Jewish philosophy, and we have just finished the chapters of the Guide for the Perplexed in which Maimonides explains the sacrificial commandments. In discussion, one of my brighter students waved away a careful question about the details of the Temple service by announcing that, for Maimonides, sacrifice was merely a concession — God permitted the Israelites to keep slaughtering animals on altars because they were habituated to pagan worship and could not have been weaned off it in one step. The slogan did real work in the conversation: it was being used to suggest that the sacrificial laws carry no intrinsic significance and that attending to their minutiae is antiquarian rather than philosophical. I let it pass at the time, but on reflection I am unsure the slogan bears the weight placed on it, and I would like to bring a cleaner treatment back to the seminar.

What I want is a disambiguation of this concession claim, treated as a piece of language rather than as settled doctrine. Please lay out the distinct precise theses it might be expressing — for instance, a purely historical-psychological claim about why the commandments were given to that generation, a stronger axiological claim that sacrifice has no value in itself at all, a claim about divine pedagogy and gradualism in legislation (what commentators call the second-intention doctrine), and the more radical extension that other commandments might likewise be accommodations to the limitations of their original recipients. For each reading, I would like a verdict: is it a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or something the Guide's own text would strain against — I am thinking in particular of the fact that Maimonides elsewhere offers reasons for the particulars of the sacrificial rites, which sits awkwardly with treating them as meaningless residue, and of his apparent expectation that the cult will be restored in messianic times. Finally, and most importantly for my purposes: which of these readings, if any, would actually license my student's dismissive move, and does the most defensible reading license it?

Please keep the whole response under 600 words, presented as a numbered menu of readings with verdicts, followed by no more than three sentences addressing the student's use of the slogan.
