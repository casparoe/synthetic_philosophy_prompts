For as long as I've read rationalist blogs and forums, Pascal's mugging has been the standard stress test for expected-utility reasoning: a stranger offers you an astronomically large payoff — vastly many lives saved or created, say — in exchange for the cash in your pocket, and although every intuitive judgment says to walk away, multiplying the tiny probability of the promise being kept by the enormous payoff seems to say you should pay. What frustrates me about the recurring threads is that they collapse into people naming a preferred decision theory rather than stating which feature of the scenario is doing the work for them. So I'm designing a "which solution to the mugging do you actually hold?" sorting quiz, to post on my blog and run at a local meetup.

Please write the quiz as a decision tree of eight to twelve diagnostic questions ending in named positions, with the following requirements:

1. Every question must be a concrete variant of the scenario on which the positions genuinely come apart — for instance, whether the enormous payoff is guaranteed by a provably fair mechanism rather than a stranger's bare promise, whether the stakes are finite-but-astronomical or literally infinite, and whether the promised good is one a utility function plausibly values linearly (lives, years of happy life) or one where saturation is credible. Do not ask directly whether the reader thinks utility is bounded or whether tiny probabilities should matter; ask what they would do in the case, and phrase each question so someone can answer it without knowing which position it sorts them into.

2. The leaves must include at least: (a) biting the bullet — ordinary expected utility maximization with an unbounded utility function, paying the mugger in principle; (b) bounded utility functions; (c) a probability-discount or leverage-penalty view, on which the probability that any agent could or would deliver a payoff shrinks at least as fast as the promised payoff grows; (d) a minimum-credence view, on which hypotheses below some probability threshold drop out of deliberation altogether; (e) a rejection of expected utility maximization itself in favor of a risk-sensitive alternative. If you think a further position deserves its own leaf — for instance, a dissolution on which the whole puzzle concerns trust in the mugger rather than decision theory — include it.

3. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names: the first question should be the deepest fork (which feature of the scenario the reader takes to generate the problem — the tiny probability, the enormous stakes, or the untrustworthy source), with later questions subdividing each branch.

4. For each leaf, add one sentence stating the position's most awkward commitment — the case where its verdict will make most readers flinch, such as the bounded-utility reader declining a verified lottery at staggering odds.

5. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate with the questions given, and either supply the additional case that would separate them or explain why the two views may be extensionally equivalent across all cases.

6. Note any region of the logical space — any combination of answers your questions permit — that no defended position occupies, and briefly diagnose whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

7. End with a compact scoring key giving, for each leaf, the pattern of answers that lands a reader there, so the quiz works on paper.

The audience is rationalist-adjacent readers who already know the scenario but disagree about why it is a problem; assume no formal background beyond expected-value arithmetic. Wherever a question turns on the difference between the mugger's say-so and a verified mechanism, make the verification procedure concrete enough that readers cannot equivocate about it.
