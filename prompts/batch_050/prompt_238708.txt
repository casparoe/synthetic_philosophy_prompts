I cover science and technology for a general-interest magazine, and for the past two years I've been reporting on the debate over whether frontier AI development should be slowed or paused. I've interviewed people on both sides, and what strikes me is that the debate seems structurally stuck in a way that other technology controversies I've covered — nuclear power, GMOs, gain-of-function research — eventually stopped being. The pause advocates I talk to grant that the technical details are uncertain but say that when the downside includes catastrophic or irreversible outcomes, the burden of proof falls on those racing ahead, not on those asking for restraint. The accelerationists and capabilities researchers I talk to grant that the risks are real but say a pause is either unenforceable, self-defeating (it cedes ground to less cautious actors), or premised on timelines they consider speculative. Both sides are intelligent, both cite the same public events — the same model releases, the same capability jumps, the same governance proposals — and neither has budged. The conversation keeps circling back to the same exchange: "you can't prove the danger," met by "you can't prove the safety."

My working hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than accept, is that this is not primarily an empirical disagreement at all. I suspect the two sides are actually disagreeing about something more like decision theory under deep uncertainty — specifically, about what kind of evidence would even be available before the relevant capabilities exist, and therefore about whether "wait for better evidence" is a coherent policy or a way of guaranteeing you only learn the answer when it's too late. If that's right, then all the argument about benchmark results and scaling trends is a proxy war, and resolving the empirical questions wouldn't resolve the debate. But I might be wrong: maybe the impasse really does bottom out in a factual disagreement about how fast capabilities generalize, or about whether coordination among labs and states is feasible, and the philosophical-sounding parts are window dressing.

So here's what I'm asking: diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it verbal — are the sides using words like "pause" and "risk" in different senses while talking past each other? Is it substantive but empirical, in which case which specific factual question is doing the load-bearing work? Is it a disagreement about standards of evidence and the asymmetry of errors, which would make it partly normative? Or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading — one asking "what does prudence require of us now?" and the other asking "what governance arrangement would actually change outcomes?" Use what each side characteristically says (as I've sketched it, and as you know it from the public discourse) as your evidence. And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a formal result, a particular kind of empirical evidence, a sharper distinction, a reframed question — or, if you think no resolution should be expected, explain what feature of the disagreement guarantees that, so I know whether to keep covering this as a live controversy or as a permanent fault line.
