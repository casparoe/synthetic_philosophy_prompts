I'm a high school junior and I compete in debate. At a tournament last month we had a round on whether schools may restrict student speech, and my opponent closed with a line that got approving nods from the judges: your right to swing your fist ends where my nose begins. I lost the round and I've been annoyed about it ever since, because the sentence feels like it settles everything while actually settling nothing.

Here's my problem. In the round, my opponent used the slogan to argue that speech can be restricted whenever it hurts someone the way a fist does, so a student newspaper article that made a classmate feel attacked was, on his reading, a fist landing on a nose. But the week before, our civics teacher used the same slogan to explain why the government can't ban unpopular opinions -- the idea being that speech isn't a fist, so the slogan never gets triggered. Two people used the identical sentence to reach opposite conclusions, and both seemed to think the sentence did the work by itself.

So I want the slogan taken apart. What are the distinct precise claims it could be making? Presumably one reading is purely about physical violence, another extends to anything that sets back someone's interests, another counts psychological pain and offense as a "nose," and another is really about risk -- my swing ends not where your nose is but somewhere before it, since I don't get to swing freely as long as I miss. There are probably readings I'm not seeing. For each one, tell me: is it a platitude nobody would deny, a substantive and defensible principle, or something that's actually false once stated precisely? And for each, say whether the harms caused by speech -- hurt feelings, damaged reputations, stirred-up hatred, misinformation -- count as "noses" on that reading, because that's where all the disagreement in my round was hiding.

Finally, I want verdicts, not just a menu. Which reading is the one people usually have in mind when they reach for this slogan, and which reading -- if any -- would actually justify what my opponent did with it? My suspicion is that the slogan only works for him on a reading of "harm" that would also censor basically everything, and only works for my teacher on a reading that makes the slogan trivial. If my suspicion is wrong, I'd like to know exactly where it goes wrong, since I have to debate this topic again next semester.
