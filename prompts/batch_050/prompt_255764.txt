In the seminar I'm TAing this spring, the knowledge-how literature keeps generating the same standoff, and I've come down on the intellectualist side: knowing how to do something is a species of propositional knowledge -- knowing how to F is, roughly, knowing, of some way w, that w is a way for one to F, where that proposition is entertained under what gets called a practical mode of presentation. What persuades me is less any single argument than the convergence: the anti-intellectualist's central regress argument seems to prove too much (if acting on knowledge requires a prior intelligent act of contemplation, propositional knowledge generally, not just know-how, would be threatened, so something is wrong with the regress premise rather than with intellectualism); there are apparent cases of know-how surviving the loss of ability (the retired concert pianist with paralyzed hands who can still correct a student's fingering) and cases of ability without know-how (the lucky novice who lands the trick on a first fluke); and the linguistic unity of "knows" across know-wh and know-how constructions looks like evidence the anti-intellectualist has to explain away rather than explain. I'll grant that the practical-mode-of-presentation clause is the part of the view I understand least well, and that it sometimes gets deployed as a catch-all.

So: play it straight. What would have to be true for intellectualism about know-how to be wrong? Map the exposure. Which kinds of cases would be genuinely decisive rather than merely awkward -- for instance, would well-confirmed cases of robust skilled performance in creatures or systems that plausibly lack the relevant propositional attitudes (infants, non-linguistic animals, subpersonal motor routines) refute the view, or can the intellectualist absorb them by weakening what counts as entertaining a proposition under a practical mode? Would dissociations of the sort neuropsychology reports -- preserved procedural competence alongside lost declarative access, or the reverse -- cut against the view, or do they only embarrass a crude version of it that no careful defender holds? And on the other flank: what would show that the ability-based counterexamples I rely on are actually equivocations on "ability," or that the linguistic evidence generalizes badly across languages where know-how constructions behave differently?

Please distinguish three tiers: defeaters the view's own defenders would concede as fatal (a consequence they're committed to that turns out untenable, a collapse back into a disguised dispositionalism), findings that would be uncomfortable but survivable through qualification, and objections that only look threatening because they target a straw version. Then give me your honest verdict on whether the practical-mode-of-presentation clause makes the position dangerously well insulated -- a clause flexible enough to accommodate any recalcitrant case starts to look like it risks nothing, and I want to know whether intellectualism as actually defended is falsifiable in any interesting sense, and whether that matters.
