I serve on the data governance board of a mid-sized municipal data trust — the sort of body that holds anonymized mobility and utility records and licenses them out for urban planning research. Years of reading the consequentialism-versus-deontology debates on the rationalist blogs have left me with the sinking feeling that our current case is a live instance of the paradox of deontology, and I want a memo that doesn't pretend otherwise. The facts: an internal audit revealed that one of our analysts, suspecting a colleague of running unauthorized re-identification queries against individual residents' location histories, bypassed our access controls and read that colleague's query logs without authorization — itself a serious breach of the very privacy constraints the trust exists to enforce. Her suspicion was correct: the colleague had re-identified and been quietly selling location histories covering several thousand residents. The colleague has been fired and reported to the police. What remains is our decision, and it has two parts.

First, the disciplinary question: what, if anything, should happen to the analyst whose unauthorized access exposed the whole affair? Second, the policy question: our security team, pointing out that the audit only caught this by luck, proposes a narrow internal program under which anomaly-detection flags on possible re-identification would trigger a warrant-style review of employee query activity — that is, a standing, institutionally sanctioned version of exactly the privacy violation our charter prohibits staff from committing. Our privacy officer argues that authorizing the program means the constraint was never really a constraint, only a heuristic we follow until the numbers look bad; the security lead responds, in effect, with the classic "utilitarianism of rights" move — if unauthorized access to personal data is bad enough to forbid, surely we should prefer arrangements with fewer such violations, even when achieving them requires one of our own. I find I can't dismiss either of them. Refusing the program protects the constraint at the cost of knowingly permitting more violations of the very right it protects; authorizing it seems to concede that the constraint was always up for consequentialist renegotiation.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the board before our next meeting. It should give a clear recommendation on both the policy question (authorize, reject, or authorize with specified conditions) and the disciplinary question, and the two recommendations should cohere — the board will notice immediately if we condemn in the analyst what we license in the program. The memo should steelman the losing side rather than caricaturing it, and it should address directly whether a principled distinction can be drawn here — between rule-level and act-level violation, between one-off and institutionalized breaches, or via some threshold version of the constraint — without that distinction collapsing into "constraints bind until inconvenient." Above all, I want the memo to state plainly what our choice commits us to about why privacy constraints bind us at all, since whatever we decide will be cited as precedent the next time someone proposes violating our own rules for the greater good.
