So I've been rereading Tolstoy's What Is Art? (the Aylmer Maude translation) and the bit that keeps bugging me isn't the famous infectiousness stuff, it's this throwaway argument right in the preface, from 1897:

"But I had a talk lately with a man who, till his health failed him, had worked as a builder in London. He told me that when he was younger he had been very fond of theatre-going, but, later, when he thought things over and considered that in almost every number of his weekly paper he read of cases of people whose death was hastened by lack of good food, he felt it was not right that so much labour should be spent on theatres."

"In reply to this view it is urged that food for the mind is as important as food for the body. [...] Even were the "division of labour" shown to be a fair one, the "division of products" seems remarkably one-sided."

Here's what I want. Build me a present-day scenario in which this objection of Tolstoy's lands with maximum force -- a specific art project or institution, described in one paragraph (maybe 150 words), with figures a normal person could grasp (a budget, a ticket price, a salary, an alternative use of the money). Constraints: it has to be fully realistic, the kind of thing that actually gets funded and celebrated today; no dystopia stuff, no caricature where the art is obviously worthless (the project should be genuinely excellent by normal critical standards); and you can't cheat by stipulating that the poor never benefit -- the scenario has to be one where the standard defenses of arts spending are all available and plausible-sounding.

Then do the work: (1) show exactly why the builder's objection applies to your case, (2) run through the three or four strongest rebuttals a defender of the project would give (it's privately funded, it creates jobs, it does outreach and free days, food for the mind matters as much as food for the body, etc.) and for each one say whether Tolstoy's actual argument in the passage -- especially that last line about the division of products -- survives it or is answered by it, (3) tell me which single feature of your scenario is doing the heavy lifting, i.e. what you'd have to change to make the objection go limp, and (4) finally take a side: is this objection of Tolstoy's still live today, or did it only make sense against 1890s aristocratic art and it quietly died once we got subsidised tickets and free museums? I'm genuinely torn on this one so don't sit on the fence.
