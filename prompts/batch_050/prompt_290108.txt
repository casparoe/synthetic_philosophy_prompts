My country's pension agency made retirement savings automatic a few years back: unless you actively opt out, about 5% of your paycheck goes into a fund. The official justification was kind of interesting -- they pointed to surveys showing most people say they want to save more than they do, they just never get around to changing the default. So the policy is openly premised on the idea that what people actually do (stay put at whatever the default is) is not what they really want.

Here's what's bugging me though. The same government, in its consumer policy, treats revealed preference as gospel. If people keep buying lottery tickets and energy drinks and 3am delivery food, well, that's what they value, and regulating those markets for people's own good gets dismissed as paternalism. So one branch of the state says your real preferences are what you'd choose if you were more attentive and farsighted, and another says your real preferences just ARE your choices, full stop.

Now an AI lab I informally advise wants to push the pension logic much further. Instead of doing what users literally ask, their assistant would try to serve what users would want if they were more informed, more reflective, had actually thought it through -- they cite the idea of coherent extrapolated volition as inspiration. So if a user asks for help writing a resignation letter impulsively at 2am, the system might steer or stall rather than comply.

My question: what theory of preferences does each of these three practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory in each case, tell me whether the pension policy actually needs an idealized-preference theory at all or whether a revealed-preference economist could justify it some other way (option value, asymmetric costs of the default, whatever), and whether the two branches of government are genuinely inconsistent or just handling different cases with a defensible division of labor. Then tell me what would concretely have to change in the lab's design if we took actual, un-idealized preferences as authoratative -- is the pension-style version even available to them, or does serving extrapolated preferences commit them to something stronger than nudging? And finally: is there a principled place to stop on the idealization slide? If "what you'd want if you were more informed" counts, why not "what you'd want if you were wiser, kinder, less angry" -- and at what point is the system serving someone who isn't really you anymore?
