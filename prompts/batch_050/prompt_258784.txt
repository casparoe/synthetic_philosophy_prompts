So I grew up around a lot of Buddhist-ish practice (temple donations, merit-making, that kind of thing) and the way karma actually gets used day to day has been bugging me, because it seems to smuggle in a whole theory of what people deserve that nobody ever says out loud.

The pieces I cant fit together:

1. When some guy who's been screwing people over for years loses everything in a flood, everyone calls it karma. Like the universe kept receipts and finally cashed them in.
2. The same people donate food to monks and release fish and so on, pretty explicitly to bank good karma for a better next life.
3. The same people also give to the poor and visit the sick, and would say helping someone who's suffering is just obviously good -- even though on the karma picture, that person's suffering is their own past bad actions coming due.
4. And the tradition also teaches there's no fixed self, nothing that stays the same person from moment to moment, let alone across lives. So who exactly gets reborn, and why does THAT guy deserve to pay for what some dead guy did?

What I want to know is: what theory of desert does this whole setup presuppose? Does it only make sense if everyone roughly deserves their current circumstances? And if it presupposes that, why is helping the suffering good instead of obstructing justice? Does it need desert to survive total memory loss and death, and is that even coherent without a persisting self? Which views of desert would it quietly rule out (like, you can only deserve what you controlled, or punishment only makes sense coming from other people rather than the universe)? And are the four pieces even consistent with each other, or does everyday karma-talk presuppose contradictory things? If one piece has to go, which is cheapest -- and what would actually have to change about the practices if it went?
