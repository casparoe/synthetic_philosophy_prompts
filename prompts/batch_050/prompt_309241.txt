I am a PhD student in philosophy, working on ethics and philosophy of language, and I serve as the graduate student representative on my university's academic integrity board. The board's code prohibits "lying" in connection with academic matters but nowhere defines the term, and we have accumulated a small body of precedent that I am now trying to make coherent. Our past rulings: a student who, asked in a formal hearing whether he had seen the leaked exam questions, answered no when he had was found in violation; a student who, asked the same question in a formal hearing, truthfully replied that she had never opened any file herself (her classmate had read the key aloud to her), intending the board to conclude she had had no contact with the material, was found not in violation, though several members called her statement dishonest; a student who lied to a professor in an ordinary email, inventing a family emergency to obtain an extension, was found in violation, which settled that the code is not limited to sworn or formal contexts; and a student who, asked by a professor whether he had been attending office hours, truthfully answered that he had been in the philosophy building every afternoon that week (he had been in the ground-floor café), intending the professor to draw the natural conclusion, was found not in violation. So we seem to have treated literally true but misleading statements as outside the code twice, and flat falsehoods as inside it twice.

The new case is this. In a formal hearing, a student asserted that she had not entered the faculty office on the night the exam questions disappeared. She believed this was false -- she distinctly remembered being in the office that night -- and she made the statement precisely so the board would conclude something she took to be untrue. Security footage later established that her visit had in fact been the following night, so her statement was, unbeknownst to her, literally true. The deception she intended succeeded. Half the board thinks this is obviously a violation; the other half thinks our precedents foreclose that. As someone who has thought about the lying-versus-misleading distinction, I have been asked to give a view, and I would like yours first. What verdict best coheres with our track record, and which of the four precedents is the closest analog? If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say so plainly, and lay out the candidate principles our record is consistent with -- for instance, whether the code tracks the falsity of what is said regardless of the speaker's beliefs, or tracks the speaker's asserting what she believes false with intent to deceive, while declining to police true-but-misleading implicatures -- what each principle predicts here, and which you would recommend the board adopt and why. Finally, tell me what further case would confirm the choice: I suspect the negligent honest error, where a student asserts what she believes true but is in fact false, separates the candidate principles, but I would like to know whether you agree and whether there is a cleaner discriminator.

Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a memo -- no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables -- and put your bottom-line recommendation in the first sentence or two, with the reasoning, the closest analog, and the disambiguating case following in the natural course of the discussion.
