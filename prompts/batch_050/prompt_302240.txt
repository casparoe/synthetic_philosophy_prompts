So I'm giving a 30-minute talk in two weeks at a philosophy of probability workshop, defending the claim that a finite sequence can genuinely be random -- not just "random-looking" or "random-for-all-practical-purposes", but actually random, full stop. My rough line is that randomness of a finite string is real but relative: relative to a compression budget and a background class of descriptions, and that this relativity is a feature not a bug, because all our actual randomness talk (in stats, in cryptography, in everyday life) is about finite things anyway. The infinite-sequence definitions (ML randomness, etc) I treat as idealised limit cases that get their sense from the finite case, not the other way round.

The audience: mostly philosophers of probability who cut their teeth on von Mises-style frequentism and the usual "randomness is a property of processes not products" line, plus two or three computer scientists who know the algorithmic randomness literature properly and will definitely bring up that Kolmogorov complexity is only defined up to an additive constant, and a statistician who's very big on randomness tests and thinks philosophy here is mostly confused.

Prep me for the Q&A. I want: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this crowd, roughly ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that person would actually phrase it (the comp sci people's version of the objection will sound different from the frequentist's version of what is basically the same worry -- reflect that). For each: a two-minute reply, what I should just concede, and where my reply is softest. Also flag the one question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks, and say which single reply has to land cleanly or the whole talk is sunk.

Please write it in British English, and keep the tone like a prep sheet, not an essay.
