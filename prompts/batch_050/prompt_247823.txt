I finally got around to rewatching Everything Everywhere All at Once and something bugged me this time. The whole plot runs on verse-jumping: Evelyn taps into other-universe versions of herself to borrow their skills, Alpha-Waymond pilots our Waymond's body, and the emotional climax depends on Evelyn recognizing Jobu Tupaki as her daughter Joy and refusing to let her go. So here's my question: read the film through the David Lewis vs. Kripke fight about cross-world identity. On Lewis's counterpart theory, individuals are world-bound -- the Evelyn in the hot-dog-fingers universe is a counterpart, not literally her, and strictly speaking she shouldn't matter to our Evelyn the way her own future self matters. On the Kripke-style transworld identity picture, the other Evelyns genuinely are Evelyn under different circumstances, which seems to be what the film needs for its stakes to land: Evelyn grieves, envies, and learns from these women as if they were her own unlived lives. But then the film also does very Lewis-y things: counterparts resemble rather than are each other, verse-jumping works by locating a nearby world where a suitably similar person did something improbable, and the nihilism of the everything bagel looks a lot like the worry that if every counterpart exists, nothing you do matters. Which metaphysics is the film actually committed to? Where does it smuggle in whichever view it needs scene by scene -- I'm thinking especially of the Waymond body-possession thing, where identity of person and identity of body seem to come apart entirely -- and does the ending (choosing this universe, this family) settle the question or just change the subject? If it helps, say what the film adds to the debate that the journal articles don't: does watching Evelyn's choices make one of the two views emotionally unintelligible in a way no argument about modal realism could?
