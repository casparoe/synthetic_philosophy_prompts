Two friends of mine, Asha and Ben, were helping a third friend, Sam, think through a career decision, and they ended up in a dispute they asked me to settle. Sam has three options: keep his current stable job, join a startup, or take a position with an NGO abroad. Everyone agrees the outcome depends on how his industry fares over the next decade, and everyone agrees there is no meaningful basis for assigning probabilities — it might boom, it might muddle along, or it might crash. After long discussion, both of my friends accepted the following table, which gives rough lifetime wellbeing for each option in each state:

- Keep his job: 6 if the industry booms, 5 if it muddles along, 4 if it crashes.
- Join the startup: 11 if it booms, 5 if it muddles, 1 if it crashes.
- Join the NGO: 9 if it booms, 4 if it muddles, 2 if it crashes.

The table itself is not in dispute; the disagreement is entirely about how one ought to choose under genuine ignorance.

Asha opened by saying the answer is straightforward: with no probabilities available, Sam should maximize the worst-case outcome, which means keeping his job, since a floor of 4 beats floors of 1 and 2. Under real ignorance, she said, any rule that chases upside is gambling with ruin on the basis of nothing.

Ben replied that Asha's rule is myopic: maximin treats every outcome except the worst as irrelevant, so it would prefer a guaranteed 4 to an option paying 10 in nearly every state and 3.9 in the last. He instead computed a regret table — for each state, how far each option falls short of the best available option in that state — and showed that keeping the job has a maximum regret of 5, the startup a maximum of 3, and the NGO a maximum of 2. Sam should join the NGO, since that minimizes the worst missed opportunity.

Asha answered with two objections. First, regret is defined relative to the menu of options: the best outcome in each state depends on what else happens to be on the table, so adding or removing an option that nobody would ever choose can flip what Ben's rule recommends. Whether Sam should keep his job or join the NGO should not depend on whether a startup he was never going to join happened to be listed. Second, regret is an emotion one might feel afterward, not a quantity of value; Ben's rule protects Sam's future self from a painful feeling rather than protecting his actual interests.

Ben conceded the menu-dependence point was troubling but said insensitivity to all foregone value is the worse flaw, and then pivoted to what he called the deeper principle: in genuine ignorance one should treat the states as equally likely and maximize expected value, which here favors the startup (17/3 against 5 for each of the others). Rawls-style maximin reasoning, he said, only looks compelling because it smuggles in unlimited risk aversion and presents it as rationality; even Rawls's own conditions for maximin fail here, since one of them is that the chooser cares little for gains above the guaranteed minimum — and Sam plainly does care about the upside, which is exactly why he is agonizing.

Asha's final move was to insist that Rawls's conditions are closer to being met than Ben allows: the crash outcomes of 1 and 2 represent genuine hardship Sam could not absorb, additional gains above security have sharply diminishing value for him, and treating three states as equiprobable is not knowledge but a bookkeeping convention — no less arbitrary, she said, than refusing to bet at all. Assigning equal probabilities feels like rigor, she argued, only because it uses fractions.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: who is right about the maximin verdict on this table, who is right about the regret calculation and its significance, whether the menu-dependence objection and the regret-is-not-value objection succeed, whether Ben's equiprobability argument answers Rawls or merely changes the subject, and whether Rawls's conditions for maximin genuinely apply to Sam's situation as Asha claims. Finally, say what, if anything, both of them are missing.
