I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on axiology, and lately I've been obsessed with a view called tranquilism, which (roughly) holds that nothing is good in itself except states free of craving and suffering. On this view pleasure has no positive value of its own; it matters only insofar as it relieves a craving or a disturbance, and a perfectly contented, untroubled state is as good as it gets. It's inspired by some Epicurean and Buddhist ideas but it's a contemporary position and my cohort keeps arguing about whether it's obviously true or obviously insane. Here's where I need help: I'm also teaching a section on The Great Gatsby this semester, and it struck me that the novel might be the best possible test case for tranquilism in literary form. Gatsby's entire existence is organized around a single enormous craving, the green light, Daisy, the whole recreated past, and the novel seems to show both that the craving is what makes him magnificent and that it's what destroys him. Meanwhile Nick ends the book retreating to the Midwest wanting, apparently, nothing more than for the world to be uniform and at a sort of moral attention forever, which reads to me like a tranquilist ideal stated almost explicitly.

Act as a first-rate literary critic who is also fluent in value theory, the kind of person who could publish this reading in a good journal. Think carefully and brainstorm a bit before committing to a reading. What I want is a genuinely two-way engagement: read Gatsby through tranquilism, but also read tranquilism through Gatsby. Where does the novel seem to endorse the view? I'm thinking of how Daisy's voice is described as full of money, how the actual reunion can only disappoint relative to the years of longing that preceded it, and how the famous closing passage about boats beating back against the current suggests that desire just is this doomed forward striving that never arrives anywhere. But where does the novel resist the reading too? Nick says Gatsby turned out all right in the end and that it's the people around him who disgust him, and there's that stuff about Gatsby's extraordinary gift for hope and his romantic readiness, which the book seems to genuinely admire rather than diagnose. If craving is the disease, why does the novel treat the man with the biggest craving as worth more than the cured or the numb? And does a tranquilist have to say that a reader who finds Gatsby admirable is just confused, or can the view absorb that reaction? If the novel poses a real problem for tranquilism, I want you to say so honestly rather than force a tidy fit, and conversely if tranquilism illuminates scenes that standard readings (the American Dream stuff, the class stuff) leave flat, tell me which scenes and why. End by telling me what, if anything, the novel contributes to the axiology debate that another philosophy paper couldn't.
