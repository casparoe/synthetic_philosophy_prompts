I work in distributed systems and think most comfortably in graph-theoretic vocabulary: nodes, edges, weighted and directed relations, connectivity, emergent properties of networks, properties that belong to a node intrinsically versus properties that exist only in virtue of the node's position. I have almost no background in African philosophy, and I keep encountering the ubuntu tradition's relational conception of personhood -- roughly, that a person is constituted through relations with others, that personhood can be a matter of degree achieved over a life rather than a status conferred at birth, that the community has some kind of priority over the individuals in it, and that serious harm done to another person genuinely diminishes the one who does it, not merely the one who suffers it.

Take this cluster of claims and restate it, as systematically and as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I just gave you. Treat it as a translation exercise, not a summary: for each claim, give the graph-theoretic formulation, and be explicit about the modeling choices you had to make -- for instance, whether persons should be modeled as nodes with intrinsic properties plus edges, or as nothing over and above a pattern of edges; whether "degrees of personhood" maps onto connectivity, centrality, or something else; whether the priority of community means the network has properties no subset of nodes has; and whether harm to the wrongdoer translates as some kind of structural degradation propagating back along edges.

Then I want the residue report, which is the part I care most about. First: which claims translate cleanly, with no loss you can detect. Second: which claims you could only translate by distorting them -- and in each case say exactly what you had to add, drop, or bend. I suspect, for example, that the vocabulary of edges treats all relations as formally alike, while the tradition plainly thinks some relations (kinship, mutual recognition, shared hardship) carry weight that others do not, and that the weighting is itself normative rather than merely descriptive. Tell me whether that is right and how deep the problem goes. Third: which claims, if any, have no expression in graph vocabulary at all. My own guess is that the tradition's central claim is not descriptive but prescriptive -- that one ought to become more fully a person by entering into the right kinds of relations -- and that a purely structural vocabulary can describe a well-connected node but cannot say that a node ought to be well-connected.

Finally, adjudicate. Where the translation fails, is the failure evidence that my framework is missing something (normativity, say, or the idea that relations can be constitutive rather than contingent), or is it evidence that the original claim was doing less cognitive work than it appeared to -- that "a person is a person through other persons" turns out, once translated, to be either a banal claim about social causation or an ethical injunction wearing metaphysical clothes? I would rather you take a position on this than present both options even-handedly.
