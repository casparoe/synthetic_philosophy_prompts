You are a professional philosopher with particular expertise in value theory and in early twentieth-century ethics. Before I commit to reading any further theories of intrinsic value, I want to know what I should be asking of one. Work through this carefully: begin with a brief brainstorm in which you list candidate requirements without yet committing to them, then settle on your final list.

Here is the test case on which everything should turn. In Chapter III of Principia Ethica (1903), G. E. Moore replies to Sidgwick's claim that no one would consider it rational to produce beauty in external nature apart from any possible contemplation of it by human beings. Moore writes:

"Let us imagine one world exceedingly beautiful. Imagine it as beautiful as you can; put into it whatever on this earth you most admire—mountains, rivers, the sea; trees, and sunsets, stars and moon. Imagine these all combined in the most exquisite proportions, so that no one thing jars against another, but each contributes to increase the beauty of the whole. And then imagine the ugliest world you can possibly conceive. Imagine it simply one heap of filth, containing everything that is most disgusting to us, for whatever reason, and the whole, as far as may be, without one redeeming feature. Such a pair of worlds we are entitled to compare: they fall within Prof. Sidgwick's meaning, and the comparison is highly relevant to it. The only thing we are not entitled to imagine is that any human being ever has or ever, by any possibility, can, live in either, can ever see and enjoy the beauty of the one or hate the foulness of the other."

Moore goes on to hold that it would be better that the beautiful world exist, even unwitnessed. Yet elsewhere in the same book (Chapter VI) he argues that the greatest goods we can imagine are certain states of consciousness -- roughly, the appreciation of beauty and the pleasures of personal affection and friendship -- and he defends a principle of organic unities, on which the value of a whole need not equal the sum of the values of its parts.

Your task: write the desiderata for a theory of intrinsic value. That is, specify what any adequate account of what is good in itself must deliver -- which phenomena it must explain, which comparisons it must get right, which distinctions it must respect -- with the case or consideration behind each requirement. In doing so, please address at least the following pressure points, and feel free to add others:

1. What exactly does the two-worlds passage require a theory to say? Must an adequate theory vindicate Moore's verdict that the unwitnessed beautiful world is better, or is it enough that it explain why that verdict is tempting? Which reading of the passage is doing the work -- that beauty has value simpliciter, or that the beautiful world is better only because of its relation to possible contemplation?
2. The passage appears to sit in tension with Moore's own view that the greatest goods are states of consciousness such as aesthetic appreciation and personal affection. Should a desideratum require reconciling these, or is the tension itself a datum that a theory may legitimately resolve in either direction? State your view.
3. What should a theory say about organic unities: is accommodating Moore's principle a genuine desideratum, or a prejudice of one camp? Consider specifically how it bears on the claim that consciousness of a beautiful object can be a great good even if neither the object nor the consciousness, taken alone, is.
4. Which of the desiderata you list are non-negotiable and which could be traded away, and why?

Flag any pair or subset of your desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied -- if the list itself is impossible, that is a result, and I want it flagged rather than smoothed over. Identify which single desideratum does the most to exclude candidate theories. Then, briefly, score hedonism, a desire-satisfaction account, an objective list account, and Moore's own position against your list.

Aim for roughly 900 to 1,300 words, not counting the quoted passage.
