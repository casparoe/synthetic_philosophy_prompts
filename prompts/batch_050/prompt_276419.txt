I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and I am fact-checking a colleague's review of a popular book on human nature. The review contains the following summary of evolutionary psychology, and before I sign off on it I want to know how much of it a careful practitioner of the field would accept. Here is the passage, quoted from my colleague's draft:

'Evolutionary psychology holds that the human mind is a bundle of specialized instincts, each one an adaptation engineered by natural selection to solve some problem our hunter-gatherer ancestors faced during the Pleistocene. Its method is to take a widespread modern behavior -- men's sexual jealousy, women's preference for high-status mates, children's wariness of strangers -- and infer the ancestral problem it must have been designed to solve. Because we cannot observe the Pleistocene or run experiments on our ancestors, these inferences are not testable, which is why critics describe them as just-so stories: plausible tales told after the fact. The field further holds that human nature was fixed by our genes before the invention of agriculture, so that we are cavemen loose in a shopping mall, fundamentally mismatched to modern life; and it follows that social reforms aimed at changing deeply rooted behaviors are doomed, since you cannot fight your own genome.'

My instinct is that this is broadly the right picture but that something in it is off, and I do not trust my instinct enough to mark up the draft. Could you give me an accuracy audit I can act on? Specifically: go through the passage claim by claim and tell me which parts a competent evolutionary psychologist would endorse as stated, which parts are defensible simplifications of the field's actual commitments, and which parts misrepresent it -- whether by overstating a modest thesis, treating a standard objection as a settled refutation, or attributing to the field a claim its leading figures explicitly reject. Where the passage describes the field's method and its testability, I would particularly like to know whether the described method (observe a behavior, infer an ancestral function) is what serious work in the area actually does, and whether the inference from unobservable past to untestable story is sound. Finally, my colleague concludes, on the basis of this summary, that evolutionary psychology is ideology in a lab coat rather than a research program. Does that verdict follow from the parts of the summary that survive correction, or does it depend precisely on the parts that do not?
