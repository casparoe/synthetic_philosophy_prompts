I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade, and although I remember the wave of posts about evidential decision theory, updatelessness, and cooperating with agents you will never meet, I will admit my grasp of the details has gone hazy. The topic came up again last week at a meetup, and a friend who had clearly read the material more recently than me gave the following summary of the idea he called evidential cooperation in large worlds. I am reproducing his summary from memory, in his voice, more or less as he put it: "The universe is vast, and plausibly contains enormous numbers of agents whose circumstances and reasoning closely resemble yours. Your own decision in a given situation is strong evidence about what all those similar agents decide in their situations, because they are running broadly the same cognitive algorithm. So when you choose to cooperate rather than defect, you are not just affecting the person in front of you -- you are effectively making it the case that legions of agents scattered across the cosmos cooperate with beings like you. This means that cooperation is essentially always the rational choice, regardless of how weak the resemblance is or how much the other party's values differ from yours, and it means defection is cosmically catastrophic: every time you cut a corner, countless copies of you are doing the same. In effect the argument shows that ordinary self-interested reasoning, once you take the large world seriously, converges on something like universal altruism, so the whole debate between selfishness and ethics dissolves into a confusion about physics."

Something about this rubs me the wrong way, but I cannot put my finger on precisely where. I am fairly confident the original idea does not require any particular interpretation of quantum mechanics or a literal multiverse, and I seem to recall that the whole point was supposed to be that this is an evidential rather than causal connection -- yet my friend keeps slipping into language about "making" the other agents cooperate, as though one's decision reached out and moved them. I also have a nagging memory that the argument was supposed to be sensitive to how strongly one's decision is actually correlated with the other agents' decisions, and that it was about cooperation among agents who stand in some suitable relation to one another (possibly including agents with different values, via something trade-like), rather than a blank cheque for altruism towards everyone. But I might be conflating several different posts and positions myself.

Could you give me an accuracy audit of my friend's summary? Please identify which parts a careful proponent of the view would accept as fair, which parts are overstatements or conflations (and of what with what), and whether his grand conclusion -- that self-interested reasoning in a large world simply dissolves the conflict between selfishness and ethics -- actually follows from the defensible parts of the idea, or whether it depends on precisely the misrepresentations you have identified. Please answer in British English.
