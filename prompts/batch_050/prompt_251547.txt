I am inclined to defend a strong claim: the Frege–Geach problem has been solved, not merely worked around. The solution I have in mind is the higher-order-attitude strategy — moral sentences express con-attitudes rather than beliefs, and logical vocabulary applied to moral sentences expresses higher-order attitudes toward combinations of attitudes, so that "stealing is not wrong" expresses disapproval of tolerating stealing, and a valid moral argument is one whose premises together with the negated conclusion form an inconsistent set of attitudes, by analogy with inconsistent beliefs. Embedding, inference, and disagreement are thereby recovered without a truth-conditional semantics for moral language. Before committing, I want a map of this position's exposure: what would count against it? Consider three embeddings I have not seen addressed in the standard discussion:

1. Supposition without endorsement: "Suppose, just for the sake of argument, that cheating on taxes is always wrong. Then the people who did it have no complaint against the penalty." No disapproval is expressed — the speaker may be entirely neutral — yet "wrong" embeds and the reasoning goes through. What, on this strategy, does the supposition operator do to an attitude?

2. Probability operators: "It is probably wrong to eat factory-farmed meat." Is this a credence in a normative proposition (which the view claims not to need), a hedged or graded disapproval, or a probability distribution over one's own attitudes? Each reading seems to cost something — for instance, one can apparently assign wrongness probability 0.7 while fully disapproving, which suggests probability is not attitude strength.

3. Optatives about wrongness itself: "If only lying in such cases weren't wrong!" The strategy's paraphrase makes this a wish that one stop disapproving of lying. But the wish is not self-directed in that way — one can have it while being glad one disapproves.

For each case, would a committed practitioner of the strategy concede it as a genuine threat, or dismiss it as a further instance of a problem already solved? Beyond these: which results would be fatal — say, a demonstration that the strategy validates classically invalid argument forms, or that it collapses into simple subjectivism about one's own attitudes — and which would be merely awkward? And is there any conceivable embedding the strategy could not absorb by positing yet another bespoke higher-order attitude? If there is not, I want an honest verdict on whether that flexibility is a virtue or a sign that the position risks nothing and therefore settles nothing. I would personally find most persuasive a case where the paraphrase changes the subject — where the analysis mentions the speaker's psychology although the original sentence plainly does not.
