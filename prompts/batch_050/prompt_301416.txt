Two friends of mine -- one did a behavioral ecology masters, the other works in marketing -- got into a long argument at a barbeque last weekend about whether wastefulness is what makes signals trustworthy, and I genuinely can't tell who came out on top. It started when someone mentioned engagement rings. (1) My marketing friend said rings are a perfect example of the handicap principle: a signal is only believable if it's expensive to produce, because cheap words are free for liars, so the whole point of the diamond is that it's a waste of money -- the waste IS the message. (2) My ecology friend pushed back: it's not waste per se that does the work, it's that the same signal costs different amounts to different types. A degree is cheap for someone smart and diligent, ruinously expensive in effort for someone who isn't, so the signal sorts people without anyone needing to burn money. On her view, if a signal is just "money set on fire," it actually proves nothing, because a rich low-quality guy and a rich high-quality guy can both afford it. (3) He said that's a distinction without a difference -- "costs more for the low type" is still costliness doing all the work, so she's just restating the handicap principle in fancier language, and anyway in cases where interests genuinely conflict, like prey signaling fitness to predators, some real waste is unavoidable because any cheap signal gets faked immediately. (4) She said no, he's conflating two claims: "a signal must be costly to fake" and "a signal must be wasteful to produce." She gave the example of signals that are cheap for everyone but impossible to fake (a smell you either have or don't) and signals that are cheap because interests are aligned (a friend telling you which movie she actually liked), and said honesty can come from verification or aligned incentives with zero waste anywhere in the system -- so waste is at most one mechanism among several, not the essence of signaling.

He then said (5) her counterexamples sneak cost back in through the side door: the smell is "impossible to fake" only because faking it would be metabolically expensive, and the friend's movie reccomendation is trustworthy only because lying would cost her the friendship if found out -- so there's always a cost structure underneath, and his original claim survives in a generalized form. She said (6) at that point the claim has been stretched so thin it's trivial -- if "cost" now includes opportunity costs, reputational risks, and hypothetical counterfactual costs, then the handicap principle predicts nothing in particular and can't be tested, and the strong interesting version (signals must be wasteful) is just false.

Can you go through the six moves one by one and say who's right at each step -- and where, if anywhere, each of them is being sloppy? Also: if the disagreement dissolves once you define terms properly, I'd like to know what the actual substantive disagreement underneath is, if there is one.
