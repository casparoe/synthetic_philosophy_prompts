I recently came across Aunt Hillary and the Ant Fugue, and I can’t stop thinking about a weird issue it raises. Forget for a second whether individual ants are smart. Suppose a colony really does have something like a memory or preference at the colony level: it remembers where food was, chooses one nesting site over another, and changes strategy after a route becomes dangerous. Now imagine that over a year every individual ant in the colony dies and is replaced, but the trails, roles, and patterns of behavior continue. If the colony had a mind, is it still the same mind? And if the colony splits into two colonies, each taking some ants and some chemical traces of the old routes, both might behave as if they remember the old colony’s past. Which one has the original belief or memory?

I’m not just asking whether minds can emerge from lots of mindless parts, since that seems too broad. The phenomenon I mean is more specific: a possible subject whose mental life can continue despite total replacement of its parts, but also divide into two successors with no obvious fact about which successor inherited the original mental states. Does this have a name in philosophy? I’ve seen phrases like collective intelligence and group mind, but those seem mostly about whether the group thinks at all, not about identity over time and splitting. Please explain the nearest established concepts, how this case differs from each, and if there isn’t a good term, propose one. I’m also interested in whether this makes colony minds seem less real, or whether human minds have the same problem in a less obvious form. Sorry if this is phrased awkwardly — I dont have any philosophy training.
