I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long feature on societal collapse -- the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya, Easter Island -- and on the current scholarly pushback against the very word. My comparative advantage, and my readership's, is that I've spent years reporting on ecology and complexity science, so I'm fluent in that vocabulary: regime shifts, alternative stable states, hysteresis, critical slowing down, basins of attraction, adaptive cycles, the difference between resilience understood as persistence and resilience understood as adaptability.

What I'd like from you is a disciplined exercise in translation, not an explainer. Take the debate between, on one side, the tradition that treats these episodes as genuine breakdowns -- the complexity-and-diminishing-returns line of argument associated with Tainter, and the popular ecocide narratives associated with Diamond -- and, on the other side, the revisionist scholarship that prefers to speak of transformation, reorganization, and resilience, that stresses regional variation and continuity of ordinary life, and that asks pointedly for whom, exactly, the end of a political order counts as a catastrophe. Restate both positions, as faithfully as you can, entirely in the vocabulary of ecological resilience and dynamical systems. The Maya 'collapse' as a regime shift between attractors; the Bronze Age as a coupled system undergoing cascading failure; the revisionists as people who think the analysts have mistaken a transition between basins for a fall out of state space altogether, or misidentified which system's state variables matter. That sort of thing, but done properly and at length.

Then -- and this is the part I actually need -- give me the residue report. For each position, distinguish three things: which claims translate cleanly into systems vocabulary; which can only be translated by distorting them (say what gets bent, and in which direction); and which have no expression in that vocabulary at all. I have my own suspicions about where the residue will accumulate: the normative valence of 'collapse' (resilience theory is indifferent to whether a regime shift is a tragedy or a liberation); the question of who counts as the system whose state we're tracking; the role of agency, ideology and elite decision-making, which don't obviously correspond to anything in a state-space picture; and whether 'complexity' in the sociological sense is even the same kind of quantity as complexity in an ecosystem. But I want your analysis, not a confirmation of mine. Where the translation fails, say also whether you think the failure indicts the target vocabulary or reveals that the original claim was vaguer than it looked -- those are very different diagnoses and my article will hinge on which one is right in each case.

Two requests about how you answer. First, please be scrupulous in distinguishing what is genuinely settled among archaeologists and historians (for instance, I take it that the simple 'ecocide' story of Easter Island is no longer respectable in its strong form, but tell me if I'm wrong) from what remains live and contested, and don't let a contested revisionist position masquerade as the new consensus any more than the old one. Second, please write in British English throughout.
