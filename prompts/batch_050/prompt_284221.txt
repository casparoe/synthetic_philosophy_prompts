In his essay "The Unreality of Time" (Mind, 1908), J. M. E. McTaggart, having just introduced his distinction between the A series and the B series, argues that the B series cannot by itself constitute time:

"It would, I suppose, be universally admitted that time involves change. A particular thing, indeed, may exist unchanged through any amount of time. But when we ask what we mean by saying that there were different moments of time, or a certain duration of time, through which the thing was the same, we find that we mean that it remained the same while other things were changing. A universe in which nothing whatever changed (including the thoughts of the conscious beings in it) would be a timeless universe.

If, then, a B series without an A series can constitute time, change must be possible without an A series. Let us suppose that the distinction of past, present and future does not apply to reality. Can change apply to reality? What is it that changes?"

Write a dialogue (roughly 900–1,200 words, not counting the passage above) between two philosophers arguing over what this passage establishes. One is a presentist who accepts the passage's reasoning almost wholesale: time requires change, change requires that events really do pass from future to present to past, and McTaggart's only mistake was to conclude that time is unreal rather than that the B-series picture of the world is incomplete. The other is an eternalist who denies that the passage has any force against the B-theory: what McTaggart calls change, she argues, is fully captured by a thing's having incompatible properties at different times, and his demand to know what changes trades on refusing to count that as an answer.

The dialogue must do real work rather than recite positions. In particular:

- The eternalist should press the analogy with spatial variation: a poker that is hot at one end and cold at the other does not thereby change across its length, so why should a thing's being hot at one time and cold at another count as anything more than variation? The presentist must answer this directly, and the eternalist must engage the answer she actually receives rather than repeat the objection.
- The eternalist should also turn the passage's logic against the presentist herself: for the presentist, past and future events do not exist, so what makes it true that the poker was hot? If nothing grounds the past-tensed truth, the presentist seems left with a single present state plus an unsupported story about how things were — which is arguably less change, not more, than the B-theorist's variation. The presentist must respond with her best account (primitive tensed facts, an ersatz past, or whatever she chooses) and concede whatever cost is genuinely there.
- They should address McTaggart's closing claim in the passage — that a universe in which nothing whatever changed would be timeless — including the question whether time could pass in a world frozen in every respect for an interval. Each should say whether her own view can accommodate such a case, and what follows if it cannot.
- Both interlocutors must be equally competent. Neither caricatures the other; each responds to the other's actual arguments, and genuine concessions are made where they are earned.
- The dialogue should end not with agreement but with each stating, as precisely as she can, the single proposition over which they disagree and why nothing said has settled it.
