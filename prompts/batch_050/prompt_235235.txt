I have recently been hired to write the screenplay for a biographical film about a public intellectual who died a little over a year ago and whom I knew personally late in their life, which is how this stopped being an abstract question for me. The estate is cooperating, the subject's adult children will read drafts, and I am already being asked to make the standard dramatizing moves: inventing dialogue for scenes no one recorded, compressing timelines, merging two minor figures into one, and attributing motives for decisions the subject took to the grave without explaining. I keep saying "I'll know it when I see it," which I recognize is not a standard anyone can hold me to.

What I want is a reusable decision procedure I can run on any individual deviation from the documentary record before I put it in a draft: the questions to ask about a scene in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure returns a flat no, and where it has to hand the decision back to me because the remaining question is one of values rather than analysis. It should draw on whatever ethics actually has to say about obligations to the dead, to their survivors, and to living third parties who appear in the story, and about what, if anything, an audience's likely belief does to the writer's obligations — but it must be usable by someone (me, my producers) who will not read the journal literature, so tell me which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which can safely be dropped. Where the theory does not settle the matter — for instance, whether the dead can be wronged at all, or whether a prominent disclaimer genuinely shifts responsibility to the viewer — say so plainly rather than presenting one side as established. I would rather the procedure flag its own contested load-bearing assumptions than hide them.

Please then run the procedure on three cases from my current outline: (1) an invented private conversation between the subject and their spouse on the night of a well-documented public decision, which I need for structure and cannot source; (2) merging two of the subject's colleagues into a composite character who betrays them, when the actual betrayal was committed by one person who is still alive and litigious; (3) depicting the subject making a cruel remark to a graduate student, alleged by a single hostile memoir and denied by everyone else I have interviewed. I expect at least one of these to come back with an answer I will not like — do not soften the procedure to spare me. Finally, tell me honestly what a procedure like this cannot do: the failure modes, the kinds of distortion it will wave through because they fall outside its questions, and whether, in your judgment, a writer who followed it perfectly would still owe anyone anything.
