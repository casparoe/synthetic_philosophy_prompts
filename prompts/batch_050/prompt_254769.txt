A friend and I got stuck on tranquilism — roughly the view that the only thing of intrinsic value is the absence of craving and suffering, where pleasure counts only insofar as it relieves craving, and the best life is the one with the least inner disturbance. The lazy objections you always hear are that tranquilism implies we should want a universe with no sentient beings, or that it's just negative utilitarianism in a robe. I want something sharper than that.

Please argue for this specific claim: tranquilism implies that, other things equal, there is nothing to be said in favor of creating a person whose every desire will be promptly and reliably satisified — such a life adds no value at all over that person never existing — and argue that this implication is a feature of the view rather than a reductio of it.

Two requirements. First, engage seriously with the strongest countermove: the objection that promptly satisfied desires never ripen into cravings at all, so this frictionless life is actually perfectly tranquil, which would mean tranquilism should favor creating it (maybe even favor it over a real tranquilist sage, who after all still has to keep choosing equanimity). You have to explain why the countermove fails, or quietly concedes what it needs to deny. Second, take a side. I don't want a balanced survey of positions on offer, I want an actual argument that ends somewhere.
