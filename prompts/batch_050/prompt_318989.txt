I am a junior in high school and the elected student representative on my school's Conduct Advisory Committee. Before our vote next Tuesday I must circulate a short written opinion on the case below, and I would like you to draft it.

The case: For two years students have used a school-sanctioned online message board. Signing up requires a school email address, but posts display no name, and the vendor built the system so that no one — including school administrators — can trace a post to its author. For most of its existence the board carried a harmless mix of jokes, confessions, and advice. This semester, however, it has hosted two sustained harassment campaigns against named students that no one could be held responsible for, a false rumor that seriously damaged a teacher's reputation, and the coordination of cheating on a take-home exam. Our principal has asked the committee to recommend exactly one of three options: (a) shut the board down; (b) keep it running but switch to persistent pseudonyms that administrators can trace after a formal complaint; (c) leave it exactly as it is.

I believe our dispute is a live version of Glaucon's challenge in Book II of Plato's Republic, and I want the opinion to treat it that way. Glaucon argues that if a just person and an unjust person each possessed a ring that made its wearer invisible, and therefore immune to detection and to reputational cost, their conduct would converge: on his view, people practice justice only for its consequences, and no one would remain just for justice's own sake. Some committee members essentially accept this. They say the board demonstrates what students do the moment consequences vanish, so the only real question is how best to restore consequences. Others push back. They point out that the same board has been the one place students report bullying, disclose mental health struggles, and criticize school policy without fear, and they argue that Glaucon's thought experiment does not prove that anonymity uncovers our true motives — it may instead show that a consequence-free setting manufactures new motives, or that the misconduct of a small minority tells us nothing about the character of the majority.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate, roughly 600 to 800 words, meeting these requirements:

1. It recommends one of the three options clearly, includes a concrete timeline for implementation, and states one measurable criterion under which the committee would revisit the decision.
2. It frames the recommendation as an answer to Glaucon's challenge, stating explicitly which reading of the thought experiment the committee would thereby be endorsing — and it confronts the uncomfortable point that choosing (b) seems to concede Glaucon's claim that we behave only under surveillance, while choosing (c) seems to accept the harms as the price of refusing him.
3. It presents the strongest honest case for the option it rejects, and explains what would have to be true about human motivation for that rejected option to be the right one.
4. It relies on no empirical studies, statistics, or outside sources — only the facts of the case as I have stated them and the thought experiment itself.
