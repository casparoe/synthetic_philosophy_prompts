So I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind and, in a moment of weakness, I agreed to sit on my university's new "AI experiments review panel" — basically an ethics commitee for ML research, modelled loosely on the animal welfare review boards. Once a month I get sent protocols from labs who want to train reinforcement learning agents of one kind or another, and we have to decide whether the training setup raises a welfare concern that warrants actual oversight (monitoring requirements, limits on training runs, sign-off conditions, that sort of thing). The rest of the panel is computer scientists, I'm the only person who's ever read anything about sentience, and I get maybe 30-40 minutes per protocol. Decisions have to be signed off by the chair, who's a roboticist and fairly sceptical of the whole exercise.

What I need is to turn the philosophical mess around reward and suffering into an actual decision procedure I can apply — an ordered set of questions or tests I run through for each protocol, ending in one of: no concern / monitor / escalate. The conceptual work I'm stuck on is the translation. Everyone on the panel keeps sliding between two bad positions: "negative reward is just a number in a config file, nothing to worry about" and "the agent got a negative reward, that's basically pain, shut it down". Neither is right, and I want the procedure to be honest about *why* neither is right — e.g. that the scalar reward a designer specifies isn't the same thing as whatever the agent actually learns to optimise, that behavioural avoidance doesn't settle felt valence, that the markers we'd lean on have to be borrowed from animal welfare science (trade-off behaviour, motivational persistence, flexibility, how the system responds to the things it 'wants' being blocked) and that borrowing them is itself contestable.

Please write the procedure with the reasoning visible at each step, and say explicitly which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which I'm allowed to drop for a 30-minute review. Then show it working on three cases:

1. a small tabular Q-learning agent in a gridworld, trained with shocks on certain tiles over millions of episodes;
2. a large language model fine-tuned with human feedback, where the training involves the model being shown its own 'bad' outputs;
3. an agent in a simulated ecology with an energy budget, predators, and something like pain-asymmetric reward shaping.

At least one of these should come out with an answer I won't like — either the procedure flags something I find obviously silly to worry about, or clears something I find genuinely creepy. Don't soften it to make me comfortable; if the honest procedure does that, that's data.

Also include a section on where the procedure will misfire — the cases where no checklist settles it and the panel just has to exercise judgement (or punt), and what we should *say* in our minutes when that happens rather than pretending the rubric gave a verdict.

Please write in British English.
