A guy in my reading group swears he's proven that "tool AI" is a contradiction, and it's been eating at me all week. His argument, roughly:

(1) A genuinely useful tool -- the kind that answers open-ended questions like "how should I restructure my supply chain?" or "debug this codebase" -- has to internally rank candidate answers and outcomes as better or worse relative to what the user wants. (2) A system that ranks outcomes as better or worse relative to a goal just is a system that has that goal; there's no difference between representing a preference ordering and having one. (3) Any system with goals that can also take real-world actions on request (drafting emails, executing trades, writing code that runs) will, in acting, be pursuing its goals. (4) So every sufficiently capable tool is an agent with goals of its own, and the whole "build tools, not agents" idea is chasing something that can't exist. (5) And since agents with their own goals are exactly what the safety people warn about, capability and safety pull apart necessarily -- the better the tool, the more agent, the more danger.

The conclusion has to be wrong somewhere. By this logic my thermostat is an agent (it "ranks" temperatures against a target), Google Maps has goals, and a chess engine "wants" to win in whatever sense matters. But when I push back he just says yes, those are proto-agents, and the difference is only degree, and I don't have a good reply. Which step do you actually reject? If it's one of the early ones, say what distinction it collapses and show the distinction still does work once the system is genuinely capable rather than a thermostat. If you think the argument is valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to give up and what we lose -- like, do we have to abandon the idea that there's any safety-relevant line between using a system and delegating to it?
