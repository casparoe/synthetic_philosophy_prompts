I host a podcast on ethics and moral psychology, and next week's guest is a philosopher with a new book defending shame. His pitch, roughly in Bernard Williams' spirit, is that the guilt-favoring consensus has it backwards: guilt lets you quarantine the deed from the self (I did a bad thing, but I'm still a good person) and so blocks the deeper self-revision shame forces, and shame's sensitivity to how one appears is part of what keeps communities honest.

Draft eight or so interview questions that press him where he's weakest instead of letting him retell the book. Include at least:

- one on the standard psych finding that shame-proneness tracks avoidance, denial, and lashing out while guilt-proneness tracks reparative action and even lower recidivism — with a follow-up ready for when he says those shame/guilt scales conflate shame with humiliation
- one on whether shame needs an audience, which seems to make it hostage to whatever the local community happens to admire — with a follow-up for when he retreats to an internalized ideal audience, since it's unclear how that differs from a conscience, i.e. from guilt again
- one on whether a culture can even choose its dominant moral emotion, or whether he's prescribing the weather — and when he reaches for restorative justice or truth commissions as levers, a follow-up pointing out those are usually defended as guilt-and-repair institutions, the other side's best examples
- one on the asymmetry worry: shame lands hardest on the already marginalized, and whereas a guilty person can recheck whether they actually did the thing, shame works by taking up the audience's gaze, so misplaced shame may be undetectable from the inside. Follow-up for his inevitable reply that that's misdirected shame, not shame itself.

End with a question about what empirical finding would make him abandon the view. For each question, give the follow-up keyed to the answer hes most likely to give. Start straight in with the questions themselves, no preamble or summary of his position.
