By trade I am a novelist; for the past three years I have also served on the ethics committee of our national writers' association, and it has fallen to me to draft the written opinion in a case that has divided us evenly. A memoirist, a member in good standing, published a chapter on the collapse of her first marriage. Every checkable sentence in the chapter is literally true. Yet by selection and juxtaposition — most damagingly, her observation that in that final spring her husband "came home night after night smelling of a perfume I had never worn" — the chapter leads nearly every reader to conclude he was unfaithful. In fact he had been spending those nights at the bedside of his dying mother, who wore that perfume; the author knew this, and knew what readers would infer. The former husband has lodged a complaint under our code of practice, which obliges members publishing work presented as true "not to deceive their readers," a phrase the code nowhere defines. Her defense is simple: she asserted no falsehood; readers' inferences are their own responsibility; and if arranging true sentences so as to suggest a false conclusion counts as deception, then every novelist on our membership roll — myself included, with my red herrings and withholding narrators — stands condemned alongside her. Half the committee finds this decisive; half thinks the distinction between lying and misleading does no moral work here.

My own unease has been sharpened by a passage early in Augustine's On Lying (De Mendacio, written about the year 395). Setting jokes to one side, Augustine writes: "jokes, which have never been accounted lies, seeing they bear with them in the tone of voice, and in the very mood of the joker a most evident indication that he means no deceit, although the thing he utters be not true." He seems to hold that the moral standing of an utterance turns not on the truth of what is said but on what the occasion itself signals about whether deceit is meant. If that is right, then the joker — and perhaps the novelist — is acquitted by his genre, while the memoirist, writing under a genre that invites belief and offering no countervailing signal, may stand convicted though every sentence she wrote is true. But I am not confident the passage will bear that weight, and a colleague on the committee reads it as cutting the other way.

Please draft the committee's written opinion, of no more than 900 words. It should recommend a definite ruling on the complaint, including any remedy; state how "deceive" in our code ought to be understood, working through Augustine's remark rather than merely invoking it; answer the memoirist's defense, and my worry about fiction, in their strongest forms; and close by naming the strongest argument against the ruling you recommend and explaining why it should nonetheless lose. Where Augustine's principle cannot settle a question the opinion must answer, it should say so plainly rather than pretend otherwise.
