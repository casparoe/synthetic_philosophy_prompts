I am a retired research biologist who has recently taken up philosophy, and I have been reading about Goodhart's law: the principle that a measure, once it is made into a target, tends to stop measuring what it was meant to measure. I have been collecting instances, and my list so far runs as follows: (1) the colonial bounty on dead cobras, answered by cobra breeding; (2) the Soviet nail works of the familiar anecdote, which turned out enormous useless nails when paid by weight and tiny useless ones when paid by count; (3) schools teaching narrowly to the tests on which their standing depends; (4) cardiac surgeons turning away high-risk patients once their mortality statistics are published; (5) the bibliometric targets of my own former profession, answered with salami-sliced publications and citation stacking; and (6), stretching the concept into ethology, supernormal stimuli, in which one species exploits another's evolved proxy cues — the cuckoo chick's exaggerated gape outcompeting the host's own nestlings — much as a specification gamer exploits a proxy. What I should like is one further instance not on this list. Ideally it would be of a kind I have not seen discussed: a case in which no one intends to game anything at all, and the correlation between proxy and goal simply collapses once selection presses hard enough on the proxy. Failing that, a strategically novel case will do, but it must be genuinely new rather than a rewording, a special case, or a combination of the six above. Please explain why your example is an instance of Goodhart's law proper rather than a merely perverse incentive, how it relates to the items on my list, and whether its failure mechanism is distinct from any of theirs. If nothing you can offer clears that bar, say so plainly rather than padding. Kindly write your answer in continuous prose, as one would in a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
