So I write novels for a living, and my publisher has roped me into writing a short essay for the back of the paperback of my next book, one of those "a note from the author" things. The novel is about a small town that, fifty years after a massacre during an occupation, holds a referendum and votes to stop commemorating it -- the memorial comes down, it gets cut from the school curriculum, the annual march is cancelled. The essay is supposed to be me reflecting on the ideas behind the book and I have got myself thoroughly tangled. Comments wanted.

Here are my rough notes so far:

1. Setup. I want to pit two positions against each other. On one side, what I take to be Avishai Margalit's thing (his book on the ethics of memory -- I've forgotten the exact title): memory isn't just private, communities are held together by shared memories of thick relations, and there's something close to an obligation to remember certain people and events, especially victims, because if the community won't remember them nobody will, and they suffer a kind of second death. On the other side, David Rieff's case for forgetting (a polemic from maybe a decade ago?): collective memory is mostly myth dressed up as fact, it keeps grievance and revenge cycles alive -- his examples are things like the Balkans and Northern Ireland -- and sometimes forgetting is the healthier and more humane path. My town is essentially running Rieff's program.

2. The distinction I invented. The novel has an archivist character, and the essay leans on a distinction I made up, which I'm calling the difference between "keeping the record" and "keeping the wound." Keeping the record = archives, dates, names, court documents, anything a historian could later reconstruct. Keeping the wound = commemorations, marches, the stories grandparents tell, everything that keeps the past emotionally live. My essay's central claim: a community can rationally and decently keep the record while letting the wound close, and this dissolves the Margalit/Rieff standoff. Margalit's real worry (the second death of the victims) is answered by the archive; Rieff's real worry (grievance spirals) is answered by letting commemoration lapse.

3. The wrinkle I'm proud of. In the novel the referendum passes, but with a quiet clause: the town pays one family, the archivist's, to be "the rememberers," so that everyone else can forget with a clean conscience. The essay asks whether outsourcing memory like this buys you the benefits of forgetting or is actually the worst of both worlds: the remembering becomes one family's private burden and private grievance rather than a public practice, so it ends up as neither a living community memory (Margalit) nor genuine forgetting (Rieff).

Where I need comments:

a) Accuracy first. Am I getting Margalit and Rieff roughly right, or have I flattened them into cardboard cutouts? I read both years ago, I'm working from memory, and I really dont want to misstate either in print. Specifically: would either of them even accept my "record vs. wound" framing as a fair rendering of their concerns? It occurs to me that Margalit's whole point might be that archives emphatically do NOT count as memory in his sense -- memory for him is lived, shared, thick -- in which case my neat dissolution collapses before it starts. If that's right, tell me.

b) The philosophy. Does the record/wound distinction actually do the work I claim, or is it one of those distinctions that's clean on paper and falls apart at the first push? If you've got a killer counterexample -- real or invented -- of a community that kept the record, let the wound close, and clearly went wrong (or clearly went right, against Rieff), I'd rather hear it now than in a review.

c) The ending. Right now the essay ends by saying the novel "refuses to adjudicate" between memory and forgetting. My editor flagged this as a cop-out. Is there a braver ending that doesn't betray the novel? I'm allergic to essays that pretend fiction settles arguments, but I concede "it's complicated" is not a position.
