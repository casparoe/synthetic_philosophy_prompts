I teach a weekly discussion section for a business ethics course, and last week we worked through a case I constructed for the occasion: a mid-sized bank whose incentive structure produces, over several years, a vast number of fraudulent accounts, although no single employee intended the scheme as a whole or even knew its full scope -- each person made a locally reasonable decision, and the misconduct exists only at the level of the whole. A student of mine used the case to argue that the bank itself cannot be blamed, only the individuals can, and her argument strikes me as proving too much. It runs: (1) Blaming something for an outcome is appropriate only if that thing brought the outcome about intentionally, which requires it to have had beliefs about what it was doing and goals the outcome served. (2) A corporation has no beliefs or goals over and above those of the people who staff it: its "knowledge" is a document in someone's files or a summary of many employees' judgments, and its "decision" is just the output of a procedure applied to their inputs. (3) No procedure for aggregating the judgments of many minds generates a new bearer of mental states; tallying votes yields a statistic, not a thinker. (4) So a corporation never acts intentionally. (5) So a corporation cannot be blamed; talk of corporate guilt is a bookkeeping fiction, and whatever blame there is lands on identifiable individuals. When I presented this as settled, another student ran the parallel: (6) everything a person believes or wants is realized in the activity of neurons, no one of which believes or wants anything, and a person's decision is likewise just the output of neural aggregation; (7) so if (3) is sound, persons are bookkeeping fictions too, and blame evaporates everywhere; (8) blame does not evaporate everywhere; therefore (3) has to go, and with it the case against blaming the bank. The class left the room thinking my student had been refuted, but I keep feeling the reversal was too cheap -- yet when I try to defend (3) I find myself defending (6)'s reasoning as well, which I refuse to do.

If I have to choose, I would rather end up able to blame the bank than be forced to exonerate every fraudster, and I would rather not populate the world with extra minds if it can be avoided; but I cannot tell which of those preferences I will have to sacrifice. Please identify the premise or step you would reject, explain why it looked plausible in the first place, and price the rejection honestly -- in particular, say whether the no-single-intender structure of my bank case is better handled by denying (1), (2), or (3), and whether the person-neuron parallel in (6) is a genuine analogy or conceals a relevant difference (and if the latter, whether that difference is one a theory of blame is entitled to lean on). Also, treat your own diagnosis as provisional: state explicitly what argument, case, or consideration would lead you to locate the flaw somewhere else, and flag the point in your reasoning where you think an error is most likely to be hiding.
