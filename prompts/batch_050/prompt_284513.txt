I teach a political philosophy seminar (~16 undergrads, one 75-min session) and we're doing a week on justice and the state. I want an in-class simulation that makes students *feel* the dilemma behind armed neutrality -- the Switzerland/Finland/old-Sweden model -- rather than just nod at it. The core thing I want them to run into themselves: neutrality is only credible if it's visibly armed, being visibly armed either means conscription (with all the fairness problems about who serves) or a small pricey professional force that probably can't deter anyone alone, and deterrence lives in the *attacker's* beliefs, so what matters isn't whether you'd actually fight but whether the neighbor is convinced you would. Design the game: teams as small neutral states, a points/budget system split between things like professional forces, reserve/militia training, civil defense, and alliance-seeking, 3-4 rounds, me (or a TA) playing the neighboring power who decides each round whether to "attack" based only on what's publicly visible -- so students have to grapple with the fact that hidden capabilities don't deter and visible ones are expensive. Include a debrief mapping what happened onto the real tradeoffs, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I assume someone tries to free-ride on disarmament and I need a plan for that), and be honest about which feature of the real situation the game structurally can't reproduce. And since you obviously can't playtest this, flag which design choices are your best guesses, say how confident you are overall, and single out the part you're least sure actually works as intended.
