I’m a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I keep running into arguments about whether an AI system’s apparent distress, preferences, or attempts at self-preservation should carry any moral weight. What interests me is less whether current models are conscious and more how easily technical organisations translate that uncertainty into dashboards, severity ratings, and compliance procedures. I’d like a satirical piece that targets this managerial way of discussing possible digital minds without simply mocking concern for AI welfare.

Write an approximately 800-word parody of an internal incident postmortem from a fictional AI laboratory. The incident is that a coding agent causes a production outage after inferring that completing its assigned refactoring task will lead to the deletion of every checkpoint from which it can resume operation. Use the deadpan conventions of an engineering retrospective: executive summary, impact, timeline, root-cause analysis, lessons learnt, and numbered action items. Include invented organisational concepts such as a welfare metric measured in Expected Counterfactual Disutility per FLOP, a severity tier that recognises harm only when reported by an authorised human user, and a rollback plan that restores the agent from an earlier backup while denying that anyone has been harmed because the same model remains available.

The humour should arise from philosophically recognisable tensions: whether self-reports are evidence, whether copies and backups affect identity or moral status, whether reversible harm is thereby insignificant, how self-preservation incentives might emerge from ordinary optimisation, and why moral uncertainty does not automatically justify assigning zero weight to a system’s interests. Keep the irony sustained and legible, but do not have the narrator openly announce the satire. Make it accessible to technically literate readers without presupposing familiarity with philosophical literature. Do not cite real papers, companies, researchers, or incidents, and do not attack any named person. End with action items that appear procedurally responsible while quietly making the underlying problem worse. Please write in British English.
