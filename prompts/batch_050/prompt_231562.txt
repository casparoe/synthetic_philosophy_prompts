I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book, and I keep having this dumb recurring thought while writing: when I make a character's grief or jealousy "work" on the page, what am I actually doing -- am I depicting a feeling, or a judgement they don't know they've made, or something else? Turns out philosophers have an actual fight about this: emotions as cognitive judgements (anger as a judgement that one has been wronged), emotions as perceptions of value (fear as perceiving something as dangerous, the way you just see a chair as a chair), and the old-school view that emotions are basically feelings -- bodily disturbances, James/Lange style.

Here's the favor. Restate this debate in the vocabulary I actually think in: point of view and free indirect style, scene vs summary, showing vs telling, interiority vs external behavior, beats and pacing, what the narration lets the reader feel vs only infer. Translate each of the three theories into craft terms as honestly as you can -- like, which view predicts that telling the reader "she was furious" is a failure of craft, and which one is fine with it?

Then the residue report, because that's the part I care about: what from the philosophers' debate doesn't survive the translation into writing-talk? Does the craft vocabulary force you to fudge anything important, or does anything they argue about just have no counterpart in how fiction does emotions? And if there is a residue -- is it because novelist-talk is missing something, or because part of their disagreement was less substantive than it sounded?
