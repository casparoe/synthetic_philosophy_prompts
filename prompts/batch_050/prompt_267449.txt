I advise the rotating chair of an EU ministerial working group on energy security. The group must decide next week whether to endorse a proposed Household Energy Emergency Allocation System. Please draft the recommendation memorandum that I will circulate before the vote.

Treat the following facts as fixed. During a declared continental electricity emergency, a new EU-level authority would use smart-meter, weather, generation, and transmission data to impose binding fifteen-minute household load limits. These limits would override national priority categories and municipal social tariffs. Hospitals and designated critical infrastructure would remain protected under every option. The Commission’s simulations, which the working group accepts as sound, predict that centralized allocation would reduce expected blackout duration by 31 percent relative to coordination among national operators. Roughly three-fifths of that gain, however, comes from shifting curtailment toward older, densely populated housing districts. Municipal utilities identify medically dependent households about 14 percent more accurately than the central model because they can combine consumption records with local health and welfare information.

National operators presently lack the interoperable telemetry system needed to run a joint allocation model. This incapacity is partly endogenous: agreement on a common standard has stalled in EU-level negotiations for six years, although the member states could probably establish one within thirty months if given funding and a deadline. The working group must therefore decide whether the lower levels’ present incapacity makes centralization necessary, or whether it instead shows that the higher level has manufactured the very necessity it now invokes.

Three positions are represented. Centralizers argue that electricity cascades cross borders within minutes and that democratic niceties are irrelevant if decentralized delay produces blackouts. Decentralists argue that decisions about which households lose power are judgments about vulnerability, desert, and local social priorities, not merely technical calculations. A third group favors centralized determination of how much load each region must shed and when, with municipalities deciding which households bear the cuts, subject to a narrowly defined override when a cascade is imminent.

Write a formal memorandum recommending one of these positions, or a clearly specified alternative. Begin with an unambiguous bottom line. The memo should:

1. State the operative conception of subsidiarity being applied, rather than treating subsidiarity as a slogan.
2. Distinguish cross-border physical externalities from locally embedded knowledge about vulnerability and social need.
3. Explain whether and why the endogenous incapacity of the member states strengthens or weakens the case for centralization.
4. Address the distributive concern that aggregate efficiency gains are produced by imposing predictable burdens on particular communities.
5. Guard against emergency creep, meaning the gradual conversion of exceptional emergency powers into routine instruments of grid management.
6. Give the strongest argument for the losing side and explain what evidence or institutional safeguard would make that argument decisive.

If the recommendation is hybrid, specify the division of authority precisely enough to govern a real emergency: who determines the emergency threshold, who sets the aggregate reduction, who selects households, when override is permissible, who is politically accountable, and when the arrangement expires. Do not rely on treaty citations, case law, or external authorities.
