I'm a novelist working on a book set in a near-future where the many-worlds interpretation has gone mainstream the way mindfulness did -- not settled science exactly, but culturally dominant, with startups and lifestyle gurus built around it. One of my running jokes is a company called Everett Mutual that sells "branch-weighted life insurance": they promise to look after the versions of you in the low-amplitude branches, the ones where everything goes wrong. I need the company's actual brochure, and I want it to be a real piece of satire rather than a cheap gag, so I'd like you to write it for me.

Format: a customer FAQ, maybe 650-750 words, six to eight numbered questions and answers, in that soothing corporate voice that sounds reassuring while quietly saying nothing. The comedy should come from the company genuinely trying to run a business on Everettian principles and slamming into the real problems: why premiums should scale with the square of the amplitude when every outcome happens in some branch anyway; what a claims department even does when a claim is both paid and denied with full certainty; whether you should take comfort in your fortunate counterparts or whether that's just feeling smug about strangers; the awkward fact that the founders' decision-theoretic justification for the whole pricing model keeps getting challenged in the philosophy journals and the legal team keeps having to issue statements. I want the reader to feel the Born rule problem pressing through the fine print without the brochure ever breaking character or lecturing about it. No winking, no puns about cats -- deadpan all the way, and internally consistent with the interpretation (no sneaky collapse talk, no "you really only experience one branch" hedging, since the whole joke is that they refuse to hedge).

Since this is for a novel and I'll be putting it in front of readers who include at least one friend who works on quantum foundations, I also need you to play fair with the physics and the philosophy. At the end, after the brochure, add a short out-of-character note for me as the author: say how confident you are that the satire exaggerates real features of the Born rule debate rather than inventing fake ones, and single out the single point in the piece you're least sure you've represented correctly -- whether it's the decision-theory stuff, the "why should I care about measure" objection, or something else -- so I know where to tread carefully.
