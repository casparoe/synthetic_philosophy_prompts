I'm a PhD student in political theory and this semester I'm TAing our department's grad seminar on Marx and contemporary critiques of capitalism. The prof has asked each of the TAs to contribute one essay question to the final exam, and I want mine to be good because, honestly, the students this year have been driving me a bit crazy. Every single paper I graded said the same two things: alienation means that work makes you feel bad, and exploitation means your wages are too low. Then they gesture at "species-being" and stop. My supervisor wants a question on the exam that a student literally cannot answer that way -- one where they have to actually do interpretive work on a text and then draw a consequence out of it. I want to anchor the question on this passage from the "Estranged Labour" section of the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844, which I think is the hinge of the whole argument and which none of them ever quote:

"The product of labor is labor which has been embodied in an object, which has become material: it is the objectification of labor. Labor's realization is its objectification. Under these economic conditions this realization of labor appears as loss of realization for the workers; objectification as loss of the object and bondage to it; appropriation as estrangement, as alienation."

So here's what I need from you. Write the exam question itself: it should be answerable in 90 minutes, worth 20 marks, and built so that the passage does real work in it. Specifically I want candidates to have to figure out what the phrase "under these economic conditions" is doing -- i.e., whether Marx is saying that making things and putting yourself into objects is inherently a kind of self-loss, or only under certain social arrangements -- and then to run a test case: a well-paid software engineer with stock options, flexible hours, and a strong sense that her job is meaningful. Is she alienated on the reading the passage supports, or not? That case is exactly the one where the "alienation = feeling bad at work" crowd falls apart, because she's happy, so a student who can't seperate the structural claim from the psychological claim has nowhere to go. The question should also force them to say what this implies for the standard complaint that exploitation is basically wage theft -- does the passage locate the problem in distribution, or somewhere else entirely?

Then give me a model answer of about 600 words that an excellent student might actually write (don't make it a superhuman publishable essay, make it a plausible A), and a marking scheme with exactly five must-hit points at 4 marks each, totalling 20, where for each point you add a one-line note on the specific way a mediocre candidate typically fumbles it. Finally, after all that, summarize the marking scheme as a table with these columns: Point (the name of the must-hit move), Marks (out of 20), What an A answer does (the concrete move in the essay), Typical fumble (the wrong move that caps the answer at a B or below), and Quick marker's check (one thing I can scan the essay for to tell whether they hit the point).
