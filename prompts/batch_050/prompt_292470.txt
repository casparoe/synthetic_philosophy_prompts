I'm a grad student who's been roped into running one 50-minute session of an undergrad 'minds and categories' course, and I've picked Hofstadter's old question: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? The students are smart but have no background in type design or perception beyond intro psych, and all I get is a projector and a whiteboard. Don't explain the concept to me, plan how I should explain it. I want the order of ideas and what has to be in place before what, since I suspect the session falls apart if they meet the 'it's about the role in a writing system, not the shape' point before they've felt the failure of the shape story. For the carrying example I'm tempted to build the whole thing around the double-story printed 'a' versus the single-story handwritten one, since they share almost no parts and yet everyone reads both instantly without noticing there are two of them -- say whether that can carry the whole session, what an example needs to have and to lack to do that job (I think it needs cases where the answer is obvious and cases where it genuinely isn't, like a mirrored 'a' in a kid's writing), and if you think there's a better carrier, what it is. The misconceptions I expect, roughly in order: that 'a' is definable by a checklist of features; that recognition is matching a stored template or some average letter; that style is decoration laid over a plain styleless 'a' underneath (when actually there's no such thing as an 'a' in no style at all); and last that anything shaped like an 'a' is one, even a squiggle on an alien artifact or a corporate logo that never plays the 'a'-role. For each one tell me where in the sequence I should head it off and with what move, since killing the checklist theory too early leaves them with nothing to stand on. Also tell me what to openly refuse to cover at this level -- I'm guessing the whole question of how neural nets actually learn these categories -- and one closing question or tiny task that would show me whether they got it, e.g. having them describe an 'a' that breaks the checklist but is unmistakably an 'a'. And please, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, just continuous prose like you'd write in an email, definately no tables.
