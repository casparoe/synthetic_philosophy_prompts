I am a doctoral student in philosophy working between epistemology and Indian philosophy, and I am drafting the opening chapter of a thesis on testimonial knowledge. Before I engage any particular account — classical or contemporary — I want to fix the standard against which such accounts will be judged. Please write, in British English, the desiderata that any adequate theory of testimony as a source of knowledge (what the Nyāya tradition calls śabda-pramāṇa) must meet, with the case or consideration motivating each requirement.

The specification should cover at least the following, though you should add requirements I have overlooked:

1. The reduction challenge. The theory must state whether testimonial knowledge is irreducibly testimonial or a species of inference, and either way it must handle the standard pressure points: Vaiśeṣika-style arguments that śabda collapses into anumāna, on the ground that the hearer reaches the truth only via the speaker's reliability, and the modern Humean parallel. If the theory resists reduction, it must say what testimonial uptake has that inference lacks, without merely asserting sui generis status and moving on.

2. The reliability constraint without verificationism. The theory must explain how a lying or mistaken speaker defeats knowledge, whilst not requiring the hearer to have independently checked the speaker's competence and sincerity — since requiring that check converts testimony back into inference. State explicitly whether you think these two demands are jointly satisfiable; I suspect this is where most accounts fail, and I want the reasoning, not just a verdict.

3. The trustworthy-speaker condition. The theory must do something principled with the classical characterisation of the āpta — a speaker who possesses direct knowledge of the matter, is competent to communicate it, and is moved to communicate it truthfully — saying which elements are essential, which are idealisations, and what happens to testimonial knowledge when each fails in turn: the competent liar, the sincere incompetent, and the knower who simply misspeaks.

4. Understanding versus knowing. The theory must respect the distinction between grasping a sentence's meaning (śābdabodha) and knowing that what it says is true, including the role of the speaker's intention (tātparya) in fixing the meaning of ambiguous or elliptical utterances, and it must say whether a hearer who misconstrues the utterance but lands on a true belief thereby counts as knowing.

5. Scope. The theory must cover both testimony about matters the hearer could in principle perceive and testimony about matters beyond the hearer's possible experience — the classical case being scripture's claims about dharma and other supersensible objects — and must say whether one mechanism covers both or the category splits into two.

For each desideratum, give the motivating case rather than a bare label, and mark which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which look like the commitments of one camp. Flag any pairs of requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied — if the list itself proves something, that is the part of the exercise I most value.

Conclude with a brief assessment of how three families of account fare against the list: the Nyāya anti-reductionist position, an inferentialist reduction (Vaiśeṣika or Humean), and a contemporary interpersonal or assurance-based view of testimony. Keep this short relative to the specification itself — the criteria are the deliverable, and I will do the detailed scoring in the chapter. Roughly 1,200–1,500 words, prose with a numbered list of desiderata, in British English throughout.
