A friend of mine took a political theory class last semester and was explaining Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument to me over coffee, and honestly his version made it sound way more airtight than I remeber it being. Here's roughly what he said:

Nozick says: start with whatever distribution of wealth you personally think is just — even a perfectly equal one, call it D1. Now suppose a million people each voluntarily pay 25 cents to watch Wilt Chamberlain play basketball. Wilt ends up about $250,000 richer than everyone else, call that D2. Since everyone started out holding what they were entitled to, and every single transaction was fully consensual, there is no point at which injustice could have crept in — so D2 must be just as well. From this Nozick concludes that taxation is basically theft: taking money back out of D2 to fund redistribution is taking what rightfully belongs to people, which he compares to forced labor. And the bigger upshot is that any theory of justice built on a pattern — equality, need, merit, whatever — is simply refuted, because the only way to keep your pattern intact is to constantly stop adults from spending their own money as they please. So egalitarians have to pick between equality and liberty, and Nozick thinks the choice is obvious.

I have a nagging feeling he's smuggling something in around the "everyone was entitled to what they started with" bit, but I can't put my finger on whether that matters. So: how fair is this? Which parts would Nozick actually sign off on, which parts subtly shift what the argument is doing, and which parts are his own views but don't actually come from this argument? And if we fix whatever's wrong with it, does the strong conclusion — patterns refuted, taxation exposed as theft — still stand, or does it shrink into something more modest that an egalitarian could live with?
