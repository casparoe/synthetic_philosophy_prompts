Write a dialogue between two members of a three-person fellowship committee, Mara and Tomasz, in the hour after their final meeting. The committee awards a research fellowship only if the candidate (a) has a strong enough research proposal AND (b) is a good enough teacher — both criteria required. The votes went like this:

- Mara: research strong enough, yes; teaching good enough, no → votes against
- Priya (not present in the dialogue): research no, teaching yes → votes against
- Tomasz: yes on both → votes for

So if you aggregate criterion by criterion, a majority endorses each criterion and the candidate gets the fellowship. If you just count bottom-line verdicts, it's 2-1 against. The dean wants a single decision plus the committee's reasons by Friday.

Tomasz thinks they have to go criterion-by-criterion: those are the criteria the committee was told to apply, and a body whose official position is "her research is strong and her teaching is good, therefore we're rejecting her" is incoherent, and leaves the candidate nothing to appeal or improve on. Mara thinks that's insane: two of three members think this person shouldn't get the money, and premise-voting lets one member's view win while the two no-votes become irrelevant. She'd rather report the verdict and the vote split and skip collective reasons entirely.

Have them actually argue, not perform their positions. Each should land at least one real hit on the other. For instance, Tomasz can press on what the committee could even say to the candidate or the dean under verdict-voting, and Mara can point out that the criterion-majority outcome is fragile — if she'd switched which criterion she failed the candidate on (research instead of teaching) while still voting no overall, the criterion-vote flips the result even though nobody's bottom-line verdict moved. Neither should strawman the other, nobody should win cleanly, and it should end with them locating the actual crux of the disagreement (something like: is a committee a reason-giver or a verdict-counter?) rather than reaching some fake compromise. Naturalistic dialogue, around 500-700 words, interuptions and half-finished sentences are fine.
