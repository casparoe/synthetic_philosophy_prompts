I host a tiny philosophy-adjacent podcast (listenership is mostly the LW/rationalist orbit) and next week I'm interviewing a political philosopher whose new book defends a position she describes as a cleaned-up descendant of Michael Dummett's late work on immigration. Her view in brief: states have a near-absolute duty to admit refugees -- no caps, no deterrence regimes, no offshoring asylum processing -- but for ordinary economic migration, governments may legitimately limit inflows when the scale and speed of arrival would predictibly inflame nativist backlash. She frames that second claim as a duty owed to already-settled immigrant communities, who pay the price of backlash, rather than as a concession to racists.

I've read my Caplan and the trillion-dollar-bills-on-the-sidewalk literature, so no questions at that level. Draft me 8-10 questions that press where the view is actually soft: the heckler's-veto worry (doesn't the cap just hand policy power to the angriest nativists?); whether backlash is endogenous to policy (caps signal that migrants are a threat and thereby manufacture the hostility they cite); whether her refugee/economic line survives real mixed-motive migration; and the internal tension that backlash against refugees is usually the *most* intense, so why doesn't her own principle license turning them away too. For each question, give the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Her standard move is to dodge moral objections by retreating to empirical claims about what actually reduces backlash, so sequence things to close that escape hatch, and end with a question about what evidence would change her mind.
