I am trying to determine whether philosophers have an established name for a problem I keep encountering in debates about consciousness and, where no name exists, I would like a careful coinage rather than a shrug.

The position in the background is the view that experience is a basic feature of matter, present in some minimal form wherever matter is found at all: electrons and quarks enjoy faint glimmers of feeling, and richer minds are somehow built up out of them. I am not asking about that view's plausibility. What I want identified is a specific difficulty that arises for it, and a specific maneuver its defenders make when the difficulty is raised.

The difficulty is not the familiar one of how anything physical could give rise to experience in the first place. On this view, experience is never generated; it is already there, in vast numbers, wherever there is matter. The difficulty concerns what happens next. My brain contains, on this view, an enormous population of tiny subjects or proto-subjects, each with its own minute experience. Yet there is also, apparently, a single subject — me — for whom one unified experience is had. What could make it the case that these many experiences are had together, by one subject, rather than merely existing side by side in the same skull, the way the experiences of the passengers on a bus exist side by side in the same bus? Co-location does not do it. Causal interaction does not obviously do it either: the passengers converse, influence one another, coordinate their plans, and remain many. Something further seems required, and nothing in the view says what it is.

Now the maneuver. When this is pressed, defenders frequently reply that the rival view faces the same problem: the materialist, too, must explain how a hundred billion neurons, none of which is me, constitute a single subject of experience. The discussion then tends to proceed as if the two difficulties were one difficulty under two descriptions, so that neither side is worse off than the other. My suspicion is that this is an error — that the materialist's problem (how experience of any kind comes from matter stipulated to have none) and this view's problem (how experiences that already exist, each of them already someone's, come to be jointly one subject's single experience) differ in structure, and that candidate solutions to one do not transfer to the other. But I may be wrong about that, and I want to be told if I am.

My questions, then, are three. First, does the second problem — the merging or summing of already-existing experiences into one — have an established name, distinct from whatever name the first problem bears, and if so, is the surrounding literature careful about the distinction I have drawn, or does it too tend to run the two together? Second, is there a recognized name for the defensive move of answering a theory's distinctive problem by pointing out that a rival faces a superficially similar one, where the similarity dissolves under scrutiny? Third, if any of these lacks an established name, please propose one, define it precisely, and give cases falling just inside and just outside the definition. In particular, I would like to know whether split-brain cases, in which the unity of a subject appears to come apart, fall inside or outside the scope of the merging problem, and why. I am not interested in a bare label; I want the carving.
