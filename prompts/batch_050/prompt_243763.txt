I am a high school junior and co-president of my school's community service club, and I am writing a short civics paper on how organizations fail. I keep noticing two patterns that everyone around me lumps together as losing sight of the mission, and I want to know whether either one has an established name, and whether treating them as a single phenomenon hides something important.

Pattern one is deliberate steering. Three years ago, our school's coding club was taken over by a group of older students who joined not because they cared about the club's purpose, which was teaching younger kids to code, but because running the club's annual competition looked good on college applications and they wanted control of it. They were competent, they won the officer elections fairly, and within a year the club existed mostly to run a competition that charged an entry fee and looked impressive. In this case there is a clear before and after, and specific people to point at.

Pattern two is my own club, and it is nothing like that. We were founded eleven years ago to tutor kids at the elementary school down the road. Nobody ever took us over. I have read the old minutes, and every individual decision along the way was reasonable: raise a little money to buy the kids supplies, then raise more because supplies got expensive, then enter a regional competition for service clubs because it brought grant money, then spend meetings preparing for that competition because the grant money paid for the supplies. This year we tutor about four hours a month and spend roughly fifteen fundraising or preparing competition materials. The charter never changed. Every officer, including me, sincerely describes us as a tutoring organization. There is no villain, no single moment when anyone chose this, and no one to expel.

My questions:

1. Is there an established name for pattern two specifically, and is it treated anywhere as genuinely distinct from pattern one? Please distinguish it from mission creep, which I understand as an organization adding goals, because our situation feels like replacement without addition: we did not gain a new purpose, we slowly swapped one out while keeping the label. Also distinguish it from deliberate capture. I especially want to know whether anyone has argued that the two patterns require structurally different defenses, since rules designed for bad actors, like election oversight or conflict-of-interest policies, seem useless against pattern two, where everyone involved is acting in good faith.

2. What makes pattern two so hard to detect from the inside? My hypothesis is that because each step is individually defensible, there is never a decision point where someone can say this is where we abandoned our purpose. But that seems to imply the failure has no location in time, and I am not sure a failure can have no location. If that is actually a known feature of this kind of failure, I would like to understand it rather than just gesture at it.

3. Here is the complication my faculty advisor raised. She said the club may simply have adapted: perhaps the grant money and the competition now do more total good than tutoring a handful of kids ever did. If drift can sometimes be an improvement, then drift cannot be defined merely as change away from the founding purpose. Does any established account say what separates failure-drift from healthy evolution, using some criterion other than whether the current members approve of the result? That criterion cannot work, because we approve.

If pattern two turns out to have no established name of its own, please coin one and define it precisely, with boundary cases: what would count as drift versus adaptation, and would a club that drifted, noticed, and then honestly renamed itself still count as having failed?
