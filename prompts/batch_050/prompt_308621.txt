I need advice on a decision I have to make by Friday, and I'd like the reasoning to be rigorous rather than intuitive.

I work as a software engineer on a twelve-person infrastructure team. A vendor whose platform we rely on has announced a one-time bug bounty with unusual terms: a fixed pool of $240,000 will be divided equally among everyone who submits a qualifying vulnerability report by the deadline, no matter how many people submit. (Normally bounties go to the first reporter; here there is no advantage to being first.) Each of us on the team is roughly equally capable of finding a qualifying bug, but doing so would require giving up a weekend, with maybe a 50% chance of success per person.

Here is where I'm stuck. If I am the only one on the team who spends the weekend hunting, my expected payout is large. If all twelve of us hunt, the expected payout per person is well below what a weekend of my time is worth, and we would all have been better off doing nothing. Every one of us can see this. So the ideal outcome seems to be that exactly one or two people hunt and the rest abstain -- but each of us would rather be one of the hunters, and nobody has any private information or special standing that would justify why it should be them rather than someone else. Coordination is impractical, and the vendor's rules prohibit pooling or side payments.

A teammate has proposed a solution I find both compelling and suspicious: each of the twelve of us independently consults a random number generator, calibrated so that each person hunts with a modest probability (adjusted for the 50% success rate), such that in expectation about two people hunt. Nobody has to argue over who deserves the slot, and everyone's expected value is positive.

My questions:

1. Is this randomization scheme actually rational, or does it merely feel rational? It seems to rest on the premise that since we all have identical information and incentives, we should expect to reach identical conclusions -- so the real question is which identical conclusion is best, and randomizing with a tuned probability is the best conclusion available to all of us. But reasoning about my own decision by reasoning about what eleven other people will decide feels circular to me. Is it?

2. Suppose I accept the scheme and my random number generator tells me to abstain. Once I see that result, don't I now have a straightforward incentive to ignore it and hunt anyway, since hunting raises my personal expected payout at the others' expense? If everyone reasons this way, the scheme collapses. Does that show the scheme was never rational, or does it show that the scheme requires a kind of commitment that pure expected-value reasoning cannot supply on its own?

3. One teammate has already publicly announced she is hunting no matter what anyone else does. Does that change the correct probability for the rest of us, and does it change whether I should participate in the scheme at all?

I am not looking for generic career advice. I want to know whether there is a principled framework for decisions like this -- situations where the right move depends on what other symmetrically situated reasoners will do, and where everyone individually doing what is best for them produces an outcome everyone agrees is worse. If such a framework exists, please explain it and apply it to my case concretely, including what probability, if any, I should actually use.
