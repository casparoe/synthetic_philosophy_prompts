I have no academic background in philosophy, but I've been thinking on my own about "griefbots": chatbots trained on a dead person's messages, emails, and voice recordings that let the bereaved keep conversing with them. Imagine two friends who have argued about this for years. One holds that griefbots are a natural extension of what mourners have always done — rereading letters, keeping voicemails, talking to the grave — and that denying the bereaved this comfort is pointlessly cruel. The other holds that a simulation which answers back crosses a line that letters never do: it puts new words in a dead person's mouth, cannot be corrected by them, and tempts the mourner to replace the real person with a pliable fiction. Write the joint statement these two could both sign after a patient, good-faith adversarial collaboration. It should contain: (1) the claims about griefbots, memory, and what can be owed to the dead that both accept — for instance, both might agree that a bot frozen at the moment of death raises different questions than one that keeps learning afterward, and that ordinary memory also distorts; (2) their remaining disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; (3) the cruxes — sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, like whether harming the dead requires them to have surviving interests — and for each crux, a concrete case or observation both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Format: a numbered list of agreed claims, then a table with columns: Disagreement | Side A's wording | Side B's wording | Crux it turns on | Agreed test or case | A's prediction | B's prediction.
