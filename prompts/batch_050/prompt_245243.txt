Having retired last year after four decades in experimental physics, I have joined a local philosophy society whose members are, like me, educated amateurs rather than trained philosophers. I have volunteered to lead our next session on Fitch's paradox of knowability, and I should like a detailed teaching plan rather than the explanation itself. My audience's instinct will be that every truth is in principle knowable, given enough time, instruments, and effort; the result I must land is that this fails on purely logical grounds, via the observation that the particular truth that some fact obtains while nobody knows it cannot itself be known, since knowing that conjunction would require knowing both conjuncts at once, and knowing the first contradicts knowing the second. Please set out the order in which the session's ideas must be introduced and what each depends on (I suspect the audience must first grasp that the claim concerns knowledge in principle, not merely what we happen to know, and must accept that knowledge distributes over conjunction before the proof has any bite); the one example that should carry the argument, stating what it must contain and what it must avoid (a fact about a buried coin or an unobserved event seems apt, but I worry about examples that invite quibbles about verification); the misconceptions my audience will bring, in the order they will voice them -- I anticipate, among others, the objection that this is mere practical limitation, a confused appeal to Gödel's incompleteness theorems as though this were the same sort of result, and the complaint that the knowability principle only fails for artificial self-referential cases -- and indicate at what point in the sequence each should be forestalled; a check I can run at the close of the session to confirm the argument has been understood rather than merely conceded; and what I ought deliberately to leave out at this level, saying so plainly. Please write your answer in British English.
