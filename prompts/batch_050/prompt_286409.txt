I serve on the allocation committee of a small philanthropic fund whose members vote each cycle on which charities receive grants. Two other members, Elena and Marcus, have been arguing for weeks about a proposal Elena made, and I cannot decide who has the better of it. I report their exchange here as neutrally as I can; each numbered step is one move in the dispute.

(1) Elena proposed a pact: she would vote for Marcus's preferred cause (malaria prevention) in this funding cycle if he would vote for hers (cage-free farming campaigns) in the next. Since the committee's votes are otherwise split and both projects repeatedly lose to compromise candidates, she argued, the pact would produce more of what each of them values than either could get alone -- a straightforward case of gains from trade.

(2) Marcus rejected the premise that moral commitments are tradeable goods. Tastes can be bargained over, he said, because trading one taste against another costs the trader nothing but satisfaction; but treating a moral conviction as a bargaining chip converts it into a preference, and if Elena's conviction about factory farming can be traded, it was never the kind of commitment she claimed it was.

(3) Elena replied that Marcus's purity came at a price paid by third parties: on his view, the outcome of refusing the deal is that both malaria prevention and cage-free campaigns receive less funding than they otherwise would, so his refusal is worse even by his own lights. Someone who declines to trade because it feels like selling out, she said, is treating the cleanliness of their own conscience as more important than the outcomes they claim to care about.

(4) Marcus answered that her argument proved too much. The same logic would vindicate moral offsetting -- doing something one believes is wrong while paying to prevent twice as much of it elsewhere -- and would oblige a legislator to trade her vote on a matter of fundamental rights if offered two votes on matters she cared about more. A theory of compromise that cannot distinguish an efficient bargain from complicity in wrongdoing, he concluded, has no principled stopping point.

(5) Elena's final move was to concede the stopping point but deny that it mattered: her pact governed only the allocation of discretionary funding between two causes both members regarded as genuinely good, so the rights case was excluded by the deal's own structure, and nothing in it required either party to act against anything they considered a duty. Vote-trading of exactly this kind, she added, is routine and accepted practice in legislatures.

(6) Marcus responded that appeal to legislative practice cut against her, since logrolling is precisely where vote-trading can be corrupt, and that her description of the support as discretionary relocated the objection rather than answering it: if her vote for her own cause is genuinely discretionary, then her claim that the cause is a moral priority is hollow, and if it is not discretionary, then she is selling something she has no right to sell.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and justify the verdict briefly. Then say who is more right overall, whether Elena's concession in (5) actually saves her proposal or merely narrows it, and what -- if anything -- both parties are missing about the conditions under which compromise between genuine value commitments is legitimate rather than corrupt.
