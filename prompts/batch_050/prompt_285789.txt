I'm preparing to moderate a philosophy department colloquium with the author of a new book defending illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. Her central claim is that there is nothing it is like to be us, and that introspection misrepresents certain brain states as possessing phenomenal properties when in fact they only seem to, where seeming is cashed out entirely in functional and representational terms. She is formidably disciplined, and she has one move that reliably ends exchanges: whenever someone presses her on the fact that the seeming itself surely has to seem some way to us, she says that the question is just the hard problem asserting itself, and that an appearance-reality gap cannot be demanded at every level without begging the question against her. I need a sequence of questions that makes this move progressively more expensive for her to repeat. I'd like the sequence to begin by granting her framework in good faith and asking what, on her own account, an illusion of phenomenality actually consists in, given that an ordinary illusion requires that something appear to a subject and she denies that anything appears to anyone; then to press her on why her functional description of introspective seeming does not merely relabel the datum, since every functional fact she cites, the reports, the discriminations, the pain behavior, could obtain in a creature her view classifies as having no illusion at all, which suggests she has explained our judgments about consciousness rather than the apparent phenomenality that motivated them; then to ask about the asymmetry in her epistemology, since she trusts introspection enough to identify exactly which target needs debunking but not enough to let any of its deliverances stand; then to ask whether her project is the modest one of explaining why we mistakenly believe in phenomenal consciousness or the strong one of showing the seeming is eliminable, and to force her to say which, since a perfect psychological explanation of the mistake that leaves the seeming fully intact would satisfy most illusionists' critics rather than refute them; and finally to end with a question about what possible evidence, experience, or experimental result could count against illusionism, and whether, if the answer is none, the view is a scientific or philosophical prediction at all rather than a stipulation about what must be the case. Please write the questions in the order I should ask them, each with a brief note on the reply I should expect and the follow-up that forecloses it. Write everything as continuous prose, as you would in an email to me, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
