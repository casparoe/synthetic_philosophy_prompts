I'm reading Adam Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments for the first time, and I've hit a wall on the impartial spectator. As I understand the theory, my judgment that an action is right just is my sense that the spectators around me sympathize with it: moral approval is a matter of tracking the sentiments of one's community, and the "spectator" in question is ultimately the public — the people who actually watch us and whose praise or blame we learn to anticipate. But if that's the view, I can't see how Smith can make any sense of moral reformers. When someone in eighteenth-century Britain condemned a practice that virtually every actual spectator in their society approved of, weren't they, by Smith's own standard, simply mistaken? Worse, since actual spectators' sentiments plainly differ across times and places, the theory seems committed to saying that practices like exposing infants really were permissible for the ancient Greeks and really are wrong for us, full stop — which flattens the reformer's claim that her society is getting morality wrong into the empty observation that she is unusual.

So my question is this: does Smith's account give the moral reformer any resources at all, or on his view is "I am right and everyone around me is wrong" a straightforward contradiction? If there are such resources, I want to understand the mechanism precisely — what makes the reformer's solitary judgment better grounded than the crowd's, and how does a spectator who exists nowhere in the flesh acquire authority over the ones who do?
