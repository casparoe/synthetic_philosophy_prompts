I'm a third-year grad student and I've been roped into a working group that's trying to write down a "reference decision theory" for agents that interact with predictors, simulators, and other agents who model them -- think grant-funded, slightly overambitious. Every meeting goes the same way: someone proposes a rule, someone else pulls out a case where the rule does something insane, and we never agree on what the rule was supposed to do in the first place. I want to short-circuit this by getting the requirements list on paper before we argue about candidates again.

So: write the desiderata that any adequate decision theory for these situations has to meet, with the case or consideration behind each one. The cases it must get right should include at least: the transparent-boxes Newcomb problem, Parfit's hitchhiker, counterfactual mugging (the coin comes up against you and Omega asks for $100, having committed to give you $10,000 on the other side of the coin only if you'd have paid in exactly this branch), and XOR blackmail (the blackmailer's letter demanding money is sent if and only if either you have the termites and were predicted not to pay, or you don't have them and were predicted to pay -- so paying looks good given the letter, yet a policy of paying is what attracts the letters). Also include one case I keep bringing up and getting ignored on: an agent offered, before a future Newcomb-like situation, a one-time chance to permanently modify its own decision procedure -- should the desiderata say anything about whether a theory endorses replacing itself with a different theory, or is that out of scope? Beyond cases, I want the structural requirements: what it must not require of the agent (perfect self-prediction? logical omniscience? knowledge of its own source code?), what dynamic story it has to tell (is it choosing actions or choosing policies, and does it have to agree with itself over time), and how it should treat the difference between losing because you were unlucky and losing because your policy predictably loses.

The part I care most about: tell me which desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied -- for instance I have a strong hunch that "never reward an extortionist" and "get the money in counterfactual mugging" pull against each other, and that "match what a rational agent would do on the spot" conflicts with "get the lift in the hitchhiker case" -- and mark each requirement as genuinely non-negotiable versus a preference of one camp that's been laundered into common sense. Then score the usual suspects (CDT, EDT, some FDT-ish functional view, and an updateless/policy-selection view) against the list in a short table -- keep this part brief, the criteria are the deliverable, not the verdicts. If the right conclusion is that no theory currently meets the whole spec, say that plainly rather than rounding some theory up to passing.
