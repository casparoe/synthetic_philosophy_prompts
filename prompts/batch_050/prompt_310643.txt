I'm a science-and-ideas journalist writing a piece on why audiences balk at certain fictional worlds, and I want to pressure-test the philosophy of imaginative resistance against Lolita. The theory, as I understand it: readers happily imagine all kinds of wild stuff for a story -- dragons, time travel, the dead walking around -- but dig in their heels when a fiction seems to flatly assert that something morally awful is actually good, like those toy cases philosophers use where a story just says it was right to kill someone for no reason and readers refuse to go along. The debate is over whether we can't imagine it, or won't, or whether the fiction somehow fails to make it true. Now the episode: Nabokov's novel is narrated by Humbert Humbert, a child abuser who is witty, charming, and endlessly self-justifying, and generations of readers have reported the queasy experiance of catching themselves half-seduced and then recoiling; critics still argue over whether the book seduces the reader or springs a moral trap on them. What I want is the analysis run in both directions. First: what exactly is the novel asking us to imagine, and where does the resistance actually kick in -- is it resistance to a moral claim, to a narrator, to a perspective we're being asked to occupy? Does Lolita even fit the standard puzzle, given that Humbert is unreliable and the fiction itself never asserts his crimes are okay, or is it a different animal altogether? Second: what does the case do to the theory -- does it vindicate the "won't, not can't" camp, or does it suggest the whole debate was built on one-line toy cases that real fiction never resembles? And be honest about where the theory is just silent and I'd be forcing it to say something.
