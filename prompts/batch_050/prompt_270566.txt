Ok so I build anomaly detection models for industrial pumps (vibration, temperature, pressure, the usual), and I've been reading philosophy of science stuff about mechanisms on the side, and I've cooked up an argument that seems to show my whole job is impossible, which is annoying becuase my job demonstratably exists. Walk me out of it.

(1) Anomaly detection for a system means: comparing the system's current behavior against the recorded behavior of the same mechanism at an earlier time (the baseline), and flagging deviations that aren't just noise.
(2) A mechanism is individuated by its component parts, the activities those parts perform, and how they're organized — that's basically the standard definition I keep finding.
(3) The parts of any real mechanism turn over: seals get replaced, bearings get swapped, and even without maintenance the metal fatigues and material diffuses around; in a living cell it's worse, the proteins are constantly degraded and resynthesized.
(4) So after enough turnover, the mechanism in front of you is numerically a different mechanism from the one you took the baseline on.
(5) Every anomaly detection run happens after some turnover.
(6) So every run compares mechanism A's current behavior against mechanism B's baseline, i.e. against a different mechanism. Any deviation you flag might just be the difference between two distinct mechanisms, so every flag is a false positive. Anomaly detection is impossible.
(7) But anomaly detection works — we catch bearing failures weeks early, and oncologists detect tumors in bodies whose cells have largely turned over since the last scan. So something in (1)-(6) has to give.

I feel the squeeze is on (4) or on (1) but I can't pin it down. Which step do you reject, why does it look convincing anyway, and what does rejecting it cost me? Specifically: if I say mechanisms survive part replacement, am I committed to saying a pump whose every part has been swapped is still the same pump (Ship of Theseus style), and if I deny that, am I just back at (6)? And is "the same mechanism" in my baseline claim really a token-identity claim or a type-identity claim — does anomaly detection even need the token, or does rejecting (1) that way prove too little and make it impossible to catch gradual degradation within a type? I'd rather keep (2) if possible since it seems to be the actual consensus definition, but tell me what I'm paying.
