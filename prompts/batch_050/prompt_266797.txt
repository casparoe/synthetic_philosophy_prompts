I retired from a career in condensed matter physics about six years ago, got bored of gardening within a year, and have been falling down philosophy rabbit holes ever since. The latest thing is that I ended up on the grants board of a small private research foundation (a former postdoc of mine roped me in), and we've run into a genuine fight. One of the younger trustees, an economist, has proposed we stop deciding between our two big fellowship programmes by committee deliberation and instead run what he calls decision markets: open betting markets on some measurable success metric -- say, the five-year publication and citation record of the fellows we fund -- conditional on us picking programme A, and separately conditional on picking programme B, and then we just fund whichever one the market says will do better. His argument is that markets aggregate dispersed information better than any panel of middle-aged academics ever will. Half the board thinks this is brilliant, the other half thinks letting anonymous gamblers steer a science foundation is somewhere between reckless and insane.

I've been asked to draft the decision memo we'll vote on next month, and I'd like your help writing it. It needs a clear bottom line: adopt the proposal, pilot a limited version of it, or reject it, and whichever I choose it has to actually engage the strongest argument on the losing side, not just wave at it. A few things I specifically want the memo to work through, because this is where the philosophy comes in as far as I can tell. First, the epistemic case for markets presumably only holds when the bettors actually know something the committee doesn't -- so which parts of our decision are plausibly like that, and which parts are just us pretending a market can conjure information nobody has? Second, there's a difference between predicting an outcome and deciding what's worth wanting: citations are the measurable proxy, but what we actually care about is good science, and I've read enough by now to be suspicious of letting a proxy do the steering. Third, the legitimacy worry -- even if the market is right more often, does something go wrong when a foundation hands a values-laden decision over to whoever is willing to put money on it? And practically, with a fairly thin market, what stops an interested party from moving the price?

Write it as a memo a board could actually act on -- recommendation up front, reasoning after, maybe with conditions attached if I go the pilot route. Keep in mind I'm the philosophy amateur on a board of hard-nosed scientists, so the memo shouldn't lean on jargon I can't defend in the room. Aim for something I could plausibly pass off as my own thinking at the next meeting, typos and all.
