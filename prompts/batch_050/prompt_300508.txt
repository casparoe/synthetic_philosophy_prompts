Last night I had a long and unresolved argument with a close friend about the epistemic value of thought experiments in philosophy. She defended the standard diet — runaway trolleys, twin planets where the water-like substance is XYZ, famous violinists hooked up to strangers' kidneys, brains in vats — as legitimate instruments for testing theories against our intuitions. I found myself pressing the suspicion that many of these devices work less like experiments than like machines engineered to deliver a verdict: the controversial premise is smuggled into the framing, the scenario is stripped of every detail except the ones that push toward the intended answer, and the reader is asked for a judgment before being given anything like an argument. She conceded that some thought experiments are rigged but insisted that rigging is an abuse, not the nature of the genre. We parted without settling it.

To make my side of the case in a form she might actually enjoy, I would like you to write a parody: the instruction-and-safety manual for a consumer product called an "intuition pump," of the kind philosophy departments supposedly purchase to manufacture intuitions on demand. Aim for roughly 450 to 550 words, in a deadpan regulatory-technical voice throughout; the manual must never acknowledge that anything is amiss, and the comedy should come entirely from its describing, in neutral product-documentation language, practices that are genuine, recognizable features of how philosophical thought experiments persuade. The jokes should reward a reader who knows the territory: factory-set "knobs" the operator is advised never to adjust (varying the scenario's details may void the warranty); a normative premise pre-installed inside the casing so that it is mistaken for part of the setup; calibration procedures ensuring one verdict feels compulsory; a warning against running the pump on nearby variants of the approved scenario; troubleshooting advice for when a reader's intuition fails to match the intended output (re-narrate the case with more vivid detail on the approved side); and a maintenance schedule for keeping the intuition fresh against contrary argument. You may gesture at well-known pumps — trolleys, twin earths, vats — but keep the references oblique enough to fit the manual's voice.

Since a satire has no verdict to issue, do the nearest equivalent of flagging your epistemic commitments: after the manual, add two or three sentences distinguishing where the piece rests on something fairly uncontroversial — that framing, ordering, and presentation affect the intuitions a case elicits — from where it takes a side in the genuinely contested question of whether intuitions are evidence at all, and be careful not to present that more skeptical side as the consensus among philosophers who work on this.

One constraint on form: no headings, no numbered warnings, no bold text, no bullet points, no tables. Continuous prose only, as though the manual had been transcribed into a letter, with the deadpan surviving the loss of formatting.
