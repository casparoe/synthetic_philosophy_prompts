A small private foundation has earmarked funding for three postdoctoral researchers, three years each, to work exclusively on conceptual problems surrounding luminosity -- roughly, whether one's current mental states are always such that one is in a position to know that one is in them -- and the KK principle, the claim that knowing a proposition requires, or at least puts one in a position for, knowing that one knows it. The trustees accept that margin-for-error arguments of the sort associated with Williamson, together with familiar failures of KK in iterated and borderline cases, have shifted the burden of argument, but they hold no views about what should come next. They want a defended agenda, not a survey of the literature.

Draft the case you would put to them. Which open questions in this territory most deserve those three positions, and in what order? Consider, among others you may add or discard: whether margin-for-error reasoning generalizes from perceptual belief to rational credence, phenomenal states, and normative or evaluative belief, or whether these cases resist the argument in instructive ways; what, if anything, the failure of KK does to accounts of assertion, evidence, and justification that tacitly assume agents can track their own epistemic standing; whether anti-luminosity's reliance on a safety condition imports a contentious modal epistemology that could be attacked on independent grounds; and how the single-agent results bear on multi-agent settings, where coordination and convention are routinely analyzed in terms of arbitrarily deep iterations of knowledge.

For each item on your list, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers working without empirical resources, and what development over the next few years -- a theorem, a counterexample, a formal model, an application -- would move it up or down the ranking. Close by identifying one implication commonly drawn from this literature that you take to be overrated, and explain why it does not in fact follow.
