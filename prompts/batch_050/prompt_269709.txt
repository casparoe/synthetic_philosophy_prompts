Two friends of mine were arguing last weekend about a charity they both donate to. The charity can spend its budget in one of two ways: distribute medicine that modestly improves the lives of ten million people who already exist, or fund a program that would result in two million additional children being born into decent but difficult lives — lives clearly worth living, but much harder than the lives the medicine would touch. Mark said the choice is easy: two million new worthwhile lives contain more total well-being than a modest improvement spread over ten million existing ones, so the births should be funded. Elena said that's a mistake: you don't make the world better by adding people to it, only by making things better for people, and a smaller world of well-off people is preferable to a crowded world of people who are only just glad to be alive. Mark replied that her principle has a disturbing consequence of its own: it implies a world containing a single ecstatic person could be better than a world of a billion very happy people, and nobody actually believes that. Elena countered that his principle has a worse one: taken seriously, it says the best world is always the biggest world — trillions of people living lives barely above the point where existence stops being worth having — and she finds that conclusion appalling. Mark said appalling isn't an argument, and that enormous numbers reliably feel bad to minds built for small ones; Elena said that's not an argument either, and there they stalled. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: who is right about each specific point, what both of them are missing, and whether a dispute like this can be settled just by comparing the embarrassing consequences of each principle, or whether something else has to do the deciding.
