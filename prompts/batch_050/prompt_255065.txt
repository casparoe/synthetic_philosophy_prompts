I lead a small safety-engineering team at a robotics company, and we've started putting reinforcement-learning agents into shared spaces — warehouse floor robots for now, a household prototype next year. Our leadership has latched onto the phrase "low impact" from the AI safety literature and now wants it in our deployment sign-off documents, but when I sat down to write the criterion I realised I couldn't say what it commits us to. We don't have anything like a full specification of what we value about the world the robot operates in, and everyone I ask gives me a different intuition: one engineer says low impact means the world looks roughly the same afterwards, another says it means the robot shouldn't foreclose options, a third says it means nothing happens that we'd later have to undo. I've read enough to know these come apart — a robot could keep the warehouse looking identical by frantically compensating for its own messes, or preserve the state while quietly making everyone worse off — but I don't know how to turn any of this into something an engineer can apply to a proposed reward penalty in a design review.

Please write me a decision procedure we can run, in under an hour, on any proposed "impact penalty" before it goes into a reward function. I want the questions to ask in order: what the penalty is actually measuring (states, action effects, other agents' future options), what baseline it's comparing against and what happens when there is no neutral baseline, whether it can be gamed by the agent offsetting or pre-empting its own influence, and what it does when the task itself requires large changes. For each question, say what counts as a passing answer, what a red flag looks like, and where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to a human because the question is really about values rather than measurement. Be explicit about which parts are genuine checks and which are judgement calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on two concrete proposals from our current queue: first, a warehouse robot penalised for reducing the future attainability of a set of hand-picked "default" outcomes (a tidied floor, intact shelving); second, a household robot allowed only actions from a pre-approved whitelist, expanded by a human supervisor on request. Ideally one of these should come out looking better than my team currently assumes, and one worse — I want the procedure to bite, not to ratify what we already believe. Finish with a short section on what no such procedure can settle, so I can quote it back at leadership when they ask for a guarantee.
