I've been rereading Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals" (Book I, chapter 30 of the Essays, in the old public-domain Cotton translation) and something about it has been nagging at me. The essay opens with the Pyrrhus anecdote and then Montaigne announces his method: "By which it appears how cautious men ought to be of taking things upon trust from vulgar opinion, and that we are to judge by the eye of reason, and not from common report." But then, barely a paragraph later, his entire account of the Tupinambá of Brazil rests on hearsay -- he writes "I long had a man in my house that lived ten or twelve years in the New World, discovered in these latter days" -- and everything that follows, including the famous payoff where the cannibals who eat only the already-dead come off looking less barbarous than Montaigne's own countrymen (who, in the religious wars raging around him as he wrote, tortured and roasted living people in the name of piety), is built on that servant's report.

Here's my question, and it's about the essay as a practice of judgment, a method that people still imitate constantly today whenever they deflect criticism of some foreign custom by pointing at something worse back home. What theory of cross-cultural judgment does this method presuppose? Because it looks to me like there's a real tension, maybe a flat contradiction. To rank two cultures' cruelties against each other you need a standard that belongs to neither culture, and Montaigne tells us the standard is reason, not custom. But this essay is routinely read as a founding text of cultural relativism, and relativism says precisely that there is no standpoint outside custom from which customs can be ranked. Worse, if custom really is the measure, then French judicial torture is simply the French custom, and a Frenchman condemning it is in exactly the same position as a European condemning cannibalism -- judging by his own parochial lights. So either Montaigne is no relativist and the eye of reason is doing real universalist work, or he is one and the essay's most famous move is illegitimate by his own lights.

Reconstruct for me the implicit theory the essay's procedure actually needs: does the comparative ranking strictly presuppose a culture-transcendent standard, or could a consistent relativist still run the same move (as immanent critique, say, or as therapy against one's own smugness rather than as ranking)? What does the reliance on the servant's testimony do to the stated principle of judging by reason rather than report -- is there a coherent epistemology of testimony buried here, or is it just a cheat he hopes we wont notice? And then tell me what each purified version of the essay would lose: what has to go if Montaigne commits to being a thoroughgoing relativist, and what has to go if he commits to reason as a universal tribunal? I have my own guess but I'd rather hear yours argued out.
