I am a novelist by trade, currently at work on a book whose central character is a fastidious magistrate haunted by the suspicion that none of his past rulings ever genuinely followed from the statutes he believed he was applying — that any of his verdicts can, with enough ingenuity, be redescribed as conforming to some rule or other. Because I also teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of language, I want to convert this obsession into a take-home essay question on Kripkenstein's rule-following paradox — one that students cannot answer by reciting the plus/quus example, summarizing the skeptical solution, and declaring Wittgenstein the winner. The question should force them into the crux that matters to me as a writer: the paradox purports to show that no fact about a speaker — not dispositions, not mental images, not the entirety of past usage — fixes what they meant by a sign. I want students to test whether that conclusion, if sound, also collapses the distinction between interpreting a text (or a statute) charitably and simply inventing its meaning, and whether the skeptical solution's appeal to communal practice and assertibility conditions actually restores a workable notion of correctness — and if so, at what cost to the intuition that an author or lawmaker can mean something her entire community misreads. A complete answer must present the dispositionalist response and engage at least one of the standard objections to it (the finitude problem or the normativity/mistake problem), and it must take a stand on whether the paradox is a genuine skeptical threat or instead a reductio of the picture of meaning-as-inner-fact that the skeptic assumes.

Please write the assessment materials as exactly five numbered items, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the essay question itself, phrased as it would appear on the exam paper and answerable in roughly two hours of writing; (2) a model answer of about 500–600 words that does everything the question demands; (3) a marking scheme out of 20 marks, organized around five must-hit moves with the marks each carries; (4) a short note identifying the specific move that separates an A answer from a B answer — the move a student makes only if they actually understand why the paradox is supposed to be paradoxical rather than merely skeptical in the ordinary way; (5) a short note on the single most common way students mishandle this material (for instance, treating the skeptical solution as straightforward relativism about meaning, or treating quus-like hypotheses as empirical doubts about memory) and how the marking scheme is designed to catch and penalize exactly that error.
