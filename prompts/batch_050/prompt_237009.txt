I got into stoicism a couple of years ago through the usual route (Holiday, then Pigliucci, then a Daily Stoic journal I abandoned in March), and one line keeps showing up absolutely everywhere: "focus on what you can control." It gets deployed for everything -- my mate used it when I was anxious about a visa decision, a podcast host used it to explain why we shouldn't doomscroll about politics, and my therapist-adjacent coworker used it to argue I shouldn't feel guilty about a project failing at work. But the more it's said the less I think it means any one thing. Sometimes it sounds like an empirical claim (you literally cannot affect most outcomes), sometimes like a decision rule (only spend effort where your effort changes things), sometimes like a claim about what deserves emotional weight (you should only *feel* bad about what was up to you), and sometimes almost like a metaphysical claim that there's a sharp line between what's "in our power" and what isn't -- which seems obviously false, since almost everything interesting (my health, my reputation, whether the project ships) is partly up to me and partly not. And occasionally I suspect people mean something much weaker, like "worrying without acting is a waste," which is a platitude nobody would deny.

So what I'd like is a disambiguation of that one slogan. Lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing -- I suspect there are five or six, from the truism up to the strong Stoic claim about externals being "indifferent" -- and for each one, give a verdict on whether it's trivial, substantive-and-defensible, or just false, and say which readings would actually justify the uses I listed above (the visa anxiety, the politics doomscrolling, the guilt about the failed project). I'm also curious whether the strong readings survive the obvious objection that control comes in degrees, or whether the slogan only really works in its weak, boring form. If you think my framing here is confused somewhere -- e.g. if I've conflated two things that need separating, or the slogan just isn't doing argumentative work at all -- say so plainly rather than politely working around it.

Format-wise: please give the menu of readings as a table with four columns -- "Reading" (a one-sentence precise statement of the thesis), "Verdict" (platitude / substantive-defensible / false, with a sentence of justification), "Degree-of-control objection" (does the partly-up-to-us problem sink it, weaken it, or miss it), and "Supports which uses" (which of my three cases it would actually licence, if any). A short paragraph after the table on what modern Stoic popularisers probably mean when they say it would be great. And British English throughout, please.
