You are a professional epistemologist with particular expertise in modal and tracking theories of knowledge (Dretske, Nozick, and their critics). Please think carefully and work through this systematically: begin by brainstorming at least three candidate principles that might unify my verdicts, test each one against every case I list, and only then tell me which principle survives, what it predicts for cases I haven't considered, and which of my verdicts I have to abandon.

Last night I had a long, inconclusive argument with a friend about skepticism and epistemic closure. She is a staunch defender of closure: if you know something and competently deduce a consequence of it, you thereby know the consequence. I found myself denying this, and she challenged me to state the principle behind my denials. Here are my verdicts on the cases we argued over, reported as honestly as I can manage:

1. Looking at my hands in normal light, I know that I have hands. Yes.
2. From 'I have hands' I can validly deduce 'I am not a handless brain in a vat,' but performing that deduction does not give me knowledge that I am not one. No knowledge gained.
3. At the zoo, looking into the zebra enclosure, I know the animals are zebras — and I also know they are not mules painted to look like zebras. Reputable zoos don't do that, and it would be paranoid to demand that I check. Yes to both. My friend insists this pair is inconsistent with verdict 2, since 'zebra, therefore not a painted mule' is exactly as good an inference as 'hands, therefore not a brain in a vat.' I feel the cases differ, but I cannot articulate how.
4. Henry is driving through countryside full of convincing barn facades, glances at the one genuine barn, and judges 'that's a barn.' He does not know. In ordinary countryside, the very same glance yields knowledge. No in the first setting, yes in the second.
5. Holding one ticket in a fair million-ticket lottery before the draw, I do not know that my ticket will lose, however good the odds. Yet I do know that I won't be able to afford a yacht this year, even though winning that very lottery is just about the only thing that would make that false. No to the first, yes to the second — and I admit these sit awkwardly together.
6. A grandmother visits her grandson, sees him romping about, and concludes he is well. She knows this, even though the family is resolved that were he gravely ill they would tell her he is fine, to spare her feelings. But if she had merely been told on the phone by that same family that he is well, she would not know. Yes in the visit case, no in the phone case.

My tentative principle, offered in the heat of the argument, was sensitivity: S knows that p only if, were p false, S would not believe that p. My friend replied that this principle is straightforwardly refuted by verdict 3, and that if I relativize belief to a 'method' in order to save it, I can individuate methods so finely or so coarsely that the principle can be made to say whatever I want.

Here is what I would like from you. First, state sensitivity — or whatever principle you ultimately settle on — precisely, including whatever truth, belief, and method clauses it requires, and identify which of my verdicts each clause is doing the work to capture. Second, tell me honestly which of my six verdicts the principle cannot accommodate; I suspect at least half of one of them has to go, and I would rather know which than be flattered. Third, say whether some closure-preserving rival — a 'nearby possible worlds' safety principle, or a relevant-alternatives principle — saves a different and on balance better subset of my verdicts, and lay out the trade-off plainly. Finally, give me your principle's predictions for at least two cases I did not list: whether I know the time by glancing at a stopped clock that happens to be showing the right hour, and whether deduction extends knowledge for 'lightweight' consequences (from 'I was born in 1990' to 'I was born before 2000') even if it fails for heavyweight anti-skeptical ones.
