I am writing the final exam for a master's-level course in research ethics, and I need one essay question on clinical equipoise that students can answer in 75 minutes. Most of my students can already recite the standard definition — genuine uncertainty within the expert medical community about the comparative merits of the trial arms — and can explain why Freedman proposed community-level equipoise to replace the older requirement that each individual physician be personally uncertain. I want a question that cannot be answered by reciting any of that.

Please build the question around the following scenario, which you should present to candidates in two or three sentences: an adaptive platform trial comparing several treatments for a serious illness, in which the randomization algorithm is response-adaptive — as outcome data accumulate, new patients are assigned with progressively higher probability to the arms that currently look best, and arms that fall sufficiently far behind are dropped entirely. The investigators defend the design on two grounds: it reduces the number of participants assigned to inferior treatments, and at every point in the trial the community retains at least some residual uncertainty about which arm is best, since the evidence is never conclusive when a reweighting or dropping decision is made.

The exam question should force candidates to confront the crux: if equipoise is what morally licenses randomization, then a design engineered to exploit accumulating evidence against equipoise seems to spend down its own justification as it proceeds — so either response-adaptive randomization is a reductio of equipoise as the licensing condition, or equipoise was never really doing the licensing work people attributed to it, or the tension dissolves on a more careful formulation. The question should push candidates to say which of these they think is right and why, and to reckon with what the licensing condition for randomization would have to be if it is not equipoise (for instance, whether it could rest instead on the net expected benefit to participants, on the social value of the knowledge produced, or on some combination).

Deliver three things:

1. The exam question itself, worded as it would appear on the paper, including the scenario, the specific task, and any explicit instructions about scope.
2. A model answer of roughly 600 words, written to the standard of the best student you have taught.
3. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, stating how many marks each is worth out of 20 total, and for each point a one-line note on the characteristic way weaker candidates fumble it — the specific mistake or omission that should cost them the marks, not generic complaints about vagueness or poor structure.
