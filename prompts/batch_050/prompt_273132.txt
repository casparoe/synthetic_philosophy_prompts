Before starting my PhD I spent six years as a literary translator, and I still keep a foot in that world, which is how I got myself into this: a friend who leads a research group at a machine-translation company has invited me to speak in their internal seminar series, and I have agreed, possibly against my better judgment, to defend the Hofstadter-style claim that large-scale statistical translation systems, including the current generation built on language models, do not understand what they translate, and that this is a structural limitation rather than a temporary one. My talk has two moving parts. The first is what I call the concentration argument: because these systems translate by exploiting distributional regularities, their failures should be expected to cluster precisely on stylistically marked, ambiguous, or genuinely novel passages, which are exactly the passages where statistical evidence is thinnest and where recovering what the author meant matters most -- so improvements in average-case evaluation scores systematically overstate progress toward anything like comprehension, and the better the average gets, the more the remaining error concentrates where it costs the most. The second is the premise underneath it, roughly Gricean: translation is the recovery of intended meaning under constraints of style and voice, and there is a real difference between modeling an author's communicative intentions and modeling a distribution over texts, even in the many cases where the two produce identical output.

The audience will be about twenty people: mostly research engineers and applied scientists who have spent their careers making these systems better and have, in several cases, personally shipped the models I will be discussing, plus two or three computational linguists and the head of product, who will be listening for what my claim implies about how the system is marketed. None of them has patience for the Chinese Room, which they regard as intuition-mongering, and I suspect at least a few quietly believe that human translators, including my former self, are doing something fundamentally statistical as well. Prepare me for the question period. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience -- not the ones a philosophy colloquium would raise -- ordered by how much damage each does to the talk if I handle it badly, phrased the way an engineer would genuinely put it, in their vocabulary and with the kinds of examples they would reach for. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, a candid note on where that reply is soft, and what I should concede up front rather than burn credibility fighting over. Finally, flag the objection I am hoping nobody raises -- the one that goes after the Gricean premise itself, by pressing whether there is any determinate intended meaning for the system to fail to recover -- and tell me honestly whether my best two-minute reply to it would survive a competent follow-up.
