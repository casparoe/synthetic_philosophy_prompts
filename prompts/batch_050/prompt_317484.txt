The passage people point to is in Book II, chapter 3 (the one on whether the general will can err), where Rousseau says the will of all is just the sum of private wills, but if you take away the pluses and minuses that cancel each other out, what's left -- the sum of the differences -- is the general will. And then he adds that if the people deliberated with adequate information and citizens had no communication with each other, you'd always get the general will out of all the little differences, whereas once factions and parties form, you stop getting it.

Here's what I want. Restate the general will doctrine, as it shows up in that passage and the surrounding argument, in the vocabulary I actually work in: the general will as an estimand, each citizen's vote as a noisy measurement of it, private interest as idiosyncratic error, the no-communication clause as an independence assumption, factions as correlated errors or maybe cluster structure, majority rule as the aggregation step. Be as precise as you can -- e.g., does "the pluses and minuses cancel" commit him to errors being mean-zero, or symmetric, or additive at all? Is the no-communication condition really statistical independence or is it doing some other job? Is the estimand even well-defined before you aggregate, and does that matter?

What in Rousseau's position survives the translation cleanly, what only survives if you fudge it, and what has no expression at all in estimation vocabulary -- I'm guessing stuff like the general will being something each citizen has rather than something the electorate measures, the bit about being forced to be free, maybe the whole question of whether the general will can be wrong. For each piece of residue, tell me whether it points to something my framework genuinely can't say, or to something Rousseau was saying less clearly than he thought -- i.e., whether the statistical reading exposes a real limitation of statistical vocabulary for politics, or exposes that parts of the doctrine were never a claim about aggregation in the first place.
