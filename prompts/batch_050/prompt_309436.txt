Design a classroom activity for a university reading group on effective altruism and moral uncertainty (8–10 participants, 80 minutes) that lets participants experience the promise and the fragility of moral trade -- the idea that people with different moral priorities can sometimes both get more of what they value by trading concessions across issues, rather than by compromising fifty-fifty on each issue separately. The activity should proceed roughly as follows. In a first phase, the group is split into two "foundations" with genuinely different values (give each a private charter: e.g., one ranks preventing animal suffering far above everything else, the other ranks preserving cultural institutions far above everything else, with middling weights on a few other causes) and must jointly allocate a fixed budget across five funding areas by negotiating a split on each area independently. In a second phase, allow them instead to trade whole funding areas ("you decide this one, we decide that one") so they discover that bundling issues lets both sides do better by their own lights than issue-by-issue compromise did. Then, in a third phase, inject two complications: (a) give each foundation a private "intensity card" stating how much it actually cares about each area, hidden from the other side, with an explicit incentive to exaggerate, so participants run into the verification problem -- trade works only if you can trust the other side's reported priorities; and (b) introduce one funding area where each charter describes the other side's preferred use of money as not merely wasteful but positively wrong (e.g., one side sees funding a particular institution as complicity in harm), so participants feel the pull of the objection that some things should not be treated as bargaining chips no matter what is offered in return. Specify the concrete materials needed (charters, intensity cards, a scoring sheet where each side tallies its own satisfaction by its own weights), a minute-by-minute timeline, and the exact rules for what may and may not be communicated between foundations in each phase. The debrief should map each phase onto the corresponding feature of the idea (gains from trade across issues versus compromise within an issue; the epistemic and strategic problems of trusting stated values; the complicity objection and the worry that moral trade treats convictions as currency), ask participants to identify the moment they first considered misreporting their intensities and what that implies for real-world moral trade between strangers, anticipate what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run (e.g., participants ignoring their charters and playing to "win" a shared scoreboard -- say how the facilitator should prevent this), and state frankly which features of real moral trade the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply in discussion (e.g., that real counterparties cannot be relied on to honor deals over time, that real moral stakes are not points, and that real trades are often proposed between parties who suspect each other's values are corrupt, not merely different).
