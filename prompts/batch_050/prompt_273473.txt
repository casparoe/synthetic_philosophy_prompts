I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently drafting a long feature on the philosophy of time travel for an audience that knows the grandfather paradox from films but has never encountered the literature on it. In discussions with a philosopher colleague I keep hitting the same snag. The standard move against the paradox -- the Novikov-style consistency constraint, the claim that a traveller on a single timeline simply cannot kill his grandfather because, as a matter of fact, he did not -- always gets illustrated with coincidences: the gun jams, the traveller slips on a banana peel, he mistakes the wrong man. My colleague says this is a rhetorical failure, because it makes consistency look like a run of flukes rather than a structural feature of the scenario, and readers come away thinking the paradox was merely postponed. I want a case that does the job properly, and I would like you to construct it to order.

Here are the specifications. Build a thought experiment in which a time traveller attempts to kill his own grandfather and fails, on a single timeline with no branching, no parallel worlds, no quantum mechanics, and no appeal to a cosmic censor or chronology-protection agency -- but where the failure is guaranteed by something other than luck or accumulated improbable accidents. The guarantee should be diegetic: a character inside the story who understood the setup could in principle see, in advance, why the attempt must fail, without being told that the timeline is consistent. Ideally the mechanism should be something like a self-undermining or self-certifying loop in the information available to the characters, rather than a physical obstacle. Keep the scenario to one or two paragraphs, set it in an ordinary world (a village, a family, a recognisable decade), and do not smuggle the conclusion in by stipulation: the traveller must be genuinely free to pull the trigger in every sense that matters to a compatibilist, and the grandfather must genuinely be his grandfather.

After the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the brief: walk through the attempted killing step by step and show why failure is overdetermined or structurally required rather than merely probable. Then do the philosophical work. State which feature of the case is doing the heavy lifting, and say what happens to the story if that feature is removed -- does it collapse back into a banana-peel narrative, or into outright contradiction? Explain what the case shows, if anything, about whether the grandfather paradox is a genuine contradiction, a constraint on consistent stories, or merely an epistemic puzzle about what the traveller can know. Finally, steelman the opposition twice over: first, say how a defender of the impossibility of time travel would attack the case (I expect the charge to be that the guarantee smuggles in the very determinism or fatalism the scenario pretends to avoid), and reply to that charge; second, say how a many-worlds theorist would complain that the single-timeline constraint makes the exercise idle, and whether you think that complaint has force.
