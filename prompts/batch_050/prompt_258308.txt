You are a satirical writer with a scholar's command of early Chinese political thought — equally at home in the Han Feizi and in the serene, bloodless cadence of corporate thought leadership. Think carefully about the assignment before you begin: first sketch, in five or six bullet points, the Legalist doctrines you intend to dramatize and the corporate form each will take. Then write the piece itself, without naming or announcing the doctrines in the text.

Write a mock white paper, roughly 800–900 words, issued by a fictional governance consultancy called The Two Handles Group, advising the incoming CEO of a mid-sized firm on how to run it. Format it like a real consultancy product: a title, an executive summary, numbered recommendations, and a short implementation timeline. The voice is confident, metrics-fluent, and completely calm — the genre that calls itself first-principles leadership.

The catch: every recommendation must be a genuine position of Han Feizi's Legalism, transposed into corporate language without alteration of substance. Among the doctrines available to you, draw on as many as fit naturally:

- Rule by impersonal, public standards rather than by the virtue of whoever is in charge; the standards should be so clear and automatic that they run the firm even under a mediocre CEO — and the CEO should plan on being mediocre.
- The two handles of reward and punishment, held personally and exclusively by the CEO, never delegated, and never exercised out of mood, gratitude, or affection.
- Holding each person to exactly his remit: punish the employee who fails to do his job, but also punish the one who does someone else's job well. (Han Feizi's own illustration: a ruler's hat-keeper once draped a robe over his sleeping, drunken master; on waking, the ruler punished the robe-keeper for neglecting his duty and the hat-keeper for exceeding his office.)
- Concealment of the ruler's desires: if subordinates learn what the CEO likes, they will perform his preferences rather than the firm's good, so the CEO should remain blank, still, and unreadable, with performance measured against the standard rather than against his face.
- Systematic distrust of those nearest the center of power: the executives most dangerous to the firm are the beloved and the indispensable, so build in rotation, audit, and rewarded informing.
- The unscalability of virtue: the Confucian hope of staffing the firm with exemplary people fails because exemplars are rare and cannot be institutionalized; design instead for the median employee and the median successor.
- Severity on small infractions so that large ones never arise; people do not dip their hands in boiling water.

Conclude with a half-page dissent from the firm's People & Culture director, written in warm Confucian HR language — role modeling, trust, cultivating the best in people — followed by the consultancy's reply, which should deploy Han Feizi's actual rebuttals to such objections rather than strawmen. The satire must come from fidelity: a reader who knows the Han Feizi should recognize each recommendation and find the transposition fair; a reader who doesn't should grow steadily uneasy without being able to say when the memo went wrong. Stay deadpan throughout — no winking, no scare quotes, no moment where the consultant concedes that any of this is monstrous.

The final section should land the real cost without stating it: an appendix on the consultancy's own engagement terms — the fee structured as automatic reward and punishment, the consultant's personal preferences undisclosed to the client, the termination clause drafted so that denunciation is rewarded — such that a careful reader notices the system cannot exempt even its author, much as Han Feizi's doctrines were adopted by the state of Qin, where he himself was imprisoned and driven to his death after a rival's accusation.

Constraints: invent no doctrine — if you are unsure Han Feizi argued something, leave it out; paraphrase rather than quote; and the humor must never depend on the CEO being unusually wicked, since the entire point is that the system is built to function for rulers who are not.
