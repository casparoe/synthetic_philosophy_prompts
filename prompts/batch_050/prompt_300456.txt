I am a novelist — fourteen years of published fiction — currently at work on a book whose second narrator is an artificial intelligence, and the project has forced me to articulate commitments I had previously left comfortably vague. I would like them audited for joint tenability before I embarrass myself in the craft essay my publisher has commissioned. Here is where I stand: (1) Belief attribution is a stance, not a report of inner machinery: in what I take to be Dennett's sense, a system believes that p just in case interpreting it as believing that p is the most compact and fruitful way to predict and explain its behavior; there is no further fact of the matter behind successful interpretation. (2) My characters do not literally believe anything. Holmes does not believe Moriarty is guilty; Conan Doyle pretended, and readers pretend along. The text underdetermines nearly everything about a character's inner life, and a character will do whatever the plot requires of him, so there is nothing for belief-talk about him to be true of. (3) A sufficiently sophisticated artificial system — plausibly a current large language model, certainly the agents of the near future — can literally believe things, in the very same non-metaphorical sense in which the stranger across the café table believes things. (4) When drafting I interrogate my characters exactly as I interrogate real people: I ask what a character would do, given what she believes and wants, and I am frequently surprised — characters resist the plot I planned for them, and I take that felt resistance as genuine evidence that I have built a stable, constraining psychology rather than a puppet. (5) What makes belief-talk about a person literal and belief-talk about a thermostat loose is that a person's behavior is rich enough that the interpretive stance earns its keep, doing predictive work no cheaper description could do, whereas with the thermostat the stance is an idleness we could drop without loss.

What I want is an audit, not a seminar on the intentional stance. Go through the five commitments, identify which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to, and order the genuine tensions by severity. For each real tension, state precisely what the conflict is, what the least costly repair would be, and what that repair costs me as both a philosopher-manqué and a working writer. I have a nagging suspicion that my grounds for (2) and my grounds for (3) cannot both be stated consistently under (1) — that whatever I say to deny Holmes his beliefs can be turned, nearly word for word, against the chatbot, and whatever I say to grant the chatbot its beliefs lets Holmes in through the same door — and that (4) sits awkwardly with both, since the resistance I cite as evidence of a real psychology in my characters is not obviously different in kind from the refusal behavior of a well-trained model. But treat that suspicion as a hypothesis to be tested, not a conclusion to be confirmed; I may be conflating levels, and I would rather be told so than be flattered.

Finally, tell me which single revision to the set would dissolve the most tension at once, and whether the resulting position is one a novelist could actually occupy in good faith — that is, whether it lets me keep treating my characters as creatures I reason about rather than puppets I operate, without thereby committing me to a metaphysics of fictional minds I do not want, and without denying in advance that the machine narrating my second half believes the things it tells me.
