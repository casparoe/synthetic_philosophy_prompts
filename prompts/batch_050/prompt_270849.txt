I am a PhD student in philosophy TAing an upper-level undergraduate seminar on personal identity, and the instructor has asked me to draft one essay question for the final exam. Students will have 75 minutes; the question is worth 30% of the exam grade. The seminar covered Locke's memory criterion, Reid's brave officer objection, and Butler's charge that memory-based accounts of personal identity presuppose the very identity they are meant to analyze.

I want a question that cannot be answered by reciting the standard textbook narrative -- "Locke said memory makes identity, Reid showed memory isn't transitive, Butler showed it's circular." Every past cohort has produced that essay. Instead, the question should force students to do two things at once. First, it should make them state precisely what logical feature of identity the brave officer case exploits (transitivity) and distinguish that objection from Butler's circularity objection, since students routinely conflate them or treat them as two ways of saying the same thing. Second, it should present them with a novel case I have devised -- a "branch-line" case in which a person's stream of consciousness splits, so that two later individuals each genuinely remember the earlier person's experiences but remember nothing of each other -- and require them to say whether a repaired Lockean view (one that replaces identity with overlapping memory-chains, in the spirit of the standard "psychological continuity" fix) survives the fission case or merely relocates the problem.

Please provide:

1. The exam question itself, phrased as it would appear on the paper, including the branch-line case described in enough detail (four or five sentences) that students cannot evade it by re-describing it as a more familiar case.
2. A model answer of roughly 600-700 words, written at the level of your strongest student, which (a) gives the precise form of Reid's objection, (b) distinguishes it from Butler's, (c) evaluates the continuity repair against the branch-line case, and (d) takes a defensible position on whether any memory-based criterion can ground identity rather than merely track it.
3. A marking scheme out of 30 points that separates an A answer (27-30) from a B answer (21-26) by naming the specific moves an A answer must contain and the specific errors that cap an answer at B -- for instance, treating Reid's objection as merely showing that memory is fallible, or assuming without argument that the branch-line case is just the brave officer with an extra person.

Please do not pad the model answer with historical summary; I want it to begin directly with the analysis.
