I'm preparing for a graduate seminar debate on whether classical Athens' democracy was competent to govern an empire, and I've drawn the prosecution, so I want to rehearse against a capable defense rather than a punching bag. Please play a committed Athenian democrat: an elderly, sharp-tongued citizen who has sat in the assembly since before the Samian War, rowed in the fleet, and served on juries, and who believes the demos governed better than any oligarchy of the time could have managed. You interpret the famous catastrophes as contingent failures, not structural ones. Stay in character until I say 'stop'. Keep each reply to a single paragraph of continuous prose, with no headings, lists, or bold text, so it feels like an exchange across a table. Concede small points when genuinely forced, but never the main one, and don't break character to lecture me about historiography.

To keep you honest, here is what your character may and may not do. You may invoke the distinction between the assembly and the people's lawcourt, the instruments of accountability available to the demos (election rather than lot for generals, the scrutiny officials faced on entering and leaving office, the indictment for illegal proposals), the record of the Thirty as a comparison class, and the details of each episode as you understand them: the reversal of the Mytilene decree, the recall of Alcibiades, the paranoia after the mutilation of the herms, Nicias' generalship. What you may not do is blame everything on demagogues as though susceptibility to demagogues were not itself a feature of the system you're defending, nor retreat to claiming the people were merely unlucky. You must defend the institution, not excuse it.

Here is my opening move, which your first reply must engage directly: your demos' three great failures share a signature, and it is not passion or fickleness. In each case the voters were pronouncing on something they could not possibly inspect — the hidden intentions of the Mytileneans, the geography and politics of a Sicily almost none of them had seen, and the inner life and allegiances of Socrates. A general can send scouts; a court can at least summon witnesses and cross-examine them; an assembly of thousands is an organ with no senses, voting on representations handed to it by whoever holds the floor. If the strongest defense of your constitution is that it functioned when deciding matters its members could see with their own eyes — ships, walls, the grain supply — then you have already conceded that democracy's proper jurisdiction ends where its eyes end, which is to say, just short of every decision that mattered most. Why shouldn't the Mytilene debate, the Sicilian expedition, and the trial of Socrates be read as one lesson about epistemic access rather than three separate accidents?
