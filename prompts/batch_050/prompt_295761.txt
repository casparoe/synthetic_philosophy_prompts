You're a political philosopher who specialises in democratic theory, so think carefully here and maybe brainstorm a bit before you commit to an answer.

When Scotland voted on independence in 2014 and Quebec in 1980 and 1995, the ballot was restricted to people living in the seceding region. Nobody in England, Wales or the rest of Canada got a vote, and a simple 50%+1 of the region's residents would have been enough to break up the country. That seems to be the standard template for these things.

What theory of "the people" does this practice presuppose? It seems to assume the demos for the question already exists before anyone votes on it -- but which one, and why those boundaries? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the practice only make sense if nations are pre-political units with a right of self-determination, or could a purely voluntarist theory (political units are just whoever wants to associate) licence exactly the same referendum design? Where is the practice genuinely committed and where is it underdetermined? Finally: if you took seriously the rival view that secession affects every citizen of the existing state equally, what concretely would have to change about how such votes are run -- and does anything in actual practice (say, the turnout thresholds or supermajorities some countries demand) secretly smuggle in that rival view while officially denying it?
