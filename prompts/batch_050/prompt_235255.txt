I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on the political economy of development, organized around the question of whether history can ever tell us why some nations are rich and others poor. Each week, one student submits a proposed causal inference, described at the level of its research design, and the professor rules on whether the design as described actually establishes the claim. Her rulings so far:

1. The post-1945 divergence between North and South Korea shows that institutions, rather than geography or culture, drove the income gap between them. Verdict: established.

2. A cross-country regression of present-day income per capita on a present-day index of institutional quality, with geographic controls, across 140 countries. Verdict: establishes nothing causal; she said she would not even count it as weak evidence.

3. An instrumental-variables design using historical settler mortality as an instrument for present-day institutional quality in former colonies. Verdict: credible, but she insisted the conclusion is licensed only for the former colonies in the sample, not as a general law about institutions and growth.

4. A richly documented archival narrative arguing that one country's nineteenth-century land reform caused its twentieth-century industrial takeoff, with no comparison cases. Verdict: excellent history, no causal force whatsoever.

5. A study comparing a country's growth after a major trade liberalization to its own pre-reform trend. Verdict: not credible.

6. A study of that same liberalization comparing the country's post-reform trajectory to a synthetic control built from a weighted blend of similar economies that never reformed. Verdict: credible, though she called it the weakest design she would accept.

This week's submission, which she has not yet ruled on: a study of present-day household incomes in villages straddling a centuries-old imperial boundary within a single country. The boundary was drawn for reasons unrelated to local conditions and long ago ceased to mark any administrative division, yet incomes jump discontinuously at the old border today. The researcher documents the discontinuity carefully but offers no evidence about the channel — nothing on whether the old border left behind different institutions, different norms and trust levels, different networks, or something else entirely.

Respond as an expert in causal inference and the methodology of comparative historical political economy. Think carefully, and before committing, privately brainstorm the full space of rules consistent with her track record. Then answer the following. First, predict her verdict on the new submission. Second, lay out the candidate rules her six rulings are jointly consistent with — at minimum, consider a rule on which the quality of the counterfactual comparison is both necessary and sufficient, a rule on which clean identification must be supplemented by a documented mechanism, and a rule on which the scope of the claim must match the reach of the design — and state what each predicts about the new case. Third, give your best guess as to which rule she is actually running, with your reasons. Fourth, note whether, on your predicted verdict, the study credibly establishes that something caused the discontinuity while failing to adjudicate between institutions, culture, and other channels — and whether she is likely to treat those as separate questions. Fifth, describe the single next submission whose ruling would best discriminate among the leading candidate rules. Lead with your bottom line — the predicted verdict — in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
