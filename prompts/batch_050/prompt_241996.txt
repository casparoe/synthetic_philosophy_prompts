I’m a retired biochemist who has only recently gotten into philosophy, and I keep going back to Pascal’s scattered remarks about justice and force. As I understand him, he thinks justice is supposed to rule, but by itself it has no practical power; force does rule in practice, but by itself it is tyranny. He also seems fascinated by the way custom, geography, and political success turn contingent arrangements into what people call natural justice. That already makes his position harder for me than the usual slogan that might makes right, or that justice should ignore power altogether.

So here’s my question: what do you think Pascal would have made of modern humanitarian military intervention? Imagine a present-day case where a government is massacring part of its own population. An international body condemns the massacre but cannot act because powerful states veto intervention, so a coalition invades anyway, claiming that justice authorizes it to use force without the usual legal approval. The intervention probably saves lives, but it also sets a precedent that strong countries can invoke humanitarian justice whenever they want to bypass international law. Would Pascal regard this as justice finally acquiring the force it needs, as force dressing itself up as justice, or as an unavoidable tragedy in which the two cannot be cleanly separated? Please don’t just give a generic realist or liberal answer. Reconstruct how Pascal might reason from his broader views about custom, fallen human nature, political authority, and our tendency to mistake the strongest for the most legitimate. Also mention one modern feature of the case that might have surprised him enough to complicate his usual picture.
