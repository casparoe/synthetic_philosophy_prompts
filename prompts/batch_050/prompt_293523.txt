I'm a third-year undergraduate double-majoring in philosophy and economics, and this semester I'm serving as one of the student section leaders for our department's Philosophy of Probability course. The professor has handed me next week's 75-minute discussion section (about 14 students, all of whom have just learned the probability axioms but have not yet seen the Dutch book argument) and told me I may run it however I like, provided the students genuinely engage with the material rather than just copying a theorem off the board. I'd like you to design the activity I run. The goal is that students come away feeling, in their wallets or their pride, that incoherent degrees of belief are exploitable -- and, just as importantly, that coherent ones are not, since the converse result is the half of the argument everyone forgets.

Concretely, I want a game with real (small) stakes -- I'm willing to supply chocolate or coffee vouchers -- in which students state odds or prices on propositions they can actually be incoherent about (upcoming sports fixtures, the weather on campus next Tuesday, the composition of an urn they've only partially sampled), rather than fair-coin cases where they'd trivially report coherent numbers. Two design constraints matter to me. First, I don't want to rely on the assumption that a bettor can be forced to accept any bet at their stated odds, because a sharp student will just refuse suspicious bets and the argument dissolves; instead, structure the market so that I (or a designated bookie) can both buy and sell at each student's announced prices, or offer to sell them bundles of bets as a package, so the exploitation arises from their own prices rather than from coercion. Second, I want a phase where the roles reverse and students play bookie against one another, so that they discover for themselves that no book can be made against a classmate whose prices satisfy the axioms. No lecture up front: they should realize what's happened to them before I name anything. Please include a materials list, a timing breakdown, and sample bookie scripts showing how to assemble a guaranteed-loss portfolio from a concrete set of incoherent prices.

Then I need a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the game onto the structure of the argument -- which step corresponds to additivity, which to the normalization of the unit, what the package construction shows about why the bookie never needs the bettor to act twice -- and that anticipates what will predictably go wrong: students who simply won't post prices, students who revise their odds the moment they see me trading against them (which raises the question of whether exploitability requires a bookie who knows your dispositions in advance, and I'd like help phrasing that), and the inevitable student who anchors everything at even odds. Finally, be honest with me about what the activity cannot reproduce: my sense is that the diachronic version of the argument, where the bookie must know the victim's future updating rule ahead of time, can't really be staged live, so tell me what I'll have to supply by hand afterward, and give me two or three sentences I can say that connect the game's pragmatic, money-losing moral to the stronger epistemic claim that incoherent credences are irrational as beliefs -- along with a note on where that inference is contestable, since I'd rather flag the seam than have a student tear it open in front of me.
