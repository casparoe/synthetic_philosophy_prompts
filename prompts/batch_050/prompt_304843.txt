Need some advice because my sister and I are going in circles on this. Our grandfather left us a painting that's been hanging over his fireplace my whole life. It was always understood to be by a fairly well-known early modernist -- grandpa bought it from a dealer in the 60s and the provenance paperwork looked solid. We had it appraised after he died and it came back around 800k, which obviously matters a lot for settling the estate.

Well. We submitted it to the artist's authentication board (needed for insurance) and they just came back saying it's a "studio work" -- meaning it came out of the artist's workshop, same period, same materials, probably designed and sketched by him, but the actual brushwork is mostly by his principal assistant. Apparently this was totally standard practice at the time and the artist signed plenty of these, but the market treats them completely differently. Value now estimated at maybe 40-60k.

Here's the weird part. The painting didn't change. Not one molecule. I have looked at this thing for thirty years, I love the composition, I know it better than any artwork on earth. But I'd be lying if I said my feelings toward it haven't shifted, and I hate that about myself. Meanwhile my sister wants to just sell it and take the 50k or whatever, and her argument is basically: if the only thing that changed is what people believe about it, and the market is what actually pays out, then the "value" was never really in the object and we should stop being sentimental. Also there's a complication -- the assistant in question has recently become kind of famous in her own right, and one dealer told us there's a real chance these studio works get reappraised upward in ten years as people re-think her contribution. So selling now might be selling low on the assistant's name instead of the master's.

So my questions, I guess: is my sister right that the value was never in the object, and if so why does that feel like it proves too much (nobody would say a wedding ring becomes worthless when the marriage ends, or whatever)? When I loved this painting for thirty years, what exactly was I loving -- and is there a coherent way to say my earlier appreciation wasn't a mistake even though it rested on a false belief about who held the brush? And practically: keep it, sell it now, or hold it as a bet on the assistant's reputation? I'd rather not get generic "do what feels right" advice -- I want to know whether the market price and my experience are actually tracking two different kinds of value, and which one should win when they come apart.
