I sit on the risk committee of a mid-sized insurance firm, and we've been running an internal forecasting programme for a year -- staff predict things like claim volumes and whether regulators will approve new products, and it's been decently calibrated. Now our head of analytics wants to open markets on the committee's own decisions: whether we'll approve a big reinsurance contract next month, whether we'll pull out of the coastal-property market, and so on. The idea is that traders would get better information to us and we'd make wiser calls. I have a nagging worry I can't quite articulate, though: these aren't events out in the world we're trying to track, they're things we *decide*, and I keep going around in circles. If the market prices approval at 80%, is that a prediction or a pressure? Can I, as a member, even trade honestly on a question whose answer partly depends on what I do, and is my belief about my own future vote a forecast or just... an intention I'm reporting? My deputy thinks this is all precious philosophising and that a forecast is a forecast, but another member thinks self-predictions are somehow categorically different and wants the whole thing shelved. Please draft the memo I'll table before we vote, recommending either that we allow the self-referential markets, ban them, or allow them with specific guardrails -- and give my deputy's "a forecast is a forecast" line its best possible run before explaining whether it survives. Format it as exactly five numbered points of a sentence or two each, with no preamble and no closing summary.
