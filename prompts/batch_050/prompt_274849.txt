I teach an upper-division philosophy of mind seminar, mostly juniors and seniors, about 14 students, one 75-minute meeting. In a few weeks we hit the causal exclusion argument (Kim-style: causal closure of the physical, no systematic overdetermination, so mental causes get squeezed out unless they're identified with physical causes). Every year they nod along on paper and it never lands -- it feels like wordplay to them, "sure, fine, whatever, the mind still obviously does things."

Design me an in-class activity that makes them feel the exclusion pressure before anyone names it. My rough thought: the students themselves physically enact some system whose low-level rules completely fix the output (they can be "neurons" passing tokens or whatever, you design something better), and one student or team is explicitly assigned to be the mental level -- the decision, the pain -- and told their job is to make the outcome happen. The rules should be rigged so they discover, mid-game, that there's no work left for them: every lever they reach for turns out to already be a low-level lever. I want the visible frustration of that.

Then ideally a second phase where they get to propose rule-patches to give the mental level its job back, and the patches line up suspiciously well with the standard moves -- identify the mental cause with a pattern in the low-level stuff, allow double-causing, poke a hole in closure -- and each patch should visibly cost them something or break part of the game. The mapping shouldn't be announced beforehand; they should propose the patches as just "how to fix the game" and only afterwards realize what they reinvented.

Give me: concrete rules, materials, roles, a payoff scheme with small stakes (I can do candy or participation points), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief script that maps specific moments from the game onto the premises of the argument and onto each reply. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce -- e.g. I suspect a deterministic toy system can generate the "no work left" feeling but can't show why we should believe closure about the actual world, so flag that gap and anything else like it.

No lecture up front, they shouldn't hear the words "causal closure" until the debrief. Keep it runnable by someone who is not a game designer and a little disorganized.
