So I'm in a history reading group and we've spent the last couple months slogging through Gibbon's Decline and Fall, and last week we got into a genuinely heated argument that I want an outside opinion on. Halfway through volume 3 someone pointed out that basically everything Gibbon took for granted has been challenged by later scholarship: the Pirenne thesis says the ancient world didn't really end until the Arab conquests cut the Mediterranean, the whole "late antiquity" school associated with Peter Brown argues the fifth-century West was more of a transformation than a catastrophe, and there's a bunch of work on how the "barbarian" successor kingdoms — the Ostrogoths especially — actively presented themselves as continuations of Roman government rather than its destroyers. Gibbon obviously never read any of this. So here's my question: if Gibbon could somehow be brought up to speed on the last two and a half centuries of historiography, how would he respond? Would he double down on his own account — that the empire rotted from within, that Christianity sapped civic virtue, that the barbarians were more symptom than cause — or would he concede anything to the transformation people? Feel free to reason from his temperament and method as much as his actual arguments: his irony, his enlightenment assumptions, the fact that he was writing while watching Britain fight (and losing to) its own colonists.

A couple of us think he'd actually love the revisionist stuff, since he was already a skeptic of his own era's pieties and the "Rome fell because decadence" story is exactly the kind of edifying moral tale he'd have skewered in someone else. Others think his core thesis is pretty robust to the new evidence and he'd just absorb it — like, "fine, call it a transformation, the transformation was still into something worse and the cause is still internal decay plus monks." Honestly I'm not even sure those two predictions disagree. So: make the case for what he'd most plausibly say, but also flag where your prediction is doing the most speculative work — if there's some fact about Gibbon's views, or some piece of the post-Gibbon evidence, that would flip your verdict if it turned out differently than you're assuming, tell me what it is. And if the honest answer is that this kind of counterfactual mind-reading is more about us than about him, that's fine too, but say why rather than just hedging.
