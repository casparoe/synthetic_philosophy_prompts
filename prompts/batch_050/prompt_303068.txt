I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm planning a year-long reporting project -- probably six long features and a short book -- on moral compromise: not compromise as splitting the difference on preferences, but the cases where people treat one of their own values as a bargaining chip to secure another, the way trading partners accept a deal that's worse in some dimension because it's better overall. Think of a gun-control advocate accepting a sentencing provision she considers unjust to get the bill passed, or a devout environmentalist campaigning for a candidate whose trade policy she finds unconscionable. Political scientists and moral philosophers have pieces of this -- logrolling, the ethics of compromise, integrity constraints, moral uncertainty as a reason to hedge -- but my strong impression is that the genuinely hard conceptual questions are scattered across fields and mostly not being worked on as a coherent cluster. I want to spend the year where it counts.

Here's a distinction I keep reaching for in my notes, which I'd like you to use or improve on: some compromises are *external* (I accept an outcome I disvalue because the coalition arithmetic requires it), and some are *internal* (accepting the deal actually changes what I take my values to require of me -- the compromise gets into the deliberation itself, as when activists start sincerely believing the diluted position is the right one). Whether a compromise is corrosive, praiseworthy, or neutral seems to me to track this distinction more than it tracks the stakes, but I can't yet say why.

Given that framing: which three or four open conceptual questions in this area are most worth my year, and in what order? For each one, tell me (a) why it's upstream -- what reporting on concrete cases, or what other questions on my list, depends on having a workable answer; (b) how tractable it is for someone whose method is interviews and case studies rather than formal proof, i.e., where journalism can genuinely move the question rather than just illustrate it; (c) how neglected it is relative to its importance; and (d) what I'd learn in the first few months that should move it up or down the ranking. I'd also like you to name at least one question that people in this space treat as central but that you'd deprioritize for my purposes, with the reason. The deliverable I'm after is a defended reporting agenda with explicit ranking criteria, not a survey of the debate -- and if my internal/external distinction collapses under scrutiny, say so early, since several of my planned features presuppose it.
