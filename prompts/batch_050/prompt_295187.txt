I'm presenting my working position in formal epistemology at a workshop next month, and I want a consistency audit before I circulate the handout. Here is where I've landed, roughly in order of how much I'd mind giving each piece up:

(a) Gradational accuracy -- closeness of credences to the truth -- is the sole fundamental epistemic value; every epistemic norm, if legitimate, must be vindicated in terms of accuracy rather than practical or moral considerations.

(b) Probabilism is true: rational credence functions obey the probability axioms, and the right justification for this is the accuracy-dominance argument.

(c) Only strictly proper accuracy measures are legitimate. A measure under which an agent could expect, by her own lights, to do better by reporting credences other than the ones she actually holds is not really measuring accuracy at all.

(d) Imprecise credences are sometimes rationally permissible or even required: where the evidence is sparse, conflicting, or of unknown reliability, an agent should not be forced to a sharp probability.

(e) It is always epistemically rational to gather cost-free evidence before making a decision; evidence you could have had for nothing is evidence you should have.

(f) A verdict on a case: an agent who knows she will suffer memory loss tonight -- or who knows that, when the evidence arrives, she will process it irrationally -- can rationally decline free information now. Rational evidence avoidance is genuinely possible.

(g) Practical stakes never affect what credence one epistemically ought to have; a high-stakes situation might change what one does, never what accuracy itself recommends.

My questions: which of these actually stand in tension with one another, and which only appear to? Please rank any genuine tensions by severity, and for each one state precisely what would have to be revised or qualified to restore consistency, and what that revision costs the overall view. Where theorems are doing the work, state their premises -- I want to know exactly which assumptions my commitments are collectively leaning on. I'd also like you to separate what is genuinely settled in this literature (mathematically or by near-consensus) from what remains contested among specialists, rather than presenting contested moves as established results. The answer should be in British English.
