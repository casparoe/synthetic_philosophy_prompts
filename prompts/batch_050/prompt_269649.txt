Quick background so you know where I'm coming from: I'm a retired experimental physicist, condensed matter mostly, and since retiring I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole the way retired people do. I just worked my way through Book XI of Augustine's Confessions, the famous bit on time, and it's been nagging at me because it keeps colliding with stuff I took for granted for forty years in the lab.

For those who haven't read it recently: Augustine asks what time is, complains that he knows perfectly well what it is until someone asks him to explain it, and ends up arguing roughly that the past doesn't exist anymore, the future doesn't exist yet, the present has no measurable extent, and that when we "measure time" we're really measuring something in the soul — memory of what's gone, attention to what's here, expectation of what's coming. He calls time a kind of stretching-apart of the mind (distentio, I think; my Latin is awful and my translation is from the 50s). Whether that's a theory of time or just giving up with extra steps is part of what I want to know.

Now here's the collision. In physics, time is what clocks measure, end of story. Relativity says moving clocks run slow, gravity slows clocks down, simultaneity between distant events is frame-dependent, and a lot of physicists will casually tell you the "block universe" — past, present, and future all equally real, laid out like a landscape — is the natural reading of the equations. GPS satellites genuinely need relativistic corrections or your phone's location drifts by kilometers a day. None of this is controversial; nobody in my old department would blink.

So run the counterfactual: Augustine gets resurrected, sits through a modern relativity course, and actually understands the math. What does he say? Concretely:

1. Does time dilation confirm his view or refute it? I can argue it both ways and keep flip-flopping. Clocks disagreeing with each other looks bad for objective time (point Augustine), but the disagreement is perfectly lawlike and predicted by theory, which looks bad for the idea that time is something psychological (point against Augustine). Which way does he go, and what's the strongest version of each reading?

2. Would he just say physics never touches his question — that relativity tells him how clocks behave but not what time is, the way a scale tells you readings but not what weight is? Or would he have to concede that part of his puzzle was confusion that physics actually clears up?

3. The block universe should horrify him, since his whole account rests on the past being genuinely gone. But it also sounds suspiciously like the view from eternity he attributes to God — everything present at once. Could he take the block universe as a picture of how God sees things while insisting creatures live in psychological time, or is that move unavailable to him?

4. He leans hard on measurement: you can't measure what doesn't exist, so what are we measuring when we time a long syllable against a short one? Physicists measure intervals all day, and atomic clocks definately exist. Does that embarrass the argument, or does it survive?

Please don't just summarize the chapter back at me — I've read it twice. I want the actual counterfactual worked out, with reasons from the text where possible, and if parts of it are underdetermined by what Augustine wrote, say that plainly instead of inventing an Augustine who conveniently agrees with you.
