Here's the homework question as set:

"The 'tautology objection' holds that the principle of natural selection is circular: the fittest are defined as those that survive, so 'survival of the fittest' is vacuous. Explain the objection carefully, then explain how the propensity interpretation of fitness is meant to answer it. Does the propensity interpretation fully solve the problem, or does it merely relocate it? Discuss at least one difficulty for it."

Here's the student's answer:

The tautology objection says that if we define fitness in terms of actual reproductive success, then the claim that the fittest survive becomes an analytic truth that can't explain anything, since "fitness" just means "the ones who in fact reproduced most." Any putative law of the form "the fittest reproduce most" would then be true by definition and hence no more predictive than "bachelors are unmarried."

The propensity interpretation responds by redefining fitness as a probabilistic disposition: an organism's fitness is its expected reproductive success, its objective propensity to survive and reproduce in its environment, rather than how many offspring it actually ends up having. On this reading "the fittest tend to survive" is a synthetic, contingent claim that could be false — chance can always intervene — so the circularity is avoided. Fitness becomes a genuine causal property of organisms that explains why they reproduce, rather than merely redescribing the fact that they did.

I think the propensity interpretation essentially solves the problem. Because fitness is now a propensity rather than an actual outcome, natural selection can be used to make determinate predictions: we can measure an organism's fitness in advance and then check whether the fitter individuals actually do survive and reproduce more, which is what evolutionary biologists do in the field with comparative offspring counts. This turns selection into a testable scientific law like those in physics. The one remaining difficulty I can see is a minor epistemic one: propensities are hard to estimate precisely, since a single organism's actual offspring count is only a noisy sample from its underlying propensity, so in practice researchers have to average over many individuals. But that's a measurement problem, not a conceptual one, so the interpretation relocates the difficulty from logic to statistics without anything fundamental remaining.
