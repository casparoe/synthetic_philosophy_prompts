I'm a retired molecular biologist who's fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole in his old age, and I keep running into an arguementative move I don't have a name for. Here's how it goes. Someone argues that our moral judgments can't be trusted because natural selection shaped them to promote survival and reproduction, not truth — my gut feeling that it's wrong to hurt a stranger is there because it helped my ancestors cooperate, not because it tracks anything correct. Fine, I can follow that. But then when I ask whether the same logic applies to, say, my conviction that 2+2=4, or that the past actually happened, or that other people have inner lives — all of which presumabely also come out of brains built by selection — the same person usually refuses to generalize. So moral beliefs get thrown out on genealogical grounds while equally evolved beliefs get a free pass, and nobody seems to say where the line is drawn.

I know the first move gets called a debunking argument (I've seen "evolutionary debunking" floating around). What I'm asking about is the second thing: the reply that says "careful, this argument proves too much, because by its own lights it would sink beliefs you yourself want to keep — maybe including the reasoning you used to make the argument." Does that reply have an established name? And more importantly, is there any principled criterion in this literature for which beliefs an evolutionary genealogy debunks and which it leaves untouched — or is the honest answer that nobody has one and that's the whole problem? I don't just want labels; I want the carving. What exactly makes a belief vulnerable to this kind of argument and another one not, and does the answer survive being applied to its own case?
