I host a podcast on moral philosophy and political economy, and next month's guest is an economist-philosopher who defends a positive pure rate of time preference in social cost-benefit analysis. On his view, a unit of welfare counts for less simply because it arrives later, quite apart from discounting for expected economic growth, uncertainty, or the opportunity cost of capital. He knows the standard objection and will open by invoking Henry Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics (1874): in the discussion of philosophical intuitionism in Book III, Sidgwick argues that rational self-love requires impartiality across the moments of one's own life, so that the mere posteriority of a good in time is no rational ground for caring about it less, and that once the same impartiality is extended across persons, future people's welfare may not be downweighted merely for being future. My guest will say he has fully absorbed this argument and rejects it anyway.

Draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions, with a follow-up to each keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. The sequence should do the following, roughly in order:

- Force him first to define pure time preference precisely and to separate it from every other component of a standard Ramsey-style discounting formula (declining marginal utility of a richer future, catastrophe risk, capital returns), so that he cannot later retreat into defending discounting on those safer grounds.
- Press the within-life versus between-lives asymmetry: Sidgwick's argument begins with prudence inside a single life, but in the intergenerational cases where pure time preference does its policy work, no present person's prudential calculus spans the gap — so what is the discount rate even expressing?
- Test the descriptive defense: if he grounds the rate in observed savings behavior or revealed market preferences, how does he propose to strip out mortality risk, credit constraints, short horizons, and hyperbolic rather than exponential impatience, so that what remains is a normatively respectable pure rate rather than a residue of imperfections?
- Put a concrete counterfactual to him: the same harm, falling on people with the same interests and no lesser claims, but shifted two centuries later — does the duty to prevent it shrink, and if not, what work is the pure rate doing?
- Engage Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason: even Sidgwick held that rational egoism and impartial utilitarianism cannot be reconciled, so the guest should say whether he defends time preference only for individual prudential choice or also for impartial social choice, and whether his position requires taking sides in that dualism.
- Confront him with the long-run consequence: at any constant positive pure rate, catastrophes a few centuries out count for almost nothing today — whether he treats that as a reductio or a bullet to bite, and what he says to those who take it as decisive.
- End with a question about what evidence or argument could change his mind, and what would follow for cost-benefit practice if pure time preference were set to zero while other grounds for discounting remained.

Keep each question to two or three sentences and each follow-up to one or two. Begin directly with the first question, with no preface, framing, or restatement of this brief.
