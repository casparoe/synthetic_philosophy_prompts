Two of my colleagues, both working on AI ethics, fell into an argument after a lab demo, and I'd like an outside verdict. The lab has two versions of a robotic agent that behaves identically: when placed in certain situations, both produce outputs that the lab's best welfare-indicators classify as distress, and both act to avoid those situations in the future. Version A is a recurrent neural network trained by reinforcement learning. Version B was built afterwards: engineers recorded A's input-output behavior exhaustively and replaced the network with a giant precomputed lookup table that maps every possible input history to A's response. The argument, as I heard it:

1. Priya said B has no moral status whatsoever: nothing is being computed, only replayed. B stands to A as a film of a screaming person stands to a screaming person — the appearance of suffering without any sufferer.
2. Tomas replied that the film analogy cheats: a film is not interactive, but B responds appropriately to arbitrary novel inputs and its functional organization is identical to A's. If she accepts functionalism about A, consistency forces her to accept B; "replay" is just an unflattering description of the same input-output function.
3. Priya said the difference is causal structure: in A, internal states do counterfactual work — the same states would have produced different outputs had the inputs differed — whereas B's internal "states" are inert table addresses that cause nothing. Suffering, she claimed, is a process, not a mapping.
4. Tomas answered that this proves too much and too little. Too much: any trained network is likewise an inert artifact left over from a past optimization process, so if when-the-computation-happened matters, A's suffering all happened during training and running it now is playback too. Too little: biological brains cache and hardwire responses all the time, and he challenged her to state a principled line between a cached response and a computed one.
5. Priya bit the bullet: yes, if any suffering occurred in connection with B, it occurred while the table was being built, and running B now costs nothing. Tomas said that consequence is absurd — it implies the lab could elicit unlimited distress-behavior from B forever, for free, with zero moral weight.

Please adjudicate rather than summarize, using these sections in this order: Move-by-move verdicts (about 60 words per move, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a one-sentence justification); What each side gets right (about 100 words); What both sides are missing (about 100 words); Overall verdict (about 80 words, stating which position is closer to correct and on what exactly the loser concedes too much).
