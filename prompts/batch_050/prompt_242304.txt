I have been persuaded by Taurek's argument in Should the Numbers Count? that, forced to choose between saving one stranger from death and saving five strangers from death, I am permitted to flip a fair coin rather than required to save the five. On this view, aggregation does no moral work: each person's claim is weighed against each other's, and the sheer number of claims on one side never tips the balance. I have also been toying with a hybrid position of my own devising — numbers never count when the competing stakes are of comparable gravity (life against life), but they do count once the asymmetry grows large enough, so that one death may be chosen over any number of mild headaches, while five lives still may not be chosen over one. I suspect this hybrid inherits the worst of both views rather than the best, but I have not been able to prove this to myself.

What I want is a map of the exposure of both positions — the pure Taurekian view and my hybrid. Please address at least the following, though not only these: cases in which anti-aggregation appears to license choosing one person's death over the prevention of astronomically many trivial harms; fairness-based objections to the unweighted coin flip, and whether a lottery weighted by numbers would honor each person's claim to a chance without reinstating aggregation outright; repeated and statistical contexts, where refusing to count numbers in each choice seems to produce foreseeably worse outcomes in aggregate, and whether this shows the view cannot survive transplantation from rescue cases to policy; small risks imposed on many against certain harm to one, and whether an ex ante perspective quietly smuggles aggregation back in; and whether my hybrid's threshold — the point at which numbers suddenly begin to count — is defensible or merely an ad hoc retreat that abandons the motivating insight.

Rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. In each case, say whether a committed anti-aggregationist would concede it as genuine pressure or dismiss it as question-begging — that is, as simply restating the aggregating intuition the view was built to reject. Finally: what I would personally find most persuasive is a case in which the view licenses an outcome even its defenders flinch at, rather than one that merely assumes what is in dispute; tell me whether any such case exists.
