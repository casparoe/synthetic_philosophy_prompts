I'm a TA this semester for a PPE-ish seminar on intergenerational ethics, and for the week on discounting I want to hand out a "what's your actual position on time preference?" sorting quiz, because every year half the class starts out saying obviously discounting the future is irrational and then, an hour later, defends using a discount rate in climate cost-benefit analysis without noticing they switched. Write me a decision tree with about 7 to 9 diagnostic questions, each built around a concrete case rather than the vocabulary -- so: choosing to defer a painful surgery when you know it'll hurt exactly as much later, a dam that only pays off by preventing a flood 200 years from now, whether you'd care less about an identical disaster happening far away rather than nearby, whether you'd reverse a preference as the relevant date gets closer, stuff like that. Each question should be answerable by someone who's never heard the phrase "social discount rate," and the branching should sort respondents into named positions by what they say about cases, not which label they like.

The leaves I definitely want covered: the Ramsey/Sidgwick-style line that the pure rate of time preference should be zero and all legitimate discounting reduces to things like growth, uncertainty, and declining marginal utility; the view that pure time preference is rationally permissable for an individual choosing over their own life but illegitimate when a government applies it to other people's futures; the view that it's defensible even interpersonally (with whatever grounds make that work); the deflationary position that there is no such thing as pure time preference once you strip out the confounds, just misdescribed risk and changing preferences; and the hyperbolic/present-bias position where discounting is real but time-inconsistent, and so a different beast from the exponential kind. Please build the tree from the logical space rather than starting from the labels -- figure out which questions actually seperate these views, note any combination of answers nobody seems to occupy and whether that's because it's incoherent or merely unpopular, and explicitly flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really distinguish (my guess is the deflationary view and the Ramsey view will cause trouble, but you tell me).

Include an answer key mapping answer patterns to positions, with one sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment. And at the end, say how confident you are that the tree actually sorts real people cleanly rather than just sorting textbook summaries, and single out the one branch or one mapping you're least sure of.
