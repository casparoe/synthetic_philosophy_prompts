I'm the ombudsperson for a mid-size professional association, and about thirty times a year someone files a grievance that lands on me for informal resolution before it can go formal. A big chunk of these come down to hurt feelings -- someone found a remark offensive, a joke cruel, an exclusion cold -- and my job at this stage is basically to figure out whether the offense is the kind a reasonable person would share, or whether its idiosyncratic to this complainant. I've been reading Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments and his impartial spectator idea seems like exactly the test I need, but it's a theory, not a method.

Turn it into a procedure I can actually run in the hour or so I get per case: the steps in order, what makes a spectator count as genuinely impartial for these purposes (fully informed about context? no stake in the outcome? not a friend of either party?), and what evidence I can realistically gather to construct that spectator's viewpoint rather than just projecting my own. Handle two things that worry me. First, Smith's own point that spectators get corrupted by custom and group loyalty -- if everyone in the office shares the offended person's reaction because they're all on the same team, does that pass the impartiality test or fail it, and how do I tell the difference in practice? Second, as I understand it Smith treats judging the motive behind what someone did as a different question from judging whether the emotional reaction to it is proportionate -- build both into the procedure and tell me what to do when they point in opposite directions. Be explicit about where judgment calls remain that no checklist can eliminate, and who (me, the parties, the board) should sign off at those points.

Then run the procedure on two of my actual recurring case types: (a) an analyst complains that a manager called her presentation naive in front of clients; (b) a team member complains that a coworker quietly eats lunch alone every day instead of joining the group table and it reads as a snub. I have strong expectations about both, so warn me in advance if the procedure gives me an answer I won't like, and don't soften it.

Last thing: say how confident you are overall that this procedure is faithful to what Smith actually argued rather than a loose modern riff on him, and single out the one step where you're least sure you've got him right.
