I teach a second-year undergraduate survey in philosophy of science, and I have two ninety-minute meetings to give my students the pessimistic meta-induction properly — as an argument they could reconstruct and defend against the standard realist replies, not as a slogan about theories being "proven wrong eventually." The cohort is mixed: roughly half have taken introductory logic, a third are science majors who arrive mildly hostile to the suggestion that their fields might be in the business of producing falsehoods, and none have read Laudan or Putnam.

Rather than building the sessions on phlogiston or the luminiferous ether — which half the class will half-remember from school, importing confusion along with it — I have prepared a fictional case: a compact invented history of "aerometry," a seventeenth-century science of atmospheric spirits, comprising three successive theories, each more empirically successful than its predecessor in specified respects (storm prediction, instrument calibration, a novel phenomenon anticipated before observation), each organized around a different fundamental ontology (vaporous humours, elastic corpuscles, a subtle weightless fluid), and each abandoned wholesale. Success and ontological discontinuity are both made explicit.

Plan the teaching arc rather than the lecture itself. Specifically:

1. The order of ideas and their dependencies. What must already be in place before the meta-induction is felt as a threat rather than a truism? I assume the no-miracles argument must come first, since the meta-induction is an attack on realism's best case for itself, and that some working notion of approximate truth must precede the replies; say whether I am right about this and why.

2. The carrying example. Can my fictional case bear the argumentative weight, or is a real historical example indispensable because the argument's force depends on the cases being actual? State the properties any carrying example must have — genuine novel predictive success rather than mere longevity, wholesale rather than partial abandonment, discontinuity at the level of central theoretical posits — and what it must lack. If the fictional case works pedagogically, indicate where in the sequence to reveal that the real historical record offers analogous cases, and what that reveal must accomplish.

3. The misconception map, in the order I should expect the misconceptions to arise: that the argument purports to prove current theories false; that it is ordinary inductive skepticism dressed up and therefore self-undermining; that the discarded theories were never really successful and so the induction has no premises. Add whatever further misconceptions you would expect from this particular cohort, especially from the science majors, and identify the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact.

4. The realist replies. Which of dividing the theory into working and idle constituents, retreating to structural continuity, and restricting the induction to theories enjoying novel predictive success belong in these two meetings, which should be deferred to later weeks, and where the line sits for this audience — including what I should openly decline to settle at this level.

5. The check for understanding: one exercise or question, administrable in the final fifteen minutes, that would show the students can run the argument themselves rather than recite it — ideally one requiring them to construct or evaluate a pessimistic induction against a case they have not seen.
