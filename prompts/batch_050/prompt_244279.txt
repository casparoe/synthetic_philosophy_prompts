Quick setup so we're on the same page: the standard problem of logical omniscience. On the possible-worlds model of belief, if an agent believes p and p entails q, then q holds at every world compatible with their beliefs, so they believe q too. Hence believers turn out to believe every logical truth and every consequence of anything they believe. Actual people obviously don't. Most of the literature responds by building models where agents can fail to be omniscient — impossible worlds, awareness filters, fragmented belief states, that kind of thing.

My paper goes the opposite direction. I argue the omniscience "problem" is only a problem if we misidentify what the possible-worlds model was ever modeling. Rough sketch:

1. Distinguish two things that both get called belief: an agent's information state (the set of worlds left open given everything they've committed to) and their retrieval system (the finite, resource-bounded machinery that answers questions on demand — is this true? what follows from that?).

2. The possible-worlds model, I claim, was never a model of retrieval. It's a model of information states. Closure under consequence isn't a bug to be engineered away; it's just what it is to be an information state. The state rules out worlds, and whatever is true at every un-ruled-out world is "in" the state the way a fact is "in" a database whether or not anyone ever runs the query.

3. The famous counterexamples are all retrieval failures on my view. Take the standard case: I believe the axioms of arithmetic but don't believe that some specific huge number is prime, because I haven't checked. Assuming I've genuinely committed to the axioms, my information state settles the primality question one way or the other — I just don't know which way, because figuring out which way is a retrieval task, and retrieval costs resources. Not believing the consequence isn't a counterexample to closure; it's what you'd predict from a cheap little query engine sitting on top of a closed information state.

4. The payoff: we keep the clean semantics and explain the data. What people actually avow tracks what's cheap to retrieve, and cheapness is a matter of indexing and inference-path length, not of what's believed. Fragmentation views, I argue, put the fragmentation in the wrong place — it's the retrieval system that's fragmented, not the information.

5. I also try to argue this is empirically distinguishable from awareness-style views, though this section is the shakiest. Rough idea: awareness views predict that making an agent aware of a consequence suffices for belief. My view predicts awareness is neither necessary nor sufficient — an agent can be fully aware of a question, try hard, and still fail (long proofs), and can "believe" consequences they were never made aware of (cheap inferences that run automatically, like peripheral perceptual inference).
