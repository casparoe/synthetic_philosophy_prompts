I'm a novelist working on a book set in a country that's closing its border, and there's a dinner party scene where one character defends the closure with the line everyone reaches for: a country is like a house, or like a private club, the members get to decide who comes in, and outsiders have no more claim on it than a stranger has on your spare bedroom. It's the old Walzer move and my editor thinks it sounds intuitively right, so the character is currently winning the argument on the page. Before I let him win, I want to know how far the analagy actually holds. Walk me through what maps onto what, in plain terms: what's the house, who are the owners, what counts as being invited in versus being born inside, and where does the mapping break? Some things I'm suspicious about: whether citizens relate to territory the way homeowners relate to a house, whether turning someone away at a border is more like refusing a dinner guest or more like blocking a public street, and whether the analogy quietly assumes a kind of collective consent or ownership that real countries never actually had. Then the bottom line: does my character's conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the comparison doing all the work for him? And if there's a better analogy I could put in his mouth, or in the mouth of the character arguing back, tell me that too. Write it like an email, continuous prose only, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, I want sentences I can steal.
