I volunteer as a non-voting observer on my hospital's ethics discussion group, and at last week's meeting two members, Daniel and Ruth, got into an extended argument that nobody could settle. The case on the table was a stylized rescue: a single rescue team can reach either of two sites before the tide turns, saving either one stranded climber at the first site or five stranded climbers at the second; everyone involved is a stranger, equally situated, and will die if not reached. Daniel argued that a contractualist framework of the Scanlonian sort straightforwardly licenses saving the five: each of the five could reasonably reject a principle permitting rescuers to leave them to die when they could have been saved at no greater cost, and since their individual complaints are exactly as serious as the lone climber's complaint, the presence of more equally serious complaints breaks the tie in favor of the larger group. Ruth objected that this smuggles in the aggregation contractualism was designed to resist: on her reading, moral justification proceeds by pairwise comparison of individual complaints, the lone climber's complaint is not weaker than any one of the five's, so no individual complaint is defeated, and the only permissible options are to save the one or to give both groups an equal chance by lottery -- never to save the five as such. Daniel countered that her reading forces the absurd conclusion that one must flip a coin even between saving one person and saving a million, and that a theory this revisionary has refuted itself. Ruth answered that counterintuitiveness is no objection from within the theory -- the whole point of deriving morality from what individuals can reasonably reject is that the verdicts may surprise us -- and moreover that Daniel's tie-breaking move reopens the very problem contractualism was supposed to solve: if equal complaints can be summed to break a tie, she cannot see the principled barrier against summing many small complaints (say, mild headaches) to outweigh one severe complaint (say, a death), which is precisely the aggregation result contractualists tout their framework for avoiding. Daniel's final move was to insist the barrier is intact: numbers may break ties only among complaints of equal gravity, and a headache complaint is never equal in gravity to a death complaint, so it never enters the relevant comparison at all. Ruth shook her head and said he was just asserting a line where his argument gave none, and they asked me to referee.

I genuinely could not. Please go through the exchange move by move -- Daniel's opening claim, Ruth's pairwise-comparison objection, Daniel's million-lives reductio, Ruth's reply about counterintuitiveness plus her slippery-slope charge, and Daniel's equal-gravity restriction -- and say who is right at each step and why, rather than just declaring an overall winner. I would also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing: for instance, whether the debate changes if the lone climber's complaint is slightly graver than each of the five's, and whether there is a stable position between pure pairwise comparison and unrestricted aggregation that survives both of their attacks.
