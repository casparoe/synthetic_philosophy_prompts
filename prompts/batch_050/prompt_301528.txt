I'm working on a long piece about what editors at my magazine have started calling "the one enthusiastic person problem" -- situations where a bunch of people each have the individual power to do something that affects everyone, and it only takes one of them to pull the trigger, so the thing basically happens as soon as the most eager person decides to go. The canonical cases keep coming up in my reporting: any one of several labs could try stratospheric geoengineering on its own, any one researcher could publish the dual-use pathogen paper, any one company could open-source its frontier model weights, any one juror in some old legal hypothetical could... you get the idea. Philosophers apparently have a name for the structure, the unilateralist's curse, and the standard observation is that even if every actor is well-meaning and reasonably well-informed, the more actors there are, the more the effective decision rule drifts toward "whatever the most optimistic or least risk-averse person thinks," which is nobody's actual preferred rule. My editors want me to write about what the right decision procedure would be, and I don't want to just relay the usual shrug of "coordinate more, I guess."

So before I talk to anyone, I want the spec. What would an adequate normative theory of unilateral action under shared risk actually have to deliver? Give me the desiderata: the cases it has to get right (including ones where the curse intuition seems right and ones where unilateral action is plainly heroic -- a whistleblower, or the one doctor willing to try the treatment), the distinctions it can't collapse (action vs. disclosure, reversible vs. irreversible, effects on consenting vs. non-consenting parties, "one actor can act" vs. "any of many could"), and the uncomfortable facts it has to stay consistent with, like the fact that asking the group first often just advertises the option to whoever was on the fence. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether you think it's a genuine constraint or a prejudice of one camp (consequentialists, deontologists, the governance crowd, the epistemic-deference people). The thing I most want flagged: any pair of desiderata that look jointly unsatisfiable -- for instance I have a nagging feeling that "the rule shouldn't just be whoever is most gung-ho" and "the rule has to be one that gung-ho people will actually follow" don't sit well together, but maybe that's wrong.

Then, briefly, score the obvious candidate fixes against the list -- strict expected-value maximization, a deliberative-poll-the-affected-parties rule, a strong presumption against irreversible unilateral acts, and the "you may act only if you'd want everyone in your position to act" style generalization test -- but keep the scoring short, since the criteria are the actual story. If it turns out no rule survives the desiderata intact, that is itself the headline and I'd rather know now.
