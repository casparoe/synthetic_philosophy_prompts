You are a philosopher of social science with particular expertise in game-theoretic accounts of convention and social norms — David Lewis's analysis in Convention (1969) and the literature that grew out of it, including work on signaling, the emergence and evolution of norms, and accounts that classify norms by the structure of the expectations that sustain them. I need your help reconstructing the classificatory scheme of a colleague of mine. Please work carefully: reconstruct the structure of each practice before attempting any classification, brainstorm before committing to an answer, and wherever the evidence genuinely underdetermines the conclusion, say so plainly rather than forcing a resolution.

My colleague has spent years sorting social practices into conventions, social norms, and neither. I have six recorded verdicts from her, stated with the facts she took to be salient:

1. Driving on the right-hand side of the road in the United States: a convention. She noted the legal requirement but held it irrelevant, since the practice holds on unmarked roads and in parking lots where no one enforces anything.
2. Accepting US dollars in payment for goods: a convention.
3. Greeting acquaintances with a handshake rather than a bow: a convention.
4. Forming an orderly queue at a bus stop: not a convention — a social norm. The salient fact for her is that queue-jumpers meet open disapproval and sometimes confrontation, and she maintains that the practice would dissolve without that enforcement.
5. At an open-air concert, everyone standing once the front rows stand, even though everyone would see better if all remained seated: not a convention. She calls it "a trap, not a convention," and points out that no one sanctions sitters — each person stands simply because, given that the people in front are standing, remaining seated leaves them unable to see.
6. Promise-keeping: she declines to classify it as either a convention or a norm, saying it is "doing different work," because people keep promises partly from conditional expectation, partly from conviction, and partly from fear of disapproval, and she cannot say which motive is doing the work.

One thing to note before you begin: the unmodified Lewisian rule — a convention is an arbitrary regularity in the solution of a recurrent coordination problem, sustained by each agent's preference to conform conditional on others conforming, with all of this common knowledge — already misfits her record. On Lewis's definition the standing crowd at the concert is a convention: given that almost everyone stands, each person prefers to stand; all-sitting is an available alternative equilibrium with the same conditional-preference structure; and the definition contains no efficiency clause. Yet she rejects the classification. Her scheme is therefore some revision of Lewis, and your first task is to figure out which.

I now need her verdicts on two new practices:

(A) Every engineer at a software firm saves and exchanges files in an outdated format because everyone else does; anyone who switched unilaterally would simply be unable to exchange work with colleagues. No rule requires the old format, no one is sanctioned for experimenting with a modern one, and everyone agrees the firm would be better off if all migrated together — but no one has.

(B) At sporting events in a certain country, the entire crowd stands for the national anthem. No one sanctions or even frowns at the few who remain seated. Those who stand report two things: that they would feel foolish standing alone, and that standing is simply the proper thing to do — they would feel they had done something slightly wrong if they sat while everyone else stood.

Here is what I want from you:

1. Brainstorm at least two candidate rules — reconstructed principles of classification — each consistent with all six recorded verdicts. For each candidate, state the rule precisely (which features of a practice it treats as classificatorily relevant) and show, verdict by verdict, that it reproduces her record. I expect at least one candidate to turn on the operative motive for compliance and at least one to turn on the equilibrium structure of the practice, but do not feel bound by that expectation if your brainstorm produces something better.

2. For each candidate rule, state what it predicts about practices (A) and (B). I expect the candidates to diverge on at least one of these; exhibit the divergence and trace it to the precise feature on which the rules differ. In the case of (B), address explicitly whether internalized disapproval — feeling that one has done something slightly wrong, absent any external sanction — should count as a sanction for purposes of each rule.

3. Give me your best prediction of her actual verdicts on (A) and (B), with reasons. Which candidate rule is more likely to be hers, given the texture of her recorded remarks — her insistence on the queue's enforcement, her "trap" language, her refusal to classify promise-keeping?

4. Describe a single further practice, which you are free to invent, whose classification would most efficiently discriminate among the surviving candidate rules, and state what each rule would say about it.

5. Finally, two assessment questions. First: which candidate rule is closest to Lewis's own analysis, and where exactly does her scheme depart from his — is her treatment of the standing crowd a correction of Lewis or a clarification of him? Second: her scheme is meant to be useful for describing how practices evolve, in particular how a mere convention can harden into a norm as disapproval attaches to deviation, and how a legally codified convention relates to its uncodified ancestor. For each candidate rule, say whether the rule can even represent such transitions: does the category of a practice change as its enforcement structure or its operative motives change, even when observable behavior is completely unchanged? Is that consequence a virtue of the scheme (it tracks moralization) or a defect (it makes classification hostage to motivational facts that observers cannot readily see)?

Before classifying any practice, set out its structure explicitly: the parties, their options, the available equilibria, the operative motives, and any sanctions, external or internalized. Wherever her six verdicts genuinely underdetermine the rule she is running, identify the underdetermination rather than papering over it.
