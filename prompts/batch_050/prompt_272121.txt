So I'm a second-year PhD student in economic history and my advisor wants a one-page statement of my position on the Great Divergence before I start the prospectus, mostly so she can attack it. Before I send it in I want an internal consistency check, because I have a nagging feeling some of these don't sit well together but I can't tell where the actual problem is. Here's where I've landed:

1. The divergence was late and surprising. Around 1750 the Yangzi Delta and other advanced parts of China were roughly comparable to the richest parts of Europe in living standards, market integration, and commercialization. Any story that builds in deep, centuries-old European superiority is a nonstarter for me.

2. What broke the symmetry was Britain's cheap, accessible coal plus its New World periphery — land, timber, cotton, sugar, the whole "ghost acres" thing — which relaxed ecological constraints that were biting in China too. So: a lot of contingency and resource luck, and it could easily have gone otherwise.

3. Still, I'm basically an institutionalist about long-run growth. Secure property rights and constraints on rulers are what explain why some places get rich and stay rich, and geography and culture are at most background conditions.

4. I also think the Scientific Revolution really mattered causally — the spread of "useful knowledge" and a culture of open inquiry is part of why British innovation kept compounding instead of petering out the way earlier efflorescences did (Song China, the Dutch golden age, whatever).

5. At the same time, I think the Needham question is badly posed. Asking why China failed to produce modern science assumes there's one ladder of development with Europe as the norm and China as the deviation, and I don't think history should be written backwards from a European endpoint like that.

6. And I want nothing to do with Weber-style cultural explanations — Protestant ethic, Confucian conservatism, all of that. Empirically dead as far as I'm concerned.

Go through these pairwise and tell me which committments genuinely pull against each other, which ones only look like they do, and how bad each real tension is. For each genuine tension, give me the cheapest revision that would fix it and what that revision costs me in terms of what I can still say. Also flag the apparent clashes that dissolve on inspection, because my advisor will definately try to pin those on me too and I'd like to be ready. Then finish by telling me which single change to the set would clean up the most overall. Don't just flag everything — I need the audit to discriminate, not to be maximally harsh.
