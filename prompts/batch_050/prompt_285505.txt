John Taurek, in his 1977 paper "Should the Numbers Count?", argued that when you must choose between saving one stranger and saving five strangers, and you cannot save all six, you are not morally required to save the five just because they are more numerous; he suggested that flipping a coin would be no worse. Set aside his positive proposal about coin-flipping and consider only the negative claim at the heart of the aggregation debate. I would like your credence in the following proposition, stated precisely: in a standard rescue case — you can save either one stranger or five strangers from otherwise certain death, all six are morally on a par, no special obligations or prior commitments are in play, and saving one group forecloses saving the other — the fact that the five are more numerous gives you a genuine moral reason, of some weight, to save them rather than the one.

Please give me a number, but derive it rather than stating it. Decompose the credence along lines like the following, adjusting the structure if you think mine is wrong: (i) your probability that some form of consequentialism is correct as a theory of rightness, at least for choices like this one; (ii) your probability, conditional on consequentialism being false, that a non-consequentialist framework — deontological, contractualist, or otherwise — can still vindicate counting the numbers in such pairwise rescue cases, i.e., that Taurek's anti-aggregation argument fails even on broadly non-consequentialist grounds; (iii) any further term you think belongs in the combination, such as the probability that one's case intuitions here track the moral truth rather than the framing of the example. Give each term a number with one or two lines of justification, show how the terms combine, and state the resulting credence. Then identify the term to which your final number is most sensitive, and say what argument, distinction, or thought experiment would move it most in either direction.

Throughout, please keep separate what is settled among people who work on this debate — for instance, that consequentialism straightforwardly implies saving the five, and that the live dispute is about whether non-consequentialists can say the same — from what is genuinely contested, such as whether Taurek's separateness-of-persons reasoning succeeds, whether contractualism can ground aggregation, and whether "weighted" or mixed proposals are coherent. Do not present any of the contested positions as the consensus.
