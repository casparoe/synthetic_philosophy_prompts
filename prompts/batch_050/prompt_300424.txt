I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book about a fictional Atlantic region voting on independence, and my six main characters keep landing in inconsistant positions when they argue about it. The worst offender is a retired schoolmaster who's built his entire politics on the chapter on nationality in Mill's Considerations on Representative Government — the bit where Mill argues that wherever a people share a strong sense of nationality there's a prima facie case for their having a government of their own, and that this amounts to little more than saying the question of government ought to be settled by the governed.

To keep everyone straight I want a decision tree that walks someone from "my region wants out" to a named position on when secession is justified. The leaves I care about: remedial-rights-only views (secession as a remedy for grave injustice, never otherwise), primary-right or choice theories (a majority in a defined territory may simply decide), national self-determination proper, and a territorial-integrity view where exit needs the parent state's consent full stop. Six to eight leaves is plenty.

Two conditions. First, the branch nodes have to be questions my characters could answer without knowing any labels, and they should run through real cases so the positions genuinely come apart: Scotland in 2014, where Westminster agreed a process; Quebec's referendums and the clarity wrangling that followed; Catalonia in 2017, where Madrid refused everything. Does consent of the state matter, or only the local majority? Second, the Mill passage has to do real work: mark where the schoolmaster ends up, and tell me whether his favourite argument actually commits him to letting any bare majority vote itself out of anywhere — which he'd loathe, since in my draft he's a staunch unionist about Ireland (long story). Note any combination of answers nobody seems to defend and say whether it's incoherent or merely unfashionable, and flag any two leaves your tree can't really tell apart.
