A doctoral student in my seminar gave me this argument, and I told her it proves too much, but then had trouble saying exactly why. Suppose a two-arm randomized trial is monitored by people who can see the accumulating outcome data:

1. Randomizing a patient is ethical only while the trial remains in equipoise: a responsible clinician could assign either treatment without knowingly giving that patient worse expected care.
2. A clinician knowingly gives worse expected care once the available evidence makes one arm more likely than the other to be clinically superior, even if the evidence has not yet reached the trial’s preannounced standard for a “confirmed” result.
3. In any trial that eventually establishes that A is better than B, the accumulating evidence must pass through an earlier stage at which A is already more probable than B, but the stricter confirmatory threshold has not yet been met.
4. So equipoise has already been lost before the trial can produce its intended result.
5. Continuing to randomize after that point is unethical; stopping as soon as the balance tips will often mean stopping on noise and never producing reliable evidence.
6. Therefore, conventional fixed-size randomized trials are either unethical or evidentially too weak.

This seems to generalize into an argument that medicine cannot ethically learn which treatments work by randomizing, which can’t be right. Which premise or step do you reject? Please explain why it initially looks plausible, how your diagnosis handles the next patient enrolled after the evidence starts tipping, and what theoretical or practical cost your preferred rejection has.
