A colleague argues that the Allais paradox poses no real challenge to expected utility theory, and his argument disturbs me because it seems to prove far too much. Recall the setup: in pair one, choose between a certain $1M and a lottery giving 89% $1M, 10% $5M, 1% nothing; in pair two, between an 11% chance of $1M (else nothing) and a 10% chance of $5M (else nothing). I take the sure $1M in pair one and the 10% shot at $5M in pair two. His argument:

(1) A pattern of choice violates expected utility theory only if it cannot be represented as maximizing expected utility over the outcomes the agent actually cares about.
(2) What I care about in pair one is not just money: part of what the risky option offers is a 1% chance of ending up with nothing when $1M was mine for the taking, and the dread of that outcome is genuinely decision-relevant.
(3) So "ending with nothing" in pair one is a different outcome from "ending with nothing" in pair two, where no certainty was passed up; outcomes must be individuated by everything that matters to the agent, and this is one of those things.
(4) With outcomes individuated this way, my two choices maximize expected utility over the outcomes as I conceive them, and no axiom is violated.
(5) The same maneuver is available for any apparent counterexample: whatever an agent chooses, outcomes can be described finely enough that the choice maximizes expected utility over them.
(6) Therefore the independence axiom, properly understood, has no counterexamples at all -- Allais included.

If (6) is right, I don't see what work the axiom was ever doing, and (5) seems to me to give the game away: a theory compatible with every possible choice pattern predicts nothing. But when I press him on (3), he says he is only taking seriously what I myself told him I care about, and he is right that (2) is true of me. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like to keep (2) if I can -- regret is real and I don't want a decision theory that legislates it out of existence -- but I want to know what holding that line commits me to, and whether my original pair of preferences can survive it.
