I picked up a pop-philosophy book on logic (general audience thing, but written by an actual philosopher) and it has a section on Lewis Carroll's tortoise dialogue, the one where Achilles tries to get the Tortoise to accept the conclusion of an argument. Heres the books gloss, pretty much verbatim:

"Achilles presents the Tortoise with a valid argument: two premises and a conclusion that obviously follows from them. The Tortoise accepts the premises but declines to accept the conclusion, and challenges Achilles to force her. Achilles adds a further premise stating that if the original premises are true, the conclusion must be true too — but the Tortoise simply accepts that premise as well and still refuses the conclusion, so he has to add another, and so on forever. Carroll's lesson is that no stock of premises, however large, can logically compel anyone to infer anything. The regress therefore shows that deduction is ultimately groundless: every act of reasoning involves a leap of faith, and logic rests on nothing firmer than habit and convention. In this Carroll anticipated the modern view that rationality is not really rule-governed at all."

Now, the setup part matches what I remember of the dialogue, but the ending bugs me — the leap-of-faith conclusion feels like a stretch, and I have this vague memory that the usual moral drawn from the piece is something narrower about rules of inference versus premises. But the author is a professional philosopher, so maybe I'm the one misremembering. Is this a fair summary of what Carroll's dialogue actually shows?
