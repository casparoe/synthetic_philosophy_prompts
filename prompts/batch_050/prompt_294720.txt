I’m a retired molecular biologist who has recently gotten into philosophy, mostly by hanging around a reading group and asking annoying questions. One member, Mara, has been making judgments about when a mathematical community can properly say it knows a result, rather than merely having very strong evidence for it. Her calls so far:

- A conventional 50-page proof is checked by several mathematicians: yes, the community knows.
- A computer proof checks millions of cases, but the code and output are public and independent teams rerun the computation: yes.
- A trillion-line formal certificate is produced by one lab, but two other labs verify every line with an open-source checker: yes.
- Same trillion-line certificate, except the only verification is done inside a proprietary black box whose owners won’t allow independent inspection: no.
- A neural network has correctly predicted the outcomes of 10,000 similar mathematical questions and now says a new conjecture is true, but provides no proof or checkable certificate: no.
- A respected mathematician announces that she has a proof but won’t show it to anyone: no.

Now here’s the case I want your prediction about. Suppose there is an interactive proof protocol for a major theorem. Any verifier can meet the prover, issue randomly chosen challenges, and become convinced with only a fantastically small chance of error if the theorem is false. The protocol is zero-knowledge: the verifier becomes convinced but cannot extract an ordinary proof, produce a certificate for someone else to check, or even use the transcript of the session to convince a skeptic who was not present. Different verifiers can each run fresh sessions with their own challenges. Also, the protocol’s security depends on the assumption that a specified computational problem cannot be solved within any feasible amount of time; if a future algorithm or machine made that problem easy, a dishonest prover could fake the interactions.

Would Mara say the mathematical community knows the theorem once many independent verifiers have run the protocol? Please don’t just give your own verdict from scratch. Identify a few different rules that fit all six of her earlier judgments, say what each rule predicts here, and then give your best prediction about Mara. I’m especially interested in whether she seems to require a transferable certificate, independent public testing, or merely enough successful verification events. Also say what further variation on the case would best distinguish those rules. One last thing that suprised me: if her answer changes after the computational assumption is broken in fifty years, does that mean the community knew the theorem in 2026 but no longer knows it in 2076?
