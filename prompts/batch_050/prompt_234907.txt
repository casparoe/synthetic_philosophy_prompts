I'm drafting a term paper for a graduate seminar in population ethics, and my group has been wrestling with a case I invented that I can't stop thinking about. Here it is: imagine a benevolent but eccentric civilization, the Archivists, who have discovered that consciousness can be re-instantiated from sufficiently detailed records. They begin making copies — every happy life in their society is recorded and re-run in simulation, once, then a hundred times, then according to a schedule that never terminates, so that in the limit each life is lived infinitely many times over, with each copy genuinely experiencing everything the original did. Suppose, as physicists in this scenario confirm, that the wider universe also contains infinitely many independently existing sentient beings spread through unbounded space, and that the total amount of welfare in reality was already infinite before the Archivists ran a single copy. My question is what, if anything, the Archivists' project accomplishes morally.

Please argue that in settings like this one — where total welfare is infinite no matter what anyone does — an adequate ethical theory must be what I'll call patch-local: it should evaluate actions solely by what they change within the causal region accessible to the agent, treating the unbounded remainder of the universe as morally inert background. Your argument should explain why the natural alternatives fail: why totalism collapses into paralysis (every option leaves the infinite total unchanged), why simply averaging or discounting over beings smuggles in arbitrary weightings that can be made to endorse anything, and why appeal to the Archivists' "adding duplicates" intuition — the sense that running the copies surely does *something* — actually supports patch-localism rather than undermining it, since what the copies change is precisely the local causal structure.

I'd also like you to anticipate and answer the two strongest objections I'll face in seminar. First, that patch-localism is just a euphemism for ignoring distant suffering, and would tell an agent who could affect an infinite region that her actions matter no more than one who can affect a village. Second, that "causal accessibility" is a matter of degree and of physics rather than of moral significance, so any theory built on it inherits an embarrassing arbitrariness about where the boundary lies. Conclude by stating the strongest version of the thesis you can actually defend, even if it ends up weaker than full patch-localism — I'd rather submit a chastened argument than a bold one that doesn't survive scrutiny.
