I've been mulling over a question that came up in a discussion thread about the Newton–Leibniz priority dispute, and I realized I don't have a stable position on it. The standard rationalist-adjacent take is something like: ideas whose time has come get discovered by multiple people independently (calculus, natural selection, the telephone), so individual genius is mostly replaceable and priority fights are status games over an arbitrary Schelling point. But there's a dissenting view I find harder to dismiss than my friends seem to: even if *someone* would have figured it out eventually, the counterfactual delay might be enormous, and the actual discoverer's particular framing might shape an entire field for centuries (imagine calculus had been developed from Leibniz's notation alone, or natural selection from a Lamarckian starting point). To sharpen this, I came up with a rough metric I call "counterfactual latency": for a given discovery, estimate how many years it would have taken for the idea to reach comparable acceptance if the actual discoverer had died in infancy. High-latency discoveries (where the field was stuck waiting on one person's idiosyncratic insight) seem to justify credit and historical attention; low-latency ones (where five people were racing to the same finish line) seem like cases where priority is basically a lottery and our obsession with "who was first" is irrational.

Write a dialogue between two equally sharp interlocutors who disagree about this. One defends the inevitabilist view: simultaneous invention shows that discoveries are overwhelmingly low-latency, that apparent lone geniuses are just the visible tip of a distributed process, and that credit-based institutions mostly misallocate prestige and resources. The other defends the contingentist view: the simultaneous-invention cases are survivorship-biased exceptions, counterfactual latency is usually high and systematically underestimated, and attributing discoveries to individuals is not mere bookkeeping but tracks real differences in how science unfolds. Both should treat counterfactual latency as a legitimate construct and argue over how to estimate it — e.g., what we'd infer from the fact that Wallace sent Darwin that letter, whether the right comparison class is "all ideas" or "ideas at the frontier," and whether a discovery's framing-dependence (the Newton-vs-Leibniz notation point) raises or lowers its latency.

The dialogue should have real back-and-forth: each character must respond to the other's actual arguments, concede points where conceding is warranted, and press on genuine weak spots rather than strawmanning. By the end, they don't need to agree, but they should have identified at least one concrete crux — a fact about the world or a measurement one could in principle make that would move them in opposite directions.
