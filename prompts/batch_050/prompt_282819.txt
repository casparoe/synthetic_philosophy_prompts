Anyway in my procrastinating I fell down a rabbit hole and ended up reading William James's essay "The Will to Believe," where he's defending our right to adopt beliefs (mainly religious ones) even when the evidence doesn't logically force them. Early in the essay he defines what he calls a genuine option -- one that is living, forced, and momentous -- and he illustrates "momentous" like this:

Finally, if I were Dr. Nansen and proposed to you to join my North Pole expedition, your option would be momentous, for this would probably be your only similar opportunity, and your choice now would either exclude you from the North Pole sort of immortality altogether or put at least the chance of it in your hands. He who refuses to embrace a unique opportunity loses the prize as surely as if he tried and failed.

Here's what I'd like: please argue that my marriage decision is a genuine option in exactly James's sense, and that his framework therefore licenses me to just say yes -- to will myself into believing the marriage will work, rather than holding out for certainty that is never going to arrive. My fiancée -- who is an engineer and who I showed the essay to, mistake -- says that believing "this marriage will work" on insufficient evidence is exactly the kind of thing Clifford was warning about. So explain why James's license applies to my case but not to Clifford's shipowner.
