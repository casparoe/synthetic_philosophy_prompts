Hey, so I'm in a phil of religion / applied ethics course right now and we're doing a unit on secularisation, and I keep coming back to something my prof mentioned in passing that I can't stop thinking about.

Here's the thing. In a lot of countries (the UK, the US, Canada, etc.) "advancement of religion" counts as a charitable purpose more or less on its own. A church or mosque gets charitable status and tax breaks basically for existing and doing religious stuff, while a secular organisation usually has to demonstrate some concrete public benefit -- relieving poverty, education, health, whatever. Some jurisdictions have been tightening this, but the baseline is still that religion gets a kind of presumption of goodness that secular activities don't get.

So my question: what does this practice actually presuppose philosophically? Like, what theory about religion's relationship to morality or social good makes that asymmetry make sense? Does it presuppose that religion makes people better in some sense -- and if so, what sense? More honest, more prosocial, more trustworthy, better at sustaining the kind of diffuse social trust that societies supposedly run on? Or does it presuppose something weaker, like religion being a private good worth subsidising the way we subsidise the arts, which would be a pretty different (and kind of deflating?) rationale?

Also: which rival views does the practice quietly rule out? E.g. if it turned out religion is morally neutral on average, or that secular societies sustain trust just fine through institutions, would the doctrine collapse or could it survive on other grounds? And are there inconsistencies here -- like, the same legal system that privileges religion in charity law is officially neutral between religions and between religion and non-religion in constitutional law, and I'm not sure those two stances fit together.

Some instructions for how to answer: please act as an expert philosopher who specialises in philosophy of religion and political theory. Think carefully before answering, and maybe start with a short brainstorm of the candidate implicit theories before settling into the analysis. Then reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories), distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit with, and be explicit about where the practice is underdetermined -- i.e. where several different theories would all licence the same doctrine. Finally, tell me concretely what would have to change in charity law if we swapped in a fully secular-neutral view where religion is just one private preference among others.
