So I've been thinking about Parfit's hitchhiker case again (stranded in the desert, driver will only take you to town if he believes you'll pay him once you're safe, and once you're safe paying is strictly irrational). I keep a running list of the standard moves people make here, and I want to stress-test it. Here's what I've got:

1. The disposition move: the rational thing is to be the kind of person who pays in these cases, so you should cultivate that disposition, and the case just shows that "act rationally in each moment" can make you worse off than "have rational character traits." Parfit's whole point about rational irrationality.
2. The precommitment move: if you could have bound yourself in advance you obviously would have, and there's no relevant difference between binding yourself then and just paying now.
3. The updateless move: you should act as the output of the decision procedure you'd have wanted all copies of yourself to be running, and that procedure pays, since drivers picking up that procedure is exactly what gets it rescued.
4. The skeptical move: the case cheats by assuming the driver reads your psychology perfectly. In the real world there's always uncertainty about whether you'd pay, so trustworthiness is just good expected value and the puzzle evaporates.
5. The iteration move: in real life these situations repeat and reputation exists, so paying is straightforwardly rational; the one-shot version is too artificial to teach us anything.

Am I missing a move that's genuinely distinct from these? I don't want a rewording or a combination of two of them (e.g. "be trustworthy because reputation" is just 5 wearing 1's clothes). If there is one, state it cleanly in a sentence or two, say which of my five it comes closest to and why it isn't actually that one, and give your honest take on whether it's any good or whether the list of five is really exhaustive.
