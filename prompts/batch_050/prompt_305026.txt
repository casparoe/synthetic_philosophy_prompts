In the literature on commitment races, the standard lesson drawn from Schelling-style analysis is that a party can gain advantage by visibly restricting its own future options — burning the boats. I understand this in the two-player, one-shot case. What I lack is a gut-level account of why commitment races remain wasteful — indeed, why they can exist at all — when both parties have common knowledge of rationality and can perfectly observe each other's commitments. If both of us can burn boats, and we both know that we both know it, the commitments seem like they should cancel out into a draw with no waste: each side simply declines to burn. Yet such races are treated as generically destructive. Please explain, at the level of intuition rather than formal derivation:

1. Why the mutual ability to commit does not cancel out. What exactly breaks the symmetry, and why "whoever commits first and credibly wins" does not collapse into "nobody commits" under common knowledge of rationality? I suspect the answer has something to do with the fact that credibility itself consumes resources, but I cannot make the argument precise.

2. The difference between commitments that pre-empt (locking in a position before the rival can occupy it) and commitments that merely raise the stakes (making one's own future backing-down costlier). My impression is that the second kind produces waste even when it "succeeds," whereas the first kind produces waste mainly through the haste it induces — but I cannot articulate why.

3. Why perfect observability of commitments can make the race worse rather than better. My naive model says transparency should allow the parties to coordinate away from escalation, yet the argument appears to run the other way: observability is what makes commitments credible in the first place, and hence worth making. I would like the intuition for why the coordination benefit fails to dominate.

4. Whether there is a principled line between a commitment race and an ordinary arms race, or whether the former is simply the latter redescribed at the level of decision procedures rather than capabilities. If the distinction is real, what observable feature of a competition tells you which one you are in?

A toy two-player story — for instance, two AI laboratories each deciding whether to publicly lock in a deployment date — is welcome if it carries the intuition, but I would like the explanation to generalize beyond any single example.
