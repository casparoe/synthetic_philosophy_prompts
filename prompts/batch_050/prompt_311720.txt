I got into a stupidly long argument with my roommate last week about how moral norms actually die. It started over dueling -- why did a practice that survived centuries of sermons against it collapse in a few decades in the 1800s? Her take was basically that the arguments finally won: the moral case got sharper, public opinion shifted, and men stopped showing up to the field. My take was that the arguments were mostly noise and what really did it was stuff like changing class interests, new ways to signal status, and the rising commercial classes having no use for aristocratic honor theater. We both knew the rough shape of the abolition-of-slavery debate too (did the economics make abolition cheap, or did abolitionists genuinely move people?), and we couldn't settle it, so I want to see the disagreement played out properly.

Write me a dialogue between two fictional historians of morality, Dr. Okafor and Dr. Lindqvist, who disagree about what kills norms. One of them holds that norms end when the surrounding honor code shifts -- the practice starts feeling ridiculous or low-status to the very people whose respect practitioners cared about, and moral arguments matter mainly as the vocabulary in which that shift happens. The other holds that honor talk is downstream decoration: norms end when the interests sustaining them disappear and new institutions make compliance expensive and defection cheap, and the moral rhetoric gets retrofitted afterward to flatter the winners. To keep them honest, don't let them just rehash slavery and dueling at the level of Wikipedia. Have them argue primarily about a made-up case I'll seed: the island republic of Varenne, where the guilds' practice of settling disputes through armed feuds ended within about fifteen years, during which three things happened at once -- a charismatic preacher's campaign against the feuds, a new mutual-insurance system that made feud participation financially ruinous, and the old elite adopting scholarly titles as their new status marker. Each character has to explain Varenne on their own theory AND explain why the other's reading of it fails.

Make both of them genuinely sharp -- no strawmen, and they have to actually answer each other's last point rather than delivering alternating monologues. By the end they should converge on what they actually disagree about (some fact about the world that would settle it, or an admission that their views make the same predictions about Varenne). Structure the response as: the dialogue itself (roughly 600-800 words, labeled speakers), then a section called "The actual crux" (~100 words) stating the one thing that would decide between them, then a section called "My ruling" (~80 words) where you say which of them explained Varenne better and why. Don't just split the difference in the ruling, pick a side.
