I don't have any background in this stuff but I've been going down a rabbit hole on the "low impact AI" idea — the thought that you could make an AI safe-ish by telling it to do its job but not change the world much in the process. I've been testing the idea against cases in my head and my gut verdicts are all over the place, so I want to know what rule, if any, I'm actually running.

Say there's a home robot whose job is to keep the house tidy. My verdicts:

1. It knocks a vase off the shelf while dusting and it smashes. Bad, obviously, the impact penalty should catch this.
2. The cat knocks the same vase off the shelf and the robot catches it. Here the robot changed what was going to happen (vase smashes either way if it just sits there) but catching it seems totally fine, even good.
3. The robot decides the safest thing is to just power itself off in a corner forever so it can't affect anything. Technically minimal impact, but clearly a failure — we bought a robot that does nothing.
4. The robot moves a chair to vacuum, then puts the chair back exactly where it was. Feels like it's gaming the metric but honestly seems fine? But if it could somehow "undo" everything — like it runs over the cat and then 3D-prints a perfect replacement cat — that's horrifying even if the house looks the same.
5. The robot stops me from rearranging my own furniture because me moving stuff changes the room and it wants to preserve the status quo. Definitely bad, preventing change is itself an interference.
6. Every time it vacuums it displaces millions of dust particles and slightly changes air currents and who knows what downstream effects. Nobody cares about this. But by any literal measure of "change to the world" it's doing a lot all the time.

So cases 1 and 2 seem to need "it's fine to change what would have happened if you do nothing, as long as the result is good" — but then who's judging what's good, isn't that just smuggling in a whole theory of value? And cases 4 and 6 seem to need something about reversibility plus some line between changes that matter and changes that don't, but I have no idea where that line is or what draws it.

Is there one principle that gets all six, or do I have to give one of them up? I'd like the principle stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing the work in which case. Also tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — like, the robot waters my plants while I'm on holiday (the plants would've died without it, so is that "impact" or "maintaining the status quo"?). And if there are two or three candidate rules that fit my verdicts but disagree on that one, lay them out.

One more ask: when you answer, be clear about what's basically settled among the people who work on this formally (impact measures, side-effect penalties, that whole literature) versus what's genuinely contested or unsolved. I don't want you presenting some researcher's pet proposal as if it's the accepted answer when it's actually one contested option among several.
