I'm a third-year taking a comparative politics course, and we just finished the unit on legitimacy. In seminar last week we got onto performance legitimacy -- the idea that a government without competitive elections can still be justified if it delivers growth, safety, clean streets, that kind of thing, with Singapore and China as the usual exhibits. One of my classmates (who I think had read an op-ed somewhere) defended the whole idea with an analogy he clearly thought settled it: a state like Singapore is basically a company -- the prime minister is the CEO, the citizens are shareholders, GDP growth and low crime are the dividends, and nobody demands that Apple hold shareholder votes on every decision, so why demand elections? If the dividends keep coming, the management stays. He said demanding democracy anyway is like selling a winning stock out of spite.

I pushed back but honestly kind of fumbled it, and it's bugging me, because the "run the state like a business" line comes up constantly in politics back home too (my uncle says it at Thanksgiving about the US, word for word). So: can you actually audit this analogy for me? Give me the full correspondence table -- what plays the role of the shareholders, the shares, the dividend, the board, the CEO's authority, the option to sell your stock and walk away -- and then go through where the mapping breaks down. I have a feeling the "you can just sell your shares" part does a lot of quiet work, since emigrating from a country is not really like selling stock, but I want the disanalogies laid out systematically, including any that work in the analogy's favour (maybe citizens-as-shareholders gets something right that citizens-as-customers doesn't?).

Once the disanalogies are on the table, does that conclusion survive, or was the analogy smuggling in exactly the premise that needed defending (i.e., that citizens' relationship to the state is really just an investor's relationship to returns)?
