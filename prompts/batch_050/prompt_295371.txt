I am a PhD candidate in philosophy of cognitive science, and my dissertation chapter concerns whether there are determinate facts about phenomenal character that outrun a subject's dispositions to report, discriminate, and remember. I want to settle my own position by reflective equilibrium rather than by literature review, so here are my considered verdicts on six cases. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures them.

Case 1 (the coffee tasters). Two professional coffee tasters have sampled the same blend for years. One reports that the blend tastes exactly as it always has but that he has ceased to enjoy that taste. The other reports that he still enjoys the taste and that the blend now tastes different to him than it used to. Their discriminative abilities, memories, and behavior are identical. My verdict: neither is right and neither is wrong. There is no fact of the matter about whether the phenomenal character changed or only the attitude toward it; any test that could be run would fit both descriptions equally well.

Case 2 (the undetectable inversion). I can conceive of a subject whose experiences of red and green are swapped overnight while every functional parameter — memory, reporting, discrimination, emotional response — is perfectly preserved. My verdict: this is not merely conceivable but describes a genuinely possible state of affairs, and if it obtained, something real would have changed even though no one, including the subject, could detect it.

Case 3 (the acquired taste). Someone who once disliked beer now enjoys it. Asked whether the beer tastes different now or tastes the same and is simply liked, they find the question unanswerable. My verdict: their bafflement is correct. The two descriptions are notational variants of a single functional change, and the demand to choose between them is a pseudo-demand.

Case 4 (pain without the hurt). Patients under certain conditions — for instance after particular frontal lesions, or under morphine — sincerely report that the painful sensation is still present but no longer bothers them. My verdict: this is a real and intelligible dissociation. The qualitative character of pain and its aversiveness are distinct components, and a theory that builds the aversiveness into the sensation by definition is simply mistaken.

Case 5 (the silicon replacement). A subject's neurons are replaced one by one with functionally identical silicon components until no biological tissue remains. Throughout, their speech, reports, and behavior are unchanged, and they never notice anything. My verdict: it is incoherent to suppose that their experience gradually faded or winked out along the way. If every disposition is preserved, there is nothing left for fading to consist in; experience is preserved throughout.

Case 6 (the one I cannot settle). A patient undergoes a procedure under a drug that blocks the formation of episodic memories. During the procedure they flinch and grimace; afterward they behave and speak exactly as someone who felt nothing, and they remember nothing. My verdict: I genuinely do not know whether they suffered. My intuitions from Cases 1, 3, and 5 suggest that the later functional profile is all there is, so no suffering occurred in any sense that matters; my intuition from Case 2 suggests there could be a fact of suffering that leaves no trace.

So: is there a single principle of phenomenal fact-determination that yields my verdicts on Cases 1 through 5, and what does it say about Case 6? If no such principle exists, tell me which verdict is cheapest to abandon and why. I suspect the conflict runs between Case 2 on the one hand and Cases 1, 3, and 5 on the other, and I would like to know whether Case 4 forces a third option.
