I am a philosophy professor whose research is in democratic theory -- legitimacy, consent, representation of future generations, emergency powers, exit and voice -- and I am trying to determine whether the governance debate over stratospheric aerosol injection is genuinely a new problem for political theory or merely an old one in unfamiliar dress. My grasp of the climate-policy literature is that of an interested outsider: I understand the moral hazard concern (that the prospect of a technological fix erodes mitigation efforts), the termination shock concern (that abruptly stopping a deployed system, after temperatures have been suppressed for decades, would produce rapid warming far more disruptive than gradual warming would have been), and the unilateral deployment concern (that a single state, or even a wealthy individual, could alter the climate for everyone, since the direct costs are within the reach of many actors).

Please restate these three concerns, and the debate about who may legitimately decide on deployment, in the vocabulary of democratic theory -- consent and its impossibility at planetary scale, trusteeship for future persons, principal-agent problems, emergency powers and their ratchet effects, exit versus voice, the boundary problem of who belongs to the demos. Translate as faithfully as you can, without quietly importing democratic theory's own commitments and presenting them as part of the original debate. Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually care about: which elements of each concern map cleanly onto concepts I already have, which can only be expressed by distorting something (tell me what gets distorted), and which have no expression in this vocabulary at all. I have in mind, as a candidate for the last category, the fact that termination shock is governed by physical timescales rather than political ones -- but tell me whether that intuition survives scrutiny, and whether any residue I cannot anticipate points to a gap in democratic theory itself or to loose talk in the policy literature.
