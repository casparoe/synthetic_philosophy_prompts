Act as a moral philosopher with expertise in the ethics of emerging technology. I am a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and after years of following discussions about digital minds and the simulation of the dead, I constructed the argument below and now distrust it, because it seems to prove too much. Please think carefully, and respond in British English.

(1) A dead person cannot be harmed or wronged: once someone ceases to exist, there is no subject in whom a harm or wrong could inhere, and posthumous changes to reputation, memory, or the use of their data are changes in the world of the living only.
(2) If the dead cannot be harmed or wronged, then the ethics of griefbots — AI systems trained on a dead person's writings, messages, and recordings to simulate conversation with them — reduces entirely to effects on the living.
(3) Among the living, a competent adult who knowingly interacts with a simulation of their dead relative consents to whatever comfort or distress follows, and third parties are at most indirectly affected.
(4) Where the affected parties consent and no third party is substantially harmed, there is nothing morally objectionable and nothing that warrants restriction.
(5) Therefore griefbots used voluntarily by the bereaved are morally unproblematic, full stop.
(6) But the same machinery, applied consistently, yields further results: breaking a promise made to someone on their deathbed is permissible the moment they die, since no subject remains to be wronged; disregarding the wishes expressed in a will is at most a legal inconvenience; and a company could ethically licence a simulation of your dead mother to strangers for mockery or advertising, since she cannot be harmed and, on a narrow reading of (3), neither can you. These conclusions seem absurd.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? If forced to choose, I would rather keep (5) — the permissibility of consensual griefbot use — than accept that deathbed promises lose all force at death, but I want to know what that preference commits me to. Identify the weakest step or premise, explain why it seemed plausible, and price the main alternatives, including positions that accept posthumous harm, interest-based accounts of wronging the dead, and views on which the wrong lies elsewhere entirely.
