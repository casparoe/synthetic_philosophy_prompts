My father has been diagnosed with a degenerative condition that will progressively destroy his hippocampus and eventually much of his cortex. His neurologists have offered an experimental treatment: a staged prosthesis in which small regions of failing tissue are replaced, one at a time, with synthetic components engineered to replicate the input-output behavior of the tissue they substitute for. In the course of deciding whether to counsel him to consent, I wrote down an argument that initially reassured me and now alarms me, because its conclusion seems far stronger than anything I am prepared to believe. I would like to know where it goes wrong, if it does.

(1) What matters for my father's survival — for whether the person who wakes up after a procedure is still him — is the continuity of his psychology: his memories, temperament, intentions, and dispositions. The material realizing that psychology is irrelevant; a heart transplant does not end a life, and neither would an artificial liver.

(2) Replacing a single neuron with a functional duplicate preserves psychological continuity completely. To deny this would imply that every ordinary neuronal death is a partial death of the person, which is absurd given how much turnover a healthy brain already undergoes.

(3) A sequence of changes, each of which fully preserves survival, fully preserves survival: if the person after the first replacement is him, and the person after the second is the person after the first, then the person after the final replacement is him. There is no non-arbitrary threshold at which survival suddenly fails.

(4) Therefore the staged prosthesis preserves my father even if it eventually replaces his entire brain, provided his behavior and inner life continue uninterrupted throughout the process.

(5) But the intermediate stages appear to be doing no real work in this argument: if the endpoint is him, it would be him regardless of the path taken to reach it. So any procedure ending in a functional duplicate preserves him — including instantaneous scanning, destruction of the original, and reconstruction elsewhere.

(6) And a functional duplicate is simply a pattern: anything organized so as to instantiate the same functional organization counts, whether it is made of silicon, gears and water pipes, or a vast team of clerks following lookup tables with pencil and paper.

(7) So my father's survival requires only that the correct pattern be instantiated somewhere, in any medium, at any speed. A sufficiently detailed functional description of his brain, executed by whatever means, is him, alive.

I cannot accept (7). It would make him unkillable by any means short of erasing every record of his brain's organization, and it would make his survival a matter of what happens to exist in archives. Yet I cannot locate the step I am actually willing to reject. I am most reluctant to give up (2), since the alternative seems to be that some precise amount of replacement kills him, which strikes me as indefensibly arbitrary; and (1) is the very premise on which the case for the treatment rests.

Please identify the step at which the argument goes wrong — or, if it is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to abandon and what exactly I must concede in abandoning it. In particular, I would like to know: is the slide from (4) to (5) legitimate, or does the gradualness of the path matter in a way my reasoning dismisses? Does (6) genuinely follow from the functionalism assumed in the earlier steps, or does it smuggle in an additional and contestable claim? And if I retain (1) through (4) while rejecting the rest, am I left with a principled position or merely with a line drawn at the point where I started to feel uncomfortable?
