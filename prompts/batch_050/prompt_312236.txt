I write a magazine column on science and ideas, and I'm currently drafting a long essay about a pattern I keep seeing in reporting on work: the products people make coming back at them as a kind of hostile force. A warehouse worker's own scan-rate history is used to discipline her; a call-center veteran trains the software that will monitor the next hire; a freelance illustrator's portfolio feeds the image model now undercutting her rates. In an early draft I called this "alienation," and a fact-checker at the magazine asked, reasonably, whether I was using the word in anything like Marx's sense or just as a synonym for feeling bad about your job. So I went back to the Economic and Philosophical Manuscripts of 1844, the chapter usually called "Estranged Labour," and I keep getting stuck on one passage, which I'll reproduce from the translation I've been reading: "This fact expresses merely that the object which labor produces – labor's product – confronts it as something alien, as a power independent of the producer. The product of labor is labor which has been embodied in an object, which has become material: it is the objectification of labor. Labor's realization is its objectification. Under these economic conditions this realization of labor appears as loss of realization for the workers; objectification as loss of the object and bondage to it; appropriation as estrangement, as alienation." He adds: "So much does objectification appear as loss of the object that the worker is robbed of the objects most necessary not only for his life but for his work."

What I can't resolve is what, exactly, is doing the work in the phrase "under these economic conditions." Marx seems to say that objectification as such is just what labor is – you make something, and your activity is realized in it – and that this is neutral or even good. But then the very same objectification is redescribed as loss, bondage, estrangement. As I read and reread the passage, I keep sliding among three interpretations and can't tell which the text actually supports. First, that the whole problem is ownership: the product is alienating simply because it belongs to someone else, so "these economic conditions" just means private property. Second, that the problem is causal and structural: the product is alienating because, accumulated as capital, it becomes the very thing that will dominate the worker in the next round of production, regardless of what the worker feels about it. Third, that Marx is making a claim about the worker's inner life – that labor which ought to be self-realization is experienced as self-loss, and "appears as" is doing real phenomenological work. These readings pull apart in ways that matter for my essay: on the first, my gig-work examples are alienation in the strict sense only insofar as the platform owns the ratings data; on the second, the trained model that automates the illustrator is almost a paradigmatic case; on the third, I'd need evidence about how workers actually experience these systems, which is not what my draft assumed. I'm also confused about a claim Marx makes a little later in the same chapter, if I'm reading it rightly: that private property is better understood as the result of alienated labor than as its cause. That seems to get the obvious causal story backwards, and I'd like to understand whether the move is already implicit in the passage above or is a genuinely separate step.

Could you walk me through the passage and say which of my three readings it best supports, or whether they can be reconciled? I would also like your judgment on whether the later inversion about private property follows from anything here or simply has to be taken on faith as a further thesis. Since this is for publication, I'd be grateful if you distinguished clearly between what the text straightforwardly says, what is your best-supported interpretation of it, and where serious readers of Marx disagree – I can't afford to present a contested reading as settled. And please push back if the problem is on my end: if my three readings trade on a conflation, if the question I'm asking of the passage isn't one it can answer, or if applying a manuscript from 1844 to algorithmic management stretches the concept past the point where it does any explanatory work, I'd much rather be told that plainly than receive a polite answer I then put in print.
