I'm a journalist who covers behavioural science, and I'm drafting a long essay on whether loss aversion and the endowment effect are best understood as cognitive biases or as legitimate features of human preference. Before I file it, I want to know whether the positions I've staked out actually cohere. Here they are, roughly in the order they appear in the piece:

1. Loss aversion is real and robust. People weigh losses more heavily than comparable gains, and this is not merely an artefact of a few famous experiments with mugs and pens; it shows up across enough settings that I'm comfortable treating it as a settled feature of human psychology.

2. The endowment effect — the persistent gap between what people demand to give up a good and what they'd pay to acquire it — is best explained as loss aversion operating on ownership: once something is mine, parting with it is coded as a loss relative to my reference point.

3. Reference-dependent preferences are not, as such, irrational. Calling loss aversion a "bias" presupposes some reference-free standpoint of true preference that doesn't exist. The debate in my headline should therefore be resolved toward "legitimate preference," not "bias."

4. That said, I endorse and praise choice-architecture interventions that exploit the status quo bias — opt-out organ donation, automatic pension enrolment — on the grounds that status quo bias is a bias worth working around to improve welfare.

5. Reference points are not fixed. They're set by expectations, recent experience, and framing, and they are readily manipulable by marketers, experimenters, and policymakers. I treat this lability as one of the best-established findings in the area.

6. Because experiments reveal genuine preferences, measured willingness-to-accept should be usable as real evidence in policy contexts — for instance, in valuing environmental goods in cost-benefit analysis — rather than dismissed as experimental noise.

7. I note in the essay that experienced traders show markedly reduced endowment effects, and I present this as evidence that the effect is partly learned noise that washes out with sophistication.

Please audit this set for internal consistency. Identify which pairs or triples of commitments are in genuine tension, ordered from most to least severe; for each, say what exactly pulls against what and what the cheapest revision would be — which single commitment I'd have to soften or drop, and what that would cost the essay's argument. Also flag any tensions that look real but dissolve on inspection, and explain why they dissolve. I don't want a lecture on the literature; I want to know where the piece contradicts itself and what it would take to fix it. Note that I haven't decided which commitments I'd sacrifice, so don't assume any one of them is untouchable.
