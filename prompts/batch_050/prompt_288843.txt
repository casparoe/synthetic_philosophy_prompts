Here's my situation. For the last seven years I've helped run a small annual fair where about thirty winemakers making what everyone calls "natural wine" come to pour and sell. When we started out, natural wine just meant, roughly: grapes grown without synthetic sprays, fermented with whatever yeast is naturally hanging around on the grapes, and little to nothing added in the cellar (maybe a whisper of sulphur at bottling, maybe nothing). Nobody ever wrote it down. We all just sort of knew who belonged.

This year three disputes have landed on my desk at once, and I have to decide the stall list by March:

1. A well-regarded winemaker whose process ticks every single box has started giving interviews saying natural wine is a marketing scam and she wants nothing to do with the movement — she just happens to like making wine that way. Half my commitee says if the wine fits, her attitude is irrelevant. The other half says the fair exists to build something, and someone publicly slagging it off shouldn't get a stall under its banner.

2. One of our founders — literally the first person to pour at the fair — had his vineyard wrecked by hail and bought in grapes from a neighbour who sprays conventionally, just to save the year. He meets every criterion except that one, this one time. Excluding him feels monstrous, but he's also exactly the person newcomers watch to work out what the label means.

3. A young winemaker is using a technique nobody had thought of when the term settled (a cultured yeast strain he developed himself). The finished wine is indistinguishable from the traditional stuff by taste or lab analysis. The purists say it's a lab product and out; others say the point was never the specific techniques but what they're *for*.

On top of this, the word has been drifting — big retailers now slap "natural" on wine that meets none of the old criteria, so our fair's name means a bit less every year. One committee member wants to trademark a strict definition with a checklist and enforce it. Another wants to drop boundary-policing entirely and just have each producer list what they do and don't do, like ingredients on a menu. A third says admit everyone and let drinkers vote with their wallets.

I keep going in circles because it feels like the word is doing two or three jobs at the same time and people slide between them mid-argument. What should I actually do about the three winemakers, and which of the three proposals for the fair's future is least bad? I'd like concrete recommendations I can defend at the meeting, plus the general framework behind them so I'm not just improvising. Somewhere around 800-1000 words is ideal.
