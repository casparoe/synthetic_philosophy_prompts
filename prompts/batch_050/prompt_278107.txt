I don't have any formal education in philosophy, but I've been reading about John Rawls on my own, working slowly through A Theory of Justice and now starting Political Liberalism. While I was reading online discussions to orient myself, I came across a summary of Rawls's position on religion in a blog post by someone who describes himself as a longtime teacher of political philosophy. It ran roughly as follows:

Rawls held that religion, being based on faith rather than reasons that everyone can accept, must be kept strictly out of politics. In a well-ordered society, citizens may not bring religious arguments into public debate about laws or constitutional questions at all; political decisions have to be justified in neutral, secular terms. This is because Rawls regarded religious doctrines as inherently unreasonable -- or at least as claims nobody is epistemically entitled to believe in a pluralist society. His famous 'overlapping consensus' is essentially a pragmatic truce: religious and nonreligious citizens agree to liberal rules because the alternatives are worse for each of them, and each side privately hopes the arrangement will last only until its own view can prevail. In short, Rawls wanted religion privatized the way earlier liberals wanted it separated from the state, and his theory is hostile to faith even if politely so.

Parts of this feel right to me -- I recognize the vocabulary, and something like the basic worry about religious justifications in a diverse society does seem to be there in what I've read. But other parts don't sit well with my own impressions of Political Liberalism, particularly the claim that he regarded religious belief as unreasonable, and the description of the overlapping consensus as a mere truce. I can't tell, though, whether my reservations reflect the text or just my own sympathy for religious believers.

Could you audit this summary for fairness? I'd like to know, for each of its main claims: whether it is accurate, whether it is a common simplification that flattens an important distinction Rawls draws, or whether it attributes to him a position he explicitly rejected. Where the summary misleads, I'd appreciate an account of what Rawls actually says instead -- especially about what he takes the burdens of judgment to show, what his later qualifications on public reason allow, and how he distinguishes the overlapping consensus from other ways a pluralist society might hold together. Finally, does the conclusion the blogger draws -- that the theory is fundamentally hostile to religion -- survive once the summary is corrected, or does it depend on the errors?
