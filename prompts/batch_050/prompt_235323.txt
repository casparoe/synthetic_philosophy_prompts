I'm presenting a paper at a history of economic thought workshop next month arguing that Adam Smith's writings on colonies in the Wealth of Nations add up to a genuinely anti-imperial position — not just the technical point that the colonial trade monopoly loses Britain money, but a moral critique of conquest and of chartered companies like the East India Company, whose rule in India he basically treats as organized plunder. The audience is the tricky part. About half are Smith scholars who think the four-stages theory of history makes him a founder of liberal imperialism: commercial society as the pinnacle, hunting and shepherding peoples ranked below, so European expansion comes out excusable or even progressive. The other half are political theorists who'll happily grant he's anti-mercantilist but deny he's anti-imperial, pointing to his praise for settler colonies, his proposal to fold the American colonies into a parliamentary union with Britain, and his musing that the seat of empire might eventually cross the Atlantic. Prep me for the question period: which objections will each half raise, phrased the way they'd actually phrase them, which one is most damaging to my reading, what should I just concede up front, whats the one reply that has to land, and which question should I be quietly hoping nobody asks?
