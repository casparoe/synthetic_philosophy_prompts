Draft the joint statement that a committed incongruity theorist of humor and a committed benign-violation theorist could both sign after a long, good-faith adversarial collaboration on what makes things funny. By incongruity theory I mean the view that humor comes from perceiving a mismatch between what we expect and what we get, sometimes followed by a moment of "resolving" the mismatch; by benign violation theory I mean the view that humor occurs whenever something registers as wrong, threatening, or norm-breaking while simultaneously registering as okay, safe, or acceptable. Start your reply with the statement itself, no preface about how you approached it or summary of what the two theories say.

The statement should have three parts. First, a numbered list of claims both sides sign. These should include their shared take on a batch of test cases that are awkward for at least one of them: tickling (and why you can't tickle yourself), puns and other wordplay where it's hard to say what got violated, the mishap that only becomes funny once everyone turns out to be fine, the offensive joke that bombs because the room won't grant it benign status, distant tragedies becoming mockable while fresh ones aren't, and why most jokes die on retelling yet little kids laugh at the same one forty times. For each case, if the two camps explain it differently, say so rather than papering over it. Second, the precise statements of where they still disagree, each worded so its own side would accept the phrasing of its own position, and flag at least one apparent disagreement that dissolves into terminology once you push on it, if you find one. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one camp. For each crux, name some observation, experiment, or class of cases both sides agree would bear on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance -- the incongruity theorist's prediction and the benign-violation theorist's prediction, stated concretely enough that they could later be checked.

Format it as the numbered agreed-claims section first, then the disagreement statements, then a cruxes table (columns: crux, evidence both sides accept as relevant, incongruity prediction, benign-violation prediction). Try to keep the tone like an actual document two research groups would co-sign, not a book report.
