Six months ago my grandmother recovered from an illness her physicians had described as terminal and untreatable, and my family has split into two camps: some insist her recovery was miraculous, others that it was a misdiagnosis or a rare spontaneous remission. The dispute has driven me back to Hume's essay "Of Miracles," which I last read years ago, and I find I no longer understand two of its most famous passages as well as I thought I did.

Hume writes: "Nothing is esteemed a miracle, if it ever happen in the common course of nature. It is no miracle that a man, seemingly in good health, should die on a sudden: because such a kind of death, though more unusual than any other, has yet been frequently observed to happen. But it is a miracle, that a dead man should come to life; because that has never been observed in any age or country." Later he adds: "When anyone tells me, that he saw a dead man restored to life, I immediately consider with myself, whether it be more probable, that this person should either deceive or be deceived, or that the fact, which he relates, should really have happened."

Here is my difficulty. If a miracle is defined as an event that has never been observed in any age or country, then calling an event a miracle seems already to concede that the whole weight of experience stands against it, and Hume's conclusion -- that testimony can almost never outweigh that experience -- appears to follow by definition rather than by argument. Yet Hume clearly thinks his maxim could, at least in principle, be satisfied, since he holds that testimony whose falsehood would be more miraculous than the reported event would command belief. So either the argument is circular, or I am misreading what the definition contributes to it.

Could you help me resolve this? Specifically: Is there a reading on which Hume's definition of a miracle does not prejudge the evidential question, and what exactly is the independent work done by the appeal to uniform experience? And applying the sudden-death passage to my family's case: does Hume's distinction imply that a recovery doctors cannot explain is merely unusual rather than miraculous, and what further fact about my grandmother's case would have to hold for it to count as a miracle in his strict sense?
