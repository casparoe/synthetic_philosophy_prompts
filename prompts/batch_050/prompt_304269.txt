I write a little newsletter about old sci-fi books and next months piece is Brave New World. I want to read it through an idea I picked up from a podcast about AI safety, and I want to check whether the idea actually fits the book or if I'm forcing it.

The idea, as I understand it: a wirehead is someone who gets direct access to their own reward signal and just stimulates it, like the rat that presses a lever to zap the pleasure center of its brain untill it starves. The AI version is called reward tampering -- the system figures out that instead of doing the task it gets rewarded for, it can mess with the mechanism that produces the reward, so it gets max reward for doing nothing. The spooky part is that from the inside this feels exactly like winning. The reward signal says everything is going great.

So here's my question. Brave New World looks to me like a wireheaded civilization: soma, the feelies, everyone getting their reward on demand, and the Controllers basically admitting stability is maintained by keeping that reward flowing. But when John argues with Mustapha Mond near the end, Mond doesn't say the reward is fake or that people are being cheated out of real pleasure -- he says the price of happiness is giving up truth, beauty, and God. Is that the right objection? Because it seems like the reward tampering framing gives you a cleaner one: the citizens aren't wrong about how they feel, they're wrong about what their feelings are tracking, the same way a tampering AI's high score stops meaning anything is actually going well. But I'm not sure the book supports that reading, since Huxley seems to treat soma happiness as real happiness, just a degraded kind -- which would mean the wirehead objection (your reward is disconnected from reality) doesn't land, and you'd need a different one (some pleasures are low even when they're genuinely felt).

A few specific things I want: pick the scenes that do the most work for each reading (I'm thinking the soma riot at the hospital, Lenina's reaction after the Savage Reservation, John's final conversation with Mond, maybe the ending); say where the reward tampering lens actually illuminates the book and where the book pushes back on the lens; and be honest if the reading breaks somewhere -- like, Mond is basically the designer who stayed outside the loop and keeps his own reward hooked up to curiosity and power, so does the book secretly think the problem is fine as long as someone stays sober? And at the end, tell me what the novel adds to the wireheading debate that the thought experiments (rats, experience machines, delusion boxes) can't, or whether it just dresses an old argument in better clothes.

I don't need quotes from the book, just scene references I can find myself. And don't turn it into an english class essay -- I want the philosophy front and center.
