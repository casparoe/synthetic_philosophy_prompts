I'm trying to come up with a decent ethical theory of irreversible ecological interventions, covering both gene drives and de-extinction, and I want the desiderata before I hear anyone's pet view. What would the theory have to get right?

Please include concrete test cases like these:

- a self-sustaining drive intended to wipe out a disease-carrying mosquito everywhere
- a drive engineered to die out after a few generations, but with a small chance it won't
- releasing a genetically engineered proxy for an extinct species into an ecosystem that has meanwhile changed
- restoring an extinct species mostly for wonder or historical repair, with uncertain ecological benefits

For each desideratum, give the case or intuition motivating it. Some things I'm especially unsure how a theory should handle: whether irreversibility is binary or comes in degrees; whether an intervention can be procedurally reversible even when its effects aren't; how to compare preventing human deaths with losing a wild evolutionary lineage; who gets standing when effects cross borders and generations; whether consent from affected communities is necessary, and whose consent; what counts as an adequate ecological baseline; and whether creating something wonderful can compensate for destroying something old rather than merely replacing welfare.

Mark which requirements seem non-negotiable, which look like disguised commitments of one camp, and which pairs may be jointly unsatisfiable. Then briefly test a precautionary principle, expected-value cost-benefit analysis, and a rights/consent-based approach against your list. Don't just say we need more research -- every framework says that. Say what amount and kind of evidence would have to change the verdict. A table is fine. Also, if one requirement ends up ruling out alot of plausible views, tell me whether that's a discovery or a reason to drop the requirement.
