I teach a course on formal epistemology and social choice, and every semester at least a third of my students walk away from our week on the Condorcet jury theorem with what I've started calling the magic-wand reading: if each voter is even slightly better than a coin flip, just add voters and truth falls out of the sky. Nobody remembers the independence condition, and nobody remembers what the theorem says when individual competence dips below one-half. For the course website, I'd like a piece of satire that skewers this habit by taking it completely seriously. Write it as a deadpan investor letter (or product announcement) from a fictional decision-technology startup -- I'm imagining something like "Majoritarian Insights, Inc." -- whose entire business model is answering arbitrary yes/no questions (medical diagnoses, merger approvals, parole decisions, whether it will rain) by polling an enormous, cheaply hired crowd of raters, each of whom is right 51% of the time. The letter should boast, in the fluent idiom of a quarterly shareholder update, that accuracy asymptotically approaches certainty as headcount grows, complete with a growth chart and a slogan.

The humor must come from the theorem itself, played straight, so that someone who actually knows the result laughs in recognition rather than winces. Specifically, I want the letter to keep brushing past its own fatal problems in the voice of a CEO who doesn't notice them: a breezy aside revealing that all 40,000 raters consult the same single source (a morning newsletter, say) and therefore fail the independence assumption in the most thorough way possible; a jubilant announcement that the firm's raters turned out to be right only 49% of the time, followed by the inspired pivot of simply inverting every vote, without anyone asking whether a crowd that is wrong only sometimes can be reliably inverted; and an FAQ section in which the question of independence is raised and then dodged with increasingly creative euphemisms ("our raters enjoy a strong shared culture of excellence"). A passing sneer at critics who keep mentioning "conditions" and "theorem statements" as if mathematics came with fine print would also be welcome. The target is the practice of invoking the jury theorem as a free lunch -- not any real person or company -- and the satire should be fair enough that a specialist concedes the startup is only saying out loud what the magic-wand reading already implies.

Keep the irony legible but never winking: the CEO believes every word, and the reader should be able to locate exactly which assumption is being violated at each moment without any narrator pointing it out. Include the FAQ as a distinct section after the letter body. Aim for roughly 700 to 850 words total, written in American English, in the polished, slightly overconfident register of real investor communications. Please don't name or allude to any actual theorem by citation; the piece should work for a reader who has never taken my course, while quietly rewarding one who has.
