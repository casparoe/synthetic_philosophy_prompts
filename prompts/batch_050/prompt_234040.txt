I'm designing a diagnostic instrument for a graduate seminar in philosophy of language, to be distributed before any readings are assigned so that students locate themselves by their commitments rather than by labels they've heard. Please construct a decision tree that sorts respondents into the main positions on the semantics of fictional names, using the case of "Sherlock Holmes" throughout.

Build the tree from the logical space first, not from a list of names. The branching questions should be phrased as concrete verdicts on particular sentences, answerable by someone with no vocabulary from the debate. At minimum, use these as diagnostic materials, since the positions genuinely come apart on them:

- the plain sentence "Sherlock Holmes is a detective," uttered in a discussion of the stories;
- "Sherlock Holmes does not exist," uttered in reply to a child who thinks he is real;
- "Sherlock Holmes is more famous than any real detective who has ever lived";
- the comparative "Sherlock Holmes is smarter than Hercule Poirot";
- a sentence from a reader's letter: "I admire Sherlock Holmes."

Each branch should be labeled with the claim that splits it, and the first fork should be the most fundamental one: whether the name "Sherlock Holmes" refers to anything at all. Subsequent forks should concern, among other things, whether an affirmative answer commits one to a merely possibilia-style object, a nonexistent object with genuine properties, or something created by Conan Doyle's act of writing; and whether a negative answer is compatible with taking "Sherlock Holmes is a detective" as literally true, as true only under a story-relative operator, or as the product of a game of make-believe in which utterers pretend to refer.

The leaves should be sized so that the following positions all land somewhere distinct: a Millian or direct-reference view on which the name is genuinely empty and simple sentences containing it are neither true nor false; a descriptivist view that supplies a meaning for the name but treats ordinary utterances as false or truth-valueless; a pretense or make-believe account; a Meinongian account; and a creationist account on which fictional characters are abstract artifacts that come into being with the stories. Where two of these cannot be separated by sentence-verdict questions alone, say so explicitly and identify what further kind of question would be needed, rather than inventing a spurious fork. Where a combination of answers yields an unoccupied leaf, note it and diagnose in one sentence whether the gap reflects incoherence, mere unattractiveness, or simple neglect in the literature.

Each occupied leaf should carry the standard name of the position, the name of a philosopher commonly associated with it where one exists, and one sentence stating the verdict it is most embarrassed to give among the five diagnostic sentences above. Aim for seven to nine questions along any root-to-leaf path, and close with a brief note on which single diagnostic sentence does the most sorting work and why.
