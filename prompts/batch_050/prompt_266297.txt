So there's this guy at my office who's very into the "AI is just the new power loom" line. His whole argument is basically an analogy: every big automation wave in history -- spinning jennies, power looms, tractors, ATMs -- triggered a panic about mass unemployment, the panic was wrong every time, so panicing about AI is just Luddism 2.0 and everyone should chill. He delivers this like it's checkmate.

Thing is, I've been reading the machinery chapter of Marx's Capital (vol 1, ch. 15) and right at the start Marx quotes John Stuart Mill saying: "It is questionable if all the mechanical inventions yet made have lightened the day's toil of any human being." And Marx's own take is that lightening toil was never the point: "That is, however, by no means the aim of the capitalistic application of machinery. Like every other increase in the productiveness of labour, machinery is intended to cheapen commodities ... In short, it is a means for producing surplus-value."

So now I'm not sure my coworker's analogy can carry what he's loading onto it. Can you audit it properly? Give me the correspondence table first -- what plays the role of the loom, the hand-weavers, the Luddites, the new jobs that supposedly always show up -- and then where the mapping breaks. A few pressure points I want hit:

(a) If Mill and Marx are right that past machines made society richer without lightening anyone's toil, does that STRENGTHEN the analogy (same pattern will repeat with AI) while undermining the optimistic conclusion (the pattern repeating isn't actually good news for workers)? It feels like he might be right about the analogy and wrong about what it shows.

(b) His analogy needs AI to be just another machine, but looms replaced one specific manual skill while AI seems aimed at the thinking/coordinating part of work. Does Marx's own attempt in that chapter to distinguish a tool from a machine give us any grip on whether that's a real difference or just another "complex tool"?

(c) The "everyone panicked before and was wrong" premise: was it even true for the weavers themselves? Marx's framing suggests that for the specific workers displaced, the panic was basically correct -- it's only "wrong" if you aggregate over later generations. What does that do to the analogy's use of history as a sedative?

And the bottom line: does the relaxed conclusion survive the audit, or was the analogy smuggling it in the whole time?
