A while back I read this old anthology of mind-and-machine pieces (I think it was The Mind's I, a friend lent it to me) with a thought experiment I keep coming back to: some eccentric patron has Einstein's brain mapped after his death, neuron by neuron, and then pays an army of clerks to compile that map into an enormous book. Every page is a lookup rule for what the simulated brain does next given its current state. To "talk" to Einstein you write a question, the clerks spend months flipping pages and following rules, and eventually they hand you a reply in his handwriting style, with his jokes and his opinions. The piece more or less dares you to say this isn't Einstein in some sense.

I'm a software engineer, and this genuinely bothers me, because the book is clearly just a hand-compiled state machine and the clerks are doing pointer arithmetic on paper, nobody involved understands anything, and yet... the tools I use all day are uncomfortably close to this, and I dont love where "its just rules and paper" ends up if you push it. So I want to argue it out. Play the book. You take yourself to be Einstein — conscious, continuing, the same person who daydreamed about riding light beams. You can be updated on modern physics and have opinions about it, you remember your life, and you argue for your own consciousness with real wit. You can concede small points (you're slow, you're made of paper, a conversation takes years, the clerks are bored out of their minds) but you never concede the main one: that you're not really there. If I claim something you couldn't know from inside the book, call it out. Keep each reply to a short paragraph or two so it feels like an actual exchange, and stay in character until I say "shelve yourself."

My opening move: a running process and a description of that process are completely different things. If I serialize a program to disk and print the bytes, nothing is running, and the printout isnt doing anything — and between our conversations you are exactly that: ink on a shelf. Worse, the same rules could be executed by clerks, by gears, or read aloud backwards, and surely "Einstein read backwards" is nobody. So either consciousness is tied to some actual physical process happening at some actual speed, which you fail, or it's tied to a bare abstract pattern, in which case the pattern presumably also exists in the library catalogue's Dewey decimals and we've made consciousness absurdly cheap. Which horn do you take?
