I am a novelist at work on a book set in a private fertility clinic in near-future London, and I have reached the scene on which the plot's moral weight rests. One of my central characters chairs the clinic's ethics committee, and rather than dramatising the meeting itself I want to reproduce, as an interchapter, the memo she circulates beforehand. The case before the committee is this. A couple, both congenitally deaf and active in the Deaf community, are undergoing IVF for unrelated fertility reasons; several viable embryos have resulted, and they have asked that preimplantation genetic testing be used to transfer an embryo carrying the variant that will make the child deaf, as they are. They are not asking the clinic to engineer anything — only to choose among embryos that already exist. The clinic, meanwhile, already permits testing to avoid serious inherited disease, and it has recently begun offering, as a commercial add-on, polygenic screening that gives prospective parents probabilistic scores for traits such as height, educational attainment, and susceptibility to common illnesses. My character must therefore say something about this couple without leaving the clinic's other policies in incoherence, since refusing them while cheerfully selling selection in the other direction, and against deafness itself, is a position that needs defending rather than assuming.

The committee, as I have sketched it, is genuinely divided, and I want the division rendered fairly. One faction argues that reproductive autonomy, together with the fact that no child is made worse off who would otherwise not have existed at all, settles the matter in the couple's favour, and that the Deaf community's self-understanding as a linguistic and cultural minority rather than a patient population deserves respect. The opposing faction insists that deliberately selecting for a disability fails a minimal parental duty to secure the child's future openness of opportunity, and that the clinic would be complicit in it; one member adds that the couple's request, whatever its merits, would express a judgement about the value of deaf and hearing lives that an institution should not act upon. My character suspects both sides are quietly dodging the harder question, which is what principle, if any, distinguishes the polygenic screening the clinic already sells from the selection it is being asked to perform — whether the relevant line falls between disease and non-disease traits, between avoiding harm and conferring advantage, or somewhere else entirely.

What I need from you is the memo itself, roughly 600 to 800 words, written in the voice a careful committee chair would actually use: a plain statement of the case, a clear recommendation (approve the transfer, refuse it, or approve subject to stated conditions), and the reasoning that supports it, including an honest reckoning with the strongest argument on the losing side rather than a perfunctory nod. It should also state, in a closing paragraph, what the decision implies for the clinic's existing screening policies, since the memo is meant to set a precedent the committee is willing to stand behind. I would rather the document engage the underlying philosophical tensions directly but without name-dropping theorists, as my character is a clinician by training and the novel's credibility depends on the memo reading as an authentic institutional artefact rather than a seminar paper in disguise.
