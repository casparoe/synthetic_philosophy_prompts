Philosophers and psychologists have been arguing for decades about whether delusions are really beliefs. One camp says yes: patients insist on the claim, defend it, and act on it more or less like any belief. The other camp points out that delusions often fail to act like beliefs at all — someone with Capgras doesn't seem to draw the obvious inferences, the delusion stays oddly compartmentalized, and patients sometimes don't behave the way you'd expect if they literally believed their spouse was replaced by an impostor. The two-factor theorists mostly sidestep this and just explain where the delusion comes from (anomalous experience plus a deficit in belief evaluation), but even they disagree among themselves about what status the end product has.

Here's my hypothesis: the debate is mostly verbal, because "belief" in philosophy of mind is already doing like three jobs (representation with a functional role, something you avow, something you stake action on), and the two sides are just privileging different jobs. But maybe I'm wrong and there's a real empirical disagreement buried in there.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction we should draw, evidence that would settle it, or should we just stop asking the question in this form?
