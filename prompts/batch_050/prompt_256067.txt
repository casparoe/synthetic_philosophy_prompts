I'm a junior in high school and I run our school's physics-and-philosophy club, which meets for about 75 minutes after school with usually 12 to 16 students attending. Last month we did a session on the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, and during the discussion we got stuck on a question I still can't shake: if every possible outcome of a measurement actually happens on some branch, what could it possibly mean to say one outcome is more probable than another? My co-leader argued probability just becomes meaningless; I think the Born rule must still be doing something, but neither of us could articulate what. Rather than lecture the club about self-locating uncertainty or decision-theoretic derivations of the Born rule (most members haven't read anything on this and would tune out), I want to design an activity where they run headfirst into the problem themselves and only hear it named afterward.

Here's my seed idea, which you're welcome to modify or replace if you have something better: I'm imagining that at some point the club physically splits into two groups of unequal size (say, 4 students in one room and 12 in another) to simulate a measurement whose branches have different weights, and that before the split happens, each student has to make some kind of bet or choice whose payoff depends on which branch "their" successors end up in. My hope is that students will feel, in their gut, that it seems rational to bet on the larger group even while fully knowing that both outcomes occur and that "they" continue in both -- which is exactly the conceptual tension I want surfaced. The activity needs concrete rules, any materials or envelopes or payoff structures required, a rough timeline fitting the 75 minutes, and clear instructions for me as facilitator, since I've never run anything like this before.

Please also include a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the activity onto the real philosophical moves: pre-branch uncertainty about which future self is "you," the idea that rational agents might weight branches by a measure rather than just counting them, and the branch-counting objection that says if there are two branches the odds should be fifty-fifty no matter what. I'd like you to anticipate what will predictably go wrong when I actually run this (for instance, students insisting the bet is irrational because both outcomes happen, or students gaming the payoffs in ways that break the analogy) and tell me how to handle each. Finally, be honest with me about which features of the actual Born rule problem the activity cannot reproduce -- I assume a classroom split into integer-sized groups can't capture continuous amplitudes or genuine quantum branching -- and what I'll need to supply in discussion to fill that gap.
