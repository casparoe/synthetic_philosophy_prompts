I'm a novelist working on a book where a hospice offers terminally ill patients a device that puts them into a perfect simulation of the rest of their lives -- they believe they recovered, went home, grew old, everything feels real -- while their actual body dies within the year. The family outside knows. My main character refuses it, her daughter plugs in, and I want readers to be genuinely torn about who's right. Please describe the most important implications of the big theories of well-being -- hedonism, desire-satisfaction theories, objective list views -- plus Nozick's experience machine argument, for how a thoughtful reader should judge each of them. Under which theory is the daughter's choice obviously rational, and does the sister's refusal only make sense on a view where contact with reality has value in itself, or can a hedonist defend her too? And separately: under which theory does the hospice itself come out looking sinister rather than merciful, given that nobody inside ever finds out? One twist in my book: the simulated daughter occasionally does things that matter to real people outside because the hospice passes her simulated letters on to them -- I'd like to know if that changes the verdict on any of these views or just muddies the intuitions. Please answer in plain continuous prose, like a long email -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
