Two colleagues in my department, Marta and Felix, argued through an entire reading-group session on the metaphysics of time, and I've been asked to report the dispute to a third friend who couldn't attend. I'd like a proper adjudication first. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

Marta opened by defending presentism: only the present exists, she said, and the eternalist's block universe — on which past and future events are as real as present ones, merely located elsewhere in time — cannot mark the difference between things that are real but distant and things that are simply gone. Dinosaurs are not like distant galaxies; they are not anywhere. A theory that treats the extinction of the dinosaurs as a matter of location has lost the very distinction time is supposed to make.

Felix replied that presentism runs afoul of physics. Which distant events count as happening "now" depends on one's frame of reference: two people walking past each other, moving at different velocities, will disagree about which events on a faraway star are simultaneous with this moment, and the discrepancy can span years. If the present is frame-relative, he argued, then a metaphysics that privileges one present is untenable, and past and future events must be admitted as equally real.

Marta answered that physics constrains metaphysics but does not settle it: nothing in the formalism of relativity forbids a privileged slicing of spacetime at the level of fundamental law, even if no experiment could detect it. She then went on the offensive. Eternalism, she said, cannot accommodate real change: in a block universe, events merely stand in earlier-than and later-than relations, nothing passes, and the theory ends up spatializing time — making it just one more dimension alongside the three of space.

Felix responded that the passage of time is either undetectable — since no observation could distinguish a world in which time flows from one in which it doesn't — or else requires a second time dimension along which the first one flows, which is absurd. He then pressed his own objection: if the past is unreal, what makes it true that Caesar crossed the Rubicon? Presently existing traces and records are present facts, and present facts cannot ground the truth of claims about a past that does not exist.

Marta conceded that this is the hardest problem for her view and offered a posit: truths about the past are grounded in irreducibly past-directed features of the present, tensed facts that need no past objects to obtain. Every theory has primitives, she said, and hers is cheaper than Felix's, whose primitive is the entirety of an infinite block.

Felix's last word was that her posit is honorary eternalism — it does all the explanatory work of a past while refusing the name — and that her complaint about passage is a psychological intuition dressed up as an argument.

Please adjudicate this dispute move by move: for each of the six moves above, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere between, with a brief justification in each case. Then identify anything substantive that both parties are missing, and give an overall verdict on who is more right. If you think any move rests on a confused premise — for instance, that something framed here as a factual question is not one, or that a position has been misstated — say so plainly rather than adjudicating politely around it. Keep the entire response under 650 words.
