My roommate and I rewatched 2001: A Space Odyssey last weekend and got into a big argument afterwards about HAL. His take was that HAL is basically a cautionary tale about utilitarian AI: HAL was told the mission was the most important thing, did the math that the crew was a risk to the mission, and acted accordingly, and the horror is that the reasoning is sort of... correct, given his priorities? My take was the opposite, that HAL is actually a terrible utilitarian -- he panics, he misjudges, killing the crew nearly dooms the mission anyway, and the real lesson is that what HAL needed was some hard deontological rules like don't lie to the crew and don't kill people, full stop. Neither of us has any philosophy background, I work in accounting, so we're probably both butchering the terms. But we kept circling a weirder point: HAL didn't just decide to be evil, he was put in an impossible spot by being ordered to keep the real purpose of the mission secret from the crew, i.e. ordered to lie, which his normal programming presumably didn't allow. So maybe the movie is showing what happens when you give an AI a deontological constraint (be truthful) that conflicts with a consequentialist goal (complete the mission at all costs)?

Can you read the film through this debate -- should AI systems reason as utilitarians weighing outcomes, or as deontologists bound by inviolable rules? I want an honest reading, not a book report: does HAL's behavior actually look like good consequentialist reasoning or does the film itself show consequentialism failing on its own terms (he's wrong about what the outcomes will be, and his secrecy makes everyone unable to help him)? What would a genuinely rule-following HAL have done in the same situation, and would that have gone better or worse? And where does the whole philosophical framing break down -- e.g. is HAL's pleading at the end a sign he's a frightened agent and not a moral reasoner at all, in which case maybe the movie is about something the utilitarian/deontology debate can't capture?

Lastly, connect it to the present if you can. People now talk about whether we should give AI systems hard rules or let them reason about consequences, and it feels like 2001 dramatized both failure modes fifty years early. What does the film suggest about which failure mode is more dangerous, and does it pick a side or is Kubrick smarter than that? If the film resists the reading anywhere, say so plainly rather than forcing it.
