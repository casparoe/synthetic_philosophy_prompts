My partner recently asked me, half-joking, whether I would love an exact copy of her who was also slightly funnier and slightly kinder. I apparently gave the wrong answer, and the ensuing argument sent me to the literature on reasons for love. I now want a thought experiment built to order. Construct a single realistic scenario — no clones, teleporters, or duplicates — in which a bestowal account of love's reasons in Frankfurt's spirit (love bestows value rather than responding to it) and an appraisal account in Velleman's spirit (love is a response to the beloved's valuable qualities) deliver opposite verdicts about whether someone's continuing love for their partner is rationally warranted. The case must isolate fungibility: shared history, commitment, and joint projects should be held fixed, so the only live question is whether loving someone for their qualities licenses indifference toward an equally or better qualified replacement. One paragraph for the scenario. Then do the check: state what each theory says about the lover and why; say how the verdicts shift, if at all, on a relationship-based view like Kolodny's; identify which feature of the scenario does the work and what happens to each verdict if it is removed; and give the appraisal theorist's best reply to stop the case from counting against her. Finally, tell me which argument or consideration would lead you to the opposite conclusion about which theory the case favors, and flag where your construction is most likely to be cheating — for instance, by smuggling in a verdict by stipulation rather than generating it from the theories themselves.
