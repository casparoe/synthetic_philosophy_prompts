I'm teaching my political theory seminar on corruption in a couple weeks and I need a worked example I can basically steal for class, so humor me on this one. Take on the role of an expert in the political theory of corruption who also knows the anthropology of gift exchange well. Think carefully, brainstorm a bit before you commit to a structure, and don't rush to a verdict -- I want the mapping done properly first.

Here's the case. The 2019 US college admissions scandal, the Varsity Blues one with Rick Singer as the fixer and various wealthy parents (a couple of actresses among them). People sometimes talk about three doors into elite universities, and I want you to work with that framing: the front door (the kid is genuinely qualified, though helped along by pricey test prep and private counselors), the back door (the family donates serious money to the university itself -- a building, an endowed chair -- through the development office, and the kid gets a friendly look from admissions; all perfectly legal, even tax deductible), and the side door (parents pay Singer, who bribes a sailing or water polo coach to flag the kid as a fake athletic recruit, or pays someone to correct the kid's SAT answers after the fact). Now analyze all three doors through three lenses: the classic Nye-style definition of corruption (behavior that deviates from the formal duties of an entrusted role for private gain), Lessig's notion of institutional corruption (the institution systematically bending away from its purpose because of its dependence on moneyed influence, even when nobody breaks any rule), and Mauss's account of the gift (every gift creates an obligation to reciprocate). For each door, tell me precisely who deviates from which duty, where the money actually goes and whose pocket it lands in, and what the quid pro quo even is -- because in the back door case there is often no explicit deal at all, just a shared understanding.

Then the hard part, which is the actual point: the back door and the side door seem to produce the same outcome (money buys a seat that grades alone wouldn't), and Mauss would presumably say the big donation creates a reciprocal obligation just as much as the envelope to the coach does. So is the standard definition of corruption tracking a real moral difference here, or is it mostly encoding which class of exchange the powerful have already legalized? And push on the theories in return: does the Nye-style definition even apply cleanly to private universities, where the coach's duties are set by the employer rather than the public? Does institutional corruption let you condemn the back door without condemning ordinary fundraising that keeps the lights on -- and if not, is that a feature or a defect? Finally, tell me where each theory is just silent rather than forcing it to speak. I dont want a tidy conclusion if the case doesn't support one.
