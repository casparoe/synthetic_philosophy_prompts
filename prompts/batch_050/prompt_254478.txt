I am a retired molecular biologist who has taken up philosophy in earnest over the past year, and I have been trying to organize my thinking about the aggregation of harms, the family of cases in which one asks whether some enormous number of trivial discomforts (a dust speck in the eye of each of an astronomically many people, say) could ever outweigh one instance of a genuinely severe harm (a single person being tortured). I find the landscape confusing because the positions seem to differ along several independent axes at once, and the literature's labels (aggregationism, lexical thresholds, partial aggregation, and so on) do not obviously map onto those axes. I would like you to build me a decision tree that sorts an interlocutor by what they actually believe rather than by which label they find congenial.

Please proceed as follows. Begin from a starting commitment that nearly everyone shares, namely that one headache suffered by each of a million people is, all else equal, a worse state of affairs than one headache suffered by one person, and phrase it as a question. From there, each branch node should be a single concrete case or claim on which the positions genuinely come apart, phrased as a question that someone could answer without knowing in advance which position it funnels them toward. Among your nodes, please include at least these diagnostics: whether some number of dust-speck-level harms could outweigh one death; whether there is a threshold level of harm below which harms never aggregate against harms above it; whether the tiny harms must befall distinct people for aggregation to apply (i.e., whether a million specks across a million eyes differ morally from a million specks in one eye over a lifetime); and whether the answer changes when the large number of minor harms is compared not to one severe harm but to a large number of moderate ones. You may add further nodes if they sharpen the classification, but keep the tree to at most eight branch questions.

Each leaf should carry the standard name of the position it corresponds to, or should be explicitly marked as an unoccupied combination, in which case give one sentence diagnosing why nobody defends it (incoherence, mere unattractiveness, or simple neglect in the literature). Where two positions are standardly distinguished in the literature but your tree cannot separate them, flag the pair and explain which further question would be needed. Finally, note at the end which single node does the most work in driving respondents toward the unintuitive conclusion that the specks win, and whether a respondent who wants to resist that conclusion has a principled place to stop, or only a choice among ad hoc ones. I would like the whole thing to be usable as a handout for a reading group of fellow scientists, so please keep the questions free of jargon and make the cases vivid enough to provoke disagreement.
