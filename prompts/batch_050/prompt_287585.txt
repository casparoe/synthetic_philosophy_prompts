I am a retired molecular biologist, and my career overlapped with the early clinical use of preimplantation genetic diagnosis, which I watched arrive with great promise and considerable unease. In retirement I have been reading philosophy, and I recently tried to write down, as a numbered argument, the reasoning I take to underlie the position that prospective parents should select the best child they can have. To my discomfort, the argument seems to run further than I can follow it, ending in a conclusion I spent my career assuming was beyond the pale. Since every premise looked innocent when I wrote it down, I would be grateful for a diagnosis of where it goes wrong.

(1) When a couple must choose which of several possible children to conceive, no child is made worse off by the choice: whichever child is passed over never exists, and so there is no one who has been harmed or wronged by not being chosen. Choices of this kind therefore cannot wrong any of the possible children.

(2) If a choice among possible children cannot wrong any of them, then the only consideration that can bear on the choice is the expected quality of life of the child who would in fact be born.

(3) If the expected quality of life of the resulting child is the only relevant consideration, then one ought to choose, among the available options, the child with the best expected life. I understand this is more or less the principle of procreative beneficence.

(4) Expected quality of life is influenced not only by the absence of serious disease but also by ordinary, non-disease traits: temperament, cognitive capacity, susceptibility to depression, and so on.

(5) Therefore, whenever safe means of selection exist, prospective parents are obligated to select for such advantageous traits, and not merely to screen out serious disease.

(6) If an outcome is obligatory, then any safe and effective means of bringing about that same outcome is likewise obligatory; the obligation attaches to the end, not to the particular technique.

(7) Hence the obligation in (5) extends equally to embryo selection, to gene editing, and to any future safe intervention, provided the resulting child is the same.

(8) Obligations that protect children from serious harm are legitimately enforced by the state; that is the settled basis of compulsory schooling and mandatory vaccination.

(9) The disadvantages that selection under (5) would avoid are serious harms to the children who would otherwise bear them; hence the state may legitimately require selection and editing, not merely permit them.

(10) But a state mandate of that kind is coercive eugenics wearing liberal dress, which is absurd. So at least one of my premises must go.

My inclination is to keep (1), since the point about non-identity strikes me as the best-established thing in the whole chain, and I am equally reluctant to surrender (4), which is a straightforward empirical claim. I would much rather give up something in the middle, but I cannot see which step is doing the damage. Which step or steps do you reject, and what does rejecting them cost me? In particular, if I keep (1) and (2), am I really committed to saying that parents who decline a harmless selection do something wrong, or is there a principled stopping point between (2) and (10)?

Please give me your analysis in prose first, and then summarize it as a table with one row per numbered step and four columns: Step (the number, plus a gist of five words or fewer); Verdict (accept, reject, or accept only in a qualified form); Why it looked plausible (one sentence); and Price of rejecting it (one sentence on what else in the argument, or in ordinary moral thinking, I would have to give up or revise).
