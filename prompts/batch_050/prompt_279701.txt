After years of going back and forth on this — most recently in a long argument with a friend who works on neuroprosthetics — I've settled on a position about mind uploading that I now want stress-tested rather than defended. My view: gradual, neuron-by-neuron replacement of a biological brain with functionally equivalent synthetic components preserves the person, so that whoever is walking around at the end of the procedure is genuinely you; but destructive scan-and-upload, even if the resulting simulation is behaviorally and psychologically perfect, does not preserve you — it produces a successor, a copy. What matters, on my view, is the uninterrupted continuity of the physical process realizing the mind, not the substrate, and not merely the informational pattern. My friend thinks this asymmetry is superstition: if the pattern survives, she says, the method by which it survives is irrelevant, and my attachment to "continuity of process" is just a dressed-up fear of death.

So: tell me what would count against my position. I don't want a generic rehearsal of fission and teleportation puzzles; I want a map of where the view is genuinely exposed. Some pressure points I'd like addressed directly. Does the view survive arbitrary variation in the replacement rate — one neuron per month versus one per millisecond? If the rate makes no difference, does the position collapse into the patternism I reject; and if the rate does matter, is there any non-arbitrary threshold, and what would it even look like to discover one? Would empirical facts about the brain bear on it at all — say, if it turned out that neurons' contributions to experience are not cleanly separable in the way the procedure assumes, or that consciousness is constituted at a timescale finer than any feasible handover? And what should I make of the intermediate cases: one hemisphere replaced gradually, the other in a single operation; a handover protocol in which each synthetic component shadows its biological twin for weeks, carrying duplicate signals, before the original is dissolved; replacement performed under anesthesia versus while the subject is awake and continuously reporting that nothing has changed? For each candidate defeater, say whether a committed continuity theorist would concede it as a genuine cost or dismiss it, and whether the dismissal is a principled consequence of the view or a patch. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and close with an honest verdict: is my position actually risking anything, or has it been insulated by qualifications — and if the latter, whether that insulation is itself a defect.
