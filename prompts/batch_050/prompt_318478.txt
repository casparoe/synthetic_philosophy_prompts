My roommate and I got into an argument last night about whether you can genuinely assign probabilities to your own future choices, and we ended up leaning hard on an analogy I want pressure-tested. Her position: predicting what I'll do is no different in kind from predicting what she'll do. I watch her all week, I notice she always caves and orders takeout by Thursday, so I say "80% chance you'll get Thai food tonight" -- and she says I can do exactly the same to myself, since I have even MORE data about me than about her. She even has a practice she swears by: every Sunday she writes down probabilities for her own week (60% I go to the gym twice, 90% I finish the report, etc.) and scores herself at the end, like a one-person prediction market. She claims this has made her better calibrated and more honest about her own akrasia.

My intuition is that the analogy quietly falls apart exactly where it matters. When I'm DELIBERATING -- like right now I'm deciding whether to quit my job -- it feels like asking "what's the probability I'll quit?" is a category error, like a judge asking "what are the odds I'll rule for the plaintiff?" mid-trial. The probability question seems to only make sense once I've stepped out of the deliberating seat and started watching myself from the outside, treating my future self as just another person whose behavior I forecast. And stepping out like that seems to CHANGE the thing: if I predict "70% I chicken out and stay," haven't I partly decided to stay, or at least licensed staying? The prediction seems to feed back into the deliberation in a way that predicting my roommate's takeout doesn't feed back into hers (well, unless I tell her, which is its own interesting case).

So: audit the analogy "forecasting your own choice is like forecasting someone else's behavior." Give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the observer, the observed, the evidence, the track record -- and then tell me where it breaks. Specifically: is the deliberator-vs-spectator distinction a real disanalogy or just a feeling? Does the self-fulfilling/self-defeating feedback problem apply to her Sunday practice, or only to mid-deliberation cases, or to neither? And does her conclusion -- that self-prediction is basically fine and even useful -- survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the roommate-comparison carrying all the weight? If there's a better analogy for what's going on when she assigns 60% to her own gym attendance, I'd like that too.
