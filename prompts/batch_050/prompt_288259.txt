I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of psychiatry, and a postdoc in our department has drafted a set of lecture handouts for next term's offering. I've asked her to revise the handout on delusion, but before I write my comments I'd like a second opinion on one passage, because I can't tell whether my unease tracks real inaccuracies or just differences of emphasis. The passage reads:

"Contemporary neuropsychological approaches explain delusion through the two-factor theory. A delusion arises when (1) damage to the right prefrontal cortex produces an anomalous experience whose content determines the content of the delusion, and (2) a further deficit in the patient's reasoning prevents the anomalous experience from being corrected, which explains why the delusion is maintained rather than abandoned. Since the first factor fixes content and the second fixes persistence, the theory shows that delusions are essentially rational responses to strange experiences, of the same kind anyone would form under the same conditions. It follows that delusions are beliefs in the ordinary sense, and that older motivational or psychodynamic accounts — on which the delusion serves a defensive function — have been empirically refuted."

A few things to know about my situation. The students will not have read the primary literature; the handout is their first exposure. I am sympathetic to the two-factor framework myself, so I don't want to saddle the postdoc with objections that are really just my own theoretical commitments. And the last two sentences are doing the argumentative work she cares about — she wants to close the unit by moving the class from explanation of delusion to the question of what kind of state a delusion is, and she intends this passage to license treating doxasticism as settled by the empirical theory.

Please audit the passage with that use in mind. Specifically: identify which claims a careful proponent of the two-factor theory would endorse as stated; which claims involve a conflation, a misattribution of roles between the two factors, or an overstatement of what the theory was designed to explain; and which claims are contested even among sympathizers. Then, taking the corrected picture as given, assess whether the final two sentences follow — that is, whether the two-factor theory, accurately stated, settles or even bears directly on whether delusions are beliefs, and whether it refutes or merely competes with motivational accounts. If the empirical theory is in fact largely silent on the doxastic question, say so plainly and indicate what the postdoc would need to add to the handout to make her intended transition legitimate rather than illicit.
