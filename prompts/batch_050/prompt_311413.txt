I am taking a university seminar on medieval ethics, currently focused on Abelard's account of sin. My professor's approach is to test candidate principles against a sequence of cases discussed in class, and I am trying to work out precisely which principle she is committed to before the next session. Her verdicts so far have been:

1. A man lies with a woman he sincerely believes to be another man's wife; unbeknownst to him, she is in fact his own wife. Verdict: he sins.
2. A servant, cornered by his violent master, kills him in self-defense and grieves the deed. Verdict: no sin.
3. A knight consents fully and without reservation to killing his lord and sets out to do it, but is arrested before he reaches him. Verdict: he sins, and as gravely as if he had succeeded.
4. A man feels a powerful desire for a married woman but refuses to consent to it and keeps his distance. Verdict: no sin; if anything, merit.
5. A judge condemns a defendant he honestly, though mistakenly, believes to be guilty; the defendant is in fact innocent. Verdict: no sin.

I can see at least two principles that fit all five verdicts. On one reading, what matters is whether the agent consents to what he himself takes to be wrong -- that is, whether he shows contempt for God as he understands God's commands -- regardless of how things objectively stand. On another, what matters is whether the agent acts from ill will or malice toward his neighbor. Both seem to generate verdicts 1 through 5, since the cases as described pair consent-to-perceived-wrongness with malice, and absence of the one with absence of the other. Perhaps there are further principles I have missed that also fit the record.

Here is the new case for next week. A man believes, mistakenly, that today is a fast day on which eating meat is forbidden. Knowing of the supposed prohibition, he goes ahead and eats meat anyway, not out of defiance of God or anyone else, but simply because he prefers the meal. It is in fact an ordinary day, and no obligation of fasting binds him.

Please predict my professor's verdict on this case, and explain which principle she is implicitly running if I am right about her -- the consent-to-perceived-wrong reading, the ill-will reading, or some third principle I have overlooked that also fits the five earlier verdicts. I would also like to know: which of the five earlier verdicts does the most work in narrowing the field; whether the new case actually discriminates between the surviving candidates or whether they happen to converge on it; and, if they converge, what case I should propose in seminar that would force her to choose between them. Finally, if any of my candidate principles turns out to predict a different verdict on one of the five earlier cases than I have assumed, please point that out.
