I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm fact-checking a feature on the growing debate over insect sentience and what, if anything, we owe to the animals we routinely crush, spray, and starve. The piece leans on an analogy I now want to stress-test before it goes to print, because it is doing real argumentative work.

In an interview, a neuroscientist who studies invertebrate behavior told me that we should stop asking which creatures are conscious and start thinking of moral considerability as a dimmer switch rather than a light switch. On his picture, there is no threshold where the light clicks on; there is a single scale of brightness running from a thermostat at the bottom, through insects and shrimp, up to chickens, pigs, and humans at the top, and our duties simply track the setting. He used this to argue that insects merit some protection, but strictly less than vertebrates, in proportion to their position on the dial -- so, for instance, an insect farm should face looser welfare rules than a poultry operation, by roughly the same factor. A philosopher I consulted for balance pushed back hard: she said the dimmer metaphor is where the entire argument happens, and that once you spell out what the brightness is supposed to be, the conclusion about proportional duties no longer follows.

Please audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the bulb, the dimmer position, the brightness, the power supply, and the observer reading the dial, and note every point where the mapping is ambiguous -- in particular, whether brightness is meant to stand for the intensity of experience, the richness or complexity of experience, the probability that there is any experience at all, or the moral weight that experience carries, since the scientist slid between all four in our conversation. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for his intended conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Candidates I'd like assessed include: a dimmer's output is measurable on one continuous physical scale while sentience may be multidimensional or discontinuous; with a real dimmer we know there is a bulb and the only question is its setting, whereas with insects the live question may be whether there is a bulb at all, which turns a question of degree into a question of uncertainty; the analogy assumes a common currency of brightness that lets many dim sources be weighed against one bright one, which is precisely what the aggregation of small harms across billions of insects calls into doubt; and the picture presupposes that moral status is graded at all, rather than attached to a threshold that some creatures cross and others do not. Finally: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the proportional-duty conclusion survive in any form, was it ever supported by the analogy as opposed to assumed by it, and if the dimmer should be retired, is there a better analogy for gradations of moral status -- or is the request for a better analogy itself part of the problem?

Please answer in American English, in a register suitable for a general-interest magazine's fact-check memo.
