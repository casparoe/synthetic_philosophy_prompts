Two colleagues in my department have been locked in a running dispute over the Epicurean argument about death for two years. One defends the Epicurean position: since a person who no longer exists cannot experience anything, and harm seems to require someone there to be harmed, one's own death is not bad for one — only the process of dying can be. The other defends a deprivationist position: death is bad for the person who dies because it deprives them of goods they would otherwise have had, and a misfortune need not be experienced to be a misfortune. Their arguments keep circling, and the rest of us suspect they agree on more than they admit and disagree at exactly two or three load-bearing points they have never isolated. Draft the joint statement they should have produced instead of another year of arguing past each other. It should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both can sign, formulated without the vocabulary that belongs to one side only — I suspect these include that the question is about death as such and not the process of dying, that fear of death can be irrational even if death is bad, and that a thought experiment about prenatal non-existence is probative; (2) a table of the residual disagreements, each stated neutrally and then re-stated in each side's preferred terms, with any disagreement that turns out to be merely verbal flagged as such and dissolved — check in particular whether their dispute about whether harm requires a subject existing at the time of the harm is substantive or a clash over the word harm; (3) a cruxes section: identify the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party, and for each crux give one case or argument both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Among the cases, include at least: the mirror case in which we dread the endless non-existence after death but do not lament the equally endless non-existence before our birth; the case of an intelligent adult reduced by injury to the contented mental life of an infant, which both agree bears on whether deprivation can be bad without being felt; and the case of humanity being extinguished painlessly and instantaneously tonight, which both agree bears on whether badness requires a persisting subject. Where the two would predict different verdicts on a case, say exactly which premise of the other's argument the case is meant to pressure. Do not adjudicate who is right; the deliverable is a document both could sign.
