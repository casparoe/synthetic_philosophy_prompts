I am a high school student taking a philosophy elective, and our class has been discussing whether freedom means simply being left alone, having the capacities and opportunities to act meaningfully, or not being subject to another person’s arbitrary power. This made me think about my own school’s rules. Students must take a broad range of required courses before being allowed to specialize, and the school confiscates phones during class because it says that constant notifications undermine concentration. The administration defends these policies by arguing that they make us freer in the long run: required education gives us more genuine options, while limiting phones helps us develop self-control rather than remaining at the mercy of impulses engineered by technology companies. At the same time, students may elect a student council, but the administration can overrule any council decision without appeal, and students are not permitted to vote on most rules governing the school itself.

What conception of freedom does this arrangement presuppose? Does the compulsory curriculum assume that freedom consists in developed abilities and effective opportunities, rather than merely the absence of interference? Could the phone policy instead be defended on republican grounds, on the theory that attention-harvesting technologies place students under a kind of external control? And is there a tension between that rationale and the administration’s unrestricted power to veto the student council: can students be protected from domination in one area while remaining dominated in another? Please distinguish what the institution strictly presupposes from what merely happens to fit its rhetoric. In particular, if the school’s aim is to maximize students’ future freedom, does that justify present restrictions without committing the school to a positive account of liberty, and would replacing the administration’s veto with a requirement to give reasons or follow fixed procedures change the freedom at stake in a philosophically important way?
