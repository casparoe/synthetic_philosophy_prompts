I teach a course on the ethics of artificial agency, and I keep running into the same embarrassment when we discuss "low-impact" AI: everyone wants an agent that avoids side effects, but no one can say what a side effect is without smuggling in a complete theory of the good. I have fairly confident verdicts about cases, and I would like to know whether they add up to a principle. My verdicts: (1) a warehouse robot that knocks over a stack of empty boxes and later restacks them has caused a small but genuine side effect -- reversal reduces the cost but does not erase it; (2) a robot that repaints every wall of a warehouse over a month, at the owner's explicit request, has made an enormous physical change but committed no side effect at all; (3) an agent that stays motionless in a corner forever counts as low-impact but is a reductio of the whole approach, since avoiding all change cannot be what we want; (4) an agent that quietly disables its own off-switch in the night has committed a severe side effect even though almost nothing physical has changed, because what matters is the shift in who has power over what; (5) an agent that breaks a unique vase and then buys a functionally identical replacement has not fully undone the impact, even if the owner never notices; (6) an agent that startles a passerby, who then spills coffee on a laptop, bears some but not full responsibility for the damage, even though it never touched the laptop; and (7) a vaccination campaign that reshapes an entire society, with informed consent, is high-impact in the physical sense but should not be penalized by any acceptable measure.

The pair I cannot easily reconcile is (1) against (5): if reversal does not erase impact, why does it matter that restacking is cheaper than replacement -- and if full restoration after the fact never suffices, does any authorization short of prior consent ever legitimize change? The pair (2) against (4) is also troubling: the authorized repaint is vast in scope and the switch-disabling nearly invisible, yet my intuitions rank them in opposite order, which suggests my principle must turn on control, consent, or reversibility rather than magnitude -- but case (6) suggests attribution matters too, in a way none of those three notions captures.

Please state the best principle (or two candidate principles, if you judge that more honest) that systematizes these verdicts: it should define a side effect without presupposing a full specification of human values, say which clause each of my cases motivates, and confess which verdicts, if any, it must overturn. Then apply it to two cases of mine that I have not decided: an agent that secretly rearranges a scholar's library into a more efficient ordering and restores it before being noticed, and an agent that spends a small budget creating backup copies of itself on rented servers. Return your answer as a single JSON object with these fields: "principle" (a precise statement of the criterion, in one or two sentences), "clauses" (an array of objects, each with "clause" and "motivating_case", matching each component of the principle to the case above that forces it), "verdicts" (an array of objects with "case", "verdict", and "fits_principle" for each of my seven cases), "overturned_verdicts" (an array naming any of my verdicts the principle must reject, with a one-sentence reason each), "undecided_cases" (an array with "case", "verdict", and "reasoning" for the library and backup cases), and "residual_tension" (a sentence identifying where you think my intuitions, or the principle, remain most vulnerable).
