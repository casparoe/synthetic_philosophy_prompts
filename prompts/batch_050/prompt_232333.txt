My roommate is doing a PhD on distributive ethics and last night he walked me through an argument that he says shows prioritarianism was stillborn -- that it can't actually escape the leveling-down objection it was designed to escape. I've been turning it over all day and I can't find the exit, but the conclusion can't be right. Here it is as I remember it:

(1) Prioritarianism: benefiting someone matters more, morally, the worse off that person is. A unit of well-being going to someone at level 1 counts for more than a unit going to someone at level 10.

(2) So the moral value of an outcome isn't a function of total well-being alone. Two outcomes with the same total can differ in value: e.g. a world where two people are at (5, 5) is better than a world where they're at (9, 1), because in the move between them the worse-off person's gain counts for more than the better-off person's loss.

(3) But if two outcomes with equal totals can differ in value, then part of what makes an outcome good is the *shape* of the distribution -- how the well-being is spread across people -- and not merely what happens to any individual.

(4) Anything that can make an outcome better without making it better for anyone is an impersonal value. So prioritarianism is committed to the impersonal value of distributional shape, i.e. of equality.

(5) Now consider leveling down: going from (10, 1) to (6, 1). Nobody gains anything; the person at 10 just gets knocked down. But the shape of the distribution improves dramatically.

(6) If distributional shape has impersonal value, then there's *some* amount of shape-improvement that outweighs a given welfare loss. So prioritarianism has to say that at least some leveling down is good in at least one respect, and plausibly that some leveling-down acts are worth doing all things considered.

(7) But leveling down is good in no respect whatsoever -- that's the whole force of the leveling-down objection, the thing prioritarianism was invented to avoid saying.

(8) So prioritarianism collapses: either you give up distribution-sensitivity and you're just a utilitarian, or you embrace (6) and you're a full-blown egalitarian who thinks wrecking the better-off can be good in a way. There's no stable middle position.

His conclusion annoys me because it would make a whole literature look confused about its own foundations, but each step seems fine when I look at it individually. If you forced me I'd most like to keep (1) and (7) -- I really don't want to say that hurting the well-off with no benefit to anyone is even a little bit good. Which step do I reject, why did that step look okay in the first place, and what does rejecting it cost me? And if you think the argument is actually valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to drop.
