I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course in game theory, and I am grading homework submissions. Below you will find the homework question as distributed to the students, followed by one student's answer. Please grade the answer out of 20 points, and justify the grade with specific, point-by-point comments. Conclude with a brief summary of the answer's main strengths and weaknesses.

**Homework question**

Consider the following two-player game in strategic form. Player 1 chooses a row (T, M, or B), player 2 chooses a column (L, C, or R), and each cell lists the payoffs (player 1, player 2):

|       | L      | C      | R        |
|-------|--------|--------|----------|
| **T** | (3, 0) | (0, 3) | (1, 1)   |
| **M** | (0, 3) | (3, 0) | (1, 1)   |
| **B** | (1, 1) | (1, 1) | (0.5, 2) |

(a) Perform iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, where dominance by mixed strategies is permitted. At each step, state which strategy you eliminate and exhibit the strategy (pure or mixed) that strictly dominates it. If at some point a strategy is dominated only once another strategy has been removed, say so explicitly. State the final surviving set of strategy profiles.

(b) Does the order in which strictly dominated strategies are eliminated affect the final surviving set? Justify your answer in two or three sentences.

(c) True or false, with justification: a strictly dominated strategy can be played with positive probability in a mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium, provided the probability assigned to it is sufficiently small.

**Student's answer**

(a) Begin with player 1's strategy B. B is not dominated by either of player 1's other pure strategies: T does worse than B against C, and M does worse than B against L. However, strict dominance requires only that some mixed strategy do strictly better. The mixture that plays T and M with probability 1/2 each yields an expected payoff of 1.5 against L, 1.5 against C, and 1 against R, while B yields 1, 1, and 0.5 against those columns. Hence B is strictly dominated and can be eliminated. With B removed from the game, turn to player 2's strategy R. R is strictly dominated by C, which yields 3 rather than 1 for player 2 against T, so R can be eliminated at this second step. What remains is the 2×2 game on {T, M} × {L, C}, and no further elimination is possible: for player 1, T is the better reply to L while M is the better reply to C, and the situation is symmetric for player 2, so neither player has a dominated strategy in the reduced game. The surviving set is therefore {T, M} × {L, C}.

(b) The order of elimination can matter. When more than one strategy is dominated at a given stage, eliminating them in different orders may remove different strategies and can therefore lead to different surviving sets, so a careful analysis must consider the possible orders separately. This mirrors the well-known order-dependence of iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies.

(c) False. If a strategy s_i is strictly dominated by some mixed strategy σ_i, then σ_i yields a strictly higher expected payoff than s_i against every strategy profile of the opponents. It follows that s_i is never a best response to any belief player i might hold about the opponents' play. Since a Nash equilibrium assigns positive probability only to strategies that are best responses, s_i must receive probability zero in any Nash equilibrium, whether pure or mixed, no matter how small that probability would be.
