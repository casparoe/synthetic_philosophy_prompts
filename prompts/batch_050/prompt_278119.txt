"Virtue ethicists hold that morally good action flows from stable character traits. A recent line of criticism, drawing on social psychology, challenges this picture. (1) State the situationist critique of virtue ethics, and explain how two specific experimental findings are taken to support it. Here is the student's answer:

"Virtue ethics takes the central question of morality to be not 'What should I do?' but 'What kind of person should I be?' On Aristotle's view, the virtues are stable, robust dispositions: a genuinely courageous person acts courageously across a wide range of situations, and this cross-situational consistency is part of what makes the trait a virtue rather than a passing mood.

The situationist critique, developed by Gilbert Harman and John Doris, argues that this picture conflicts with the empirical evidence. Personality psychology since Walter Mischel's 1968 work found that measured traits predict behavior only weakly, with correlations between trait measures and trait-relevant behavior rarely exceeding about 0.3. Meanwhile, experimental social psychology showed that small situational factors have surprisingly large effects. In Milgram's obedience studies, most ordinary participants were willing to administer what they believed were increasingly dangerous electric shocks to a protesting learner when an experimenter simply insisted, and obedience rose or fell with situational details like the experimenter's proximity rather than with anything about the participants' characters. In Darley and Batson's Good Samaritan study, Princeton seminary students on their way to give a talk passed a man slumped in a doorway; whether they stopped depended significantly on whether they had been assigned to speak on the parable of the Good Samaritan rather than on career prospects, with those preparing the parable stopping at much higher rates. Situationists take such results to show that the fundamental attribution error — our pervasive tendency to explain people's behavior by citing the situations they were in, when it is really driven by their stable dispositions — has misled us, and virtue ethicists in particular, into believing in robust character traits that the evidence does not support. Harman concludes that there is no empirical basis for attributing such traits, and Doris argues instead for a fragmented picture of character, in which behavior is governed by narrow, situation-specific dispositions rather than unified virtues. Both conclude that if conduct is caused by situations rather than character, then our ordinary practice of praising and blaming people for their actions is fundamentally unfair.

The virtue ethicist has several replies. The most direct is the rarity response: Aristotle never claimed that most people are virtuous — full virtue is rare and difficult — so experiments showing that ordinary people behave badly under situational pressure do not touch the theory, which is about the character of the virtuous few. The situationist can counter, however, that the experiments do not merely show an absence of full virtue; they suggest that ordinary people lack even modest, widely held traits like compassion, since trivial pressures (being told to hurry) swamp them. I think this rejoinder has real force against the complacent assumption that most people are basically decent, but it does not follow that virtue is impossible or that character plays no role; the experiments show average behavior in groups, not that no individual ever resists. The situationist critique is therefore best read as a challenge to how we should think about moral education and institutional design, rather than as a refutation of virtue ethics."
