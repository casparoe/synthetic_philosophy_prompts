I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I sit on the seven-person interim governance council of a large open-source project — a cryptographic library that ends up embedded in web servers, consumer routers, and some hospital equipment. The project is incorporating as a nonprofit foundation next year, and the council has been given exactly one job: draft the section of the charter that defines the electorate, meaning who gets to vote in foundation elections and referenda, and present it for ratification in January. While preparing, I read Rousseau's Social Contract, and a short passage early in Book I (Chapter 5, the one often titled as being about the need to return to a first convention) has been bothering me ever since. Rousseau argues there that before we can examine the act by which a people chooses its rulers, we ought to examine the prior act by which a people becomes a people at all, since that earlier act is the true foundation of everything that follows. I won't pretend to follow all of his argument, but the structural point seems to describe our situation uncomfortably well: we are performing the act that constitutes the "people" of this foundation, and Rousseau, as far as I can tell, says nothing about who is entitled to perform it. Whose act is our charter supposed to be?

The concrete dispute is this. Last spring, the eleven committers voted 9–2 to drop support for a legacy processor architecture. The maintainers of a small distribution that supplies clinics running old hardware learned of the decision from the changelog, and they are now demanding voting rights in the new foundation, arguing that decisions about the project are decisions about them. They are not alone. Long-serving translators and documentation writers, who have never had commit access and whose work is not code, want in. Two large companies that embed the library — and that between them employ three of the eleven committers — have each asked for a corporate seat or a delegate vote. One council member has proposed open registration, under which anyone who creates an account can vote; our security people counter that we cannot even reliably tell accounts apart from sockpuppets, and that open registration invites a coordinated takeover of a security-critical project by whoever bothers to show up. Four options are on the table: (a) committers only; (b) anyone with a merged contribution of any kind in the past five years; (c) that, plus delegate seats for downstream distributions meeting some usage threshold; (d) open registration. The committers-only camp argues that legitimacy follows from demonstrated competence and accountability — the people whose judgment is on the line when something breaks should decide. The open-electorate camp argues that a decision's legitimacy depends on the consent of those it affects, and that the architecture vote shows what a narrow electorate does with its power.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the council before our December meeting. It should recommend one electorate definition (or a principled hybrid of the options above) and give reasons that turn on what the electorate is for rather than on which faction we happen to like. It should engage seriously with the strongest argument for the option it rejects: if you recommend a restricted electorate, take on the claim that everyone affected by a decision deserves a say in it, and explain where that principle has to stop if it is not to include the entire internet; if you recommend an open or broad one, take on the competence and capture objections as the serious positions they are rather than as gatekeeping. Finally, the memo should say what Rousseau's point about the prior act implies for the ratification procedure itself: the electorate our charter defines does not exist yet, so by whom, and by what voting rule, should the charter be ratified — and is there any non-circular answer to that question, or is the best we can do a transparently acknowledged leap?
