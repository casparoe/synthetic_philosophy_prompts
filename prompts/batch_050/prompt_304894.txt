I chair my university’s animal research ethics committee, which must rule on a proposed study of planning in scrub-jays. The researchers will train birds to store several kinds of food in different locations, then vary which locations are made accessible on the following morning. They claim that if birds cache more of a food where it will otherwise be unavailable, the birds are anticipating a future need rather than merely repeating an association between location and reward. Critics on the committee invoke Morgan’s canon—the principle that animal behavior should not be attributed to a higher psychological process when a lower one can explain it—and argue that successful performance could reflect learned location preferences, sensitivity to relative value, or simple behavioral flexibility. The study also imposes overnight food restriction, so the methodological debate has welfare consequences.

Draft a formal decision memo for the committee recommending approval, rejection, or approval with specified conditions. The memo should give a clear bottom line, distinguish parsimony of psychological mechanisms from mere preference for familiar explanations, and specify what experimental controls would make the planning interpretation evidentially stronger without requiring certainty that no lower-level explanation is possible. It should also address the welfare issue directly, including whether added deprivation could ever be justified by a more decisive test of animal cognition. The strongest objection to the memo’s recommendation should be stated fairly and answered with reasons the researchers could actually use in revising the protocol.
