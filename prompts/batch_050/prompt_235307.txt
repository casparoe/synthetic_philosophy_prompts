I run a rationalist reading group that meets monthly — usually 8 to 12 people, and we can take a full evening, say two and a half hours. Most attendees have read the standard material on AI safety via debate and on obfuscated arguments: the proposal that you can extract honest answers from a powerful system by having two copies argue in front of a human judge, and the worry that a debater can win with false claims by making arguments whose flaws are real but practically undetectable. They've read about it. I don't think any of them have *felt* it — the specific vertigo of realizing, as a judge, that you cannot tell whether you're being fooled, and the specific temptation, as a debater, of discovering that honesty is the losing move. I want to build an evening around producing those two experiences.

Design a runnable game for us that does this. Some requirements and constraints:

First, the core mechanic should be information asymmetry: debaters have access to some ground truth (cards, a hidden text, a generated problem set — your choice, but it should be cheap to produce in bulk) that the judge cannot directly inspect within a reasonable time budget. Each round, one debater is secretly assigned the true answer and the other a false one, and they argue over what the truth is. Crucially, the false answer should be *defensible-looking* — the game needs domains where plausible-sounding but wrong arguments are cheap to generate, so that the dishonest debater's job is hard enough to be interesting but easy enough that smart people can actually do it on the fly.

Second, run it as a progression. I want at least three phases: an early phase where the judge has generous tools (cross-examination, the ability to demand that debaters drill down into specific sub-claims and recurse), and later phases where the complexity of the subject matter scales up while the judge's time and tools stay fixed — so the group watches the judge's reliability degrade as a direct function of complexity. Ideally the moment the judge starts losing should be legible to everyone, including the judge.

Third, the scoring. No money. I want a payoff structure that makes the tension explicit: debaters should be rewarded for *winning*, not for being right, so that the honest-assigned player feels the pull toward fudging and the group gets to observe whether anyone does. But there should also be some mechanism — hidden roles, end-of-night reveal, judge confidence ratings, whatever you think works — that lets us measure afterwards how often the judge was fooled and whether judges knew when they were fooled. Calibration data on our own gullibility is half the point of the evening.

Fourth, the debrief. Map specific moments from play onto the moving parts of the debate-as-alignment proposal and its critics: the assumption that an honest debater with optimal strategy beats a dishonest one; the judge-verification gap; whether recursion into sub-claims actually closed the gap or just relocated it; and what it felt like from inside each role. I also want the debrief script to pose the question our group will actually argue about: does what happened tonight generalize to the case that matters (superhuman debaters, human judges), or is the analogy broken in a specific, nameable way?

Finally, two honest notes: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time we run this — I'm thinking of things like judges refusing to commit, debaters colluding socially, or the asymmetry collapsing because our false answers are too obviously false — and how to preempt each. And tell me which feature of the real proposal the game structurally cannot reproduce, so I know what to supply with framing rather than pretending the game covered it.
