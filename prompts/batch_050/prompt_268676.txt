I am preparing a guest lecture for a master's program in public policy: sixty minutes, one whiteboard, an audience comfortable with informal argument but with no formal training in social choice theory, who will spend their careers designing and evaluating real decision procedures. My subject is Arrow's impossibility theorem. I do not want you to explain the theorem to me -- I understand it. I want a plan for how I should teach it.

Specify, in order:

1. The dependency structure of the lecture. Which ideas must be in place before the theorem's statement can even be parsed, and which must be in place before it can be felt as surprising rather than definitional. In particular, advise me on whether the Condorcet paradox should come before or after the formal conditions, and defend the choice pedagogically rather than just narratively.

2. The single example that should carry the entire hour. I am considering a committee of five choosing among three candidates for a job hire, with full preference rankings. State what such a carrying example must contain (so that pairwise majorities can cycle and a third option can flip a social choice between two others) and what it must conspicuously lack, and explain why a national presidential election -- the example every student will have in their head -- is the wrong vehicle.

3. The misconception map. Tell me the errors this audience predictably forms, in roughly the order they will form them, and the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off. My own guesses, which you should correct and extend: that Arrow has proven democracy impossible; that the "dictator" in the conclusion is a political dictator rather than a technical artifact; that majority rule obviously satisfies all the conditions; that independence of irrelevant alternatives is obviously unreasonable (or obviously reasonable -- I suspect the room will split and I need to know how to use that split); and that the theorem is about strategic voting, which it is not. For each, indicate whether I should defuse it before it forms or let it form and then break it, and why.

4. How much proof to show. I assume the full proof is out, but some skeletal argument -- a decisive-set or pivotal-voter intuition -- may be what makes the result stick as more than an authority claim. Tell me how much machinery is worth the whiteboard time, what the sketch must establish versus merely gesture at, and where in the hour it belongs relative to the statement of the theorem.

5. The exit routes, and how to present them honestly. The students will immediately reach for fixes: restrict the domain, allow cardinal information or intensities of preference, weaken independence, accept lotteries. Indicate which two escape routes deserve the closing ten minutes given this audience's future work, and how to present them without letting the room conclude either that the theorem is a mere technicality to be engineered around or that institutional design is futile.

6. One check for understanding I can run in the final five minutes -- a single question or mini-scenario whose answer would reveal whether a student has grasped the theorem's content (for instance, whether they can correctly diagnose which condition a proposed concrete voting rule violates) rather than memorized its slogan.

7. What I should openly refuse to cover at this level -- I am thinking of the connection to strategic manipulation and the full proof apparatus -- and how to flag those refusals so they read as honest delimitation rather than evasion.
