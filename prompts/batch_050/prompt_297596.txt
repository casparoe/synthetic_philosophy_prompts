Right, I'm putting together my position statement for a grad reading group on aggregation and the ethics of saving groups, and before I let a room full of sharp postgrads tear into it I want an outside audit of whether the thing actually hangs together. Here's roughly where I've landed, after a lot of time with Taurek on the numbers:

(1) Taurek is basically right about the classic case: faced with saving one stranger or five strangers, it's permissable to save the one, because you don't aggregate claims across separate persons. There's no duty to save the greater number as such.

(2) The right framework for thinking about all this is Scanlon-style contractualism -- rightness is about what could be justified to each person, on grounds they couldn't reasonably reject.

(3) Where claims really are equal and you can't save everyone, fairness requires giving each person an equal chance, e.g. a coin toss between the one and the five, rather than just picking.

(4) At the policy level, public health bodies allocating scarce resources (vaccines, ambulance fleets, triage protocols) should aim to save the most lives, or maximise QALYs, which plainly involves aggregating across people.

(5) One person's paralysis takes priority over any number of mild headaches, however many headaches there are. Some harms just don't trade off against others.

(6) It's permissible -- I'd even say obligatory -- to save your own child over five strangers.
