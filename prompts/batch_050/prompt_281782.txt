So my roommate is in this behavioral econ class and I'm a philosophy major, and we've been going back and forth about akrasia (weakness of will) for like a week. His position, as far as I can reconstruct it charitably, is that hyperbolic discounting doesn't just explain weakness of will, it dissolves it, and that philosophers have been fretting over a puzzle that was already solved by a discount curve. He walked me through an argument and I have to admit I can't find the hole, but the conclusion can't be right, because it seems to prove that literally nobody has ever acted against their better judgment, which... come on. Anyway here's the argument as best I can write it down:

(1) Every case of so-called weakness of will -- eating the cake after deciding to diet, doomscrolling instead of writing the paper, snoozing the alarm -- can be modeled as hyperbolic discounting: the agent's preference ranking flips as the tempting reward gets temporally closer, and flips back once it's past.
(2) If at the moment of action your preferences rank X above Y, and you do X, then you did exactly what you most preferred to do at that moment. It doesn't matter where the preferences came from or what they were an hour ago.
(3) So in every case from (1), the agent did what they most preferred at the moment of action.
(4) But to act against your better judgment, you'd have to do X while holding, at that very moment, that Y is better.
(5) If you did what you most preferred, then in any sense of "better" that actually matters for explaining action, you took X to be better. A "judgment" that Y is better that sits there inert while you reach for the cake is just a commentary track, not a real evaluation.
(6) So nobody ever acts against their better judgment; akrasia is impossible, and precommitment tricks (Ulysses tying himself to the mast, apps that lock your phone) are just rational bargaining between your current self and your predictably different future self, same as any contract between two parties with different interests.

The step I keep circling is (5), because it feels like he's just defining "better judgment" as "whatever you end up doing," which would make akrasia impossible by fiat and can't be what Davidson and everyone else were worried about. But my roommate says if a judgment makes zero difference to what you do, then calling it your "better judgment" is empty piety, and I don't have a great comeback. Also (1) feels like it might be doing more work than it looks -- does the discounting story really cover, e.g., the person who just can't get out of bed to do something they genuinely want to do, where no immediate temptation is in sight?

So: which premise or step should I actually reject, why does it look so plausible at first glance, and what's the price of rejecting it vs. rejecting one of the others? If I have to give something up I'd rather concede (1) than admit there's no gap between preference and judgment, but I want to know what each option costs before I go argue with him again.
