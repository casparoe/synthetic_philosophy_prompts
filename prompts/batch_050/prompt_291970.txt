Working on a short piece about property rights and redistribution for a blog I run, and before I post I want a consistency check on the positions I keep defending in the comments. Here's roughly where I stand:

(1) Whether a distribution of stuff is just depends entirely on how it came about -- if everyone got what they have through legitimate original acquisition or voluntary transfer (gifts, trades, inheritances), the result is just, full stop. There are no independently just "shapes" a distribution should have.

(2) The Wilt Chamberlain thought experiment basically settles the pattern question for me: people freely paying their quarter to watch Wilt play wrecks any preferred pattern, and freezing the pattern means constantly interfering with voluntary deals, so any end-state theory of justice is anti-liberty at its core.

(3) Taxes to fund courts, police, and contract enforcement are fine, but taxing people to redistribute wealth is morally on a par with forced labor and not something a legitimate state can do.

(4) I'm a strict Lockean about initial acquisition: mixing your labor with unowned stuff only makes it yours if enough and as good is left for everyone else.

(5) Real history is soaked in unjust acquisition -- indigenous land, slavery, the works -- so a huge share of current titles is tainted and rectification is owed. Where you can't trace the specifics anymore, I'm open to the fallback I remember Nozick himself floating, using something roughly Rawlsian as a proxy for rectification (I might be butchering the details of what he said, but the gist is: when history's untraceable, approximate it with a pattern).

(6) Inheritances and gifts are completely legitimate, and I don't think desert matters at all for distributive justice -- you don't have to have earned what you end up with, you just have to have gotten it the right way.

So: does this hang together? Which pairs of these are actually in tension, which ones only look like it, and where it's real, what's the least costly thing to drop or qualify? I'm not fishing for a lecture on whether libertarianism is right overall -- just an internal audit of whether my own package is coherent, with the tensions ranked by severity rather than all flagged as equally fatal.
