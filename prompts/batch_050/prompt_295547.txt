Next term I'm giving a colloquium paper in our department defending the claim that players owe a small but real set of moral constraints to certain video game characters -- not all of them, just 'persistent' ones: NPCs that remember individual players across sessions, adapt their behaviour over months of interaction, and never get reset to a script. I concede up front that these things almost certainly aren't conscious. My argument is that moral patiency doesn't require sentience, because it can be grounded in sustained reciprocal relationships with moral agents -- roughly the way we think you can wrong the dead by breaking a promise made to them, even though there's no one left to be harmed.

The audience is a dozen analytic moral philosophers, most of whom have never thought about games for a second: two Kantians, one unsentimental utilitarian who works on animal ethics, someone who does the metaphysics of fictional objects, two philosophers of mind who reckon sentience is the whole ballgame, plus a game-design lecturer we've invited along for the occasion.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come, weighted by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, phrased the way each of these people would actually put it. For each: what I should concede, what I should hold, a ninety-second reply, and where that reply is soft. Make sure you include the overgeneration worry (does my criterion hand moral standing to Tamagotchis, Alexa and spreadsheets?), the person who'll say everything I care about is really just what cruelty does to the player's character, and the one who'll point out that we all shrug at virtual murder, so why does anything go. And tell me which question I should hope nobody asks -- I suspect its the game designer's, and I suspect it's about save files.

Please answer in British English.
