I am a retired experimental physicist who has taken up philosophy in my retirement, and I now volunteer as a grader for a friend's undergraduate course in the philosophy of mind. I would like your help assessing one student's essay. You are an expert philosopher of mind serving as my grading consultant: please think carefully and charitably, weigh each of the student's claims against Dennett's actual position, and before settling on a grade, privately work out what a fully correct answer would contain — though your visible response should contain only the assessment itself. Write your assessment in American English.

The exam question was:

"Explain Daniel Dennett's distinction among the physical, design, and intentional stances. On his view, what makes it true that a system has a belief? Would a present-day large language model count as having beliefs on Dennett's account, and how does his position differ both from eliminativism about belief and from the view that beliefs are sentence-like internal representations?"

The student's answer was:

"Dennett distinguishes three predictive strategies. The physical stance predicts a system's behavior from its physical constitution and the laws of nature. The design stance assumes the system will behave as it was designed to, so we predict that the thermostat will turn the furnace on without knowing its circuitry. The intentional stance goes further: it treats the system as a rational agent, attributes to it the beliefs and desires it ought to have given its situation, and predicts that it will act on them rationally.

For Dennett, beliefs are not concrete internal items but patterns in behavior. A system has the belief that p when attributing that belief yields reliable predictions of what the system will do. What makes a belief attribution true, then, is a fact about the interpreter: whether the intentional stance proves useful to us in predicting the system. Dennett notoriously extends this to thermostats, but he is careful to say that a thermostat has beliefs only in a loose, metaphorical sense, whereas persons have beliefs literally.

A large language model is an excellent candidate for the intentional stance. The physical stance is computationally intractable, and the design stance fails because we cannot see how the training objective maps onto particular outputs; attributing knowledge and goals, by contrast, predicts its behavior remarkably well. So on Dennett's account it has beliefs. If two rival interpretations both predict the model's outputs equally well — one attributing a desire to be helpful, the other only a disposition to produce plausible text — Dennett holds that we can break the tie by examining the model's internal states to determine which beliefs it really has. This shows Dennett is no eliminativist: he thinks beliefs are real, and he requires no internal sentence-like representations, unlike the language-of-thought view."
