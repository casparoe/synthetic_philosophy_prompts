I am a novelist by trade and currently serve on the five-member jury of a mid-sized national literary prize awarded annually for a debut novel. This year's complication is one I never anticipated: one of our shortlisted entries, a genuinely accomplished work of historical fiction, has been revealed (by the publisher, voluntarily) to have been drafted almost entirely by a large language model. A human editor devised the premise, iteratively prompted and steered the system, selected and rearranged passages, and performed substantial line edits, but the sentences themselves were generated by the machine. Our rules say nothing about this; they require only that the submitted work be an original debut novel in the relevant language, and the publisher attests that no human wrote the prose. Two of my fellow jurors argue the entry is ineligible because the words on the page were never *meant* by anyone -- the system, they say, shuffles linguistic forms without any connection to the world those forms describe, so there is no author behind the text and therefore no novel to reward. One juror counters that we judge manuscripts, not minds, and that our prize has never required an account of how a text came to exist.

What strikes me, as someone who writes for a living, is that the eligibility dispute conceals a harder question I would like our opinion to face squarely rather than legislate away: whether a text whose sentences were produced by a system that has never seen a battlefield, felt grief, or smelled rain can nonetheless *say* something about battlefields, grief, and rain -- or whether it can only ever recombine the statistical shadow of things other people once meant. I am not asking the jury to resolve the metaphysics of machine understanding, but I think we cannot decide the case without taking some defensible position on whether the meaning of a literary work resides in the causal history of its sentences, in the intentions of whoever selected and arranged them, or in what readers can make of the finished artifact. The human editor's role matters here too: she chose among outputs, rejected most of them, and imposed a final shape, and I am unsure whether that curatorial activity is enough to make her the work's author in the sense our prize presupposes.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate to the jury before our final vote next month. It should recommend a concrete ruling -- eligible, ineligible, or eligible under a new criterion we would adopt and announce for future years -- and it should give reasons my colleagues could actually argue with, including a fair and rigorous statement of the strongest case on the side the opinion rejects. I would like it to be usable as the public rationale for our decision, so it should be measured in tone and free of rhetoric about the end of literature or the soullessness of machines.
