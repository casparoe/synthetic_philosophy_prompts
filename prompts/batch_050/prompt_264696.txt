I have no academic background in philosophy, but I've been chewing on an argument I came across in a magazine essay, attributed to the philosopher Galen Strawson, against the idea that anyone is ultimately morally responsible for what they do. As I understand it, it goes roughly like this: what you do flows from the way you are — your character, motives, temperament; so to be truly responsible for what you do, you would have to be responsible for the way you are; but to be responsible for the way you are, you would have had to deliberately make yourself that way, and any such deliberate self-making would itself have to flow from some way you already were, which you'd then also have to be responsible for — and so on, without end. Conclusion: ultimate responsibility is impossible.

Whenever I explain this to friends, I reach for a physical image: holding yourself responsible for your own character is like trying to lift yourself off the ground by pulling on your own bootstraps. Everyone nods immediately. But lately I've started to suspect the nod comes too cheaply. Bootstraps can't lift a body for reasons of physics — one force, no external anchor. A person shaping their own habits doesn't obviously work like that: people seem to change themselves all the time, often by deliberately placing themselves in new environments, which looks less like tugging on your own boots and more like rigging a pulley to something outside yourself. On the other hand, a defender of the argument might say the pulley is still attached to a you that you didn't choose.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher who specializes in free will and moral responsibility. Think carefully, and begin with a short brainstorming stage before writing your main answer. What I'd like is an audit of the bootstrap analogy as a vehicle for Strawson's argument: first, an explicit mapping of the parts (what plays the role of the boots, the straps, the person pulling, the ground, gravity, the lifting); second, the places where the mapping breaks down, distinguishing the breakdowns that matter for the argument's conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic; third, an account of what the physical image quietly smuggles in — in particular, whether it converts an infinite regress, which is the argument's actual engine, into a physical impossibility, which feels more decisive than it may have a right to; and fourth, a verdict on whether the no-ultimate-responsibility conclusion survives once the analogy is stripped away, plus a better analogy if you think one exists.
