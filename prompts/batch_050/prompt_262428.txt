I’m putting together a two-page proposal for a one-year MA dissertation on Dennett, Caruso, punishment and just deserts, and I need it written up in a way that sounds serious rather than like I’ve just discovered compatibilism. The narrower issue I want to work on is this: if we give up the idea that offenders basically deserve to suffer, can a punishment system still do the jobs we expect it to do — especially proportionality, public condemnation and recognising victims — without quietly bringing desert back in through the side door?

My half-formed label for this is “desert drift”. Dennett’s practical, forward-looking account of responsibility seems vulnerable to one version of it: even if punishment is justified for protection and deterrence, we still grade treatment according to culpability, and that grading can start to look suspiciously desert-shaped. Caruso’s public health model may face the opposite problem: if offenders are treated more like sources of danger than agents who have made themselves liable to hard treatment, does that lose something morally important about wrongdoing, or does it actually expose our attachment to desert as unnecessary? I don’t want the proposal to pretend either thinker says something they don’t, so treat these as pressure points rather than settled objections.

Please write the proposal with a precise central question, a short explanation of why existing responses leave it open, and an account of what my original contribution would be. I’d like the method to involve more than summarising both views: perhaps develop a taxonomy distinguishing basic desert, institutional proportionality, liability and moral standing, then use a small number of carefully designed cases involving sentencing, restorative justice and preventive detention to test where each notion does real work. Include a realistic month-by-month plan, say what the dissertation’s likely conclusion would be while leaving room to be surprised, and explain what would count as the project failing. Also flag anything that risks making it too broad for a 15,000-word thesis.

No bibliography or invented citations — I’ll add the scholarly apparatus later. Write the whole thing in British English, and keep the tone appropriately academic without loosing sight of the fact that this is still a proposal for work not yet done.
