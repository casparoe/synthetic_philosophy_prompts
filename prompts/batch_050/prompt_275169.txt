I am preparing a graduate seminar on the philosophy of language and want to make sure I have Frege's opening argument in "On Sense and Reference" exactly right before I put it in front of students. Here is the relevant stretch of the text (1892, in the standard English translation):

"Now if we were to regard equality as a relation between that which the names 'a' and 'b' designate, it would seem that a = b could not differ from a = a (i.e. provided a = b is true). A relation would thereby be expressed of a thing to itself, and indeed one in which each thing stands to itself but to no other thing. What is intended to be said by a = b seems to be that the signs or names 'a' and 'b' designate the same thing, so that those signs themselves would be under discussion; a relation between them would be asserted. But this relation would hold between the names or signs only in so far as they named or designated something. It would be mediated by the connexion of each of the two signs with the same designated thing. But this is arbitrary. Nobody can be forbidden to use any arbitrarily producible event or object as a sign for something. In that case the sentence a = b would no longer refer to the subject matter, but only to its mode of designation; we would express no proper knowledge by its means. But in many cases this is just what we want to do. If the sign 'a' is distinguished from the sign 'b' only as object (here, by means of its shape), not as sign (i.e. not by the manner in which it designates something), the cognitive value of a = a becomes essentially equal to that of a = b, provided a = b is true. A difference can arise only if the difference between the signs corresponds to a difference in the mode of presentation of that which is designated."

Two things in this passage resist my attempts at a clean reconstruction, and I would like your help with each.

First, the arbitrariness step. Frege argues that if identity statements said that two signs co-designate, their truth would rest on an arbitrary connection, and so we "would express no proper knowledge" by them. But a sophisticated metalinguistic theorist will reply that the claim that 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' co-designate is a substantive, empirical fact about the established usage of a linguistic community, not an arbitrary stipulation -- and hence perfectly genuine knowledge. What exactly in Frege's reasoning blocks this reply, if anything does? Does the objection trade on conflating the arbitrariness of instituting a convention with the alleged triviality of reporting one already in force, or is there a deeper point about what an identity statement must be about in order to extend our astronomical or mathematical knowledge?

Second, the object/sign distinction. Frege says that if two signs differ only "as object" -- in shape -- rather than "as sign," the cognitive values of a = a and a = b collapse into one another, and that informativeness requires the difference between signs to correspond to a difference in "the mode of presentation of that which is designated." But this seems to make the informativeness of an identity depend on facts about which modes of presentation speakers happen to attach to which sign-shapes -- which looks suspiciously like the metalinguistic picture he has just rejected. Is Frege's own positive account vulnerable to a version of his own objection, and how is the sense/reference apparatus he introduces immediately afterward supposed to dissolve the appearance?

Please reconstruct the argument charitably but critically, and tell me plainly where you think the passage is simply compressed or elliptical rather than defensible as written.
