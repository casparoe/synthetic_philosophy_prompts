At a panel discussion on falling birth rates last month, one of the speakers -- a journalist who writes in defence of pronatalist policy -- closed his case for subsidising larger families with a proverb: when it comes to people, he said, the more the merrier. It got a warm laugh and it plainly did argumentative work for him, but afterwards a friend who dabbles in population ethics told me the proverb is either trivially true in a way that supports nothing, or else smuggles in a contested ethical theory without defending it. Pressed to say which, she admitted she couldn't cleanly separate the readings. I'd like the separation done properly. Take the proverb seriously as a piece of population ethics: enumerate the distinct precise theses it could be expressing -- for instance, that adding happy lives always makes the world better full stop; that a larger population raises total wellbeing and total wellbeing is what matters; that additional people are net contributors who produce more than they consume; that bigger societies are better for those already in them because of more art, genius, friendship, and division of labour; or simply the thin observation that gatherings tend to go better with more guests, extended by analogy to humanity. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or actually false, and mark where a reading runs into the standard pressure points of population ethics -- the repugnant conclusion, the mere addition paradox, the person-affecting intuition that we have no duty to create happy people who would otherwise never exist, or the slide from "a larger population would be better" to "we have reason to bring more people into existence".

Then do the applied part: identify which of these readings the journalist would actually need for his policy conclusion that governments ought to raise birth rates, whether that reading is one a careful population ethicist could accept, and whether the proverb as it functions in ordinary speech -- where "merrier" describes the enjoyment of people already present rather than the value of adding new ones -- can bear that weight at all. I'm not asking for your own verdict on pronatalism; I want the disambiguation, laid out as a labelled menu I can take back to my friend, with each entry's verdict and its dialectical cost. Please write in British English, and keep the entries crisp enough to quote in an argument.
