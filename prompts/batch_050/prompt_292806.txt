I have been persuaded by the extended mind thesis — not merely the claim that we use tools to think, but the stronger doctrine of active externalism: that certain external objects and processes are literally constituent parts of our cognitive processes, and that some of a person's dispositional beliefs can reside in a notebook rather than in the head. The passage that did the convincing is this one, from Andy Clark and David Chalmers, "The Extended Mind" (Analysis, 1998):

"In these cases, the human organism is linked with an external entity in a two-way interaction, creating a coupled system that can be seen as a cognitive system in its own right. All the components in the system play an active causal role, and they jointly govern behavior in the same sort of way that cognition usually does. If we remove the external component the system's behavioral competence will drop, just as it would if we removed part of its brain."

It is the last sentence that carries the weight for me, and I suspect it is also where the view is most exposed. I would like a map of that exposure — an account of what would count against active externalism, not a survey of the published debate. In particular:

1. The removal test seems to prove too much. Behavioral competence also drops when you take the ladder away from a man painting a ceiling, or the pen from someone mid-signature, yet nobody calls the ladder part of the man's body or the pen part of his speech act. What distinguishes a genuinely cognitive coupling from mere causal scaffolding? And what would the world have to look like for no principled line to exist here — would that refute the thesis outright, or only leave it chronically undermotivated?

2. What is the thesis's empirical exposure? Suppose it turned out that information stored externally and information stored in biological memory obey systematically different dynamics — different susceptibility to interference, decay, consolidation, and reconstruction. Would that show that the notebook case is disanalogous to ordinary memory, or only that memory comes in kinds? I want to know which experiments or findings a proponent of the view should actually fear.

3. Which of the view's awkward consequences would its own defenders concede as counting against it, as opposed to bullets they are prepared to bite? For instance: the apparent implication that my mind expanded the day I bought the notebook and contracts each time I leave it at home; or that a smartphone believes things while switched off and in a drawer; or that vandalizing someone's notebook can constitute a kind of assault on their mind.

4. Finally, rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and give an honest verdict on whether the thesis has been insulated — by added conditions on coupling, reliability, and accessibility — into something that no evidence could dislodge, and whether that would be a defect in a view of this kind or simply the normal maturation of a philosophical position.

Lead with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and do not restate the question.
