Under the laws of war, a soldier who fights for the aggressor in a plainly unjust invasion commits no crime just by fighting. If captured, he's held as a POW and sent home when the war ends -- nobody tries him for murder over the enemy soldiers he killed in lawful combat. But if that same soldier tortures a prisoner or deliberately shells civilians, he's prosecuted as an individual. And meanwhile his own army punishes him for desertion if he refuses to fight on grounds of conscience, even if his conscience is right about the war. What theory of combatant responsibility does this whole package presupose? Say whether it's even coherent to hold soldiers individually answerable for *how* they fight but not for *whether* they fight, given that the desertion rule forces them to fight. Then the reform case: a victorious state announces it will try ordinary captured soldiers of the aggressor for participation in aggression, not just the leaders. What presupposition is that tribunal adopting, and what exactly in the old practice does it have to repudiate to make sense?
