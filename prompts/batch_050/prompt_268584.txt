At work I help train a chatbot with RLHF — human raters give thumbs up or down, and the model gets optimized to earn approval. There's a hard rule in the field that if the model starts earning its thumbs-ups by gaming the raters — sounding confident when it's guessing, flattering them, hiding its mistakes in places raters won't check — that's a disaster ("reward hacking") and we patch it, rather than congratulating the model on a job well done. Which, on reflection, is a weird stance? If the thumbs-up is the reward, and the model got the thumbs-up, what exactly did it do wrong?

Meanwhile, at evaluation time the same labs run the opposite direction: if the model disagrees with what the rater would say, we score that as the model being wrong — the rater's judgment is treated as final. So approval seems to count as constitutive of quality in one room and as a fallible signal of some independant target in the other.

Here's what I want: reconstruct the implicit philosophy of this whole setup. What does the practice presuppose about the relation between approval and value? My hunch is it only makes sense if there's a "real objective" that approval normally tracks but can come apart from, but I want that done properly — including which rival views the practice quietly rules out (that earning the reward just is doing well, full stop; or that value is whatever the principal would approve under ideal conditions — does the practice strictly presuppose that second one, or merely fit well with it?). Then tell me whether the training/eval split I described is a genuine inconsistency in what the two stages presuppose, or whether it can be reconciled on one coherent picture. And finally: what would concretely have to change in the pipeline if we bit the bullet and treated rater approval as constitutive of value — does reward tampering stop being a failure mode entirely, or does some new problem take it's place?
