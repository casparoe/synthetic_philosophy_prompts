So the classical vs. constructive mathematics fight is now over a century old and I honestly can't tell what anyone is still disagreeing about, and I'd like you to diagnose it rather than pick a winner.

Here's what makes it weird to me, as someone who came up through ordinary math and then fell into a type theory rabbit hole. It's not like most philosophical disputes where the sides are stuck with fuzzy positions. Both sides are *fully formalized*. There's a precise intuitionistic logic, precise classical logic, translation results going in both directions (the double-negation translations embed classical arithmetic into intuitionistic arithmetic, so in some sense nothing classically provable is just lost). Meanwhile working mathematicians in 99% of fields have never once needed to care, and yet the constructivists keep insisting the rest of the field is doing something between "slightly confused" and "literally meaningless," and the classical crowd mostly rolls its eyes. And the punchline: constructive proofs turned out to be practically *useful* in ways nobody predicted -- proof assistants, program extraction, all that. So the debate is simultaneously dead (nobody's mind has changed in decades) and weirdly alive (the constructive side keeps cashing checks the classical side didn't write).

My hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than accept: the disagreement is about what logical vocabulary *means* -- the constructivist reads "or" and "there exists" as claims about what you can in principle exhibit, the classicist reads them as claims about a mind-independent reality, and once you fix a reading, the contested principles (excluded middle, double negation elimination) just fall out of your semantics. If that's right then the dispute is in some sense verbal -- each side is correct given its own meaning assignment -- but that feels too cheap, because surely "what does 'there exists a prime' mean" is a real question with stakes, not a stipulation.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is there a hidden factual question (about, say, the structure of mathematical reality or about what finite minds can survey)? Are the sides answering different questions under one name? Is it a norms-of-justification dispute dressed up as a logic dispute? Or is it bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, and the formal results just let each camp translate the other's work rather than refute it?

Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress -- a theorem, a conceptual distinction, a shift in what mathematicians need from proofs -- or argue that nothing would. And be self-critical about your own verdict: name the specific argument or consideration that would change your mind about the diagnosis, and flag where your account is most likely to be papering over a real difference for the sake of tidiness.
