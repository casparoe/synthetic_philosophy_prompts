I am a first-year PhD student working at the intersection of aesthetics and the philosophy of mind, and I am trying to fix the architecture of a dissertation on imaginative resistance -- the familiar phenomenon whereby a reader who will happily follow a fiction into dragons, time travel, or outright contradiction baulks when the fiction asks her to imagine that some horrific act is morally right, or that some trivial fact about the world is otherwise. I have three years, a background in philosophy of language rather than empirical psychology, and no lab access.

I want your help deciding which open questions in this area my dissertation should actually be built around. The candidate questions I have identified are roughly these. First, whether resistance is really a unitary phenomenon at all, or whether the standard cases conflate at least three distinct things: an inability to make the deviant claim vivid, a refusal to grant the author authority over that part of the fiction, and a correct apprehension that the fiction itself is incoherent at that point. Second, whether resistance is best explained by features of the reader (her moral commitments, her habits of simulation) or by features of the text (its genre, its framing devices, the way the deviant claim is introduced), and how one would even design a conceptual rather than experimental argument to separate these. Third, whether the phenomenon is specific to morality or is a special case of a wider failure to imagine grossly deviant evaluative or aesthetic norms -- the fiction in which cruelty is funny, or in which a child's death is the happy ending. Fourth, how the standard account changes when the fiction is interactive rather than fixed: in tabletop role-play, improvised theatre, and co-authored storytelling with language models, the reader is partly the author, and the question of whose authority is being resisted becomes unclear. Fifth, whether imaginative resistance tells us anything about the modal epistemology of value -- whether the felt impossibility of imagining cruelty being right is evidence of anything, or is merely a felt impossibility.

Please rank these five questions for my purposes, and defend the ranking. In particular: identify which questions are upstream of which (if the first question is resolved in a certain way, do the others dissolve or sharpen?); which are tractable for someone working with conceptual tools rather than experiments; which would most change how the rest of the literature is read if settled; and which you would deprioritise on the grounds that even a good answer would leave the surrounding debate unmoved. If one of my five questions is badly posed, say so and explain what question it should be replaced with. If I have omitted a candidate question that outranks everything on my list, add it and justify its inclusion. What I want is not a survey of the terrain but a defended research agenda, including the criteria you are using and what developments over the next few years would make you reorder it.

Please write your answer in British English.
