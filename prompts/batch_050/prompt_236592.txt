Okay so some background: I'm a grad student TAing a history of philosophy survey and I'm putting together the review sheet on pessimism, and I keep arguing with a friend (a huge Epicurus guy, unfortunately) about whether Schopenhauer's pessimism is actually a distinct position or just Epicureanism on a bad day. Help me settle it properly.

Here's the passage I keep coming back to, from Schopenhauer's essay "On the Sufferings of the World" (it's from the Parerga; I'm reading it in the old T. Bailey Saunders translation):

"I know of no greater absurdity than that propounded by most systems of philosophy in declaring evil to be negative in its character. Evil is just what is positive; it makes its own existence felt. [...] It is the good which is negative; in other words, happiness and satisfaction always imply some desire fulfilled, some state of pain brought to an end."

And immediately after he says: "This explains the fact that we generally find pleasure to be not nearly so pleasant as we expected, and pain very much more painful."

Now compare this with Epicurus's claim that the height of pleasure just is the absence of pain and disturbance (katastematic pleasure, ataraxia, all that). The weird thing is they seem to accept the same core premise -- pleasure is negative, it's the removal of a pain or a lack -- and then walk off in opposite directions. Epicurus concludes that once pain is gone you've hit the ceiling of pleasure and can stay there, Schopenhauer concludes that the same removal buys you a brief moment of relief before the next lack shows up, or boredom, wich he counts as suffering in its own right.

So here's what I actually want from you:

1. Pin down where the disagreement really lives. Is it an empirical dispute about what a desireless state feels like (blissful vs. boring vs. simply not felt at all)? Is it about whether desires can be permanently retired (Epicurus sorting them into natural-and-necessary vs. vain vs. Schopenhauer's endlessly regenerating will)? Or is the metaphysics of the Will doing the real work -- i.e., does Schopenhauer NEED the claim that willing is the thing-in-itself for his pessimism not to collapse into grumpy Epicureanism?

2. Take a side on that last option: is the metaphysics load-bearing or decoration? Note that in the passage he's picking a fight with Leibniz's privation theory of evil and basically just flipping it around. Can that flip stand on purely phenomenological grounds (the asymmetry in how pleasure and pain are felt, like in his second sentence), without any help from the Will?

3. Whichever side you take, tell me who handles the obvious counterexample better: some pleasures sure seem positive rather than mere relief -- a chord resolving, cold water on a hot day when you weren't even thursty yet.

Don't just summarize both views, I can do that myself. I want the exact point of divergence nailed down and a verdict.
