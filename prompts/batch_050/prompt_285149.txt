I serve on the ethics advisory board of a small company building what the press calls griefbots: conversational systems trained on a deceased person's text messages, emails, social media posts, and voice recordings, which the bereaved can then talk to. At our last meeting, the lead engineer defended the product with an analogy that I keep hearing in this space and that I want stress-tested properly. Griefbots, she said, are simply the next step in a long line of mourning technologies: we kept locks of hair, then daguerreotypes, then photographs, then home videos and saved voicemails, and nobody thinks a widow replaying her husband's voicemail is doing something ethically suspect. A griefbot is just a photograph that happens to be interactive -- a better preservation of what the person left behind. She used this to argue two things: that the product needs no consent framework beyond what we already accept for inheriting someone's photo albums and letters, and that our board is overthinking a difference of degree as if it were a difference of kind.

I am not convinced, but I want the case made or broken on the merits of the analogy rather than on vibes. So: please audit it. Give me the correspondence table first -- what plays the role of the photograph's frozen moment, of the photographer's framing choices, of the viewer's knowledge that this is a record and not the person, of the fact that a recording cannot produce anything the person did not actually do or say. Then identify where the mapping breaks. Some candidate disanalogies I have been turning over: a griefbot generates novel utterances the deceased never made, so it is less a record than a performance extrapolated from records; the model is trained and tuned by a company with engagement metrics, so the departed's "voice" is partly shaped by a third party's commercial incentives, the way a photo album curated and annotated by a stranger for profit would be; the deceased cannot contest or correct their simulation, whereas a living subject of a portrait can; and the interactivity may do something psychologically different to the grieving person -- sustaining the relationship rather than commemorating it -- even if the informational content were identical. Tell me which of these disanalogies actually matter for the two uses my colleague put the analogy to (the consent question and the difference-of-degree conclusion), and which are real but beside the point.

Finally, I want a committed verdict, not a survey of options. Either defend the photograph analogy against the disanalogies and explain why the relaxed regulatory conclusion survives, or tell me it fails, name the specific point of failure, and supply the better analogy the board should be working from instead -- an impersonator hired by the family, an actor playing the deceased in a biopic, a medium at a seance, an unauthorized posthumous memoir, whatever you think genuinely fits -- and show that your replacement analogy supports whatever consent and oversight requirements you think follow. If you conclude that some features of the product (proactive messages from the "deceased," the voice evolving as the model updates, a subscription model) matter more to the ethics than the underlying analogy, say so plainly and say what that implies for how the board should spend its time.
