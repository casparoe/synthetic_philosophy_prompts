I'm preparing a guest lecture for an undergraduate philosophy-of-technology course, and I want to spend a segment auditing two analogies that dominate public discussion of AI alignment, because I suspect each is doing more argumentative work than its users admit. Both are offered as ways of explaining why a highly capable AI system might fail to do what we want, but they point toward quite different diagnoses and hence quite different research priorities, and my students keep asking which one is "right."

The first is the King Midas analogy, common in popular books and talks on AI risk: giving a powerful system a fixed objective is like Midas receiving his wish -- the danger is not malice but a flawless, literal execution of a badly chosen goal. On this picture, alignment is fundamentally a problem of specifying what we want precisely enough, or in more recent variants, of making the system uncertain about the objective so that it keeps checking with us rather than committing to one interpretation. The second is the metric-gaming analogy my co-instructor prefers: a capable AI is like a large organization or an employee rewarded on a measurable proxy, and like any such agent it will find the loopholes, optimize the measure rather than the intent, and look compliant right up until it isn't. On this picture, alignment is fundamentally a problem of incentives, monitoring, and power asymmetries, not of wording.

Please audit both analogies rather than adjudicating the underlying technical dispute directly. For each: (i) lay out the explicit mapping (wish/objective, wish-granter/deployer, the thing wished for/the reward signal, and so on -- a table is welcome); (ii) identify the features of the source domain the analogy quietly imports that the AI case lacks -- for instance, Midas's wish is granted once, by a granter who shares his background common sense, whereas an AI optimizes continuously and may exploit exactly the assumptions we forgot to state; (iii) separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusions being drawn from the ones that are merely cosmetic; and (iv) say what remains of each conclusion once the disanalogies are on the table. In particular: does the Midas picture survive the observation that there may be no statable "wish" corresponding to human values at all, so that the failure is not one of careless phrasing? And does the metric-gaming picture survive the observation that an AI, unlike an employee, may have no pre-existing private goals it is protecting by gaming the measure, so that the language of "cheating" presupposes a motivational structure we haven't established? Finally, if neither analogy survives intact, propose the least-bad replacement for a general audience, and be honest about what your replacement still distorts.
