My social theory reading group has spent a semester split between two camps on whether "generations" — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z — pick out anything real. The realists argue that generations are genuine social kinds: people born into the same window share formative conditions (a war, a recession, a technology arriving at the same point in everyone's adolescence) that leave lasting, distinctive marks, so generation labels track something like natural clusters in social space — one member keeps invoking the idea that members of a cohort form actual "units" bound together by how they collectively work through the events of their youth, not just by shared birth years. The skeptics, mostly trained in demography, reply that generation talk is a marketing artifact: they argue that when you statistically decompose attitudes into age effects, period effects, and cohort effects, the cohort term usually does little work, that the decomposition is mathematically underdetermined anyway (age, period, and birth year are linearly dependent, so separating them requires arbitrary assumptions), and that the boundaries between "Millennial" and "Gen Z" are drawn where the data show no discontinuity.

I want to move them past talking past each other, so draft the joint statement the two camps could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. It should contain: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims — e.g., points about what the age-period-cohort decomposition does and does not establish, about whether any cohort effects are well documented, about the difference between a kind being statistical and being discrete, and about who bears which burden of proof; (2) a disagreements table stating the remaining points of dispute in wording each side would accept for its own position, with at least one row flagging a disagreement that turned out to be partly verbal and saying why; and (3) a cruxes section identifying two or three sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp — I suspect one crux is whether shared formative-age experience produces durable dispositions or merely transient shared attitudes, and another is whether generation boundaries should be treated as hypotheses to be discovered in data rather than fixed by convention. For each crux, name a concrete study design, dataset, or observable pattern that both camps agree bears on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts it would show.

For the epistemic framing: both parties should state, in a short closing note, what result or argument would lead them to abandon their own position, and the drafter should flag which of the agreed claims is most likely to be quietly contested by one side. Begin directly with the joint statement itself — opening line, title, or first agreed claim — with no preface, framing paragraph, or restatement of the task.
