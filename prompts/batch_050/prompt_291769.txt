I'm a software engineer (distributed systems, no formal philosophy background) who's been in this weird little reading group for about a year, and this spring we did consciousness stuff. I got stuck on something nobody in the group could settle, so now I want to try writing it up properly — there's a small grant from a local foundation that funds "neglected cause area exploration" and I want to pitch them a three-month, part-time project. They want a one-page proposal.

The background: panpsychism, roughly the idea that experience or proto-experience goes all the way down to fundamental physics. Here's my half-formed thought. The whole debate seems to be about whether experience is fundamental — the combination problem, arguments from physical science, illusionism as the rival view, and so on. But almost nobody seems to ask what strikes me as the obviously prior question for any ethical upshot: does suffering require structure? Like, even granting that electrons have some faint flicker of experience, plausibly pain and pleasure — the valenced stuff, the stuff that actually matters morally — only exists where there's the right kind of functional organization, the sort you get in brains or maybe certain computational systems. If that's right, panpsychism could be completely true and ethically almost inert: no obligations toward rocks, no astronomical sea of suffering in empty space, utilitarian calculus basically unchanged. If it's wrong, and valence can be "bare" — can attach to proto-experience with no organization at all — then panpsychism is maybe the biggest deal in the history of ethics, since fundamental-level experience would dwarf everything brains do by some absurd factor. So the question I want funded: is the ethical significance of panpsychism hostage to the thesis that valence requires structure, and can anything principled be said about whether bare proto-experience could be valenced at all?

Write the one-page proposal for me. It needs: the question stated precisely enough that a philosopher could tell wether I've answered it; why the standard debates (combination problem, illusionism, the usual arguments for and against panpsychism) don't already settle it; what I'd actually do across three months of part-time work — my default plan is conceptual analysis plus mapping which existing theories of valence imply what here, but push back if there's a better method; what a deliverable looks like; and what would count as the project failing, e.g. if the answer turns out to be obvious in week one, or if the whole thing just collapses back into the combination problem. One constraint from the grant guidelines: the proposal has to say why this matters for people making actual decisions, since they mostly fund animal welfare work. Honestly I'm not sure it does matter for decisions — part of me suspects the answer is that decisions should track expected suffering under our best theories of valence, not under speculative metaphysics, in which case panpsychism changes nothing practical. If that's the conclusion, address whether the project is still worth funding. Skip the bibliography, I'll fill in citations myself.
