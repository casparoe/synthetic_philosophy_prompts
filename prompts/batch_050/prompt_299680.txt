I want to figure out which of my moral reactions are preferences I'm entitled to act on and which are biases I should try to correct, and I'd like to get there from cases rather than from a definition. My verdicts: (1) Whether I favor a medical treatment shifts when the same odds are framed as ninety-percent survival rather than ten-percent mortality; once I notice this, I want the influence gone. Bias, obviously. (2) I care far more about my own daughter's suffering than about a stranger's child's, and I don't want to be argued out of this even though I have no non-circular defense of it. Preference, and a legitimate one. (3) Consensual adult sibling incest, in the version of the case where every possible harm has been stipulated away, still strikes me as wrong. I want to call this a bias -- but my reason is that I can't justify the judgment, and I can't justify my judgment in (2) either. (4) I would divert a trolley onto a side track, killing one person to save five, but I would not push a large man off a footbridge to achieve the same saving. I know the standard suspicion that this distinction is a quirk of our psychology, yet I still want to keep it. (5) I give more when shown a photograph of one identifiable child than when given statistics about thousands; I want to correct this -- but the pull toward the pictured child feels exactly like the pull toward my daughter in (2). (6) In general I hold that a vivid gut reaction should lose to an argument I have checked step by step -- but in (4) I am trusting the gut reaction against the consequentialist argument. Is there a principle that sorts preference from bias and saves most of these verdicts? State it precisely, say which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon if it cannot save them all, and tell me what it implies about the case I keep circling: I direct my charitable giving to research on the rare disease that killed my father, though the same money would relieve more suffering elsewhere. Is that the daughter case or the photograph case?
