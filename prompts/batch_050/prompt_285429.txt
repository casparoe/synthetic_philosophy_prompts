A sleep clinic affiliated with my university runs two programs under one roof. In the first, researchers monitor participants overnight, wake them during REM sleep, and immediately ask what they were just dreaming; the resulting reports are entered into a database as records of experiences the participants had in the minutes before waking, and the researchers treat some reports as confused, incomplete, or mistaken -- a participant can misremember or misdescribe what they dreamed. In the second program, therapists treat patients with chronic nightmares by having them write down the recurring nightmare, rewrite its storyline while awake, and rehearse the new version during the day, on the understanding that this changes the nightmare itself rather than merely the patient's feelings about it. Down the hall, a spin-off company sells a headband that detects REM sleep and plays gentle audio cues, advertised to slip into an ongoing dream and nudge its plot in pleasant directions.

I would like to know what theory of dreams this combined practice presupposes. It seems to assume that a dream is a genuine experience that unfolds during sleep, with determinate content that exists before and independently of the waking report, and that the report can therefore get the dream right or wrong. Reconstruct that implicit theory carefully, and then test it against two rivals: first, the view associated with Norman Malcolm, on which dreams are not experiences occurring during sleep at all, and a dream report is simply a judgment one finds oneself making on waking, with nothing it could be accurate or inaccurate about; second, the view associated with Daniel Dennett, on which the content of a dream may be assembled at the moment of awakening, so that there is often no fact of the matter about what, if anything, was experienced before then. Please describe these rivals in your own terms rather than assuming I know the literature.

For each part of the practice -- the REM awakenings, the status of the reports as evidence, the nightmare-rescripting therapy, and the cue-delivering headband -- tell me whether it strictly requires the presupposed theory or is compatible with one or both rivals, and what the practice would have to look like if it were rebuilt on a Malcolm-style or Dennett-style account instead. I am also curious whether the clinic is internally consistent: treating reports as corrigible evidence of an earlier experience seems to sit oddly with treating daytime rehearsal as altering the recurring experience itself, and I would like to know whether that tension is real or only apparent. Finally, if you think the whole framing is mistaken -- for instance, if none of these practices actually presupposes anything about the metaphysics of dreams, or if I have mischaracterized the rival positions -- please say so plainly rather than going along with the question as posed.
