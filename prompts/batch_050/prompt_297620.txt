I run a reading group that has just finished Book II of Plato's Republic, and for our next session we are pairing it with Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings. I want a serious reading of the novel through Glaucon's challenge -- the ring of Gyges thought experiment and the demand that Socrates show justice to be choiceworthy for its own sake, even under conditions of perfect impunity.

On the surface the parallel is obvious: both stories revolve around a ring that renders its bearer invisible. But the more I sit with it, the more the differences seem philosophically loaded. Gyges' ring confers invisibility on an otherwise ordinary shepherd, and the question is whether character survives the removal of surveillance and reputation. The One Ring confers invisibility almost incidentally; its defining feature is that it works on the bearer's character over time, and Tolkien seems committed to the claim that no one -- not Gandalf, not Galadriel, not even Frodo at the end -- could wield it and remain uncorrupted. With that in mind, I would like the reading to address three things.

First, does the novel answer Glaucon's challenge or quietly change the subject? Glaucon predicts that anyone possessing the ring would act as Gyges did, and Frodo's failure at the Cracks of Doom looks like a vindication of that prediction. Yet the quest succeeds anyway, and it succeeds because of Bilbo's and Frodo's earlier pity for Gollum. Is Tolkien offering a rival reply to Glaucon -- not Socrates' claim that justice is the health of the soul, but something closer to the idea that mercy is the only thing that can save a world of corruptible agents -- or is that a concession to Glaucon dressed up as an answer?

Second, what is Tom Bombadil doing in this debate? He is the one figure over whom the Ring has no power at all: Glaucon's impossibility, the person untouched by impunity. And yet the Council refuses to entrust the Ring to him precisely because he is indifferent to everything the rest of the world cares about. Does Bombadil show that immunity to the Ring is purchased only at the price of opting out of the moral community altogether -- and if so, does that strengthen or undercut Socrates' claim that the just soul is the flourishing one?

Third, where does the novel resist the Platonic reading entirely? Socrates' answer depends on injustice being a disorder the unjust person inflicts on their own soul; the Ring's corruption is imposed from outside, by an artifact. Does that asymmetry matter for the comparison -- or is the distinction less clean than it looks, given that the Ring seems to corrupt by amplifying desires the bearer already has, as with Boromir's rationalizations and Sam's brief temptation in Mordor?

Finally, say what the novel adds to this debate that Plato's argument does not. I am not looking for a plot summary, and I do not want biographical generalities about Tolkien's Catholicism. I want a reading that takes both texts seriously: one that shows where the Gyges framework genuinely illuminates particular choices -- Galadriel's refusal, Boromir's fall, Frodo's claim at the end -- and where an honest critic has to admit that the novel and the dialogue are, in the end, asking different questions.
