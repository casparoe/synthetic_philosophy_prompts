I direct grant allocations at a mid-sized private disaster-relief foundation. Four times a year, my committee divides a fixed emergency fund among proposals submitted by regional partners -- pre-positioned grain stocks, cash-transfer programs, cholera vaccination drives, seed distributions, and so on. The proposals are always framed in terms of need, and the needs are always real, so our meetings have degenerated into contests of who can describe the most suffering. A board member who studied philosophy recently pushed a 1974 essay on me, Garrett Hardin's "Living on a Lifeboat," and one passage has stuck with me. Hardin imagines fifty people in a boat with room for ten more, with a hundred swimmers in the water, and then writes: "(This, however, is to violate the engineering principle of the "safety factor." A new plant disease or a bad change in the weather may decimate our population if we don't preserve some excess capacity as a safety factor.)"

I am not asking you to adjudicate Hardin's politics or his conclusions about immigration, which are not my problem. What I want is narrower and, I think, harder: take the principle embedded in that passage -- that a reserve is not idle waste but a structural necessity precisely because future shocks are unknown -- and turn it into a written allocation procedure my committee can actually run in a three-hour meeting. Hardin himself concedes that the exact capacity of the boat is "a matter for argument," so the procedure cannot assume we know our true carrying capacity; it has to say what we do when the number is contested. Give me the questions we ask of each proposal, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; at which questions the procedure simply stops with a no rather than moving to deliberation; how the size of the protected reserve should be set and who may authorize drawing it down; and what we do when we have no reliable numbers for a proposal's draw on the reserve. Be explicit about which steps encode genuine reasoning from the safety-factor principle and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, because my board will press me on the difference.

Then run the procedure on three proposals, at least one of which should produce an answer I will not like: (1) a request to commit the entire fund, reserve included, to pre-positioning grain against a drought that forecasters put at forty percent likely in the Sahel this season; (2) an open-ended commitment to fund feeding centers for a displaced population whose size is growing, with no exit condition and no cap; (3) a cheap, one-off vaccination campaign in a stable region that touches the reserve only trivially but addresses a much smaller catastrophe-in-waiting. Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do: I suspect it silently assumes a settled answer to who counts as inside the boat in the first place, and I want to know what other questions it brackets that my committee will still have to answer by argument rather than by checklist.
