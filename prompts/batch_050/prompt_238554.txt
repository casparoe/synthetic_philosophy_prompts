I am a philosophy professor who, rather to my surprise, has ended up chairing the ethics advisory panel of a fertility regulator. The panel reviews around a dozen cases and policy proposals a year, and I have noticed that our deliberations fall apart whenever the identity of the future child is at stake. Our standing guidance defines harm counterfactually — a person is harmed only if they are worse off than they otherwise would have been — and in nonidentity cases this definition collapses, because the alternative to existing with a burden is, for that very person, never existing at all. At that point my colleagues (clinicians, lawyers, and patient representatives, none of whom have read Parfit) tend to throw up their hands and say either that no one has been harmed so anything goes, or that the whole line of thought is monstrous. I would like a reusable decision procedure I can put in front of them: something that operationalises the correct handling of nonidentity cases without requiring anyone to master the philosophical literature, runnable within a ninety-minute meeting, and honest about its own limits.

Please write the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions the panel must answer about a proposal, stating for each question what counts as a sufficient answer and who bears the burden of supplying it. It must, at minimum, force the panel to distinguish same-people choices from different-people choices before anyone reaches for the word harm; to separate the claim that a life is not worth living from the much weaker claim that it falls below some threshold; to handle the temptation of simply averaging or totalling welfare across possible future populations; and to mark explicitly the points at which the disagreement has become one of values rather than of logic or fact, at which point the procedure should stop and hand the matter to a recorded vote rather than pretend to a verdict. Please also flag the distinctions from the philosophical debate that you judge safe to drop in translation, and the ones that must survive even if they make the procedure harder to use, with your reasoning. Where the procedure is liable to misfire — cases it will handle badly or where following it mechanically would discredit it — say so up front rather than letting me discover it in a meeting.

Then run the procedure on two cases, showing the panel's path through the questions in each: first, a couple undergoing IVF who wish to select, from among several viable embryos, one carrying a gene for a condition that causes significant but manageable lifelong pain, because the pregnancy is already underway with that embryo and implanting another would mean a delay of years and possibly no child at all; second, a proposed national screening programme that would not treat or cure anyone but would foreseeably change which children are born across the whole population, with a small predicted reduction in average lifetime wellbeing among those born. Please ensure that at least one of the two runs delivers a conclusion you expect I will find unpalatable, and say at which step the panel is most likely to revolt and how I should handle that moment as chair.
