I’m in a university epistemology seminar and we’ve just been reading Wittgenstein’s On Certainty. My proffessor offhandedly suggested that The Truman Show is basically a film about hinge epistemology, and the more I think about it the less sure I am what that means. For anyone who hasn’t seen it recently: Truman has unknowingly spent his whole life inside an enormous television set, with everyone around him acting, until small glitches make him suspicious and he eventually tries to escape. Read the film through the idea of hinge commitments, but please don’t just say that Truman’s whole world was a hinge. Which parts of his outlook seem genuinely exempt from ordinary doubt, and which are merely very strongly believed empirical claims that happen to be false?

I’m especially interested in whether the film reveals different layers of hinges: trust in perception, assumptions about other people not being actors, confidence in ordinary physical objects, and the broader sense that one’s life has not been staged. When Truman hits the wall at the edge of the set, has he finally acquired evidence against a belief, or has one of the hinges itself shifted? And does Wittgenstein’s picture imply that Truman was rational before discovering the deception, or only that his doubts had to begin somewhere? Also say where the film pushes back against the philosophical reading—for example, Truman’s hinges were deliberately manufactured rather than socially inherited. I’d like a reading that takes both the film and the epistemology seriously, and ends with what the film shows about doubt, certainty, and escape that the usual abstract discussion misses.
