I am a TA grading undergraduate exam scripts for a second-year course in early Chinese philosophy, and I would like a second assessment of one answer before I finalize the marks. The exam question was:

"Does Zhuangzi regard death as an evil? Drawing on at least two episodes in the Zhuangzi that involve death or dying, assess whether his position is best understood as a consolation, an argument for indifference, or neither. Support your reading with close attention to what the episodes actually show."

Here is the student's answer, in full:

Zhuangzi's attitude to death is best understood as a consolation: he argues that death is nothing to fear because once we are dead we no longer exist, and so death cannot be bad for us, just as the time before we were born was not bad for us.

The clearest evidence is the story of the death of Zhuangzi's wife. When Huizi comes to offer condolences, he finds Zhuangzi sitting with his legs sprawled out, drumming on a tub and singing. Challenged, Zhuangzi explains that he did grieve at first, but then reflected that she had once had no body and no life; through a change in the qi she came to be, and now through another change she has died, like the succession of the four seasons. To go on wailing would be to fail to understand what has happened, so he stopped.

A second episode points the same way. On a journey Zhuangzi finds an old skull, asks it how it came to die, and that night the skull appears to him in a dream. The skull describes death as freedom from rulers above and subjects below and from the labor of the seasons, and refuses the offer of being restored to life. From this Zhuangzi concludes that death is preferable to life, and that the living who fear it are simply mistaken.

The same conclusion governs Zhuangzi's refusal of a lavish burial near the end of his life. When his disciples propose a proper tomb, he answers that heaven and earth will be his coffin and the sun and moon his jade disks; when they object that the crows and kites will eat him, he replies that underground he would be eaten by worms instead, and that to rob the one to feed the other would be unfair. Since the body after death is nothing to him, funeral arrangements are a superstition.

Zhuangzi is therefore offering a consolation of the same general kind as Epicurus's argument that death is nothing to us: grief rests on the false belief that death harms the dead, and once we see that the dead are beyond harm, grief loses its ground and indifference becomes possible. His view is thus a consolation, though achieved by argument rather than by comfort.
