I teach a senior undergraduate seminar on social epistemology and the politics of memory, and for the final unit I want a handout that maps the positions on a question my students keep circling: when a community's cherished collective memory conflicts with the best available historical record, which should give way? Rather than lecturing through a list of named views, I want a decision tree they can work through, so I am asking you to construct one. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a roster of authors: each branch point should be a diagnostic question, phrased as a concrete case or claim that someone could answer without knowing any terminology, and the questions should be the ones on which the positions genuinely come apart. I suggest the tree engage at least the following forks, though you should revise or reorder them if the logic of the space demands it: whether the truth of a belief about the past matters in itself or only through its downstream effects; whether a memory is functioning as a factual belief at all or rather as an identity-constituting ritual (say, a national founding story retold at commemorations); whether correcting the record would impose serious harms on the community itself, such as undermining reconciliation after violence or demoralizing a group whose solidarity depends on the story; and whether the memory's beneficiaries and its victims are the same people.

Two refinements I would like you to build in, using my own terminology. First, distinguish what I call the identity load of a memory -- roughly, how much of the community's self-understanding would unravel if the memory were abandoned -- and let the tree have at least one branch where a position accepts correction for low-load memories but resists it for high-load ones, so that this intermediate family of views is not collapsed into either pole. Second, include a branch testing what I call corrective asymmetry: the view that we owe the record deference when memory flatters the community but not when memory is the community's only safeguard against a record written by its oppressors. At least one leaf should be the position that accepts this asymmetry and one should reject it. Each leaf should carry a name for the position (or "unnamed" if nothing standard fits) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment -- the implication its defender least wants to own. Where a combination of branch answers is unoccupied, note it and say briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. Finally, flag any two positions your tree turns out to be unable to separate, rather than papering over the ambiguity.

Please write the tree so a student can start at the root with no background and arrive at a label for her own instinctive view; the questions, not the labels, should do the sorting. American English, please.
