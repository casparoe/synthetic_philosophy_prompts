The Frege--Geach challenge has been running for over half a century, and I have lost faith in the way it is usually prosecuted. The pattern is always the same: the expressivist proposes a formalism -- attitudes made consistent under embedding, higher-order attitudes of being for and against -- and the critic produces a case it mishandles, typically a negation or a disjunction; then a more elaborate formalism appears, and the cycle repeats. Nobody on either side seems to me ever to concede, or even to specify what would count as conceding. My suspicion is that the debate has stopped being about the formalisms altogether, and is really a disagreement about what must be preserved: the critic assumes that any adequate account must make moral inference formally indistinguishable from factual inference, while the expressivist takes preserving the surface grammar of moral talk to be one desideratum among several, negotiable where attitudes genuinely behave unlike beliefs. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a real, well-posed formal question underneath that could in principle be settled? If the disagreement is about the success conditions rather than the machinery, say what kind of disagreement that makes it -- verbal, methodological, or bedrock -- and identify what, if anything, would move it: a specific embedding no expressivist account can handle, a stipulated standard of adequacy, or an admission that the two sides are solving different problems. Please write in British English.
