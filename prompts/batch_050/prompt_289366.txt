A close friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the zombie argument against physicalism. She defends it; I think Kripke-style a posteriori identities dispose of it: "pain" picks out a brain state the way "water" picks out H2O, and just as we can describe water without H2O in a scenario that is coherent yet metaphysically impossible, she can describe a zombie world that is conceivable but impossible. To settle the matter, I pulled up David Chalmers's paper "Does Conceivability Entail Possibility?" (2002) — the source she kept citing — expecting it to state the argument in its strongest form. Instead, I found this early in the paper:

"Many philosophers hold that the step from conceivability to metaphysical possibility has been shown to be invalid, not least due to a number of apparent counterexamples. For example, it is often suggested that complex mathematical falsehoods (such as Goldbach's conjecture or its negation) are conceivable but impossible. It is also widely believed that a posteriori identities provide counterexamples: on this view, it is conceivable but not possible that Hesperus is not Phosphorus, and that water is not H2O."

This strikes me as decisive, and not in my friend's favor. If the argument's most prominent contemporary defender himself grants that the conceivability-to-possibility step fails precisely for a posteriori identities — and the identity between a conscious state and a brain state is, if true, an a posteriori identity — then the zombie argument rests on a bridge principle its own side concedes is broken. My friend insists I am misreading the passage but could not say how, beyond muttering about "ideal" versus "prima facie" conceivability, which sounded like special pleading: a distinction invented on the spot to rescue the argument from its own counterexamples.

Two questions. First, which premise of the standard zombie argument — the conceivability premise, or the bridge from conceivability to possibility — does this passage force its defenders to abandon? Second, given that the a posteriori-identity objection now appears to be granted by both sides, what is left for defenders of the argument to say? I intend to write my friend a short note closing the debate, and I want to state accurately what this passage concedes and what follows from it for the zombie argument.
