I teach the personal identity unit in our intro survey every spring, and every spring Dennett's 'Where Am I?' trips me up — you know the one, brain in a vat (Yorick) running a remote body (Hamlet) over a radio link, and later the duplicate computer-brain (Hubert) and the spare body (Fortinbras), if I'm recalling the names right. My students ask where Dennett is at each stage, I answer confidently, and then I notice my answers don't fall under any principle I can actually state. So here are my verdicts, and I want the principle behind them, if there is one.

1. Baseline: ordinary surgery. I go under anesthesia in the OR and wake up in recovery. Nobody thinks I went anywhere. Location tracks the living body. Fine.

2. Dennett's original setup: brain in the vat in Houston, body strolling around Tulsa, perfect zero-lag link, everything the body's eyes see feeding the brain, every motor command taking effect. Verdict: Dennett is in Tulsa, behind the body's eyes. The vat is just where some hardware happens to sit. I'm pretty firm on this one.

3. The classic body-swap: my brain transplanted into your skull, yours into mine. Verdict: I wake up in your body, looking out from behind your eyes. Person and location both go with the brain. Yes, I notice this sits badly next to 2, where I said the brain's whereabouts don't matter. Sort that out for me.

4. The duplicate brain stage: once Hubert exists and a switch can route the body's channels to either brain at a flip, flipping the switch doesn't move anyone. Whoever I was with before the flip, I'm still with after; the other brain is nobody, or somebody new. Nothing travels through the switch.

5. The bodiless interval: the body dies while the brain survives in the vat, as happens in the story. For that stretch I want to say Dennett is in the vat — but that flatly reverses 2. Or maybe the right verdict is that the question has gone slack and there's no fact of the matter until a new body comes online. I'm genuinely unsure which to say, and I'd like a principle that tells me whether I'm even allowed to be unsure here.

6. Immersive telepresence with no surgery at all: a pilot flies a Mars rover with a full wraparound feed and haptics, and after an hour she sincerely says she feels like she's on Mars. Verdict: she's in Pasadena, full stop, and the feeling is an illusion. But the sensory-motor loop here is shaped exactly like case 2's, so if the loop puts Dennett in Tulsa, why doesn't it put her on Mars? This is the one I keep going back and forth on (sorry).

7. The laggy link: same as 2 except the link has a twenty-minute delay each way, so the body can't be driven in real time — the brain recieves stale footage and sends instructions that arrive too late to be anything but suggestions to a local autopilot. Verdict: now Dennett is with the brain, wherever it is, because the body isn't really his doing anymore. But then location looks like a matter of degree — how much lag before you migrate? — and I hate that.

What I want: the best candidate principle (or two, if they're close competitors) stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing the work for which case. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't save — I assume at least one of 2, 3, and 6 has to go, but I'd rather be told than guess. Then run the principle on two cases I haven't listed: (a) one brain slaved to two bodies at once, both live, both feeding it, both obeying it; and (b) gradual replacement, where the vat-brain's neurons are swapped for Hubert-style circuitry one at a time over a month — is there a moment when Dennett moves, or when someone new arrives, or does nothing happen at any point?

End with a final line that starts with Principle: and states the winning principle in one sentence — or, if you conclude no single principle survives, Principle: none — followed by which verdict(s) I have to drop.
