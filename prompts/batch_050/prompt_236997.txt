I'm a retired experimental physicist, forty years of condensed matter and now suddenly with time on my hands, and I've fallen down the Everett rabbit hole. The thing that keeps bugging me is the probability problem: if the wavefunction just evolves unitarily and every branch with nonzero amplitude happens, what could it possibly mean that spin-up has probability 1/4 and spin-down has probability 3/4? Both happen. There's no chanciness anywhere in the dynamics. And yet somehow amplitude-squared is supposed to tell me what to expect.

What I'd like is a translation exercise. Restate this whole debate, the problem itself and the main attempted fixes, in the vocabulary I actually think in: measure theory, statistical mechanics, and frequentist inference. Concretely:

1. Restate the problem as a question about what could justify priviledging the amplitude-squared measure on the space of branches over any other measure, like the naive "count the branches" one. Why is one measure on a space where everything happens more than a bookkeeping device?

2. Take the main rescue attempts I keep seeing gestured at in the popular books -- the one where you derive Born probabilities from constraints on what a rational agent should prefer or bet on, the symmetry argument where equal-amplitude branches have to count equally and then you extend to unequal ones, and the one that sounds an awful lot like Boltzmann's typicality move, where "most" observers by the right measure end up seeing Born statistics -- and restate each as precisely as you can in terms of measures, typical sets, calibration, estimators, and so on. Don't just gesture back at the philosophy.

3. Then the part I actually care about: a residue report. Which claims translate cleanly into measure-theoretic language, which only translate if you fudge something, and which seem to have no expression in my vocabulary at all? My suspicion is that the thing that won't translate is the uncertainty itself. In my world "probability" means either relative frequencies over repeated trials or ignorance about which single outcome obtains, and in Everett neither seems to apply, since there's exactly one trial and every outcome obtains. When someone says I should be uncertain about which branch I'll find myself in, I genuinely don't know what proposition I'm supposed to be uncertain about. Is that a real problem my vocabulary can't state, or a sign the Everettians are asking probability to do a job it was never built for?

For calibration: I'm fluent in the physics side of this (measure-theoretic probability, typicality arguments, the usual frequentist/Bayesian fights) but a total amateur in philosophy, so flag any place where I'm importing a physicist's assumption that a philosopher would push back on. Also, I have a hunch the Boltzmann typicality analogy is doing more work than advertised, since in stat mech the typicality claim is backed by dynamics and phase-space volume, and I can't see what plays that role for branch weights. Tell me if I'm wrong about that, I'd rather be corrected than comfortable.
