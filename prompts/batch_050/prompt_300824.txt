In my logic seminar, the debate over the Liar paradox has been stuck for years among three positions. The gap theorist accepts truth-value gaps and a fixed-point semantics on which the Liar sentence is neither true nor false. The dialetheist accepts that the Liar is both true and false and weakens the surrounding logic so the contradiction does not explode. The contextualist holds that the truth predicate shifts its extension between contexts, so the paradoxical reasoning trades on an equivocation. Everyone agrees on the derivations; everyone agrees that unrestricted T-biconditionals in classical logic yield a contradiction; and no one has moved an inch. As an experiment, I asked each participant to construct the strongest revenge sentence against the other two positions -- a liar-like sentence formulated within each rival's own framework -- and each succeeded. Yet each treated their own theory's revenge problem as a manageable cost and the others' as fatal. My hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about which theory is correct but about which commitments are negotiable: each side ranks the costs of restricting classical logic, restricting the T-schema, and fragmenting the truth predicate differently, and those rankings rest on prior convictions about what a theory of truth is supposed to do that nobody actually argues for. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a substantive question underneath -- for instance, about which theory best predicts how truth ascriptions behave in ordinary reasoning? Diagnose the disagreement by engaging what each position actually claims, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal result, a sharper distinction, a new class of problem sentences, or an argument that the impasse is principled and should be expected to persist.
