A friend is drafting a paper around the following argument and calls its conclusion "the scandal of consent." I can't accept the conclusion, but I also can't find a premise I'm happy to deny. The argument:

(1) Consent to something is valid only if the consenter holds no deal-breaking false belief about it — no false belief such that, had they known the truth, they would not have consented. After all, deception invalidates consent, and what makes deception wrongful is precisely that it installs or exploits beliefs of exactly this kind.

(2) Whether a false belief is deal-breaking depends only on the consenter's own priorities, not on how they came to hold it. If you would have refused the operation had you known the surgeon's real complication rate, your consent is equally defective whether the clinic lied, stayed pointedly silent, or you simply never asked.

(3) Before any significant decision — surgery, a contract, sex, taking a job — people hold at least one false belief that would have changed their minds: about the other party's track record, intentions, or character, about their alternatives, or about what they will later wish they had chosen.

(4) Therefore virtually no one has ever validly consented to anything significant: nearly every operation was performed without valid consent, nearly every contract is void, and so on.

Which premise or step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you reject (1) or (2), explain why the deal-breaker test looked like the right way to capture the platitude that deception invalidates consent, then say what replaces it — restricting invalidation to false beliefs the other party actively induced, to facts they had a duty to disclose, or to deception about the very nature of the act — and give one hard case each of these replacements mishandles. If you'd rather reject (3), tell me what picture of ordinary people's epistemic position before big decisions that commits me to. And if you think the argument is sound, tell me what concrete revision to our practices of consent it actually demands, beyond hand-wringing.

End your response with \answer{...} containing the number of the single premise you reject, or the word sound if you accept the conclusion.
