A friend and I spent most of a dinner arguing about whether historians can reasonably claim to have established things about the past, rather than merely to have produced one more narrative. My example was deliberately unglamorous: I said we know, about as well as we know anything outside the present, that Athens lost the Peloponnesian War and that the Western Roman Empire ceased to exist as a political unit in the fifth century, and that no amount of interpretive disagreement touches facts at that level of grain. His rejoinder, delivered with the air of a trump card, was that history is written by the victors -- that everything I called "established" is just the version of events that whoever ended up holding power found it useful to preserve, and that my two examples only feel secure because there is currently nobody around whose interests are served by denying them. I told him I thought this proved far too much, and also that I wasn't sure what his sentence even asserted precisely enough to prove anything. He said the imprecision was a feature: it was a general warning, not a thesis. We went around in circles.

What I'd like is a disambiguation. Take the slogan in its commonly repeated form and lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. I can gesture at some candidates to show the kind of thing I mean: a thesis about the physical production of sources (winners write the chronicles and burn the archives); a thesis about transmission and canonization (whatever gets written, institutions of education and publishing decide which accounts become "the" history); a thesis about the retrospective standpoint of historians themselves (knowing how things turned out warps the narration of how they were going, quite apart from anyone's power); a thesis about whose categories and questions survive (we inherit the victors' taxonomy of what counts as an event worth recording); a deflationary thesis that everyone writes from somewhere and the victors are merely louder; and perhaps a meta-thesis that appeals to historical truth are themselves moves in a power struggle. For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or straightforwardly false -- the last seems worth taking seriously, since there appear to be prominent counterexamples, like the historiography of the American Civil War being dominated for generations by the defeated side's framing, or exiled and losing-side authors like Thucydides shaping our picture of their own wars.

Then, the part that actually matters to me: which of these readings, if any, would support the use my friend made of the slogan -- licensing the inference from "accounts of the past are shaped by power" to "there is no fact of the matter that historians get right, only narratives with different patrons"? I suspect the strong conclusion requires conflating several of the readings above, or smuggling in an extra premise that partial and interested sources can never support secure conclusions, but I want this done carefully rather than by assertion. And if the most defensible readings turn out to support only a modest methodological caution (treat sources as interested, triangulate, attend to silences), say so plainly, and tell me whether that caution, properly stated, was worth a two-hour argument.
