I'm putting together this year's comprehensive exam for our PhD students (philosophy of science / formal epistemology stream) and I need one question on the doomsday argument. Here's my problem: every year at least half the candidaates answer by retelling Leslie's urn story — you're ball number 60 out of the lottery, doesn't that make the 100-ball urn more likely than the 10,000-ball one — and then declare the argument either "spooky but compelling" or "obviously fishy" without ever identifying what actually makes it run. I want a question that makes that move impossible.

The crux I want them to hit is roughly this: the Carter/Leslie version of the argument only goes through given something like Bostrom's self-sampling assumption, and the conclusion flips (or at least collapses) if you swap in the self-indication assumption instead — the presumptuous philosopher worry cuts the other way. Meanwhile Gott's delta-t version presents itself as needing no anthropic assumption at all, just a Copernican premise that your observation time isn't special. Is that true, or has the anthropic commitment just been moved somewhere less visible? And does fixing the reference class problem save the argument or kill it? Above all I want a verdict from them, not a shrug.

So, draft for me:

1. The exam question itself, answerable in about 90 minutes, worth 20 marks. It must force candidates to take a side on whether the doomsday argument, in its strongest form, succeeds.
2. A model answer of roughly 700 words that is genuinely committed — I don't mind which side it lands on, but it has to earn the verdict rather than survey the options and fence-sit. If it concludes the argument fails, it needs to say exactly where it breaks, and it can't rest on the lazy "reference classes are undefined" complaint unless it shows why no defensible reference class choice rescues the argument.
3. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, 4 marks each, with a one-line note for each on how candidates typically botch it.

The five points I have in mind, though feel free to restructure if you think the crux actually sits elsewhere: stating the argument as a Bayesian update on the prior probability of doom rather than as the urn intuition pump; isolating the self-sampling assumption as the load-bearing premise rather than an innocent truism; the reference class problem treated as a live technical issue rather than a rhetorical gesture; how the self-indication assumption changes the verdict and why the presumptuous philosopher result makes both principles look bad in different ways; and a serious treatment of whether Gott's version dodges all of this or inherits the same disease in different clothes. A top answer must keep the two versions cleanly separated — conflating Carter's probabilistic argument with Gott's Copernican one is the classic way to fail this question, and the scheme should say so.
