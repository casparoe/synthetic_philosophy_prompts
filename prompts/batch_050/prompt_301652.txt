I keep coming back to Lewis's triviality result for indicative conditionals and I want to stress-test the menu of escape routes. Quick recap so we're on the same page (correct me if I've garbled something): Adams's thesis says the probability of an indicative conditional "if A then C" equals the conditional probability P(C given A). Lewis's 1976 result showed that if you demand this identity hold robustly -- not just for your current probability function but for all the probability functions you'd land on by conditionalizing on new evidence -- the package collapses into triviality: probabilities get forced toward taking only a couple of values and the conditional degenerates. So something has to give.

The escape routes I already know, which you don't need to explain back to me:

1. Expressivism / no truth conditions: indicatives don't express propositions at all, so there's no probability of their being true; what Adams's thesis really tracks is assertibility (Adams, Edgington, Gibbard, Bennett).
2. Restrict the thesis to simple conditionals: the proof needs conditionals embedded inside conditionals, so deny that right-nested conditionals are well-formed or deny the thesis applies to them.
3. One-probability-function contextualism: the collapse only follows if the equation holds across a whole family of probability functions; insist it only has to hold for the contextually given one (van Fraassen showed a single-function version is tenable).
4. Swap conditionalizing for imaging: keep conditionals as honest propositions but compute their probabilities by imaging on the antecedent rather than conditionalizing on it (Lewis's own fallback).
5. Go three-valued: let "if A then C" be neither true nor false when A is false; the proof leans on treating conditionals as ordinary two-valued propositions, so relaxing that blocks it (Bradley-style preservationism).

What I want: a sixth way out that is genuinely different from these -- not a rewording, not a special case, not a mash-up of two of them. Tell me which specific step of the triviality argument it targets and walk me through why that step fails on your route. Then be honest about whether it actually works or secretly collapses into one of the five above. If you don't think there's a standard sixth route in the literature, that's fine -- invent one, clearly flag it as your own construction, and give me your best guess at whether it survives scrutiny or has a fatal flaw.
