I am a novelist at work on a book that moves between three settings: the Bronze Age Aegean of the Trojan War, the post-Roman Britain of the Arthurian legends, and the Egypt of the Exodus narrative. I need a principled way to decide how much historical weight each tradition can bear before my fictional elaboration becomes irresponsibile. A fellow writer has offered me this criterion: a legend becomes history at the moment it contains at least one claim about the past that is specific enough to have been wrong, and that is confirmed by evidence arising outside the legend's own line of transmission. Please argue that this criterion fails -- that independent corroboration of a falsifiable detail is neither necessary nor sufficient for the passage from legend to history -- and in the course of doing so, propose and defend a better account of what actually changes when a community's story about its own past earns the name of history. Your argument should engage the real differences among my three cases rather than treating them as interchangeable, and it should tell me, concretely, what follows for how a novelist ought to handle each.
