I am assembling the final exam for an advanced undergraduate course in social choice theory, and I need one essay question on Sen's liberal paradox (the impossibility of a Paretian liberal) that students can complete in 90 minutes. The question must not be answerable by reciting the standard textbook example or by restating that unrestricted domain, the weak Pareto principle, and minimal liberalism are jointly inconsistent. To force genuine understanding, please build the question around the following original setup, which should be reproduced verbatim in the question itself:

Asha and Ben share a duplex with thin walls. Asha may practice trumpet in the evenings (T) or not; Ben may burn pungent incense (I) or not. Consider four social states: a = Asha plays, no incense; b = neither activity; c = incense, no trumpet; d = both activities. Each neighbor dislikes the other's activity more than they value their own, so their rankings are: Asha: a > b > d > c; Ben: c > b > d > a. Suppose minimal liberalism grants Asha decisiveness over the pair {c, d}, which differ only in whether she plays, and grants Ben decisiveness over the pair {b, c}, which differ only in whether he burns incense.

The question you write should have three parts. Part (i) should ask students to derive the impossibility within this scenario: identify the social preference forced over each relevant pair and exhibit the resulting cycle or absence of a best element. Part (ii) should ask students to evaluate two repair strategies: first, excluding "nosy" or other-regarding preferences from the admissible domain, and second, recasting rights as constraints embedded in procedures or game forms rather than as decisiveness over pairs of social states. For each strategy, students should state what it costs — respectively, tension with the anti-paternalist motivation behind unrestricted domain, and the abandonment of outcome evaluation by a social preference relation altogether. Part (iii) should press on the deepest issue: whether a Millian restriction of rights to a self-regarding sphere dissolves the paradox or merely relocates it, given that the thin walls make every action here affect the other party, so that the very interdependence generating the nosy preferences also threatens to leave the self-regarding sphere empty and the rights vacuous.

Along with the exam question, provide: (1) a model answer of roughly 600 words, written at the level of a strong but not flawless student; and (2) a marking scheme built around exactly five must-hit points, with marks summing to 20, where each point names the specific move a top answer must make and includes a one-line note describing the characteristic way weaker students get that point wrong (for instance, asserting that the paradox shows rights are simply irrational, or claiming the Millian fix works without noticing the vacuity worry). Everything should be written in American English, and neither the question nor the model answer should require citations to the literature.
