In a reading group on aesthetics we keep circling back to the same defense of AI-generated images: that the situation of Midjourney and its kin today is exactly the situation of photography in the mid-nineteenth century. The claim runs as follows. Painters then said the camera could not produce art, because the machine did the work rather than the hand; photography was accepted as art anyway; painting survived and was even liberated by the camera into Impressionism and abstraction; therefore the current complaints that image models merely automate and imitate are just the same anxiety repeating itself, and AI art will likewise be vindicated. One member of the group deploys this analogy to close every discussion, as if it settled not only that AI outputs can be art but that worrying about them is historically naive. I am increasingly suspicious that the analogy is doing all the argumentative work, and I would like it audited properly.

Please lay out the mapping explicitly: what plays the role of the photographer, the camera, the photographed scene, the act of framing and timing the shot, and the darkroom work of selection and development, when the target is a prompter, a trained generative model, its training corpus, the prompt, and the curation of outputs. Then identify the points where the correspondence breaks, and which of those breaks matter for the two distinct conclusions the analogy is asked to carry: first, that AI outputs can be genuine artworks; second, that present-day objections to AI art are merely a rerun of a prejudice that history already refuted. In particular, consider whether the camera's recording of an independently existing scene, and the photographer's continuous causal control over what the image is of, are incidental features of the historical episode or load-bearing ones -- and whether the training corpus, for which there is no nineteenth-century analogue, changes the moral and aesthetic structure of the case.

Finally, say what survives: does the photography parallel support the first conclusion even if it fails to support the second, does it support neither, and if the analogy as used is weak, is there a better historical comparison -- the player piano, collage and readymades, sampling in music, the printing press's effect on scribes -- that captures the structure of the AI case more accurately, or is the honest verdict that this episode has no close precedent and we should stop arguing by historical analogy altogether?
