While trying to forecast how long the current American-led international order will persist, I constructed the following argument, and I now suspect it proves too much. The forecasting method in question is the outside view: rather than reasoning about the particulars of American power, its rivals, its institutions, and its fiscal position, one embeds the case in a reference class of relevantly similar past episodes — say, hegemonic orders since 1500 — and takes the base rate at which such orders have survived a further fifty years.

Here is the argument:

(1) The outside view assigns a probability to a singular event only by embedding it in a reference class of relevantly similar past cases and reading off the base rate of the outcome in that class.

(2) Every singular event belongs to indefinitely many reference classes. The present order is an instance of: hegemonic orders generally; orders led by democracies; orders in the nuclear age; orders whose leader issues the world's reserve currency; orders less than a century old; maritime orders; and so on without any obvious limit.

(3) These classes have materially different base rates. So the outside view yields materially different probabilities for the same event depending on which class is chosen — anywhere from near-certain survival to coin-flip, all by an apparently legitimate application of the method.

(4) Any principle for selecting the correct class must itself be justified, and the only justification available is that forecasts made using classes selected by that principle have historically been well calibrated. But that justification is itself an outside-view inference, which requires its own reference class. So the selection of a reference class is either arbitrary or circular, and in neither case can it confer justification on the resulting probability.

(5) Therefore no outside-view probability for a singular event is ever justified. Base-rate reasoning about unique historical cases collapses entirely.

(6) But this conclusion cannot be right. Actuaries price life insurance from mortality tables and their firms remain solvent. Forecasters who anchor on base rates systematically outperform those who reason from the particulars of a case. And anyone who rejects (5) wholesale is left with the inside view alone, which is notoriously overconfident: project timelines, war durations, and business plans are all forecast more accurately by base rates than by the judgment of the people involved.

(7) Since (5) is absurd, some earlier step fails.

I would like to keep (1) and (6) if at all possible. Which step do you reject? For the step you select, explain why it seemed plausible — I still find (4) compelling on its face, and I have never seen a convincing non-circular criterion for choosing between, say, "all hegemonic orders" and "nuclear-age orders led by democracies" — and state precisely what rejecting it costs. Then work through the two most attractive alternatives to your choice and price those as well. Finally, apply the repair: given your diagnosis, does it remain rational to give any base-rate-anchored probability that the current order survives another fifty years, and if so, what does your answer imply about which reference class a forecaster should actually use, and why?
