I've been reading about the foundations of Bayesian inference, and I'm stuck on something that seems like it should have an obvious answer. The standard objection to the classical principle of indifference is partition dependence: if I say I'm completely ignorant about a factory's output rate and spread my credence uniformly over the rate, I get different answers than if I spread it uniformly over the logarithm of the rate, and there's no principled way to privilege one parameterization. So indifference can't pick out a uniquely rational prior.

My understanding is that Jaynes's maximum entropy principle was developed precisely to fix this. The idea, as I grasp it, is that in any state of partial knowledge you select the distribution that maximizes Shannon entropy subject to your constraints, and this distribution is uniquely rational because it commits you to the least information beyond what you actually know. That seems to dissolve the subjectivity problem entirely: the prior is no longer a matter of taste or convention but something you can compute.

If that's right, though, I don't understand why the literature still treats prior selection as a live philosophical problem rather than a solved technical one. Shouldn't an objective Bayesian simply compute the MaxEnt distribution in every situation and be done with it? In particular, when I apply the principle to a case where I genuinely know nothing at all — no constraints whatsoever — which distribution does it return? And why do critics keep raising the Bertrand-style parameterization objections against MaxEnt when avoiding those objections was the whole point of moving from raw indifference to entropy maximization?
