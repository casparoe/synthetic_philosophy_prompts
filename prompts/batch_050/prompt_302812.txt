Last night a friend and I got into a long argument about modal metaphysics, and we both ended up feeling that we'd talked past each other. The dispute started innocuously: I said that I could have been a chef rather than a philosopher, and my friend objected that nothing in the possible world where "I" am a chef is really me at all, since a person with a wholly different career, temperament, and life history is at best a close look-alike of me. I defended the Lewis-style counterpart picture: what makes a claim about what *I* could have done true is the existence, at some world, of someone sufficiently similar to me in the relevant respects who does it -- not strict numerical identity across worlds, which strikes me as a metaphysically gratuitous posit that also raises awkward epistemic questions about how I'd know which far-off individual is me. My friend found this genuinely appalling. Her core complaint was essentially the old worry that if my counterpart wins the lottery, it is not a piece of good fortune for *me* in any sense I can care about, so counterpart theory seems to replace facts about my possibilities with facts about strangers; and she added that the theory makes the truth of modal claims about me hostage to arbitrary choices about which similarity relations are "relevant."

Since neither of us landed a decisive blow, I'd like you to stage the argument properly. Write a dialogue between a counterpart theorist and a defender of transworld identity in which each presses the other's actual position rather than a caricature. The counterpart theorist should not merely repeat that identity across worlds is mysterious; she should explain why the transworld-identity theorist's own account doesn't obviously avoid the same charges (e.g., what grounds the identity if not qualitative continuity, and what to say about individuals whose counterparts across worlds seem to diverge in their essential-seeming properties, such as origins or constitution). The transworld-identity theorist should not merely appeal to the intuition that it would be *me*; he should explain how his view handles cases that motivated counterpart theory in the first place -- things like accidental intrinsic properties, the apparent contingency of origins in certain thought experiments, and the worry that haecceistic identity is explanatorily inert. Both characters should be philosophically competent, respond to the points actually made by the other rather than restating their opening positions, and at some point directly engage the question of whether the disagreement is really about the semantics of modal language or about something deeper -- say, what grounds the truth of de re modal claims at all.

The dialogue should run to a genuine conclusion rather than an artificial agreement: either one character concedes a real cost, or the two isolate precisely where their disagreement bottomed out and why neither could move the other. Roughly 800–1200 words, with the speakers clearly labeled.
