Ok so I teach intro philosophy at a community college and in about a month we hit the Daoism unit, which I have historically taught badly -- twenty minutes of me talking about wu-wei while twenty 19-year-olds stare at me like I'm describing a screensaver.

This year it's kind of personal, honestly. I started taking a pottery class in the fall, and I noticed something weird: when I concentrate hard on my hands at the wheel, everything turns into a lumpy disaster, and when I'm chatting with the person next to me I accidentally make decent bowls. But -- and here's the thing that got me -- when I noticed that and started actively trying to *not* try, the bowls got bad again. Trying to be effortless is just more trying. I mentioned this to a colleague and she said congrats, you've reinvented the central puzzle of wu-wei, now go teach it.

So: design me an activity for about 20 students, 75 minutes, no budget beyond normal classroom stuff, that makes them *feel* the paradox of trying not to try rather than hear about it. Some constraints:

- No lecture up front and nobody should hear the term wu-wei or Zhuangzi until the debrief. They should hit the wall themselves first -- ideally some of them should try to win by "relaxing on purpose" and discover that doesn't work either.
- The activity has to be something where conscious effort genuinely degrades performance, not just a gimmick where I claim it does. If there's a scoring element, the incentive structure should be set up so that caring about the score makes you worse at it.
- Give me a concrete timeline, any roles or group splits, and the actual rules worded the way I'd read them out loud.
- Then a debrief plan: which moments in the activity map onto which parts of the idea (effortless action, skill that's become second nature, the self-sabotage of monitoring yourself). I want to connect it to that story about the cook whose knife stays sharp for decades because he follows the natural seams instead of hacking -- but paraphrased in my own words, not as a reading.
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong when I actually run it. E.g. the gamer kid who reverse-engineers the trick, the students who walk away concluding "effort is bad, never practice anything," which is... not the lesson.
- And be honest about the gap: what part of wu-wei can a 75-minute classroom game just not reproduce? I suspect the real thing is about years of cultivation and maybe an whole way of living, and I don't want to sell them a party trick as ancient wisdom.

Casual is fine, I'm not looking for a formal lesson plan, just something I can actually run on a Tuesday.
