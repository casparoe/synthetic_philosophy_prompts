A philosopher committed to the self-sampling assumption (roughly: reason as though you were a random sample from your reference class) and one committed to the self-indication assumption (roughly: treat your own existence as evidence favouring hypotheses on which more observers like you exist) have spent years trading the standard objections — the Doomsday argument on one side, the presumptuous philosopher on the other — and have now agreed to an adversarial collaboration. Draft the joint statement they should both be able to sign.

I want four things: (1) a numbered list of claims both accept — about what Bayesian conditionalisation requires of indexical evidence, about which puzzle cases any adequate view must deliver a verdict on, and about where the reference-class problem bites each of them; (2) a short table of their residual disagreements, each formulated in wording its own side would accept; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one side to concede or revise; and (4) for each crux, a case or argument both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

To keep them honest, the statement must include their registered predictions on the following case, which I designed to prise the two views apart: an experimenter flips a fair coin; on heads she creates ten observers, all with blue eyes; on tails she creates a thousand observers, exactly ten of whom have blue eyes. You wake as one of the created observers, note that your eyes are blue, and must assign a probability to tails. Record what each view says if the answer is permitted to depend on the choice of reference class, and what each says if it is not — and whether either side is embarrassed by its own verdict.

Finally, if part of their dispute turns out to be verbal — about what a reference class is, or about what the probabilities in question are probabilities of — the statement should say which part and why, rather than papering over it.
