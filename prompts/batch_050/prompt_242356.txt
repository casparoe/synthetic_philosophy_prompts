Two colleagues of mine, both economists with an interest in history, have been carrying on a running dispute about the economic and institutional legacy of the British Raj. Their positions roughly track the well-known public disagreement between Niall Ferguson's defense of the British Empire as a net exporter of liberal institutions and Shashi Tharoor's case that the Raj was, above all, a systematic extraction. I'd like you to adjudicate their exchange, move by move. I have no stake I'm willing to declare; I just want a fair referee.

(1) Priya opened with the inheritance argument. Whatever extraction occurred, she said, the Raj left India with a railway network, a unified administrative service, a common law tradition, English as a link language, and parliamentary institutions -- the very foundations on which post-independence democracy and the modern Indian economy were built. The counterfactual, she argued, is not some idealized free India but a subcontinent of warring princely states, which would have generated none of these public goods. On a proper balance sheet, the institutional column outweighs the extraction column.

(2) Dev replied that this misprices both columns. The railways were built with government-guaranteed returns of around five percent to British investors regardless of profitability, and their routes were designed to move troops and raw materials to ports, not to integrate an internal Indian market -- infrastructure for extraction, not development, paid for by Indian revenue. He invoked the old drain-of-wealth thesis and the standard figures: India's share of world output collapsing from roughly a quarter around 1700 to a few percent by independence, literacy in the mid-teens and life expectancy in the low thirties after nearly two centuries of British rule. The real counterfactual, he said, is not chaos but indigenous modernization on the model of Japan, which colonial rule foreclosed -- not least by dismantling India's textile industry to make room for Manchester's mills.

(3) Priya answered that Dev's headline statistics are doing less work than he thinks. The collapse in India's share of world output is largely an arithmetic consequence of the Industrial Revolution raising output elsewhere, not evidence that Indians became poorer in absolute terms; per capita income under the Raj grew slowly rather than declining. The princely states, the nearest thing to a within-India counterfactual, were not obviously better governed or faster growing. And famines, she noted, long predated British rule.

(4) Dev's final move: near-zero per capita growth in the half-century before independence, during exactly the decades when similarly poor Japan industrialized, shows the binding constraint was policy, not culture or arithmetic; the late-Victorian famines and the Bengal famine of 1943 killed millions under a government accountable to London rather than to the people starving; and the princely states are not a clean counterfactual at all, since they had been stripped of control over trade and defense and bound into subsidiary alliances. As for the institutions Priya credits, Indians won them through struggle against the Raj, not as its gift.

Please adjudicate. Structure your response in three sections, in this order: first, "Move-by-move adjudication" (roughly 600 words): rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification of each rating that engages the economic reasoning, not just the rhetoric; second, "Where the disagreement actually lives" (roughly 250 words): identify which parts of the dispute are empirical and could in principle be settled by economic history, which parts turn on a counterfactual that evidence can inform but not settle, and which parts are really disagreements about how to weigh incomparable goods; third, "Overall verdict" (roughly 150 words): say who has the better of the exchange on which specific points, and what the strongest version of the losing side's position would look like.
