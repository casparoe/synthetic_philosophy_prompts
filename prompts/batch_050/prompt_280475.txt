I'm in a grad reading group on epistemology and we just spent three weeks on Williamson's anti-luminosity argument and the fallout for the KK principle (the idea that if you know p, you're at least in a position to know that you know p). The room is split and it's getting repetitive: half of us think KK, or something close, is a platitude that internalism about justification basically requires, and that the margin-for-error argument just shows knowledge is "coarse-grained" in a way nobody should've denied anyway. The other half thinks the anti-luminosity argument is airtight and that once you grant it, KK fails in full generality and a bunch of internalist projects die with it. We keep talking past each other about what exactly the sorites step assumes and whether the safety condition is a premise or a conclusion.

So: draft the joint statement that a committed KK-defender and a Williamson-style anti-luminosity theorist could both sign after an honest adversarial collaboration. I want a numbered section of agreed claims first — and I mean claims, not vibes; things like whether both can accept the validity of the margin-for-error reasoning given its premises, what both agree the argument does and does not show about iterating knowledge, and whether both agree that *something* must be luminous if KK in full generality is true. Then a disagreements table where each dispute is stated precisely, with each side's position phrased the way that side itself would insist on putting it (no strawman wording the other camp would refuse to sign). Then the cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that settling one would force at least one camp to concede something, and for each crux, a case, argument, or formal result both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

As a running test case, use a scenario our group made up: a post-op patient on a night ward whose pain slowly fades as the medication kicks in, where the question is whether "I am in pain" is luminous for her — whenever she's in pain, does she know it, and whenever she knows it, does she know she knows? Both camps should have to say explicitly what their view predicts about her epistemic situation at each stage, including the penultimate moment. If some part of the KK dispute turns out to be merely verbal on inspection (I suspect the "in a position to know" qualifier is doing a lot of work), say which part and why. Begin the response with the joint statement itself, no preface, no restating any of this.
