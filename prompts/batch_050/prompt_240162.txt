I taught a unit on Sen's impossibility of a Paretian liberal in a social choice seminar last term, and rereading the literature afterward left me with a nagging feeling that this debate stalled decades ago and nobody has admitted it. The theorem itself is fifty years old, short, and uncontested: minimal liberalism, unrestricted domain, and the weak Pareto principle cannot be jointly satisfied. Yet there is still no consensus about what it shows. One camp says the result demonstrates that rights cannot be captured by giving individuals decisive power over social alternatives, and that we should model rights as constraints on procedures or permissible strategies instead; another camp says the real culprit is the Pareto principle applied to meddlesome preferences, since Prude's nosiness about what Lewd reads in private is exactly the sort of preference that shouldn't get social weight; a third says the theorem just marks the limits of Arrovian social choice for thinking about rights at all. What strikes me is that everyone agrees on the mathematics, everyone agrees on the informal gloss of each condition, and nobody ever converts. The replies to proposed solutions all have the same shape: the game-form solution is charged with changing the subject, the preference-restriction solution with assuming what needs proving, the framework-limit solution with giving up too cheaply.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about which condition of the theorem to reject, but a disagreement about what a paradox of this kind is supposed to do in the first place -- whether it is a discovery that forces revision (in which case someone must say which condition goes and why that isn't ad hoc), or a sign that the formalism encodes a contested conception of rights as veto power over personal spheres, so that accepting or resisting the theorem just is taking sides on that conception. But I can also see a deflationary reading on which the whole thing is a verbal dispute about what to call a right, and a more optimistic one on which the disagreement is genuinely substantive and has been resolved by one of the camps, with the others simply not having noticed.

So: diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicate it. Using what each camp actually argues, tell me what kind of impasse this is -- a dispute about the interpretation of a formal result, a dispute about which intuitive commitments are negotiable, a case of three groups answering different questions under the heading of one theorem, or something else. Then, given your diagnosis, say what progress would even look like here: a new theorem, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the puzzle, an agreed criterion for when a proposed solution counts as answering the paradox rather than relocating it -- or a principled reason to expect the stalemate to be permanent.
