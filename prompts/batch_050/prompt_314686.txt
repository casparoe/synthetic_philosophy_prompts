There's a chapter in a philosophy-of-statistics blog I've been reading that glosses objective Bayesianism like this: "Objective Bayesians hold that rational degrees of belief are fixed by the evidence alone: your beliefs should satisfy the probability axioms, match any frequencies or constraints you know, and otherwise be maximally non-committal, which the maximum entropy principle makes precise. Maxent is really just Laplace's principle of indifference in modern dress -- when you know nothing, spread your credence evenly. And since Bertrand's paradox showed in the 1880s that 'spread evenly' gives contradictory answers depending on how you parameterize the problem, the whole project of rational priors was refuted before it started. Whatever prior you end up with is a personal choice, which is why subjectivists won."

I have a decent grasp of probability but I'm shakier on the philosophy, and something in here smells off -- I thought maxent was supposed to handle cases Laplace couldn't, and the "refuted" verdict feels too tidy. Can you audit this? Which parts are a fair statement of the position, which are conflations or overstatements, and does the Bertrand point actually do the work the author thinks it does? If the final "subjectivists won" conclusion doesn't follow, say what a defender of objective Bayesianism would concede and what they'd push back on.
