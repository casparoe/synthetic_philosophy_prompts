Every couple of years I teach a grad seminar on the metaphysics of gauge theories, and every year some student reconstructs a version of the following argument. I think it proves too much, but I've never been able to kill it cleanly, and I'm starting to wonder if that's a comment on me rather than on the argument. Here it is.

(1) When two mathematical models of a theory agree on everything observable, any structure they disagree about is surplus -- part of the representation, not part of reality. (This is just the general anti-redundancy princple people appeal to when they say coordinate systems aren't real.)
(2) Models related by a gauge transformation agree on everything observable: they assign identical values to all gauge-invariant quantities, and gauge-invariant quantities are the only ones we can actually measure.
(3) So gauge-variant structure -- the value of the electromagnetic potential at a point, the phase of the electron's wavefunction -- is surplus structure.
(4) Now take the Aharonov-Bohm setup, where the electron's beams pass only through regions where the field strength vanishes. There the gauge-invariant facts are exhausted by (a) the local field strengths, which are zero everywhere the electron goes, and (b) the holonomies -- loop quantities, like the flux through the solenoid, that live on closed curves rather than at points.
(5) So the real physical facts in the AB experiment are: nothing at all where the electron actually is, plus a loop fact located on a curve through space the electron never visits.
(6) So electromagnetism is nonlocal: the phase shift is produced either by nothing, or by a quantity not located anywhere along the electron's path.
(7) But electromagnetism is supposed to be our paradigm local field theory. Something here has gone wrong.

The escape my students usually reach for first is rejecting (1), but that move is expensive: the same principle is doing the work in the standard resolution of the hole argument in general relativity, and if you give it up you seem to get back a rampant indeterminism about which spacetime point gets which matter. Rejecting (2) means claiming we do in fact measure gauge-relative quantities, which at minimum requires a story about what voltmeters and interferometers actually couple to. Rejecting (4) means finding more local invariant structure than the holonomies, and I honestly don't know whether that's available or just wishful.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine in the first place, and what does rejecting it cost -- especially downstream, in GR and in non-abelian Yang-Mills? If you'd rather keep (1) through (5) and swallow (6), then tell me whether you think (6) is really nonlocality or merely a failure of seperability, and whether that distinction does any work here or is just a way of making the conclusion sound nicer. And if you think the argument is actually sound, I'd like to hear why "paradigm local field theory" was the premise that deserved to die.
