For an upper-level seminar on the paradoxes, I want to settle on a principled standard for evaluating proposed resolutions of Russell's paradox before we start reading any. Write the desiderata that an adequate resolution must meet — not a history of the paradox, but a specification.

Present it as a numbered list of requirements, each followed by the consideration that motivates it: a case, a piece of mathematical practice, or a further paradox the requirement generalizes. Then do three things with the list.

First, mark each requirement as either non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp, and where it is the latter, identify the underlying philosophical commitment driving it — for instance, whether one takes the paradox to expose a defect in our naive conception of collection or to constitute a genuine discovery that certain totalities cannot be sets.

Second, identify any subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, bearing in mind that a paradox of the same family can be derived from unrestricted comprehension without any use of negation, via a Curry-style construction. If the list therefore forces a choice, state exactly which requirement each of the main strategies gives up.

Third, say which single requirement excludes the most otherwise-serious contenders, and which requirement is most often smuggled in as if it were neutral common sense when it is in fact substantive and contested.

At minimum the list must engage with: consistency; recovery of the set-theoretic reasoning actually used in mainstream mathematics; non-ad-hocness, meaning that whatever restriction on comprehension the theory imposes must follow from an independently intelligible conception of what sets are; a diagnosis that extends to Cantor's paradox and the Burali-Forti paradox rather than Russell's alone; and the question of whether the same diagnosis should also cover the semantic paradoxes such as the liar, or whether demanding a unified solution is a prejudice rather than a genuine desideratum.

I am adding one requirement of my own, which I want treated as non-negotiable for the purposes of this seminar: the theory must supply a credible error theory — an account of why unrestricted comprehension struck several generations of competent mathematicians and logicians as obviously true, rather than as an unnoticed blunder. Call this the intelligibility requirement.

Finally, briefly score the following against the list, one short paragraph each: the iterative conception embodied in ZFC; simple type theory; Quine's NF with its stratification restriction; class theories that admit the Russell collection as a proper class; and a paraconsistent approach that retains naive comprehension by weakening the background logic. Keep the scoring subordinate to the criteria; the criteria are the deliverable.
