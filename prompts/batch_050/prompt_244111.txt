I coordinate a mixed reading group of graduate students from psychology, anthropology, and philosophy, and next session I have sixty minutes to teach them a single idea: what, if anything, cross-cultural variation in folk moral judgment implies for the objectivity of morality. The group has read the WEIRD literature (Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan's argument that Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic samples are outliers rather than defaults) and some empirical work on variation in trolley-style judgments and in the weighting of harm, fairness, loyalty, authority, and purity concerns, but nobody has training in metaethics. Plan how I should teach the inference question -- the step from "moral judgments vary across cultures" to any conclusion about whether there are objective moral truths -- rather than teaching it to me here.

Specifically, I want:

1. The dependency order. Which pieces must be established before which, and why. For instance, I suspect the descriptive claim (what actually varies, and what turns out not to vary) has to be nailed down before any normative conclusion can even be formulated, and that the distinction between a descriptive premise and a metaethical conclusion must come before any discussion of whether the inference succeeds -- but tell me if I'm wrong about either, and what the correct load-bearing sequence is.

2. The carrying example. One concrete case of cross-cultural moral variation to build the whole session around. It needs to be vivid enough that non-philosophers feel the pull of the relativist conclusion, but clean enough that it doesn't smuggle in complications (e.g., cases where the disagreement is really about non-moral facts, such as differing beliefs about the afterlife or about what harms whom, are a trap here -- or are they the most instructive choice?). Say what the example must have, what it must lack, and which commonly used example you'd reject and why.

3. The misconception map. The errors this audience will predictably commit, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact. My guesses: the psychologists will assume that demonstrating variation settles the philosophical question; the philosophers will assume that showing the inference is invalid makes the empirical work irrelevant; everyone will conflate moral objectivity with moral universality; and someone will treat Mackie's argument from disagreement as if it were just the observation that people disagree. Correct or extend this list.

4. The crux I should slow down for: the difference between disagreement as evidence against objectivity and an evolutionary or genealogical explanation of moral judgment as a debunking explanation, and why these are independent challenges that are routinely run together.

5. One check for understanding at the end -- a question or short exercise whose answer would show whether the group has grasped why descriptive variation is neither an automatic refutation nor automatically irrelevant to moral objectivity -- and one thing I should explicitly declare out of scope for this session.
