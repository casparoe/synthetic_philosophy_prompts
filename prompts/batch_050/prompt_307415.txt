I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been mulling over a puzzle from my own reading and have written up my thoughts as a short essay. I would be grateful for detailed comments.

The puzzle I am trying to understand: readers happily accept all sorts of impossible things in fiction -- time travel, talking animals, magic, violations of physics. Nobody puts down a novel because gravity works differently in it. Yet something strange happens when a story does not merely depict morally bad actions but presents them as morally good. If a narrator flatly tells us that, in this world, a character's cruelty was simply the right thing to do, many readers balk. They do not smoothly accept it the way they accept dragons. My essay tries to explain why.

---

My suggestion is that the resistance has nothing to do with a special failure of the imagination. We can imagine cruel people, and we can imagine people who believe cruelty is good. What we resist, I think, is something else: granting the author a kind of authority we grant freely for facts but not for values.

Here is how I see it. When we read fiction, we let the author tell us what is true in the story. That is the deal. But I suspect this deal covers only the ordinary facts: where things are, what happened, what people said. Moral judgment, by contrast, is something readers insist on doing themselves. When a narrator states a deviant moral verdict as settled fact, the author is, in effect, asking the reader to hand over their own conscience along with their belief in dragons -- and readers decline. So the balking is not an imaginative failure at all; it is a refusal, and arguably a healthy one.

A prediction follows from my view: the resistance should weaken when the deviant morality is shown rather than asserted -- when we see the world through a character whose values are alien, rather than being told by a neutral narrator what is right. Stories told from the perspective of someone monstrous are disturbing, but they do not seem to produce the same flat refusal as a narrator announcing that cruelty was good. In the first case the reader is still the judge; in the second the author has usurped the role.

If I am right, the puzzle dissolves: there is no special barrier to imagining morally deviant worlds, only a standing refusal to outsource our moral judgment, which fiction happens to make visible.

---

Please comment on the following: (1) whether I have described the phenomenon accurately, or whether the thing readers balk at is actually broader or narrower than I assume; (2) whether my shown-versus-asserted prediction really distinguishes my explanation from rival ones, such as the idea that moral claims are simply harder to imagine because they are not the sort of thing that can vary between worlds; (3) where my reasoning is weakest or most likely to be overlooking something; and (4) most importantly, what specific argument, counterexample, or piece of evidence would lead you to a different verdict than mine -- I would rather learn which consideration would overturn my view than receive a vague verdict on whether it seems plausible.
