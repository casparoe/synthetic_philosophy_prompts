Ok so I keep seeing people dunk on "the Enlightenment" as if it's one big thing, and I want to get more precise about it. Here's Kant in "What is Enlightenment?" (1784), the part where he draws his famously weird public/private distinction:

"The public use of one's reason must always be free, and it alone can bring about enlightenment among men. The private use of reason, on the other hand, may often be very narrowly restricted without particularly hindering the progress of enlightenment. By the public use of one's reason I understand the use which a person makes of it as a scholar before the reading public. Private use I call that which one may make of it in a particular civil post or office which is entrusted to him."

(Note that Kant's "private" is roughly backwards from how we'd use the word today -- it's the civil servant at his post, while "public" is the guy writing pamphlets.)

Here's what I want. Take this list of standard complaints about the Enlightenment legacy and sort them into tiers based on how hard they actually hit Kant's argument in this passage specifically (not enlightenment-ish vibes in general):

1. The Foucault-style complaint: universal reason is really a machinery of discipline and normalization, and the "free scholar" is just another product of it.
2. The Horkheimer/Adorno-style complaint: reason that treats everything as an object of calculation slides into domination, whatever Kant says about freedom to argue.
3. The postcolonial complaint: the "society of world citizens" Kant's scholar addresses was always imagined from Europe, so the universal audience is a cover for empire.
4. The disenchantment complaint: rationalization hollows out meaning, so a public that argues freely about everything ends up unable to commit to anything.
5. The free-speech-skeptic complaint: the "reading public" was never a level playing field, and today's version is worse, so free public use of reason just launders whatever the loudest and best-funded voices think.

For each one, give me a sentence or two on whether Kant's passage has the resources to absorb the objection, has to partly concede it, or just gets wrecked by it, and use that to justify the tier you put it in. Then as a bonus: redraw Kant's public/private line for one present-day case of your choosing (say, a tax officer who writes a political Substack in the evenings, or a military officer tweeting about defense policy) and say whether your redrawn line does better or worse against whatever you put in the top tier.
