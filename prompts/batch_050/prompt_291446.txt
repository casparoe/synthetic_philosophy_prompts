After four decades as an experimental biologist, I have taken to reading philosophy in my retirement, and I find myself returning to a habit of speech my old laboratory never once questioned. When we knocked out a gene and the organism developed abnormally, we wrote, without hesitation, that the gene's absence caused the defect; when a keystone predator was removed and the trophic structure collapsed, we said its absence caused the collapse. I now learn that philosophers divide sharply over whether such talk is literally true. One camp holds that omissions and absences can be causes in perfectly good standing, and that a metaphysics refusing them cannot account for prevention, for knockout experiments, or for ordinary responsibility. The other holds that only positive events and processes stand in causal relations, and that omission-talk is at best a pragmatic shorthand for explanations or for the events that occurred instead.

Two members of my reading circle have championed these positions for a year without progress. I want the document they should have produced had they run a proper adversarial collaboration: a joint statement both could sign. Please draft it with the following structure.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims — at least six — that are neither vacuous nor slanted, covering what each side takes causation to be for, the standing of causal explanation as distinct from causation itself, and the bearing of scientific practice on the dispute.

Second, a table of residual disagreements, each stated in a single sentence phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own view, and each flagged as a genuine disagreement or as one that was verbal all along.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. These should include, at minimum, whether counterfactual dependence of an effect on an absence suffices for causation; whether there is a principled line between the nurse who omits a patient's insulin and the millions of others who also did not administer it; and whether a theory of causation owes fidelity to the working judgments of the sciences.

Fourth, a joint verdict section applying the statement to these cases: the knockout mouse; a flood caused by a sluice gate that a caretaker left shut, compared with the same gate left shut by a vandal and by design; a crashed server whose watchdog process failed to restart it; the predator-removal trophic cascade. For each case, record each side's verdict and its reasoning, noting where they deliver identical verdicts for different reasons.

Finally, for every crux, specify an argument or further case both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance, and close with one point on which each camp concedes the other currently has the better of the matter.
