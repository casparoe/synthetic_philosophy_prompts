I've been teaching a unit on lying and misleading in my ethics seminar, and somewhere around week four I talked myself into a position I now want stress-tested. Quick setup: everyone in the literature seems to agree there's a distinction between outright lying (saying something false you believe to be false) and merely misleading (saying something true, or at least not asserted-false, while nudging the hearer toward a false conclusion), but people fight over whether the distinction matters morally. The standard intuition pump is that misleading feels like a lesser offense -- the clever roommate who says "I saw your brother downtown" knowing full well the brother flew in last night and is upstairs, versus the roommate who flat-out says "your brother isn't here." Most of my students land on: misleading is sneaky but lying is worse, even when the deceiver's intent is identical.

Here's the view I've ended up holding, which I'll call the record-corruption view. What makes lying distinctively wrong isn't the intent to deceive (misleading has that too) and isn't the falsehood of what's said (misleading can exploit truths). It's that assertion writes content onto the shared conversational record under the speaker's authority -- it's a kind of public entry other people are entitled to build on. A lie corrupts that record; misleading leaves the record clean and pushes the inferential risk onto the hearer, who drew the bad conclusion partly on their own. Two consequences I actually like about the view: first, it explains why bald-faced lies -- where nobody is fooled and everyone knows the speaker is lying, like a defendant's obviously false testimony -- still feel worse than misleading, since the record gets dirtied even when no deception succeeds. Second, the wrongness of lying should scale with how much the audience was entitled to rely on the record: oaths, testimony, a doctor's statements, official announcements should count as graver lies than cocktail-party lies, because the stakes of record-integrity differ.

What I want from you: what would count against this view? Don't just give me the generic "but intention is what matters" rejoinder. I want specific cases, intuitions, or commitments in the theory of assertion that the record-corruption view can't handle -- things like lies told in contexts where there's no shared record to corrupt, or cases where misleading seems *worse* than lying (a technically true statement engineered to humiliate or to ruin someone, say), or the problem of silence and mere non-correction, which my view seems to class with misleading but which often feels blameless. For each defeater, say whether a committed record-corruption theorist would concede it counts against the view or dismiss it as attacking a strawman, and rank the whole list from genuinely fatal to merely awkward. And be honest about the meta-question: if I keep responding to counterexamples by refining what counts as "the record" or what counts as "assertion," is the view being qualified into something unfalsifiable, and would that be a problem for a view of this kind? I'd rather find out the view is dead than spend my sabatical defending it.
