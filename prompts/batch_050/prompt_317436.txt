A small, recently independent country — call it Veldria, founded thirty years ago after the breakup of a federation, with no distinct language, a flag chosen by committee, and a population that until recently thought of itself as several different things — has hired a heritage consultancy to supply it with a usable past. Write the consultancy's pitch document: a polished, completely sincere business proposal of about 700–800 words offering the client a complete "national identity package." Its product lines should include a founding myth (with optional archaeological corroboration, deliverable within eighteen months), a folk costume whose "ancient" patterns the design team will finalize next quarter, a national anthem composed in an archaic dialect no living citizen speaks, a calendar of festivals commemorating events that did not occur, and a flagship museum whose galleries arrange the new state's census categories, border maps, and assembled artifacts into a narrative of primordial continuity. Include tiered pricing, projected timelines, and discreet references to unnamed "satisfied clients" — perhaps a kilted northern nation whose clan patterns were largely standardized in the nineteenth century, perhaps a republic whose national holiday predates the events it celebrates.

The comedy must come from getting the underlying theory right rather than from mere absurdity. The document should be a deadpan enactment of Hobsbawm's account of invented traditions — formalized, repetition-based practices serving novel political needs while claiming immemorial status — and of Anderson's imagined communities: a nation as a community most of whose members will never meet, conjured into being through print, census, map, and museum. A reader who knows this literature should recognize the census–map–museum triad and the tartan wink without the proposal ever citing a single scholar, and the satire will fail if it exaggerates anything that real nation-builders haven't actually done. The piece should also let the harder question surface without stating it: if every nation's traditions were once new, what exactly distinguishes Veldria's package from France's — is the only difference elapsed time? The consultants' serene confidence that there is no difference should be palpable but nowhere confessed.

After the pitch, append a brief out-of-character note (150–200 words) identifying where the piece takes liberties with the theory: whether it leans too heavily on the strong modernist reading and flattens the ethnosymbolist objection that nations are elaborated from genuine pre-existing cultural materials rather than fabricated from nothing, and whether the Hobsbawmian framing understates how much popular buy-in, as opposed to elite design, successful invention requires. Close the note by naming the single element of the pitch you are least confident a theorist in the Hobsbawm–Anderson mold would actually endorse, and say why.
