So I run the arts and culture page of our school newspaper (well, it's an instagram page now but whatever) with two friends, and we keep having the same fight. People submit stuff like "this movie is a masterpiece, you HAVE to watch it" or "the new album everyone's talking about is overrated garbage" -- and half the time the person writing it hasn't actually seen the movie or heard the album, they're just repeating what some youtuber or critic said. My friend says we should ban any review where the writer hasn't experienced the thing themselves, full stop. I think that's too strict, because like, if a critic I really trust says a book is amazing, that seems like actual evidence, and I tell my friends about books I haven't read all the time based on reviews. But she says there's a difference between a fact about the movie (it won an award, it's two hours long) and the claim that it's GOOD, and the second kind can't really be passed on secondhand.

In my philosophy elective we briefly talked about this idea that for aesthetic stuff you supposedly have to experience it yourself -- my teacher called it something like the "acquaintance principle" -- and also about testimony and when it's rational to just take someone's word. But we never got into how you'd actually USE any of that.

Can you turn whatever philosophers actually say about aesthetic testimony into a procedure I could give our contributors and editors? Like a checklist or a flowchart-in-words they run on each submission: which claims in a review are fine to pass on secondhand (and what makes a source trustworthy enough for that), which claims require the writer to have watched/read/listened themselves, what to do with mixed cases like "apparently the ending ruins it" or aggregation stuff like "it's sitting at 40% on review sites," and how to label things we publish anyway. Point out the steps where it's genuinely a judgement call and no rule will settle it, cause my friend will ask. Then run the procedure on three example submissions: (1) a rave review of a film the writer hasn't seen but three critics they trust loved, (2) a negative review of an album the writer listened to once while doing homework, and (3) a piece saying a classic book "doesn't hold up" based entirely on summaries and twitter discourse. Ideally at least one of them should come out in a way that annoys either me or my friend, because if the procedure just confirms what I already think it's probably useless lol.
