So I'm writing a piece for our magazine's ideas vertical about this wonderfully annoying little result called Fitch's paradox of knowability, and I'm trying to get my head around the landscape of responses before I talk to anyone. For readers I'll have to explain it crudely: start from the seemingly innocent claim that every truth could, in principle, be known, add some basic logic about knowledge (you can't know a conjunction without being in a position to know the conjuncts, you can't know falsehoods), and out pops the conclusion that every truth is, in fact, known. Most people take that as a reductio of the premise. Fine. The fun part is what to do about it, and that's where I want to be careful not to miss something.

The responses I've already catalogued in my notes, which you should NOT repeat back to me: (1) restrict the knowability principle so it only applies to truths that can consistently be known -- the Tennant-style "Cartesian" restriction, which keeps the spirit of anti-realism while dodging the proof; (2) bite the bullet and go full semantic anti-realist, treating the conclusion as an argument that truth just is idealized knowability rather than something evidence-transcendent; (3) blame the logic itself, e.g. weaken or revise the background logic (paraconsistent or intuitionistic moves) so the derivation doesn't go through; (4) Edgington's fix using an actuality operator, so the principle says every truth is possibly *actually* known, which blocks the derivation while keeping a robust realism about truth; and (5) the deflationary shrug that says the proof is just a quantifier/modal trick with no real epistemological consequences, so nobody needs to change their mind about anything.

Here's my question: is there a sixth response, genuinely different in structure from all five of those? Not a refinement of one of them, not a combination -- a different diagnosis of where the argument goes wrong (or of why it's right but harmless, or whatever). If you give me one, also tell me honestly whether you think it holds up or whether it's really one of my five wearing a fake mustache. I'm asking because my editor wants the piece to end on "and philosophers are still arguing about it" and I'd like that to actually be true rather than just a cliche.
