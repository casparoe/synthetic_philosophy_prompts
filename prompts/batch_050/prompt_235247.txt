I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-audience magazine, and I'm drafting a long essay on what historians now call "non-human actors" -- plagues, climatic shifts, volcanic winters -- and the intellectual risks of taking them seriously. Below is my working draft (about 600 words). The epigraph is from Part III of Hippocrates' treatise On Airs, Waters, Places, in the Francis Adams translation; it anchors the essay's argument, and I'd like your comments to engage it directly.

Epigraph:

"A city that is exposed to hot winds (these are between the wintry rising, and the wintry setting of the sun), and to which these are peculiar, but which is sheltered from the north winds; in such a city the waters will be plenteous and saltish, and as they run from an elevated source, they are necessarily hot in summer, and cold in winter; the heads of the inhabitants are of a humid and pituitous constitution, and their bellies subject to frequent disorders, owing to the phlegm running down from the head; the forms of their bodies, for the most part, are rather flabby; they do not eat nor drink much; drinking wine in particular, and more especially if carried to intoxication, is oppressive to them"

Draft:

Every few years a book becomes a bestseller by telling you that history was really made by something nobody voted for: a virus, a volcano, a shift in the monsoon. The plague bacterium that killed much of the Mediterranean world in the sixth century; the dust veil of 536 that darkened the sun and is blamed for everything from failing harvests to collapsing empires; the eruption of Tambora in 1815 and the "year without a summer"; the smallpox that preceded conquistadors into the Americas; the Little Ice Age shadowing the crises of the seventeenth century. The genre's appeal is obvious. It deflates human vanity and it is often true. But it has an ancestor, and the ancestor is a warning.

My epigraph is the oldest statement I know of the idea that environment writes character. Note what Hippocrates actually does in this passage. He does not say the hot winds are a challenge the city's inhabitants must answer. He says the waters are "necessarily" hot in summer, the heads "of a humid and pituitous constitution," the bodies "rather flabby," the appetites small. The chain from wind to water to organ to habit runs on necessity, and it runs one way. The people in his city do not appear as agents at all; they are outputs. This is the original environmental determinism, and modern historians rightly flinch at it -- not least because, in its later forms, it was used to rank whole peoples by latitude.

And yet the modern genre I began with seems committed to something structurally similar. When we say the plague of 541 "caused" the weakening of the empire, or that Tambora "gave us" a year of famine and unrest, aren't we also describing humans as outputs -- just with better instruments? My essay argues that the difference is real but fragile, and rests on three things. First, modern accounts treat non-human events as shocks within systems that respond: the same pathogen devastates one society and barely dents another, depending on institutions, nutrition, trust. Second, they treat these events as raising or lowering costs of human choices, not as making the choices. Third, they allow feedback: humans alter the very forces that alter them, draining marshes, spreading rats, emitting carbon. Hippocrates' causal arrows go one way; ours loop.

I then propose a test for telling good "non-human actor" history from disguised determinism: does the account specify the mechanism by which the shock was transmitted, and does it identify points where a different human response would have produced a different outcome? If a narrative cannot say what would have had to be different for the outcome to differ, I claim, it has quietly promoted a cause into a fate.

What I want from you is not a polish but a stress test, in four parts:

1. Is my reading of the Hippocrates passage fair? Specifically, am I right that the passage's machinery is one-directional and necessity-driven, or does the surrounding medical context (a treatise advising physicians to study local conditions, presumably in order to intervene) undercut my claim that the people in it "do not appear as agents"? If the epigraph is doing less work than I think, tell me.

2. Is the "specify the mechanism, name the fork" test a good criterion? Try to break it: find a historical case where the test is satisfied but the account still feels deterministic, or where the test fails but the causal claim seems fine.

3. My three distinguishing features (response-dependent effects, cost-raising rather than choice-making, feedback loops) -- are they actually consistent with each other, and do any of my examples strain them? I suspect the smallpox-in-the-Americas case is my weakest: mortality so overwhelming that "the response of the system" may be a hollow phrase. Tell me whether the framework survives that case or needs a clause for catastrophes that remove the responders.

4. On accuracy: flag any overstatement in my thumbnail sketches of the 536 dust veil, Tambora, or the sixth-century plague -- I have gestured at contested attributions ("is blamed for") without adjudicating them, and I want to know where that hedge is too thin to publish.
