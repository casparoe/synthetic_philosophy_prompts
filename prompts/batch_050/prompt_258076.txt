I run the ethics training sessions at a mid-sized biotech company, and I keep ending up in the same fight with one of our engineers, and I want ammunition for next time. His line is that moral talk is basically just venting: when someone says "fudging the test data is wrong," all they're doing is expressing disapproval, like booing a bad call at a football game. Nothing is being claimed, so nothing is true or false, so there's nothing to argue about — just different people making different noises.

For a long time I couldn't say what was wrong with this. But then last week we had an actual incident review, and I caught him doing something that seemed completely inconsistent with his own view. A manager had told a new intern to clean up some noisy data, and this engineer argued: if fudging the data is wrong, then ordering someone else to fudge it is wrong too; and fudging it is wrong; so the manager did something wrong. Everyone in the room accepted the argument, it seemed airtight. But hold on. When he says "if fudging the data is wrong," he isn't booing. He isn't expressing disapproval of anything at that moment — he's setting up a conditional. And you can't take a boo, stick it inside an if-then, chain it together with another boo, and have a valid logical argument pop out the other end. Boos don't have if-thens.

So here's what I want you to walk me through. Is his argument actually incompatible with his theory, or is there some way out that I'm not seeing? I have a feeling there's a smarter version of his position that survives this — maybe by treating the whole argument as expressing one complicated attitude rather than chaining claims — and I'd rather steelman it myself than get blindsided. Also tell me whether the same problem comes up for things like "either the layoffs were wrong or the memo about them was misleading," where nobody in the sentence is booing anything in particular, and whether his rescue plan (whatever it is) can handle that too. If it can't, I want to know exactly where it breaks, so I can push him on it without overclaiming.
