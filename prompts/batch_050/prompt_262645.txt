I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind and I'm writing the final exam. I want one essay question on personal survival through mind uploading, answerable in 90 minutes and worth 20 marks, that can't be answered by reciting the standard argument that gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement preserves the person because it preserves function. Below is the scenario I plan to put on the exam paper. You may tighten the wording, but preserve its structure, because the whole question turns on the contrast it sets up.

Scenario: Two procedures begin with the same living person, Ada, and each ends with a fully synthetic brain that is functionally indistinguishable from Ada's biological brain at the moment the procedure completes.

Procedure 1 (in-place replacement). Over one year, Ada's neurons are replaced one at a time. Each synthetic neuron is installed next to its biological target, couples to the same neighbors, and takes over the target's functional role; the biological neuron is then removed. At every stage, Ada's ongoing mental life is carried by a mixed biological-synthetic system whose parts interact in both directions.

Procedure 2 (the shadow). A complete synthetic brain, built from a scan of Ada's brain, runs in parallel with hers for one year. A one-way data link continuously streams the state of every biological neuron to its synthetic counterpart, so the synthetic system is driven into lockstep with Ada's. There is no channel back: nothing Ada thinks, decides, or experiences is ever carried, even in part, by the synthetic brain. After a year of perfect lockstep, the biological brain is switched off and the synthetic brain continues.

The exam question to be printed under the scenario: "Ada survives Procedure 1 but does not survive Procedure 2." Defend or reject this claim. Your answer must take a side, and must state what your verdict implies for scan-and-destroy uploading in which the scan is later instantiated.

Along with the question, I need two things:

(a) A model answer of roughly 600 words. It must take a definite position — I don't mind which side it lands on, but it should commit, give reasons, and not end by laying out the options for the reader to choose between. One constraint: the answer may not rest on the mere gradualness of Procedure 1, since both procedures take a year. It has to locate the relevant difference (if there is one) in the causal or constitutive structure of the two processes — for instance, in the direction in which dependence runs between Ada's mental life and the synthetic hardware, or in what the ongoing process consists of at each moment — and it must engage the strongest objection to whichever verdict it defends, rather than merely acknowledging it.

(b) A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks for each (totaling 20). For each point, state the specific move an answer must make to earn the marks, and add a one-line note on the way candidates typically fumble that point (for instance: treating the shadow procedure as just a slower version of in-place replacement; or arguing from the fact that the two end states are functionally identical straight to the conclusion that the procedures are equivalent, without addressing whether the process could matter independently of the product). Close the scheme with a short paragraph saying what separates an A answer from a B answer in terms of specific moves, not general quality — including how an answer should handle a mixed variant, in which half of Ada's brain is replaced in place while the other half is shadowed, if it is to earn full marks on the application of its own principle.
