So me and my friend got into this massive argument over dinner last night about whether you actually SEE your late grandmother when you look at an old photo of her, or whether you just see a piece of paper. He took a philosophy of art class years ago and swears he's basically reciting the standard view from some philosopher, Walton, Kendall something? Here's how he put it, roughly:

"Walton's view is that photographs are transparent: when you look at a photo of something, you literally see the thing itself, through the photo, the same way you see things through a telescope or a rear-view mirror. This works because the camera is a machine, so what ends up in the photo is caused by the scene itself and isn't filtered through anyone's beliefs or artistic choices, unlike a painting, which only ever shows you what the painter believed or imagined. That's also why photographs count as evidence in court: since no human agency intervenes in the process, a photograph can't really lie or distort anything. And if you buy all this, you have to say that watching a livestream of something counts as seeing it in exactly the same sense as looking out the window at it."

I nodded along at the time but a couple things have been bugging me. The "no human agency" bit seems obviously off — photographers make a million choices, and I've definately seen misleading photos before. The jump from "you literally see things in photos" to "photos can't lie" feels like a leap too, and the livestream thing he threw on at the end sounded like he was just riffing.

Can you go through his summary piece by piece and tell me which parts are actually a fair statement of the transparency view, which parts are exaggerations or conflations of it, and which parts a defender of the view would push back on hardest? And then the thing I actually care about: if parts of the summary are wrong, does that settle the argument we were having — seeing grandma vs. seeing a picture of grandma — or does the interesting version of the dispute survive the corrections?
