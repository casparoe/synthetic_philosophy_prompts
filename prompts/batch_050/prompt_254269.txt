I sit on the deployment review board at a mid-size fintech company, and we're voting next week on whether to promote an internal code-review model to "autonomous approver" status, meaning its sign-off alone would suffice to merge changes into our payment-processing codebase. By every behavioral measure it qualifies: over the past quarter it caught more genuine defects than any senior engineer on staff, its false-positive rate is the lowest we've ever recorded, and it passed our adversarial evaluation suite, including counterfactual probes where we seeded plausible-but-wrong suggestions to see whether it could be talked out of correct verdicts. The complication is its explanations. It flags real bugs, but when asked to justify the flag it produces confident rationales that reference nonexistent functions roughly a fifth of the time, and it can't be taught: correcting its explanation changes nothing about the explanations it gives later, even as its verdicts stay right. Our deployment policy, written with human approvers in mind, requires that any autonomous approver "understand the codebase it certifies," a phrase nobody ever defined for a model. Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the vote: a concrete recommendation (approve, approve with conditions, or reject, with a time frame and explicit review triggers), the reasoning behind it, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side. In particular, address whether reliably catching defects without being able to say why is already what "understanding" amounted to in practice, or whether our trust in human approvers has always quietly rested on their explanations rather than their track record.
