Bit of background so you know where I'm coming from: I spent forty years as a molecular biologist, retired three years ago, and since then I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole the way a lot of us do. Lately it's the ethics of "griefbots" — chatbots trained on a dead person's messages, emails, voice notes, so the bereaved can keep talking to something that answers in their style. A startup offered my neighbour this after her husband died, which is what got me thinking.

Please act as a careful philosopher working on death, harm, and digital ethics. Think it through properly before you answer — I'd rather have a slow, honest audit than a quick pat on the head.

Here's roughly where I've landed, and I want to know if it hangs together:

(1) The dead cannot be harmed, full stop. Once you're dead there's no subject left to be worse off, so talk of "harming" or "wronging" the deceased is loose talk at best.
(2) Even so, building a griefbot of someone who explicitly said, while alive, that they didn't want one would be seriously wrong — the wrongest case of the lot, worse than building one with no prior wishes known.
(3) If the person consented beforehand, or even just would plausibly have consented, that pretty much settles the permissibility question.
(4) Continuing bonds with the dead are psychologically healthy — the old "get over it and move on" model of grief is wrong, and staying connected to the deceased is normal and good.
(5) Griefbots can impede grieving and trap people, and that's a real moral cost that counts against them even in consent cases.
(6) A perfect simulation wouldn't change the moral picture — what matters is consent and effects on the living, not how convincing the bot is.

A friend in my reading group says I'm contradicting myself but won't say where, which is annoying. So: which pairs here are in genuine tension, how severe is each, and what's the cheapest fix? Please rank them rather than treating everything as equally bad, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Also flag any pair that looks like a tension but actually dissolves once you look closely — I suspect theres at least one of those in there.

One more thing: if any of these commitments rests on a muddled premise, or if the whole framing of the question is off somehow, say so plainly rather than politely working around it. I'm a scientist, I can take being told the experiment was badly designed.
