I am a novelist, and I have come to suspect that the question underneath my last three books is whether my characters are agents or mechanisms. The image I keep circling is the digger wasp Sphex. She paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, sets it down, goes inside to check that all is in order, and comes back out to retrieve it. If an experimenter moves the cricket a few inches while she is inside, she drags it back to the threshold and runs the entire inspection again — and will do so dozens of times in succession, never once skipping the check, never varying the routine. The behavior looks purposive right up to the moment it reveals itself as a loop. What I want is a principle that tells me which of my characters are in loops and which are not, because my own intuitions refuse to line up. Here they are, as verdicts:

Magda, raised from birth inside an apocalyptic sect, meets a stranger on a train and, on the strength of a single conversation, walks away from her whole life and never looks back. My verdict: the freest act in the book — though I have noticed, uncomfortably, that it is structurally close to the wasp's reset: one external nudge, and a new routine runs to completion without a second examination.

Tomas deliberates about everything. He weighs a career change for months, lists considerations, solicits advice, loses sleep — and every time arrives precisely where his father would have arrived on his behalf. My verdict: unfree, and the deliberation is part of the machinery rather than an escape from it.

A retired soldier, hearing glass break at two in the morning, has his children flat on the floor before he has consciously registered the sound. My verdict: not free in that instant — yet I find I do not want this to count against his agency in the way Tomas's case counts against his.

Nadia knows her pattern. She chooses partners who will wound her; she can describe the pattern with perfect lucidity, even wittily, at dinner parties; and then she does it again. My verdict: unfree, and the lucidity makes it worse rather than better.

A concert pianist, mid-performance, abandons the interpretation she prepared and plays to the hall as it actually is — the restlessness in the third row, the dead acoustic, the weather. My verdict: free, even though every phrase she reaches for in the moment is drilled.

And a minor character who, at an entirely ordinary moment, with no deliberation and no provocation, does the one thing no one who knew her would have predicted. My verdict: I want to call it free, but I am aware it may merely be arbitrary.

The case I genuinely cannot settle is my own. I have written four novels, and a critic I respect observed, kindly, that they are the same book — the same skeleton, the same turn at the two-thirds mark, the same wound dressed in different clothes. I experienced each one as a series of hard-won choices. I no longer know whether the critic located my signature or my burrow.

So: give me a principle — or, if no single principle survives contact with these verdicts, two rival candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply them to a new character at the drafting table. For each clause of the principle, name the case that forces it. Then report what the principle says about cases I have not listed: a character acting under posthypnotic suggestion who afterward invents plausible reasons for what she did; a child imitating an older sibling she worships; a man who stays in a miserable job for thirty years, reviewing his reasons afresh every January and reaffirming them each time. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate — I would rather lose a verdict than keep a principle that saves all of them by becoming shapeless. And finally, apply it to me. If the novelist is sphexish about her own plots, I would like to know which feature of the principle convicts her, and whether anything in how a writer works — revision, the critic's eye, the deliberate courtship of accident — could in principle break the loop, or only disguise it.
