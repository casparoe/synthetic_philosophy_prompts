So I keep going in circles on the testimony debate — reductionism vs anti-reductionism, i.e. do you need positive reasons to trust a speaker before believing them, or are you entitled to just take people's word absent a defeater. Instead of picking a side from the armchair I figured I'd write down my actual gut verdicts on cases and see if anything consistent falls out. I keep wanting to cash all of this out in priors and likelihood ratios but some of these resist that.

- A stranger on the street tells me the metro station is two blocks that way. I believe him instantly, zero evidence about his reliability. Seems fine.
- My friend texts that her new meds are making her dizzy. Believe it, no track-record check required. Also fine.
- A guy at the bus stop, seemed totally sincere, tells me the mayor got arrested this morning. I do NOT just believe this, I pull out my phone and check. But wait — why does this one need checking when the metro guy didn't?
- A five year old tells me there's a monster under her bed: no. Same kid, five minutes later, says her tummy hurts: yes, immediately.
- My calculator says 847 × 23 = 19,481. I believe it, but that's obviously because calculators have a good track record — feels purely reductionist, its just reliability all the way down. Though is a calculator even testimony?
- My conspiracy-pilled uncle tells me what he had for lunch: believe it. Same uncle tells me something about the WEF: lol no. So it's not about the person per se, it's about... what, subject matter?
- Wikipedia says the population of Slovenia is about 2 million. I believe it without knowing who wrote the article. Who is even testifying here? Do I have positive reasons to trust Wikipedia (vague ones, I guess) or am I default-entitled?
- Someone says "trust me" — adds literally nothing. Whatever principle I end up with had better predict that.
- A very earnest friend with a flawless track record tells me she saw my partner at a restaurant with someone and shes pretty sure something is going on. I genuinely don't know what I think here. I half-believe it and hate that I do, like part of me thinks loyalty should function as a defeater and part of me thinks that's epistemically insane.

My hunch is something like: default entitlement that scales down with stakes, and with whether the content is the kind of thing people are systematically wrong or motivated about. But the monster/tummy pair makes that awkward (a five year old isn't *motivated* about monsters, she just has bad ontology), and the mayor case is awkward too (the bus-stop guy has no obvious motive to lie about it). So maybe my hunch is wrong.

Give me the best principle you can, stated precisely, or two or three candidates if there's no clean winner. For each clause tell me which case motivated it. Then run it on cases I didn't try: an LLM assistant telling me a historical fact (does knowing roughly how it's trained count as "positive reasons" for trusting it?), a note I find in my own handwriting from six months ago that I don't remember writing, and a coin-flipper who sincerely reports how the coin landed. And be honest about which of my verdicts the principle has to sacrifice — I suspect the metro/mayor pair is where one of my guts has to die, but if you can save both without the principle turning into an epicycle-fest, I want to hear it.
