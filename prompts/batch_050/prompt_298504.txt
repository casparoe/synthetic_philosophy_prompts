So my intro philosophy seminar just did the Ring of Gyges from Book II of Plato's Republic -- you know, the story Glaucon tells where the shepherd finds a gold ring on a corpse inside a bronze horse, figures out that turning the setting inward makes him invisible, and then immediately uses it to seduce the queen, kill the king, and take over the kingdom. Glaucon's whole point is that nobody, not even the most upright person, would keep acting justly if they could get away with anything, that justice is something we practice reluctantly because we can't do injustice without consequences. Our TA said the story is basically a thought experiment that "predicts user behavior," and that got me and my roommate joking that if this ring were real it would obviously be a startup with a waitlist and a Substack.

So: write me the product page / FAQ for a fictional company, call it Gyges Labs or something like that, that has actually productized the ring. Around 600-700 words. The voice should be that relentlessly upbeat tech-marketing register -- hero section copy, feature bullets, a testimonial carousel, maybe an FAQ and some fine-print legal disclaimers -- and it should stay completely deadpan the whole way through. The satire should work by having the company's marketing cheerfully assume, at every single turn, exactly what Glaucon predicts: that every customer, including the ones who describe themselves as deeply ethical people in the testimonials, will use the ring for the same things the shepherd used it for. Feature ideas like "Zero Witness Mode," a referral program nobody can trace, insurance sold seperately, a blog post about "accountability-free living" framed as self-care. The just man and the unjust man should be indistinguishable in the user reviews, which is kind of the whole joke.

Two more requirements. First, don't let the page ever wink at the reader or admit there's anything wrong -- the horror should come entirely from how reasonable it all sounds, the way the original story's horror comes from how quickly the shepherd's behavior changes. Second, somewhere in the fine print or a sad little FAQ entry at the bottom, I want an echo of Socrates' actual reply to Glaucon -- the bit where he argues injustice wrecks the person who does it even if they profit -- but filtered through corporate language, like a buried disclaimer about "reported declines in user wellbeing" or a partnership with a meditation app, that undercuts everything above it without the company seeming to notice. Basically the page should accidentally concede that Glaucon's challenge might have an answer.
