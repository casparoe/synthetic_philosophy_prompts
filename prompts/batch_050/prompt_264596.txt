I’m a PhD student in moral philosophy, and I somehow got asked to give the opening toast at a 30-person workshop for the Time Capsule League, a fictional network of volunteers who preserve unglamorous things—local meeting minutes, soil samples, obsolete file formats—in case future people need them. The room is split between strong longtermists and community historians who think longtermism can flatten our obligations to the living. Write a 6–7 minute speech around this thought experiment: one time capsule can either reveal an urgent but short-lived risk to the next generation, or remain sealed to warn people 2,000 years from now about a much larger danger; opening it destroys the later warning. Use the dilemma to argue that concern for the far future needn’t mean maximizing expected lives or treating present people as instruments—it might instead be a duty to prevent future agents from being locked into the mistakes of the dead. Make both camps feel genuinely understood, avoid jargon and citations, and end with a toast that isn’t smarmy.
