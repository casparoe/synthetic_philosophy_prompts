write me the leaked minutes, maybe 500 words, of an emergency meeting of the Committee on Historical Periods -- the body I'm imagining actually runs periodization, deciding the boundaries that textbooks and hiring commitees then treat as discoveries. agenda items should include: where to file the fall of Rome now that 'late antiquity' keeps absorbing everything after about 250 AD; a motion to downgrade the Renaissance to a 'regional event' pending evidence that anyone outside northern Italy noticed it happening; a complaint from the medieval delegation that their entire period is defined by what it sits in between; and a proposal to handle the Scientific Revolution by declaring it 'ongoing' so nobody has to set an end date. the humor has to come from how periodization actually works in real historiography -- boundaries that nobody living through them noticed, dates picked for the convenience of syllabi and library catalogues, 'early modern' as a hedge, 'long' centuries for eras with good institutional backing, the way a period can be invented centuries after the fact by people who never lived in it -- so that an actual historian laughs instead of winces. keep the minutes deadpan and bureaucratic, no winking, and somewhere in the jokes the genuine question should be visible: are historical periods things scholars find in the evidence, or things they impose on it, and would anything be lost if we stopped drawing the lines?
