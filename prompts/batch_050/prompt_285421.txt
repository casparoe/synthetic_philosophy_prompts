I spent my career as an experimental physicist before retiring, and only in the last few years have I taken up philosophy of science in earnest. I now help run a small interview series at my former institute, and our next guest is a philosopher of physics who has just published a book arguing that effective field theory spells the end of reductionism as a methodological ideal: on her view, the decoupling of scales means that theories at different energies are genuinely autonomous, and the dream that low-energy physics is in principle derivable from a fundamental theory should be retired rather than merely postponed.

I want eight to ten interview questions, and I want them to do real work rather than invite her to restate the book. Specifically, I'd like the sequence to cover the following pressure points, in whatever order you think makes for the best conversation:

1. Force her to say precisely which autonomy thesis she holds: autonomy from derivation (the low-energy equations cannot be deduced from the microscopic theory in practice or in principle), autonomy of explanation (the derivation, even where available, would not explain what the effective theory explains), or ontological autonomy (the higher-scale entities are real in a way not reducible to the lower-scale ones). These come apart, and I suspect her book trades on moving between them.
2. Universality and the renormalization group cut both ways. The fact that many microscopic theories flow to the same infrared fixed point is her best evidence for autonomy; but one could equally read it as showing that the infrared is deeply constrained by ultraviolet structure, not independent of it. I want a question that makes her choose that reading.
3. Low-energy constants. The effective theory's parameters are either measured or matched to the ultraviolet theory; they are not explained at the scale where they appear. I want a question on whether a theory whose free parameters are opaque at its own scale deserves the name autonomous, with a follow-up for the likely reply that every theory, including candidate fundamental ones, has unexplained parameters.
4. Constraints running downward. There are cases — anomaly matching is the one I have in mind, where consistency conditions on the ultraviolet fix features of any viable infrared completion — where the higher theory dictates the lower one. Does she treat these as exceptions, or do they force her to restrict the autonomy thesis to a narrower claim?
5. The deflection I most want to foreclose: when pressed on cutoff dependence or the apparent need for ultraviolet completion, she tends to say the cutoff is merely epistemic, a bookkeeping device rather than a physical scale. Design at least one question, and its follow-up, to make that move costly — for instance by asking what she makes of cutoffs that acquire physical interpretation, as in condensed-matter systems where the lattice spacing is real.

For each question, give the answer you expect her to give and a follow-up keyed to it, and end the sequence with a question about what result — from physics or from philosophy — would count as evidence against her view, so the interview cannot close on unfalsifiability. Begin your response with the numbered questions themselves, each followed by its anticipated answer and follow-up, with no preface or commentary.
