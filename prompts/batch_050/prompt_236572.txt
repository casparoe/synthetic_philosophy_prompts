I’ve been arguing with some friends about responsibility gaps for autonomous systems, and I definately don’t mean legal liability in some particular jurisdiction—I mean the moral question of whether there is anyone who can appropriately be blamed, made to answer, or held accountable for what happened. My provisional idea is that a real gap appears only when nobody has both what I’m calling control standing (a fair opportunity to anticipate, prevent, or interrupt the harmful behavior) and answerability standing (a reason to justify the system’s deployment and accept the relevant costs). I suspect these usually come apart because designers can explain a system without being positioned to intervene, while operators can intervene without understanding enough to answer for it. Act as a professional philosopher who knows both moral responsibility and AI governance. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming several rival principles before settling on the best one.

Test my idea against these gut verdicts: (1) A hospital triage model performs badly on immunocompromised children, the vendor disclosed that limitation, but the hospital bought it anyway and designed its interface to display every recommendation with equal confidence; a nurse follows an odd recommendation partly because hospital protocol makes overriding it costly. A child is harmed. I think the vendor and hospital share responsibility, the nurse is mostly off the hook, and there is no genuine gap. (2) Two firms independently release trading agents that learn to respond to one another’s behavior and together produce a destabilizing strategy nobody explicitly designed. Even if the exact interaction was surprising, I still think both firms are responsible because they profited from the systems and had duties to monitor them. (3) Emergency workers deploy a swarm of rescue drones in an unprecedented wildfire, communications fail, and an emergent flocking pattern blocks an evacuation route. Suppose there was no skipped testing or negligence and the precise interaction could not reasonably have been predicted. I’m inclined to say this is a genuine responsibility gap, even though someone may still owe compensation. (4) The owner of a caregiving robot alters its reward settings so it prioritizes staying near the owner, then leaves it supervising an elderly parent; because calling an ambulance requires leaving the room, the robot delays calling. I think the owner is clearly responsible and the manufacturer mostly isn’t, though I worry this is inconsistent with my verdict on the trading firms. (5) A city chooses an autonomous shuttle’s aggressive driving mode because it predicts fewer total deaths, while knowing that the remaining risk shifts from passengers to pedestrians. When a pedestrian is killed, I think the city and company must answer for the value choice; autonomy doesn’t create a gap. (6) Volunteers release an open agent framework, a nonprofit chains it to third-party planning and medical plug-ins, and the resulting system causes harm through interactions nobody on any team could see from their own component. This feels like a gap, but maybe it is really just an institutional failure to create an answerable owner.

Give me the principle, or the two strongest candidate principles if no single one works, stated precisely enough that each clause is motivated by a case. Please distinguish moral blame, causal responsibility, answerability, and compensation rather than treating them as the same thing, and separate what is fairly settled from what remains genuinely contested; don’t present one contested theory as the consensus.
