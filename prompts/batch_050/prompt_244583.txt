I organize a monthly interdisciplinary salon at my university — mostly humanities graduate students, a few law students, one computer scientist who tolerates us — and I've volunteered to run the next session, on Gödel's incompleteness theorems and their reputation outside mathematics. The problem I'm anticipating is that everyone in the room has already absorbed the pop-culture version: Gödel showed that truth is subjective, that logic defeats itself, that human minds can't be machines, that everything is uncertain. My actual goal for the evening is narrower and, I think, harder: I want them to grasp, precisely enough to feel the weight of it, what the first theorem does show (any consistent, effectively axiomatized system rich enough to do basic arithmetic leaves some truths unproved) and then to feel why this licenses almost none of the cultural conclusions they've heard. I have about forty-five minutes and a whiteboard, and no one in the room can follow a formal diagonalization argument.

Don't explain the theorem to me; plan how I should explain it. What has to be in place conceptually before the theorem's statement can even land — I'm guessing the distinction between truth in a structure and provability from axioms has to come very early, and that they need to see formal systems as mechanical games before incompleteness means anything — and in what order? What single carrying example should run through the whole session: I was considering framing it around a hypothetical "Book of All Mathematical Truths" that must always leave a page unwritten, but I'm open to something better, and I'd like to know what the example must include (effective enumerability, presumably) and what it must exclude so it doesn't smuggle in the wrong intuitions. Map the misconceptions I should expect in the order they're likely to surface — that unprovable means unknowable, that the theorem shows mathematics is inconsistent or unfinished as a human project, that the Gödel sentence is just the liar paradox and hence a trick of language rather than a statement about numbers, and of course the leap to minds or machines — and tell me at which exact point in the sequence each should be defused, and which I should let stand until after the theorem is stated because killing it early costs me the drama. Finally: what should I openly refuse to touch at this level (the second theorem? the actual arithmetization?), and what question can I pose at the end whose good answers would show the room has understood the theorem's real scope rather than just upgraded their slogans? Please answer in American English.
