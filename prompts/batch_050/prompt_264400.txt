You're a philosopher who specializes in responsibility and the ethics of emerging tech. Think carefully, and do a bit of brainstorming before settling on your answer.

Here's a practice that puzzles me. When cars with driver-assistance or self-driving features crash, responsibility basically always lands on a human: the driver signs a user agreeement telling them to stay alert and ready to intervene at any moment, and regulators mostly treat the software as a product, whose defects are the manufacturer's problem -- nobody seriously proposes blaming or punishing the software itself. In one well-known case, a pedestrian was killed by a self-driving test vehicle in Arizona in 2018, and it was the human backup driver -- who'd been told to watch the road while the system did the driving -- who got criminally prosecuted. The company running the test program wasn't.

So: what does this arrangement presuppose about moral responsibility? Does it assume that responsibility has to terminate in a human somewhere, like a hot potato that can't just be dropped? Does the "keep watching and be ready to intervene" duty presuppose a kind of control that humans plausibly don't have over a system that's doing the driving for them? And is there a tension in treating the software as a mere product for liability purposes while marketing it as the thing that drives? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit with, note where it's philosophically underdetermined, and tell me what would concretely have to change if we instead accepted that some of these crashes involve a genuine responsibility gap where nobody is blameworthy.
