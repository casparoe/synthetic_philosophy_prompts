I'm writing a feature on proposals to govern frontier AI through its inputs rather than its outputs, and one framing keeps coming up in my interviews with policy people: training compute is like fissile material, and the right model for governing advanced AI is the nuclear nonproliferation regime. Caps on the largest training runs are ceilings on enrichment; export controls on high-end chips are controls on centrifuges; monitoring of big data centers is safeguards inspection; and an eventual international body would be modeled on the IAEA. The analogy is almost always deployed to support a specific conclusion — that a pause, or at least a hard ceiling, on frontier training runs is enforceable in a way that rules about algorithms or voluntary safety commitments are not, because compute is countable, concentrated in a few chokepoints, and hard to hide.

How far does the analogy actually hold? I'd like the correspondence table first: what plays the role of uranium ore, of enrichment, of the reactor-versus-bomb distinction, of inspections and accounting. Then the places the mapping breaks. In particular: is there anything on the compute side that plays the role of the clean civilian/military split the nuclear regime rests on, given that the same chips train both a chatbot and a weapons-relevant model? Does the fact that capability gains come from algorithmic efficiency as well as raw compute do damage the analogy cannot absorb — as if one could enrich uranium by inventing better physics? And does the analogy quietly import assumptions about visibility and attribution — that you can tell who trained what, and how big the run was — that the AI case may not deliver? Finally, once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion the analogy was carrying (an enforceable pause or ceiling) still stand on its own arguments, and if the nuclear frame can't bear the weight, is there a better historical analogue for governing a dual-use capability at its chokepoints?
