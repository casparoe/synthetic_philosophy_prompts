I've been reading the old Sequences again and got into a long argument with two friends that I want settled properly. The proximate cause: one of them claimed, over dinner, that a lot of the evo-psych-adjacent explanations circulating in our corner of the internet -- why we find gossip compelling, why religious belief is near-universal, why music moves us -- commit the error Gould and Lewontin diagnosed with their spandrels analogy. Her point was that a trait can be everywhere in a species, deeply felt, and developmentally reliable, and still be a byproduct rather than an adaptation, the way the spandrels of San Marco are a byproduct of mounting a dome on rounded arches rather than anything the architect wanted for its own sake. My other friend pushed back that the spandrels image is doing more work than it can bear: spandrels in a basilica are geometrically inevitable, identical everywhere, and utterly inert, whereas biological traits vary, are heritable, and get reshaped by selection the moment they have any effect on fitness at all -- so the analogy allegedly rigs the conclusion by picking a source domain where "byproduct" is essentially costless and permanent.

What I'd like is a proper audit of the analogy as an argument, not just a summary of the original paper. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the dome, the arches, the spandrels themselves, the later mosaic decoration, the architect's intentions -- mapped onto selection pressures, developmental and phylogenetic constraints, candidate byproduct traits, exaptation, and whatever stands in for design intent in a mindless process. Then tell me where the mapping breaks in ways that matter for the dispute at hand, as opposed to breaks that are merely decorative. I'm particularly interested in two pressure points: (1) whether the architectural case, where the byproduct is forced by geometry, overstates how forced biological byproducts are, given that selection can in principle sculpt or eliminate them; and (2) whether the fact that San Marco's spandrels were later put to splendid decorative use undercuts or actually reinforces the anti-adaptationist moral -- my first friend says it shows how byproducts get retrospectively narrated as intended, my second says it shows byproducts don't stay byproducts.

Finally: suppose the analogy does break down in these ways. Does the substantive conclusion it's being used to support -- that we should be much more suspicious of adaptationist just-so stories than the rationalist ecosystem typically is, and that "universal and functional-seeming" is weak evidence of "selected for" -- survive on other grounds, or was the spandrels picture carrying most of the persuasive weight? And if there's a better analogy for the legitimate core of the critique, something that captures genuine constraint-and-byproduct structure without smuggling in the architectural case's convenient inevitability, propose it and show it earns its keep.
