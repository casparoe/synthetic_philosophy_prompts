I write for a magazine covering science and ideas, and I'm preparing a long-form interview with a moral philosopher who has spent her career defending a position most of her colleagues regard as almost indefensible: that a positive pure rate of time preference is rationally permissible and perhaps even required. She grants that we should discount future benefits for uncertainty, for expected economic growth, and for the risk that humanity won't exist to receive them. What she insists on, against the Sidgwick–Parfit consensus, is that a benefit may legitimately count for less simply because it comes later, all else equal. Draft nine interview questions for our conversation. I want to move past the part where she restates the view and into the territory where her position is genuinely strained. Specifically: include at least one question pressing the analogy between discounting by temporal distance and discounting by spatial or social distance, and why the former is rational if the latter is bigotry; at least one on whether her own past and future selves are fair game for the same treatment, and what happens to prudence and diachronic consistency if so; at least one on what her view implies for long-horizon policy like climate mitigation, where even a small pure rate makes distant catastrophe nearly weightless, and whether she accepts that consequence or qualifies the theory to avoid it; and at least one on the empirical finding that people's actual time preferences are hyperbolic rather than exponential, and whether a theory of rational discounting can survive being built on a pattern of preference that is dynamically inconsistent. For each question, give me a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to make, so that if she reaches for her standard moves — distinguishing impersonal ethics from prudence, or treating the pure rate as an unjustifiable but unavoidable axiom — I can press one level deeper. Close the sequence with a question about what evidence or argument could actually change her mind, so the interview doesn't read as an exercise in mutual confirmation.
