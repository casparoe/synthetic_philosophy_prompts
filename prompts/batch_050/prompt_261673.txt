I am a structural engineer, not a mathematician, but infinity has recently become personally relevant to me: my teenage daughter asked at dinner whether there are "more" real numbers than fractions, and my father-in-law, a retired schoolteacher with strong finitist leanings, flatly denied that completed infinities mean anything at all. I spent two weekends reconstructing Cantor's reasoning so I could referee this dispute at the next family gathering, and in the process I produced an argument of my own that now terrifies me, because it appears to demolish the very result I was planning to defend. If it is sound, the rationals are uncountable and the whole cardinality hierarchy collapses; since I am fairly sure standard mathematics does not collapse on the strength of my weekend project, something in it must be wrong, and I want to know exactly where and exactly what it costs.

Here is the argument.

(1) Suppose, for contradiction, that the rational numbers strictly between 0 and 1 can be exhaustively listed: q1, q2, q3, and so on.
(2) Write out each listed rational as an infinite decimal expansion, choosing the expansion that terminates in repeating zeros wherever two expansions are possible.
(3) Define a new number x between 0 and 1 by the following rule: the n-th decimal digit of x is 5 if the n-th decimal digit of q_n is anything other than 5, and 6 if the n-th digit of q_n is 5.
(4) For every n, x differs from q_n in its n-th digit; and since x's digits are only ever 5s and 6s, the mismatch cannot be patched up by the two-expansions ambiguity, because that ambiguity only ever involves trailing 0s versus trailing 9s.
(5) Therefore x does not appear anywhere on the list.
(6) But x is a perfectly definite number between 0 and 1 specified by a perfectly definite rule, so if the list genuinely contained every rational number in that interval, it would have to contain x.
(7) The supposition (1) therefore leads to contradiction: the rationals between 0 and 1 cannot be listed, so they are uncountable, and since the rationals were supposed to be the paradigmatic countable infinite set, the distinction between countable and uncountable infinity is in trouble generally.

The problem is that I also worked through the standard zigzag enumeration of the positive rationals (first row, first column, anti-diagonals, skipping duplicates) and I cannot find a gap in that either. So I currently possess what look like two sound proofs of contradictory conclusions. If forced to choose, I would rather keep the enumeration proof and lose the diagonal argument above, because the enumeration feels constructive in a way I can verify line by line; but I want to know what I am paying for that preference, and whether my father-in-law gets any consolation prize from the wreckage.
