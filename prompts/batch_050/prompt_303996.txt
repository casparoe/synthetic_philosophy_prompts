I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been invited to defend a deliberately provocative thesis in a university debate series: that "better than, all things considered" is not a transitive relation. My argument is a spectrum argument built from a sequence of imagined afternoons. Afternoon A1 contains two hours of an engrossing book and one minute of a low-grade headache; each subsequent afternoon trades a barely noticeable sliver of reading pleasure for a barely noticeable shortening of the headache, so that each successive afternoon strikes me as at least as good as its predecessor; yet by the end of the sequence I'm indifferent between A1 and an afternoon of nearly pure mild headache, which is clearly worse than A1. I conclude that the pairwise judgments cannot be chained, and that rational choice theory is built on an axiom the human good doesn't obey. The audience will be mostly economics and PPE students for whom transitivity is close to a definition of rationality, plus two philosophers working on population ethics who will suspect I've smuggled in a mere-addition paradox. Prepare my Q&A: the objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased as they would phrase it, with my best two-sentence reply and a note on what I should concede versus hold; the final item should be the objection I'm hoping nobody asks. Deliver this as exactly five numbered items of two or three sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion, in American English.
