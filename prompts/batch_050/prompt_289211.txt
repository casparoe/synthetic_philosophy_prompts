I am not a scholar, but I have been reading about Maimonides’ interpretation of animal sacrifice, especially his suggestion that the biblical sacrificial system may have been a gradual concession to people accustomed to ancient forms of worship rather than an intrinsically ideal practice. Suppose Maimonides were alive today and encountered three developments: cultivated meat grown from a small biopsy of a kosher animal; animals genetically engineered so that they cannot feel pain; and synthetic “offerings” that reproduce the physical properties of traditional sacrifices without involving a living creature. What would he most plausibly say about whether each could count as a genuine sacrifice, and why?

Please distinguish the different questions that might matter to him: whether sacrifice essentially requires the death of an animal, the surrender of something valuable, obedience to a divine command, or the ritual transformation of human habits; whether eliminating suffering would solve or merely obscure the moral problem; and whether a technically valid substitute would still serve the pedagogical purpose he attributed to sacrificial law. Also consider whether he would favor restoring a future temple cult with traditional animals, accept bloodless alternatives, or reinterpret the system as historically necessary but no longer ideal. Where several answers are possible, rank them by how well they fit his broader views, and identify which parts of the prediction are especially uncertain.
