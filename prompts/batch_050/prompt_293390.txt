In my history class we've been studying the Industrial Revolution, and afterward two classmates of mine, Marcus and Lena, got into a long argument at lunch about whether economics explains everything else in history. Neither would concede anything, and they've asked me to settle it, but I can't. Here's the exchange as best I remember it:

(1) Marcus started by saying that, taken seriously, the materialist view of history is basically right: the way a society produces things -- its technology, and who owns the means of production -- explains everything else. Laws, religions, philosophies, and art are just the "superstructure" reflecting the economic "base." Feudal lords get feudal religion; factory owners get liberal individualism.

(2) Lena replied that this can't be right, because ideas clearly do causal work on their own. Christianity existed for over a thousand years before capitalism, so capitalism can't explain it, and plenty of societies with nearly identical technology and property relations ended up with very different religions and governments.

(3) Marcus answered that she was confusing two questions. The base doesn't have to invent every idea; it selects which ideas survive and spread. Protestantism wasn't created by merchants, but it flourished precisely where and when it served the interests of an emerging commercial class. Ideas that fit the economic order get amplified; ideas that don't, die out.

(4) Lena said that move makes his position unfalsifiable -- whenever an idea seems to matter independently, he just announces it was "really" economics all along, without any test that could prove him wrong. She also said that if an idea has to do real causal work in order to be "selected," then it's not a mere reflection anymore, so he's quietly conceded her point.

(5) Marcus then said the causation still runs one way overall, because the economy sets the terms on which everything else operates. But Lena answered that this is plainly false in the legal case: a market economy can't even exist until property law, contract enforcement, and the state are already in place, so at least sometimes the so-called superstructure has to come first.

They both turned to me at that point and I honestly didn't know what to say. Could you adjudicate their exchange move by move -- say who is right about each specific point, what (if anything) both of them are missing, and who comes out ahead overall?
