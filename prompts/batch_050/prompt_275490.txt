Ok so I've been trying to build the strongest possible version of an evolutionary debunking argument for a philosophy reading group and I think I've overshot, because the thing now seems to prove way more than I wanted and I can't find where it slipped. Here it is:

(1) Our moral beliefs were heavily shaped by natural selection. The capacity for guilt, reciprocal altruism, outrage at cheaters, all that — it's there because it helped our ancestors leave more descendants.

(2) Selection tracks reproductive fitness, not truth. A belief can spread because it's useful regardless of whether it's accurate. So a faculty shaped by selection gives us no reason to think its outputs line up with an independent moral reality.

(3) Therefore we have no good reason to think our moral beliefs are true.

(4) But this can't be special to morality. Our mathematical and logical beliefs were also shaped by selection — a hominid that counted predators wrong got eaten. Selection is equally a fitness-tracker and not a math-truth-tracker, so by (2) we have no reason to think our math and logic beliefs line up with mathematical reality either.

(5) Same for our epistemic norms. Believing things like "trust beliefs formed by reliable methods" or "prefer simpler explanations" is itself a product of the same evolutionary process. So those norms are off-track too, by the same reasoning.

(6) But the debunking argument in (1)-(3) only works if we trust exactly those epistemic norms — the inference patterns, the demand for evidence, all of it. If (5) is right, the argument debunks itself.

(7) So either nothing is debunked, or everything including the debunking is debunked. Both options kill the argument.

I don't want that. I want to keep the debunking of morality specifically, not end up in total skepticism about arithmetic. Which step do I give up? If it's valid then one of (1)-(2) or (4)-(5) has to be false, but they all look like the same move applied in different places. Please tell me which premise is doing the damage, why it looked fine, and what it costs to deny it — like, if you say math is different from morality here, what exactly makes it different without begging the question in favor of moral realism?
