I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, and I currently sit on an interdisciplinary university team developing a public-health campaign aimed at reducing unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions in outpatient clinics. Our draft materials included a clear, somewhat dry section explaining how population-level resistance develops and why an individual prescription can be reasonable for the patient yet costly for everyone else. Our communications consultant told us to cut it. Instead, she wants to open with a sixty-second video of a young father describing how he lost his sister to a drug-resistant infection, and to end with a simple behavioral prompt at the point of care. When I objected that this felt less like informing people and more like steering them around their own judgment, she shrugged and said: sell the sizzle, not the steak; nobody was ever argued into changing their behavior. The phrase came out of her like a settled law of nature, and several colleagues nodded. I suspect the slogan is doing real argumentative work here without having earned it, but I can't quite pin down what it even claims.

Could you unpack "sell the sizzle, not the steak" into the distinct precise theses it might be expressing? I can already see it might be a merely descriptive claim about attention (affect-laden material gets processed first), a stronger psychological claim that reasons never motivate action and only rationalize it afterward, an epistemic claim that the "steak" (the actual mechanism or evidence) is inaccessible or irrelevant to lay audiences, a piece of practical advice about sequencing (lead with feeling, follow with reasons), or a flatly normative claim that bypassing deliberation is simply what competent communication is. There may be others I've missed, and some of these collapse into each other. For each candidate reading, I'd like to know whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or a substantive claim that is false, and what consideration bears most directly on that verdict.

Most importantly, please assess which reading my consultant's argument actually requires, and whether the slogan on its strongest defensible reading licenses her specific design move: replacing the mechanistic explanation entirely with the emotional narrative, rather than merely front-loading emotion while keeping the explanation available. (yes / no / only partially, with a one-line reason).
