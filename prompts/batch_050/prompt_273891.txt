Design a thought experiment for a graduate seminar on the wrong kind of reasons problem, meeting all of the specifications below.

Specifications for the scenario:

1. The central case must involve a consideration C that is intuitively a reason for an agent S to hold some attitude, but intuitively the wrong kind of reason for it: C does not bear on whether the attitude is fitting or correct. Take belief as the central attitude, so C is a reason for S to believe some proposition p without being evidence that p is true.
2. The reason must be generated endogenously. No demon, eccentric billionaire, hypnotist, pill, or any other party offering rewards or threatening punishments. C must arise entirely from facts about S's own psychology, character, limitations, or projects.
3. The benefit that grounds C must be epistemic in flavor rather than merely practical: holding the belief must in some way improve S's subsequent cognition -- for example, by counteracting a bias, steadying her deliberation, or making her a fairer assessor of future evidence -- while still not counting as evidence that p is true. This is the crux of the design: the case should put pressure on the simple test that the right kind of reasons for belief are exactly those that bear on the truth of p.
4. The scenario must include a paired case in which the very same consideration C, directed at a different attitude or act (for example, intending to bring it about that one believes p, or trusting, or admiring), is intuitively the right kind of reason. The pair should isolate the choice of attitude as the variable that flips the verdict, with everything else held fixed.

After presenting the scenario, provide the following analysis:

(a) State the verdict each of two standard diagnoses delivers on your case: first, a diagnosis on which wrong-kind reasons are reasons that relate to the state of holding the attitude rather than to its object; second, a fitting-attitude diagnosis on which so-called wrong-kind reasons are not reasons for the attitude at all but at most reasons to cause oneself to have it. Note where the two diagnoses converge on your case and where they come apart.
(b) Identify the single structural feature of the case that does the work in satisfying specification 3, and explain what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse back into a standard pragmatic-reason case of the reward-for-belief type, and if so, at exactly which point?
(c) Give the strongest objection available to a defender of the simple truth-directed test -- either that your C really is (perhaps misleading or higher-order) evidence for p, or that the epistemic benefit you describe does not constitute a reason for anything. Sketch your reply.
(d) State briefly what the case shows about whether the notion of the right kind of reason for belief can be characterized independently of a substantive theory of evidence.

Constraints: the scenario must be realistic -- S could be an ordinary colleague, and no exotic psychology may be posited; the scenario itself must be at most 250 words; and the case must generate its verdicts rather than stipulate them. In particular, do not write inside the scenario that a consideration is obviously the wrong kind of reason; set up the facts and let the verdict fall out in the analysis that follows.
