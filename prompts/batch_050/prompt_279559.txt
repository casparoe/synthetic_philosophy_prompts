So I recently got into a (friendly) argument with a roommate about this and we couldn't settle it, so I want a careful reconstruction rather than a hot take. Suppose Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer were somehow transported to the present day and given a couple of weeks to immerse themselves in the modern attention economy: TikTok's algorithmically curated short-video feed, YouTube autoplay, Spotify's personalized playlists, Instagram influencers, the whole thing. Obviously their first instinct would be that the culture industry has achieved a total victory they could barely have imagined. But I want you to go beyond that obvious verdict and actually think through where their analysis lands cleanly and where it gets strained by features they never saw coming.

A few pressure points I'm especially curious about. First, their critique assumed a small number of centralized producers (Hollywood studios, radio networks, magazine conglomerates) churning out standardized content for passive masses -- but today a huge share of the content is made by ordinary users, anyone can publish, and there's a genuine long tail of weird niche stuff. Would they dismiss creator culture as pseudo-activity, i.e. the masses now eagerly producing their own standardized fare for free, or would the sheer decentralization of production force them to revise the monopoly-capitalism picture the original critique rested on? Second, the standardization thesis: modern feeds are hyper-personalized, no two people's feeds are alike, which looks like the opposite of everyone consuming the same product. Is algorithmic personalization just pseudo-individuality perfected (everyone gets a slightly different wrapper on the same underlying paste), or is that too glib? Third, authenticity: influencers sell relatability and unfiltered realness as an aesthetic, which seems almost too on-the-nose for them, but does the fact that audiences *know* it's manufactured and consume it anyway (irony, stan culture, hate-watching) fit their framework, or does their picture of duped, passive consumers break down there?

Also, steelman the other side before you're done: what's the strongest case that the internet genuinely escapes their diagnosis (democratized production, subcultures flourishing, educational content reaching millions), and how would Adorno and Horkheimer respond -- convincingly or not? Feel free to note where you think they'd simply be wrong or where their own elitism does the argumentative work.
