I've come to accept a restricted version of the argument from divine hiddenness: if a perfectly loving God existed, then no human being capable of a meaningful personal relationship with God would remain in a state of nonresistant nonbelief; since there appear to be such persons, theism is at best improbable. I hold this as an evidential argument rather than a disproof, and I regard the standard greater-goods responses (free will, soul-making) as already answered in the literature. What I want is a map of the argument's exposure from within. Which accounts of perfect divine love — say, that love sometimes requires temporary withdrawal, or that explicit propositional belief is not required for the relevant relationship — would genuinely undercut the first premise rather than merely relabel it? Which empirical findings about the psychology of nonbelief (for instance, if some degree of "resistance" turned out to be psychologically inevitable for embodied agents) would damage the second premise, and would a committed proponent concede that as counting against them? And would it matter if the argument proved too much — if the same reasoning told against a God who hides for goods beyond our survey, or against belief in any being whose self-disclosure is interest-relative? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which the argument's defenders could absorb without real loss.
