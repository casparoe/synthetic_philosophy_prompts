I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a long essay on why truth-seeking institutions — courts, newsrooms, scientific journals — matter beyond the mere fact that they sometimes output true claims. The philosophical peg is the old puzzle from Plato's Meno: if a true belief gets you to Larissa just as reliably as knowledge of the road does, what exactly does knowledge add? Every draft I write keeps collapsing into the claim that knowledge is more *useful*, and my editor rightly objects that this misses the point, since a lucky guess is exactly as useful as a justified one. So before I let any theory of the extra value of knowledge anywhere near the piece, I want the specification first: the desiderata that any adequate solution to this "value problem" has to meet.

Please write that specification. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it. The list should include at least: handling the original Larissa-style case, where true belief and knowledge produce identical practical outcomes; dissolving or surviving the swamping problem, the worry that once you already have the truth, any epistemic addition (justification, reliability, proper method) appears to add no value, the way a great espresso machine adds nothing to a cup that's already perfect; explaining Plato's own stability intuition, that untethered true opinions tend to run away while knowledge stays put, and saying whether stability is the whole answer or only part; distinguishing the instrumental value of knowledge from any final or intrinsic value it might have, and being explicit about which kind of value the theory is claiming; and scaling from the individual case to the institutional one, since my essay needs the answer to say something about why a newsroom that reliably produces true beliefs through lucky accident would still be worse than one that knows. For each item, say whether you take it to be a genuine constraint or the prejudice of one camp in the literature, and flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — if the list itself turns out to be incoherent, that is a finding I'd want to feature.

Then, briefly, score the main contenders against the list: reliabilist accounts, virtue-theoretic or credit accounts on which knowing is a kind of achievement attributable to the agent, and the straight stability account descended from the Meno itself. One or two sentences per contender per desideratum is enough; I care far more about the criteria and their motivating cases than about the final tally. Finally, tell me which single desideratum does the most eliminative work — the one requirement that knocks out the largest share of popular views — since that's where I'll need to spend my word count.
