I sit on the review panel at a defense ministry that has to sign off on whether new weapon systems keep humans in 'meaningful control' (whatever that ends up meaning). Our track record so far: we approved a shipboard system that fires autonomously at incoming missiles, since no human could react in time and it only ever targets projectiles. We approved a loitering munition that a human launches at a designated area to hunt enemy radar emitters, with no way to call off individual strikes once it's up. We rejected a border drone that identifies vehicles as military and fires on its own, with a human just reviewing logs afterwards. And we rejected a base-defense turret that targets approaching people it flags as combatants, even though a human could technically veto each shot, because the engagment window was about ten seconds and the human had no independent way to check the system's call. Now we're reviewing a close-in ship defense system against swarming small boats: it classifies fast-approaching boats as hostile and fires autonomously, a human supervisor can veto but has maybe twenty seconds and no independent sensor feed to verify against. Trials say it's very good at not misclassifying fishing boats, fwiw. What should we decide if we want to stay consistent with our own record? Which earlier call is the closest analog? And if our precedents genuinely underdetermine this one, what principle are we signing up for with each verdict — and what's a future case that would actually tell the candidate rules apart?
