I'm taking a university seminar on contemporary philosophy of language, and next week I'm leading our session on inferentialist semantics -- the view, associated with Robert Brandom, that a sentence's meaning is constituted by its inferential role: what would count as a reason for asserting it, what asserting it commits you to, and what it is incompatible with, rather than by reference or truth conditions. My classmates keep saying they understand the slogan but can't see why anyone would actually believe it, so I'd rather run an activity than give a lecture.

Design a 75-minute structured game for 12-15 participants that makes the idea do work before anyone names it. Play itself should force participants to run into three things: (i) an assertion from someone who can neither justify it nor accept its consequences strikes us as empty, however grammatically well-formed; (ii) grasping a claim seems to consist in being able to navigate what follows from it, what it follows from, and what it rules out; (iii) challenges to justify a claim have to bottom out somewhere, in things treated as entitled by default -- which is where the game should start to strain.

Give me the rules, the roles (including whoever keeps track of who is committed and entitled to what), a timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments of play onto the theory's apparatus, notes what will predictably go wrong when I run it, and is honest about which feature of the view the game cannot reproduce -- I suspect it's the non-inferential entries and exits, perception and action.
