I'm a novelist, deep in the weeds on a draft, and one line of dialogue is holding up a whole subplot. The setup: my two main characters, a hedge-fund quant who's read way too much game theory and an epidemiologist who's survived two actual outbreaks, get into it at a dinner party about whether some past epidemic was natural or lab-origin. Each has seen stuff the other hasn't, they've talked for hours, and my quant finally shuts her down with something like: two rational agents who have common knowledge of each other's views can't agree to disagree, so if we're both being honest our probabilities have to converge. He cribbed it from that Aumann result about agreeing to disagree, which he treats like a mathematical mic drop. My editor circled the scene and wrote in the margin, "does this theorem really prove what he thinks it proves??" and annoyingly I can't tell.

So what I'd love is a menu of the different precise claims that slogan could be making, because I suspect "rational people can't disagree forever" is doing about five jobs at once. Is it a claim about actual humans in actual conversations, or only about idealized Bayesian agents who update perfectly? Does it need the two agents to start from a shared prior, and if so, is that assumption secretly doing all the heavy lifting? Does "common knowledge" just mean they both know the other's opinion, or that whole infinite tower of knowing that they know that they know? And is the claim that they'd converge if they talked long enough, or that the mere fact of common knowledge of disagreement is already incoherent? For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, a genuine substantive theorem, or just false, and which reading (if any) would actually entitle my quant to declare victory at the dinner table.

Two more things my plot needs. First, does any version of the theorem say who should move toward whom, or how fast, or is it silent on that in a way that leaves my epidemiologist room to hold her ground without being "irrational"? Second, if the strongest defensible reading requires common priors, is there any remotely plausible sense in which real people share priors, or is the slogan basically inapplicable to messy human disagreements like mine? I want the scene to survive contact with a smart reader, so be brutal: if the quant is bluffing, I need to know, and ideally which specific precisification exposes the bluff.
