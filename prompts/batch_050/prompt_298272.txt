I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size tech company, and last week we got into this argument over beers that I keep thinking about. One of my coworkers is refusing to work on a feature because a customer could plausibly use it for something sketchy, and my position (which I defended, maybe too confidently) is roughly this: if the bad thing is going to happen whether or not you participate, then participating isn't complicity. Like, if I don't build it, someone else on the team builds it, or the customer just uses a competitor's product, or they achieve the same thing some other way. My individual contribution doesn't cause the harm, in the sense that removing me changes nothing, so I'm not on the hook for it. Maybe I should still quit on expressive grounds or whatever, but that's different from being complicit. This seems obviously right to me, but the fact that smart people push back on it so hard makes me worried I'm missing something.

So: what would count against this position? I want the cases and arguments that should actually move me, sorted by how damaging they'd be. I'm guessing stuff like firing squads or collective harms where everyone's individual contribution is redundant matters here, but I don't know how to weigh "the outcome would be identical without you" against "you still chose to be one of the people doing it." Also flag which objections the defenders of my view would themselves concede as serious versus which ones only sound decisive if you've already accepted the opposing framework. And be honest about the other direction too: my coworker's side seems to risk collapsing into "you're complicit in everything connected to anything bad, in any way," which would make the concept useless -- so what would count against their view as well, or does it at least have a principled stopping point?

One more thing I care about: I have a nagging feeling that both sides can be insulated against counterexamples. Like, whenever I give a case where participation seems harmless, they say the intention still matters; whenever they give a case where causation seems irrelevant, I say that's a threshold effect, not true redundancy. If my position only survives by reclassifying every hard case as not-really-redundant, tell me that, and whether that's fatal or just how positions like this work. For what it's worth, the thing that would most shake me is a case where my rule gives a verdict that I'd be embarrassed to defend out loud, not an abstract argument about what causation "really" is.
