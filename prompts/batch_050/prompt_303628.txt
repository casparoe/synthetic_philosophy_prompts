In my undergraduate course on personal identity I present Nāgasena's chariot simile from the Questions of King Milinda: just as 'chariot' is a convenient designation for an assemblage of axle, wheels, yoke and so forth, none of which is the chariot and which are not the chariot even taken together, so 'person' is a convenient designation for the five aggregates, beyond which no self is to be found. A student has objected that the simile proves both too much and too little: too much, because the same reasoning would dissolve every composite object, leaving nothing special about selves; too little, because nothing in a chariot answers to the psychological continuity, agency and self-ascription on which talk of persons seems to rest. Please audit the analogy. Set out the correspondence explicitly (what plays the role of the parts, the assembled whole, the name, and the one who uses it), then distinguish the disanalogies that bear on the simile's intended use — showing that there is no self over and above the aggregates — from those that are idle. Finally, say whether the simile, once its disanalogies are on the table, can carry the stronger claims sometimes built upon it, such as the momentariness of the continuum of aggregates or the irrationality of prudential self-concern, or whether some other comparison would serve the Buddhist position better. Please write your answer in British English.
