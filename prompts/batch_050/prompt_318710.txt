I'm in an undergraduate environmental ethics seminar, and we just spent two weeks on the nonidentity problem. I think I finally understand the argument, but it's left me with a question that my TA sort of waved away, and I'd like a real answer. As I understand it (from Parfit's discussion in the final part of Reasons and Persons), the choices we make today about energy and climate policy don't just affect how well future people live -- they affect which people exist at all. Different policies produce different patterns of travel, settlement, timing of conception, and so on, so the individuals alive in 2200 in a high-emissions world are numerically different people from those who would have existed had we decarbonized. It follows, apparently, that nobody in the bad future can truly say they would have been better off if we had acted differently, since under the alternative policy they wouldn't have existed. And if no one is made worse off, no one is harmed, and if no one is harmed, no one is wronged.

Here's where I think I've spotted something the literature is embarrassed about. When I raised this in section -- suggesting that the nonidentity problem seems to dissolve any duty we have to future generations, since climate policy can't wrong anyone who will actually exist -- my professor said I was "overgeneralizing" and moved on, without explaining how. But I genuinely don't see the error. So my question is twofold. First: is there a way to repair the argument so that it delivers duties to future individuals after all? For instance, could we individuate persons more coarsely, so that "the people of 2200" counts as the same population across policy choices? Or could we treat future generations as a collective entity that can be wronged as a group even though none of its members are wronged individually -- the way a company can be defrauded without any particular shareholder being? Second: if those repairs fail, is the honest conclusion just that climate ethics has to give up on future generations and justify mitigation entirely by its effects on people already alive?

I'd like an answer that takes the nonidentity reasoning seriously rather than looking for a quick way around it -- but if my inference from "no specific future individual is harmed" to "we have no duties regarding the distant future" contains a mistake, I want the mistake located precisely, not just gestured at.
