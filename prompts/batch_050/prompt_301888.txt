I'm a PhD student in philosophy (early modern, sadly) and I've gone down a rabbit hole combining my dissertation topic with my roommate's crypto obsession. Quick background in case it's useful, since this corner of Berkeley is kind of obscure: in The Querist he basically argues that money is nothing but a ticket or counter for recording and transferring claims on other people's labour, and that a country's real wealth is its industry, not its piles of gold and silver — so the value was never in the metal to begin with. Stack that on top of his famous denial of matter (to be is to be perceived, no mind-independent substratum anywhere) and you arguably get the first rigorous metaphysics of a purely substrate-free currency. Also relevant: he once sailed to Rhode Island to wait for a government grant for a college in Bermuda that never arrived, so the man knew about promised value that fails to materialize.

Write a satirical whitepaper for a cryptocurrency called "BerkeleyCoin" (or invent a better name), written by a breathless founder who has fully internalized Berkeley's metaphysics and monetary theory and keeps deploying them as selling points. 700–900 words total. The satire has to work by exaggerating REAL features of Berkeley's position — the paper should be weirdly defensible as Berkeley scholarship — not by inventing stuff he didn't hold. So: when skeptics ask what backs the coin, they get told that nothing backs anything, and that the demand for a backing substratum is exactly the philosophical fiction Berkeley dismantled; the ledger stays consistent because it is perceived (the omnipresent perceiver handily solves the double-spend problem — God as validator node); token burns are just ideas ceasing to be perceived. Deadpan throughout, no winking at the reader. A historian of philosophy should laugh because it's unfair-but-accurate; a crypto bro should get three quarters through before noticing something is off.
