I teach a small undergrad seminar on minds and machines, about 14 students, and I've got one 80-minute session on whether a chatbot-style language model is best thought of as an agent, a simulator of agents, or some third thing we dont have a good word for yet. Lecturing this goes badly: everyone nods at "it just continues text" and then ten minutes later they're saying the model "wants to be helpful" again. So design me an in-class activity where the students have to actually *be* the model, so the weirdness shows up in what happens rather than in what I tell them.

Rough shape I have in mind, feel free to restructure: one student per group plays the model, armed with nothing but a written context (the prompt so far) and a rule to continue it in whatever way best fits that context. The other students control the context and get to edit it mid-conversation -- swap out who the character is, change the setting, reveal that earlier lines were a lie or a scene from a play. What I want them to run into themselves: the model-student has no commitments that survive a context edit, the character in the text has beliefs and goals but the thing writing it doesn't have them in the same way, and yet the model-student is obviously a human agent the whole time, which is exactly where the analogy starts to strain.

I need: the exact rules (can the model-student break character, refuse a context, ask questions?), how contexts get written and edited and passed, a timeline for the 80 mins with at least two structured rounds of escalating manipulation plus a final round where the context is just "you are a helpful, honest assistant" so they see the assistant persona as one more character, and a debrief that maps each round onto the agent / simulator / something-else options. No tech, index cards and paper only. Don't let the facilitator name the simulator idea until the debrief.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (hamming it up, the one student who's read all the AI discourse spoiling it, groups colluding) and give me an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce -- the human "model" has a stable self backstage deciding how to play along, and whether theres anything analogous inside the actual model is precisely the open question, so the game might quietly presuppose an answer.
