I direct the research-synthesis team at a policy institute. Several times a month we produce two-page briefs on contested questions -- what a housing policy did to rents, whether a procurement rule is ethically defensible -- for officials who will never read the underlying literature. Our recurring failure: reviewers complain that our briefs either flatten real disagreement into false consensus or stack positions side by side without saying how they relate, and our directors complain when we refuse to give a bottom line.

I want to rebuild our drafting procedure around the Jain epistemology of anekāntavāda. Our working understanding, which you should correct where it distorts the doctrine: claims about complex matters hold only from particular standpoints (naya) and under qualification; honest assertion is conditional, marked by syāt, roughly "in a certain respect"; the sevenfold scheme (saptabhaṅgī) enumerates the ways a claim may be affirmed, denied, both in sequence, both at once in a way that resists statement, and the combinations thereof; and a standpoint can be partial without being simply false.

Write the drafting procedure my analysts (generalists, two days per brief, my sign-off required) should run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, how to translate the sevenfold scheme into actual moves on the page, and where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be flagged rather than proceduralized. Include explicit safeguards against the obvious failure mode -- treating all standpoints as equally supported -- since the doctrine itself does not say that. Then apply the procedure to two cases: a brief on whether a city's sugar-sweetened-beverage tax improved population health, and a brief on whether the institute should endorse a carbon-offset program. For at least one of these, show the procedure yielding output a director will dislike, and say what the analyst should do then.
