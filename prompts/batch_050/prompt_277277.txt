I’m a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I’m drafting a long feature on accuracy-first epistemology — the project of justifying Bayesian norms by appeal to the single epistemic goal of having credences close to the truth. Before I file, I want a consistency check on the working assumptions my draft rests on. They are:

1. Rational credences obey the probability axioms, and the strongest argument for this is accuracy-dominance: any non-probabilistic set of credences is strictly accuracy-dominated by some probabilistic set, however the world turns out.
2. Accuracy is the sole fundamental epistemic good; talk of justification, responsiveness to evidence, or epistemic virtue is legitimate only insofar as it ultimately cashes out in accuracy terms.
3. Updating by conditionalization is uniquely rational, as shown by the diachronic expected-accuracy argument: planning to conditionalize on whatever one learns has higher expected accuracy than any rival update plan.
4. It can be epistemically rational to decline free, relevant evidence — a person can rationally refuse to learn the result of a genetic test with no medical implications, for instance.
5. In cases of complete ignorance about a proposition, withholding judgment altogether can be rationally permissible, and a good accuracy-first framework should deliver that verdict rather than forcing a credence of 0.5.
6. Which measures of inaccuracy are legitimate (the Brier score and its relatives) is settled by their alethic virtues rather than by anything pragmatic, so the whole edifice remains purely epistemic.

Please audit this set rather than survey the field for me. Identify which commitments genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to; rank the genuine tensions by severity; and for each, say what would have to give and what the cheapest consistent revision would cost. If you think the set is jointly tenable as stated, explain where the obvious objections go wrong.
