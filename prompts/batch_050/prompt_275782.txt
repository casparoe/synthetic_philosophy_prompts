The course covered the intentionality debate between Searle and his critics; the midterm question and the student's answer are reproduced below in full.

Exam question: "Searle holds that some things — words on a page, pictures, machine states — have only derived intentionality: they are about things only because we take them to be. Minds, by contrast, have original intentionality. Explain this distinction and how it figures in Searle's Chinese Room argument. Then present Dennett's two-bitser example and explain how Dennett uses it to resist the conclusion that an artifact's derived intentionality disqualifies it from genuine aboutness. Finally, say which position you find more convincing and why."

Student's answer: "Searle's distinction is between things that mean something on their own and things that mean something only in virtue of being interpreted. A shopping list is about eggs and milk only because someone wrote and reads it; the intentionality is derived from a mind. The thoughts of the shopper, by contrast, are about eggs and milk intrinsically — this is original intentionality, and on Searle's view only biological minds have it. The Chinese Room presses this into an argument: an English speaker locked in a room follows a rulebook for manipulating Chinese symbols, and produces answers indistinguishable from a native speaker's, yet plainly understands no Chinese. Since the room is just a program being executed, the moral is that syntax alone never yields semantics. Searle's conclusion, then, is that no machine could ever genuinely understand anything, since any machine can only shuffle symbols according to rules. Dennett's two-bitser is a vending-machine-like device designed to sort quarters by entering an internal state Q when a quarter is inserted. Transplanted to a country where the relevant coins are balers, it enters the very same state for them. Whether state Q is about quarters or about balers seems to depend entirely on what we, the interpreters, take it to be about — so the device's intentionality is wholly derived. Dennett, however, argues that this is precisely why Searle's distinction overreaches: he claims the two-bitser actually possesses original intentionality, because once it is up and running its internal states track features of the world without any interpreter standing by, and in this respect it is just like a human whose brain states track the world. So for Dennett the derived/original line cannot be used to bar artifacts from genuine aboutness, since artifacts can be on the original side of it. My own view is that Dennett wins the exchange. Because meaning is always ultimately a matter of how a system is interpreted, it follows that the Chinese Room does understand Chinese after all — we need only choose to interpret its symbol-shuffling as understanding, and Searle's intuitions about the man inside are irrelevant to the system as a whole."
