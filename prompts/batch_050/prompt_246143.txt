I am grading closed-book responses to the following short-essay question in an upper-level undergraduate course on early modern philosophy. Please grade the student’s answer and provide the specific comments I should attach to the grade.

Essay question:

Hume maintains that a single observed instance of causation gives us impressions of temporal priority, contiguity, and succession, but no impression of a power or necessary connection by which one event produces another. Explain how, on his account, repeated experience nevertheless gives rise to the idea of necessary connection. In your answer:

1. distinguish a single objective succession, constant conjunction across resembling cases, and the mind’s determined transition from one idea to another;
2. explain the relationship between Hume’s two definitions of cause, including whether either identifies a mind-independent modal fact;
3. explain what the New Hume interpretation adds to the standard regularity reading; and
4. assess whether that interpretation can be made consistent with Hume’s copy principle.

Do not attribute a later counterfactual analysis of causation to Hume.

Student’s answer:

A single collision between two billiard balls gives us impressions of priority, contiguity, and motion, but no further impression labeled causal power. If that event occurred only once, we would have no basis for an idea of necessity. Repetition changes our situation, though not by revealing an additional perceptible feature in the later collisions. After experiencing many resembling pairs, the mind becomes habituated: an impression of the first event carries it to an idea of the second. Hume locates the origin of necessity in the felt determination accompanying this transition. It is an impression of reflection because repeated perceptions produce a new feeling in the mind. Since the feeling belongs to the observer rather than to either object, causal necessity is something we subsequently spread over the objects themselves.

This explains why Hume gives two definitions of cause. According to the first, a cause is an object precedent and contiguous to another, where all objects resembling the first are followed by objects resembling the second. According to the second, a cause is an object followed by another, where the appearance of the first conveys the mind to the second. The first definition identifies the extension of causation in the world, while the second explains the psychological process through which we recognize that extension. They define the same relation from two directions because every constant conjunction produces the relevant association and every such association tracks a constant conjunction.

Neither definition identifies a mind-independent modal fact. To say that one event causes another is not to say that, given the first, the second could not possibly have failed to occur. If the second does not occur, we conclude that the circumstances were not genuinely similar or that some further condition was absent. The necessity we feel is therefore generated by custom and expectation rather than copied from a necessary bond observed in nature.

The New Hume interpretation rejects the claim that Hume eliminated objective causal powers. On this reading, his point is epistemological: our impressions cannot reveal the intrinsic powers connecting events, but nature may nevertheless contain such powers, and Hume himself may reasonably believe that it does. There is then no conflict with the copy principle because that principle limits only what we can clearly conceive, not what can exist or what we can believe to exist. Indeed, the New Hume’s Hume claims to know that real causal powers exist, even though he denies that we know their intrinsic character. His two definitions remain useful because they specify the observable evidence by which we identify causes, while the underlying ontology contains necessary connections inaccessible to observation.
