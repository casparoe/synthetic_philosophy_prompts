I work in machine learning, and I've been reading Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments. The mechanism that interests me is the impartial spectator: we judge our own conduct by imaginatively reconstructing how a well-informed but disinterested observer would react to it, and Smith builds sympathy, propriety, and ultimately conscience out of iterated exercises of this capacity. What I'd like is a systematic translation of this machinery into the vocabulary I actually think in -- learned models, training distributions, loss functions, generalization, distribution shift, overfitting, alignment between an agent's objectives and an evaluator's -- done as faithfully as you can, followed by an honest account of what the translation fails to carry.

Concretely: sympathy looks to me like a forward model that predicts another person's affective state from their situation; the impartial spectator looks like an internalized evaluator trained on a lifetime of observing how third parties actually react, which is then run on oneself; the difference between desiring praise and desiring praiseworthiness looks like the difference between optimizing the evaluative signal you happen to receive and optimizing what the signal is supposed to track. Take the translation as far as it genuinely goes, including the parts I'm only gesturing at -- how Smith gets from predicting others' reactions to endorsing a standpoint that can condemn the reactions of one's entire society, how self-command and the rules of morality fit, and why the spectator must be imagined as informed rather than merely actual.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of Smith's account map cleanly onto this vocabulary, which require stretching (for instance, whether anything in the picture of a trained evaluator captures the normative force of conscience -- the sense that the internal judgment binds me, not merely predicts social sanction), and which seem to have no expression at all in machine-learning terms. Where there is residue, say whether it indicates something my framework is missing, or something Smith is doing that is not best understood as a mechanism at all. I also want your verdict on one specific suspicion of mine: that the impartial spectator is just a debiasing procedure -- correcting for the egocentric distribution of one's training data -- and that debiasing procedures are well understood, so the philosophical fuss is exaggerated. Tell me whether that suspicion survives a careful translation or whether it quietly drops something essential.
