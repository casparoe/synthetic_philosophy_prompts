After a career as an experimental natural scientist, I have lately been reading philosophy of biology and have been struck by how difficult it is to state precisely what is at issue between adaptationism and the Gould–Lewontin critique of spandrels. I am now advising a small institute that can fund four three-year projects on the conceptual and methodological foundations of evolutionary explanation. It cannot fund large field studies or sequencing programmes, so I am interested primarily in projects that would improve how biologists infer, test and communicate evolutionary explanations.

I have been considering what I call a causal-credit ledger. For any trait, researchers would have to apportion confidence among several histories: selection for its present function, selection for a different ancestral function, correlation with another selected trait, developmental by-product, drift or historical contingency, and artefacts produced by how the trait has been individuated. The point is not that these categories exhaust the possibilities, but that an explanatory method should say what evidence would move confidence from one column to another and which observations its favoured methods cannot detect.

Within something like this framework, which open questions should the institute prioritise, and in what order? Candidates might include, but need not be limited to:

- whether adaptationist hypotheses can be formulated so that their predictions precede, rather than follow, detailed knowledge of the trait;
- how finely traits should be individuated before asking whether they are adaptations, and whether disputes about traits are often really disputes about grain size;
- whether developmental constraints can be represented as testable alternatives to selection rather than as a residual category;
- whether comparative, population-genetic, developmental and experimental evidence can be combined without double-counting mutually dependent observations;
- whether evolvability, robustness and modularity can be explained as adaptations without rendering the theory unfalsifiable;
- and whether the spandrel category itself needs sharper positive tests rather than serving chiefly as a warning against just-so stories.

Please defend a ranked agenda, not merely provide a survey. For each priority, explain what other questions it is upstream of, why it is tractable through conceptual or methodological work within three years, how neglected it appears to be relative to its importance, and what concrete output would constitute progress. Distinguish genuine unresolved problems from verbal disputes arising from different uses of adaptation, function or constraint. I would also like to know which familiar issue you would deliberately rank lower because settling it would make little practical difference to evolutionary inference.

Where possible, illustrate the ranking with a small number of hypothetical traits rather than relying on stock examples. Finally, indicate what findings over the next several years would most change your ordering. Please do not include citations or a reading list; I am looking for a reasoned allocation of limited research effort.
