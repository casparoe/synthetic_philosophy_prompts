So I was at the pub with two mates from my old philosophy cohort, Priya and Dom, and they got into it about the language of thought hypothesis and I want an outside verdict because I genuinely can't tell who won.

Priya's opening move: the classic systematicity argument still stands. If a mind can think "the dog chases the cat" it can think "the cat chases the dog," and the only satisfying explanation is that thoughts are built from recombinable parts with syntax, like Mentalese. She said connectionist systems get this at best as a fragile accident of training, and pointed at how LLMs fall apart on novel combinations, long multi-step arithmetic, that kind of thing, as evidence the prediction holds.

Dom's reply: LLMs actually undercut the argument, because they display a lot of apparently systematic behaviour with no hand-built symbolic grammar anywhere in them. So systematicity doesn't require LOT-style representations, it can be approximated statistically. And anyway, he said, the LOT hypothesis is nearly vacuous: any system implemented on a computer has compositional structure at some level of description, so "the mind has a language of thought" comes out trivially true of everything and explains nothing.

Priya pushed back on two fronts. First, she distinguished genuine systematicity from what she called training-set systematicity -- a model that generalises only near its training distribution doesn't really recombine concepts, it interpolates, and humans clearly do the former when you hand them a nonsense sentence made of known words. Second, she said Dom's vacuity charge conflates levels: LOT isn't the claim that some description of the brain is compositional, it's the claim that the representational format cognition actually operates over is compositional, which is a substantive empirical claim that could be false.

Dom came back saying the "interpolation vs recombination" line is unfalsifiable as stated: for any successful generalisation Priya can always claim it's still within some suitably abstract training distribution, and for any failure she can blame performance rather than competence. He also said even if the format/processing distinction is real, LOT theorists have never given an independent handle on what counts as the format cognition "actually operates over" -- you can't just point at behaviour and read the format off it, so the empirical claim is doing less work than advertised.

Priya's last move was to concede that systematicity arguments alone don't get you to full Mentalese but said LOT was never meant to rest on one argument; it's an inference to the best explanation from a package (systematicity, productivity, the need for variable binding in reasoning). Dom said a package of weak arguments doesn't add up to one strong one, and variable binding is demonstrably implementable in non-symbolic architectures these days, so that leg has gone wobbly too.

Can you go through the exchange step by step and say who's right on each specific point -- not just an overall winner? If either of them is leaning on a confused premise or a distinction that doesn't do the work they think it does, say so plainly rather than being polite about it. And if you think I'm framing the whole dispute wrongly somewhere, push back on that as well. British English please.
