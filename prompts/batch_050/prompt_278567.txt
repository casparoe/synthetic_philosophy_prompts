I'm a postdoc who co-teaches a second-year undergraduate course on rational choice and social philosophy, about 24 students, and next week we have a single 80-minute seminar slot on the evolution of cooperation. In past years I just described Axelrod's tournaments and showed the results, and the students nodded along -- Tit-for-Tat is nice, retaliatory, forgiving, clear, end of story. But this year I want them to run into a complication that the standard telling glosses over: the classical tournament assumed players' moves are executed exactly as intended, whereas in real life (and in real biological interactions) attempts to cooperate sometimes misfire -- the signal is sent but read as defection, the favor is attempted but fails. Once you add even a small chance of error, two Tit-for-Tat players who bump into each other can get locked into endless mutual retaliation, and the famous winner starts to look fragile. I'd like the students to discover this themselves, the hard way, before anyone mentions it.

Act as an experienced designer of active-learning exercises for philosophy and game theory courses who has run iterated-dilemma games in classrooms many times and knows where they break down. Think carefully about incentive design before finalizing anything; if it helps, sketch two or three candidate structures and explain briefly why you rejected the losers. Then give me a complete, runnable activity with: (1) an opening phase in which pairs play an iterated prisoner's dilemma with a payoff scheme using points or tokens rather than money, long enough that reciprocal strategies stabilize and students start feeling smug about having "solved" cooperation; (2) a second phase in which you introduce implementation noise by some simple physical mechanism (dice, cards, a spinner -- your choice, but specify exactly how it works and what error rate to use and why), with the crucial feature that players are told only what happened to them, not what the other player intended, so that intent and outcome come apart; (3) rules for how much strategizing and table-talk to allow at each stage, since that decision interacts with what the noise phase teaches; and (4) a debrief plan that maps specific moments from the session onto the theoretical material -- the conditions for cooperation to evolve, why pure retaliation collapses under noise, and what forgiveness or generosity buys you -- while letting the students articulate each point first.

Please also include an honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (for instance: students colluding to split payoffs, someone figuring out the twist early and spoiling it, or the noise mechanism slowing play to a crawl) and how to handle each failure mode. Finally, tell me plainly which feature of the evolutionary story this activity cannot reproduce -- my guess is that a room of 24 people playing for 80 minutes can't show population-level selection across generations, but I'd like your view on what else gets lost and what I should simply tell them in the wrap-up rather than pretend the game demonstrated.
