I teach political philosophy and, through a quirk of departmental staffing, have been asked to deliver two guest lectures in a colleague's jurisprudence module on the legal enforcement of morality. The topic sits just outside my usual territory, so in preparing I picked up a well-reviewed popular introduction to legal philosophy and read its chapter on the Hart–Devlin debate. The chapter closes with a summary I would like to be able to rely on, but two or three things in it nag at me, and I cannot quite put my finger on whether my unease is warranted. The passage runs as follows:

"After the Wolfenden Report of 1957 recommended decriminalizing homosexual acts between consenting adults in private, Lord Devlin argued in a famous lecture that the recommendation rested on a misunderstanding of what holds a society together. A society, he claimed, is constituted by a shared moral code; a threat to that code is a threat to society itself, and a community that lets its common morality lapse will disintegrate as surely as one that tolerates treason. It follows that the criminal law may reach even private immorality whenever the ordinary person feels deep revulsion toward it: the disgust of the reasonable man on the street is the measure of what society cannot be expected to tolerate. H. L. A. Hart replied that Devlin had dressed up majority prejudice as jurisprudence. Drawing on Mill, Hart argued that the sole end for which society may rightfully coerce an unwilling person is to prevent harm to others, so conduct that harms no one is none of the law's business, and what consenting adults do in private harms no one. Hart is generally taken to have won the exchange, and Devlin's position survives chiefly as a cautionary example of legal moralism at its most confident."

My instinct, from graduate seminars I have not revisited in many years, is that the first half of this is broadly right and the distortions, if there are any, are concentrated in the sentences about Devlin's test and Hart's reply — but I am not confident enough to say so in front of students. I would be grateful for a careful audit. Which parts of the passage would a scholar of the debate accept without qualification? Where exactly would Devlin object that his standard has been rendered cruder than it was, and does that objection matter to whether his view is defensible? Where would Hart object that his actual response to the social-disintegration claim has been replaced by a simpler argument he did not quite make — and what was that response? Finally, once the summary is corrected, does its closing verdict that Hart won follow from the accurate parts, or is that itself an overstatement the corrected account cannot support?
