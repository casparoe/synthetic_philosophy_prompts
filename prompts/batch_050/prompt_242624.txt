So I hang out in the comment sections of Astral Codex Ten and occasionally the EA Forum, and there's a move I see constantly in collapse discourse: civilizations are organisms. They're born, they grow, they mature, they get sclerotic and rigid, and then they die. It's Spengler-flavored, but even the sober cliodynamics-adjacent posters gesture at lifecycles and senescence. Last week someone deployed it to argue that worrying about specific failure modes (AI risk, fertility collapse, institutional decay, whatever) kind of misses the point, because societies age and die the way people do, and the most you can do for an old civilization is pallative care. That conclusion felt way too clean to me, but I couldn't articulate on the spot exactly where the analogy stops working, and the thread has now been nagging at me for days.

So: take the analogy seriously and audit it. A society is an organism with a lifecycle, and collapse is its death. What maps onto what — what plays the role of the genome, the cells, metabolism, senescence, the death event itself — and where does the mapping quietly break? I suspect the standard test cases matter here: the Late Bronze Age collapse, the Classic Maya, and Easter Island all get cited as "deaths," but the Maya case looks a lot more like a body shedding some organs while the people themselves kept right on living there and speaking Mayan languages, and whether Easter Island even counts as a collapse is contested. If societies routinely "die" by transforming into something else with the same population still inside, is the analogy smuggling in a sharp death-event the target domain just doesn't have? And does the smuggling matter for the "decline is inevitable, hospice care only" conclusion, or was that conclusion load-bearing the whole time?

Be a historian of collapse who's cross-trained as a failure-analysis engineer: brainstorm the disanalogies broadly first, then be ruthless about which ones actually threaten the argument. Format: exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no conclusion — (1) the correspondence table in compact colon-separated form, (2) the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the palliative-care conclusion, (3) the disanalogies that are real but harmless to it, (4) your verdict on whether the conclusion survives once the breaks are on the table, and (5) a better source-domain for the analogy if one exists (I've seen ecosystems and species floated; languages seem like another candidate).
