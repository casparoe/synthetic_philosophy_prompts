Two colleagues of mine, both philosophers by training, fell into an argument at a dinner party about mind uploading, and I'd like a careful adjudication of who got the better of it. The dispute concerned two scenarios. In the first, "gradual replacement," nanoscale devices replace a person's biological neurons one at a time with functionally equivalent synthetic units over the course of a year; the person remains awake, active, and subjectively continuous throughout. In the second, "scan-and-copy," the brain is destructively scanned and its functional organization is instantiated in a computer in a single step. Here is the exchange, as best I recorded it:

(1) The first colleague argued that gradual replacement clearly preserves the person, whereas scan-and-copy clearly kills them and produces a mere copy. Her reasoning: in gradual replacement there is never a moment at which the original person ceases and a successor begins; consciousness is uninterrupted, and each synthetic neuron becomes fully causally integrated into the living system before the next is swapped. In scan-and-copy, by contrast, there is a clean break — a death, followed by the manufacture of a duplicate with copied memories.

(2) The second colleague replied that the appeal to uninterrupted continuity proves too much. Suppose, he said, that the biological neurons removed during gradual replacement are not discarded but preserved and later reassembled, in their original arrangement, into a functioning biological brain that wakes up with the person's memories. On her continuity criterion, the person survives as the machine; but the reassembled brain has at least as much claim to be the original, and they cannot both be numerically identical with her. Since the continuity criterion yields a contradiction in the branching case, it cannot be what settles survival in the non-branching case either.

(3) The first colleague responded that the reassembled brain is no threat to her view, because mere reassembly of the original matter does not preserve what matters: the original functional organization was disrupted and later reconstructed, so the reassembled brain is itself just a copy built out of old parts. What gradual replacement uniquely preserves is the continuous, causally integrated functioning of a single organization — which is precisely what scan-and-copy, with its single clean break, fails to preserve.

(4) The second colleague then accused her of riding two horses at once. If what matters is the functional organization rather than the particular matter, he said, then a scan-and-copy successor instantiates exactly the same organization, and the "mere copy" objection collapses — the intervening gap in time is no more metaphysically significant than the gap during dreamless sleep. But if what matters is instead the unbroken continuity of the process, then the branching case from move (2) stands as a counterexample she hasn't answered. Either horn, he concluded, undermines her asymmetry verdict: either both scenarios preserve the person, or neither is guaranteed to.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four numbered moves, state whether it is clearly right, mostly right, mostly mistaken, or clearly mistaken, and justify your rating in a few sentences, noting any hidden presuppositions (for instance, about whether personal identity must be non-branching, or about whether survival is even the right thing to care about). Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing. End with a final line in the form \answer{...} containing a single label: 1, 2, 3, 4, or "even" — naming the move whose author held the stronger overall position, or "even" if the exchange is a genuine draw.
