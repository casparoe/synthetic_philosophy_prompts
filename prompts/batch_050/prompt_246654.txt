I keep getting into arguments with a friend about how historians pick out "the cause" of big events, so let me just dump my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and ask whether there's a principle underneath them.

1. Franz Ferdinand and WWI. I think the war was coming anyway within a few years (alliances, arms race, mobilization timetables), so I don't want to call the assassination the cause of WWI. But I do think it explains why war broke out in summer 1914 specifically, and with Austria firing the first shot. So: cause of the timing and shape, not of the occurrence.

2. Two fires — one arson, one lightning strike — converge and burn down a barn, and either alone would have done the job. I say both fires are causes of the barn burning, even though deleting either one changes nothing. My friend says neither is "the" cause for exactly that reason, and I'm fairly sure he's wrong but can't say why.

3. The French Revolution: bread prices, state bankruptcy, Enlightenment ideas, a weak king. I think calling this "overdetermined, like case 2" is a cop-out. Bad harvests and broke monarchies happen all the time without revolutions, so no single item looks sufficient to me. It's not two converging fires; it's one package with redundancy inside it, and you can't subtract pieces one at a time to check.

4. "Rome fell because of lead poisoning." I want to reject this, but not because lead did no damage — more like the proposed cause is at the wrong grain for the explanandum, too small a lever for too big an effect? I can't articulate this one well.

5. Now flip it: imagine an assassination in 1963 that genuinely tips a balanced situation, such that without it there's no nuclear exchange and with it there is. Here I have zero problem saying the assassin caused WWIII. But that's weird, because in case 1 the loaded background is exactly what made me deny the trigger was the cause. Cases 1 and 5 pull in opposite directions and I assume one of them has to go.

So: is there one principle of causal selection in historical explanation that captures all of these, or close? A pure counterfactual "but for" test kills case 2; an "any contributing condition counts" test makes case 4 impossible to reject. If the honest answer is that I need two principles — one for "why did it happen at all" and one for "why then, in that form" — then say that and sort my verdicts under each. Either way, tell me what the resulting principle predicts for a case I haven't listed (say, the 2008 financial crisis: housing bubble, ratings agencies, deregulation, global savings glut), and be straight with me about how confident you are that it generalizes beyond my five cases — including the one verdict you think you had to bend hardest to fit.
