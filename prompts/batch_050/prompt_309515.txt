For a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, please write a decision tree that takes a student from the starting commitment "a rational agent who knows the objective chance of an outcome should set her credence equal to it" to a named position in the literature on the Principal Principle and deference to objective chance.

Requirements:

1. The tree should have between six and nine branch nodes, and every branch node must be phrased as a question the student can answer without knowing which position it leads to. At least two of the nodes must be posed as concrete scenarios rather than abstract theses. One should be an undermining-style case (for example, an agent who receives reliably truthful information about her own future from an oracle, so that the chance of the event is not one but her evidence settles it), and one should pit candidate chance-functions against each other in a way that forces a choice about what counts as admissible information.

2. The branch nodes should be built from the logical space of the debate, not from a list of names. Among the distinctions the tree must actually use: whether the principle is a conceptual truth that partially defines what chance is or a substantive norm constraining credence toward an independently understood magnitude; whether undermining shows the original principle needs restriction or replacement; whether the defense of the principle is pragmatic or accuracy-based rather than Humean; and whether objective chance exists at all or the appearance of deference is to be explained away subjectively (for instance, via symmetry constraints on credence).

3. Leaves must include: the original Principal Principle, the New Principle response to undermining, the "analyst's" treatment on which deference is constitutive of chance, Hoefer-style revisions of the expert credence that chance sets, an accuracy-dominance defense of deference, a non-Humean governing-chance view, and the subjectivist denial that there is any objective chance to defer to. If your tree produces leaves that no one defends, mark them as unoccupied and diagnose briefly whether the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored.

4. Flag explicitly any pair of named positions your tree cannot separate from the answers given, and say in one sentence what further question would split them.

5. Each leaf should carry the name of the position and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment -- the cost a defender must swallow, not a neutral summary.

Format: begin with a single sentence stating what the tree classifies, then present the tree itself, with no preface, no restating of the task, and no closing remarks. Write in American English.
