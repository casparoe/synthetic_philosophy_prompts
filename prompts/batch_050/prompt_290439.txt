You are a philosopher of language with deep expertise in the semantics of vagueness, writing for a colleague who has thought about these issues seriously but wants to stress-test a settled conviction. Take your time: before composing your answer, brainstorm a broad list of candidate considerations — formal, empirical, and dialectical — and then select and organize the most instructive ones rather than answering off the top of your head.

Here is the position I want stress-tested. I hold epistemicism about vagueness: vague predicates have perfectly sharp classical boundaries, and vagueness consists entirely in our principled ignorance of where those boundaries fall, grounded in something like the margin-for-error structure of our semantic knowledge. I accept the standard consequences — bivalence everywhere, the truth of every instance of the sorites conditional's negation, and the existence of a last heap-removal grain — and I take the usual epistemicist defenses (the unknowability of boundary facts as a special case of safety constraints on knowledge) to be adequate.

Tell me what would count against this view. Specifically:

1. Which formal results would damage it? For example, would a proof that margin-for-error principles, classical logic, and some plausible constraint on semantic supervenience are jointly inconsistent be decisive, or would the epistemicist simply have room to deny the supervenience constraint? Work through what such a result would have to look like to be fatal rather than merely inconvenient.

2. Consider two hypothetical empirical findings I have in mind. First: suppose careful psycholinguistic work established that competent speakers, under ideal conditions with unlimited time, converge on a stable boundary for a predicate like "heap" — but the location of the boundary drifts systematically with features everyone agrees are semantically irrelevant (the order in which test items were presented, say). Second: suppose instead it turned out that speakers' dispositions to apply vague predicates are best modeled by a graded, degree-theoretic competence with no sharp threshold anywhere in the underlying psychology. For each finding, tell me whether it actually counts against epistemicism, given that the view locates the boundary in the language's semantics rather than in any speaker's head — and if a committed epistemicist would shrug both off, say plainly what that implies about the view's empirical exposure.

3. Where does epistemicism risk collapsing into a rival? In particular, is there a way of spelling out the ignorance story so thinly that it becomes notational variants of supervaluationism, or so thickly that it becomes indistinguishable from the claim that there simply are no boundaries (nihilism)? Which commitments keep it a genuinely distinct position?

4. Which defeaters would epistemicism's own defenders concede as real threats, as opposed to objections they regard as merely restating the intuition that sharp boundaries are absurd? Rank the threats you identify from fatal to merely awkward, and for the merely awkward ones, explain why they stay merely awkward.

Finally, give me an honest verdict on whether epistemicism, as standardly defended, has been qualified into something close to unfalsifiability — every apparent counterexample absorbed as further evidence of our ignorance — and, if so, whether that is actually a vice for a view of this kind or just the normal condition of a metaphysical position. I will be most persuaded by considerations that the view's own machinery makes it impossible to evade, not by bare appeals to the intuitive strangeness of sharp cutoffs.
