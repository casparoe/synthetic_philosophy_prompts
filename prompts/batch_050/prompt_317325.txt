I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy, writing a dissertation on the ethics of deception, and this academic year I serve as the graduate-student representative on my university's Institutional Review Board. At our next meeting the board votes on Protocol 26-041, a social-psychology study of how people respond to moral criticism of their own work. Participants write a short personal essay and are told that an anonymous peer will evaluate it; the experimenter then informs each participant that the peer judged the essay "morally objectionable." No peer exists, and the essays go unread. Participants are debriefed within twenty minutes, receive a follow-up letter a week later, and may withdraw their data. The investigators argue the manipulation only works if participants believe the feedback, that suspicion probes would destroy the effect, and that debriefed deception of this mild kind is standard practice in their field. The board is split. One faction regards the protocol as routine, since the consent form already warns that some details of the study will be withheld. The other insists there is a difference between withholding the hypothesis and having the experimenter assert a falsehood to the participant's face, and one member warns that we will contaminate our undergraduate participant pool: students who learn that experimenters lie will stop believing instructions in every future study.

While preparing, I returned to Augustine's treatise Against Lying (Contra Mendacium, c. 420 AD), occasioned by the question of whether Catholics might pose as Priscillianist heretics in order to infiltrate and expose them. Augustine refuses, writing in chapter 1:

But I am not persuaded that it is right to unearth them out of their hiding places by our telling lies. ... How then by a lie shall I rightly be able to prosecute lies? Or is it by robbery that robberies and by sacrilege that sacrileges, and by adultery that adulteries, are to be prosecuted?

Please draft the decision memo (roughly 700–900 words) that I will circulate to the board before the vote. It should open with a clear bottom-line recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and defend it. It must give the board criteria applicable to future protocols, in particular a principled line between withholding information from participants and asserting falsehoods to them (Augustine himself relies on a distinction between keeping silent about what is true and saying what one believes to be false, and the memo should say whether that distinction survives translation into policy). It must engage the quoted passage directly rather than decoratively: state what Augustine's argument is, test whether the analogy transfers — the researchers are not prosecuting liars, but they are using falsehood to bring hidden truths about people into the open, which is close to the infiltration case Augustine rejects — and consider whether an argument Augustine grounds in his theology can be restated in secular institutional terms, such as the credibility of consent forms and the long-run trustworthiness of experimenters' testimony, or whether it collapses without those premises. Finally, give the strongest version of the losing side, whichever side that turns out to be: the investigators' claim of methodological necessity, or the strict faction's claim that a consent form one is licensed to betray is not consent at all.
