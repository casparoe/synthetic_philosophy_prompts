I serve on the governance committee of a worker-owned firm, and next month we vote on a proposal to settle certain decisions through conditional prediction markets: when a decision is on the table, participants bet on a pre-registered success metric conditional on each option being adopted, and the option the market prices most favorably is the one we take. I need to decide how to vote, and I keep catching myself reacting inconsistently. Here are my verdicts on the kinds of decisions the proposal might cover, and I want to know what principle, if any, underlies them.

Choosing between two inventory-management systems, with the metric being stockout rates over the following year: clearly fine. Choosing which of two job applicants to hire, with the metric being the new hire's one-year performance review: not fine, though I struggle to say why. Choosing whether to launch a new product line, with the metric being next year's revenue: fine. Choosing whether to dismiss a senior manager, with the metric being the firm's revenue over the following year: not fine — and this one troubles me, since the metric is every bit as measurable as in the product-line case. Choosing which of two outside researchers' grant proposals to fund, with the metric being published output after three years: fine, even though I notice this is also a bet on a person's future performance. Choosing whether the firm should issue a public apology for a past decision, with the metric being member satisfaction next quarter: not fine — the market there seems merely to be predicting our own later mood rather than telling us anything we couldn't reach by deliberation.

My tentative principle was that market settlement is appropriate whenever a decision's success can be measured independently of the decision itself. But the dismissal and grant cases appear to pass that test equally while I judge them oppositely, and the apology case fails it for a reason I cannot articulate. So: state a principle, or two candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, identifying which case motivates each clause; say plainly which of my verdicts cannot be saved and ought to be revised; and tell me what the resulting principle implies for the question actually before our committee — whether it would be appropriate to use such a market to decide whether the firm should adopt market-based decision-making more broadly.
