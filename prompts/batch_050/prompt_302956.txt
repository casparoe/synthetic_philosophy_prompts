Bit of a niche one, bear with me. I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a story about a local court case that's got me properly stuck.

A shopkeeper in the town I cover has been convicted and fined because a 17-year-old bought a can of beer in his shop using a fake ID that was, by all accounts, genuinely convincing — the officer who brought the prosectution admitted on the stand he'd probably have been fooled by it himself. The shopkeeper had trained his staff, put up signs, kept a refusals log, the lot. Nobody is claiming he was careless. The court's position was essentially: none of that matters, the sale happened, end of.

My editor wants me to write it up as an open-and-shut injustice — punishing a blameless man. But I interviewed a lawyer who defends this kind of law, and her argument genuinely made me pause. She said that if prosecutors had to prove carelessness or knowledge in every case, these laws would be basically unenforceable, that shopkeepers as a group would have less reason to be careful in the first place, and that a modest fine isn't the same thing as branding someone a bad person.

So who's right? I don't want legal advice — I want the philosophy of it. Does punishing someone who took every reasonable precaution do something objectionable that punishing a careless person doesn't, and if so, what exactly is the objectionable part? Is my lawyer friend's argument about enforceability and incentives a good enough reason, or does it amount to using this one guy as an instrument for scaring other people into compliance? And what do we make of her claim that a fine isn't really "punishment" in the full sense — does the stigma of a conviction matter even when, as here, literally nobody in the community thinks worse of him?

I'd also love to know whether there's a principled line to be drawn between cases like this and cases where most people seem happy to hold someone responsible without fault (people seem much less squeamish about, say, companies being on the hook for pollution they didn't know about) — or whether that inconsistency is just us being muddled.

Please answer in British English, and don't be afraid to go into the weeds — I want the actual fault lines of the debate, not just a verdict.
