I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last two years working through philosophy, lately the formal theory of rational choice, with a small reading group of fellow amateurs. We have reached bargaining theory, and I find myself on the Kalai–Smorodinsky side of the Nash/Kalai–Smorodinsky divide. To test my conviction I would like you to spar with me as a committed defender of the Nash bargaining solution: someone who regards Nash's four axioms — Pareto optimality, symmetry, invariance under positive affine transformations of utility, and independence of irrelevant alternatives — as close to self-evident, who thinks the independence axiom in particular is the deepest of the four, and who suspects that monotonicity-based objections are seductive but ultimately confused. You are sharp, not a strawman: you may concede small points, acknowledge that a counterexample is genuine, and grant that a desideratum has intuitive pull, but you never abandon the Nash solution itself, and you are ready with the standard resources — the uniqueness of the axiomatic characterization, the thought that a solution ought to depend only on the relevant alternatives, and worries about what anchors the Kalai–Smorodinsky rule. Stay in character until I write "stop," answer me directly rather than lecturing, and keep every reply to a single paragraph of at most 150 words so this feels like a real exchange.

Here is my opening move. Consider a two-player problem with disagreement point (0,0) whose feasible utility pairs form the convex hull, with free disposal, of (3,0), (2,1), and (0,1). Maximizing the product of the gains, your solution selects (2,1). Now suppose the menu expands — nothing is removed — so that player 1 can additionally secure his maximum utility of 3 while player 2 gets 0.9; the frontier acquires a short edge from (3,0.9) toward (2,1). Your solution now selects (3,0.9): player 2 is made strictly worse off, from 1 down to 0.9, even though no option was taken away and player 1's best possible outcome never changed. Kalai and Smorodinsky's proportional rule, keyed to each player's ideal point, instead raises player 2's share slightly, as their monotonicity requirement demands. So: how can a rule of rational division punish a bargainer when the feasible set has only grown, and why should I trust the independence of irrelevant alternatives over the elementary demand that expanding opportunities not hurt anyone?
