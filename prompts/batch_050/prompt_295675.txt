For several years I have been drafting a novel set during the revolution of an unnamed country — a composite of the French original and of other upheavals — and I have reached the point in the manuscript where my revolutionaries must either consolidate their victory or begin arresting one another. At a writers' retreat I mentioned that I was leaning toward the former: I wanted my characters, having won, to hold their coalition together, and I wondered whether history gave me any license for it. A fellow novelist dismissed the question with the old line that revolutions devour their children, as though that settled the matter of what my characters could plausibly do.

I am not satisfied, but I cannot quite say why, because I cannot tell what the sentence is even asserting. Would you separate out the distinct claims it might be making? I dimly perceive several candidates: a bare statistical observation about the fate of revolutionary leaders; a sociological thesis about mechanisms — radicalization contests, factional security dilemmas, the logic of denunciation — that operate whatever the actors intend; a psychological thesis about how the rhetoric of virtue disposes people to violence; a thesis that applies specifically to revolutions animated by ideology as opposed to class interest; or perhaps only a fatalistic metaphor with no mechanism behind it at all. For each reading, I would like to know whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or something closer to false; whether the French case genuinely supports it, given the standing quarrel between those who read the Terror as the working-out of class dynamics, those who lay it at the door of ideology, and those who insist on contingency; and — most important for my purposes — which reading would have to be true for my fellow novelist's dismissal to be warranted. If the strongest true reading still leaves my characters room to have acted otherwise, I should like to know that as well, since it will determine the second half of the book.
