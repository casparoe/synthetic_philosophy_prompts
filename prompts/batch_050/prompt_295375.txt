I've been thinking about the dirty hands problem in politics on my own, and I've come around to a skeptical position: I don't believe there are genuine dirty-hands cases. My reasoning is that an official's action is either justified all things considered — they should lie to the aggressor, bribe the warlord, sacrifice the few — in which case they did the right thing and their hands are clean, or it wasn't justified, in which case they simply did wrong and are culpable in the ordinary way. The idea of an act one both ought to perform and ought to feel guilty about strikes me as an incoherent leftover, and the guilt itself as at most a useful emotional check on power, not a sign of some moral debt still outstanding. Call my view the claim that moral ledgers always balance to zero.

What would count against this? I'd like a map of its exposure: which cases resist being redescribed in my either/or fashion; which phenomena the view must explain away rather than accommodate — the fittingness of remorse, officials apologizing or making amends for decisions they'd make again, our preference for leaders who dirty their hands reluctantly rather than easily — and how costly each explaining-away is; and which commitments in adjacent territory, about moral dilemmas in general, about role obligations, about what guilt actually tracks, the position stands or falls with. Please sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and note for each whether a committed skeptic would concede it or dismiss it as question-begging. Finally, if the position rests on a confused premise — say, an equivocation between being right all things considered and involving no wrongdoing — tell me so directly rather than working around it politely.
