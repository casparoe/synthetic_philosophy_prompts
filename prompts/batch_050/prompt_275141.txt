Next term I am running a graduate seminar on mathematical explanation in the natural sciences, and I intend to open it by distributing a specification before the students read a single page of the literature. Please write that specification for me: the desiderata an adequate philosophical theory of mathematical explanation must meet, with the case or consideration motivating each one, and each marked as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp.

Fix the purpose first: I want a theory good enough to serve as a marking scheme — when a scientist says that the mathematics explains some phenomenon, the theory must tell us what would make that assertion true or false.

The list must at minimum adjudicate four benchmark cases. First, the periodical cicadas, whose life cycles of thirteen and seventeen years are prime, where the primality is widely held to do explanatory work in protecting them from predators with periodic life cycles of their own. Secondly, the honeycomb: bees build hexagonal cells, and it is a theorem that the regular hexagonal tiling partitions the plane into equal areas with least perimeter — the live question being whether the theorem explains the bees' architecture or merely constrains it, with natural selection doing the explaining. Thirdly, the Königsberg bridges, where a fact of graph theory explains why no walk crosses each bridge exactly once. Fourthly, a contrast case of my own devising: the meteorologist who calculates the year's total rainfall as 1,207 millimetres, where the arithmetic is indispensable to the statement but explains nothing. A theory that cannot keep the first three cases on one side of a line and the fourth on the other has fallen at the first fence.

Beyond the cases, the list should contain requirements concerning: the asymmetry of explanation, and whether mathematical explanations inherit it; the distinction between explaining with mathematics and merely describing in mathematical terms; whether the account must extend to explanation within mathematics itself or may restrict itself to physical explananda; and whether mathematics that is strictly false of the target system — continuous fluids, infinite populations — can nonetheless explain. Finally, I want a requirement addressing the theory's bearing on the indispensability argument for mathematical realism: does explanatory indispensability licence ontological conclusions that mere indispensability does not, and can a nominalist deploy the account without self-stultification? I suspect that 'take scientific practice at face value' and 'remain neutral between realism and nominalism' cannot be jointly satisfied; where the list generates such conflicts, exhibit them rather than papering over them.

Close with two or three sentences each on how the main families of account fare against the list — those locating the explanatory force in the mathematics itself, those treating mathematics as representing modal structure in the world, and unificationist approaches — and identify which single requirement excludes the most contenders. Please write your answer in British English.
