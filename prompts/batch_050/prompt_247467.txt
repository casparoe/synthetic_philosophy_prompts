I am a philosophy professor who, somewhat against my better judgment, agreed last fall to chair my university's Academic Integrity Board. My term began in January, and the first case of the year is also the hardest one I expect us to see — and since it is our first case of its kind, whatever we do will quietly become policy. I need help drafting the decision memo I will circulate to my five board colleagues before we meet.

The case. A senior, an international student whose first language is not English, stands accused of submitting an AI-written essay in a seminar. The entire evidence against her is the output of a commercial text classifier: it flags her essay as machine-authored, and the vendor's validation studies report a 2% false-positive rate — studies conducted on native-English academic prose. The student flatly denies it. She says she wrote the essay in one sitting on a library computer and kept no notes, drafts, or version history, so there is nothing on her side either. Her instructor adds that the essay does not sound like her earlier work, but concedes she had only ever read two of the student's papers. Our standard of proof is "clear and convincing evidence," a phrase our policy nowhere defines. A finding against her means a transcript notation and likely consequences for her visa status, so we are not eager to be wrong in either direction.

Here is why I am writing to you rather than only to my board. While preparing, I fell into the philosophical literature on statistical proof — the gatecrasher and blue bus hypotheticals and the general puzzle of so-called naked statistical evidence. I am now convinced our case is one of those puzzles wearing modern clothes, but I cannot sort out which of its features are doing the real work. Is the problem that the evidence is statistical at all? That the 2% figure was estimated on the wrong population for this student? That an aggregate error rate does not translate into a probability about this essay? Or that she has been left with no realistic way to rebut it — she cannot produce drafts that never existed? And does the instructor's thin, individualized impression redeem the statistics, the way a witness might, or does it merely attach an unreliable witness to a naked number?

Please do the following. Take on the role of a professor of evidence law with a long-standing side career in the philosophy of proof, retained as the board's outside adviser. Before drafting anything, work through at least three defensible positions the board could take (for instance: find her responsible on the classifier output alone; find her not responsible because classifier output can never satisfy the standard by itself; find her responsible only because the classifier plus the instructor's testimony together suffice), state the strongest objection to each, and then commit to one. Then write the memo. It must: (1) give a clear bottom-line recommendation with a motion the board can actually vote on; (2) explain precisely which features of our evidence map onto the gatecrasher-style problem and which do not, for readers who are smart but neither lawyers nor statisticians — no bare name-dropping of the literature; (3) address the reference-class worry and whether a validation false-positive rate can be read as a probability of guilt about this student at all; (4) say what "clear and convincing" should mean for us, and whether any quantified error rate, standing alone, could satisfy it; (5) take seriously the strongest argument on the losing side — in particular, if we demand individualized proof here, why do other tribunals comfortably accept eyewitness identifications with worse measured error rates; and (6) close with a short general rule for future classifier-only cases, including what would count as adequate corroboration and whether requiring the student to produce a supervised writing sample would itself be genuine evidence or just another statistic wearing a lab coat.

Keep the memo to roughly 900–1,200 words, formal but readable aloud at a meeting. If anything in my description of the evidence is ambiguous in a way that changes the right answer, flag it explicitly rather than resolving it silently.
