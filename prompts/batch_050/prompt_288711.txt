I’m a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I’ve been developing a training session for early-career reporters and researchers on how claims about the distant past are actually established — a topic I keep encountering in my reporting, from disputed dates of archaeological finds to the habits historians inherit from Herodotus and Thucydides. I want the session to run as a game rather than a lecture, because I’ve noticed that people nod along to warnings about unreliable sources and then immediately treat two agreeing accounts as confirmation.

Design a runnable activity for me with the following specifications. The group is 16–20 participants, split into teams of four, with about 100 minutes available. The centerpiece should be an invented historical episode of your devising (something plausibly ancient — a burned archive, a contested succession, a vanished colony) that participants must reconstruct from a dossier of conflicting “sources”: later chronicles, a fragmentary inscription, a hostile poem, a traveler’s hearsay report, and an ambiguous excavation summary. Build in at least two staged reveals that arrive only after teams have committed in writing to a first reconstruction: one in which two apparently independent accounts turn out to share a common source, and one in which a source’s conspicuous silence on some point turns out to have a motive. The point is that teams should experience the collapse of corroboration and the seduction of the argument from silence themselves, in real time, before either is named.

The prompt for your design should include: the full materials list and what each document says (write them, don’t just describe them); the rules and timeline, including how and when teams commit to reconstructions and how the reveals are administered; a small scoring or stakes mechanism that rewards calibrated confidence rather than confident storytelling; a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the activity onto the working concepts of source criticism — independence versus contamination of the tradition, the difference between a biased source and an unreliable one, what a silence can and cannot show — and that connects these to the contrasting reputations of Herodotus and Thucydides without requiring participants to have read either; the two or three ways this activity will predictably go wrong when actually run (teams treating it as a puzzle with a hidden right answer, for instance) and how the facilitator should respond; and a candid note on what the activity cannot reproduce — most obviously, that in real source criticism there is no facilitator holding a sealed envelope with the truth inside, and no reveals — plus how the facilitator should supply that gap in discussion.

Write it as a complete session plan I could hand to a facilitator.
