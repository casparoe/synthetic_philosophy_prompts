In late 1991 the Soviet Union ceased to exist, and it did so in a way almost no one had forecast: through the mid-1980s the overwhelming consensus among Sovietologists, intelligence analysts, and political scientists was that the USSR was a durable, if sclerotic, fixture of world politics, and the handful of voices predicting collapse were dismissed as cranks. Analyze this forecasting failure through the lens of reference-class forecasting and the outside view. First, work out what forecast the outside view would actually have produced in, say, 1985, and show how much it depends on the reference class chosen: the base rate for great powers surviving the next decade is very different from that for multi-ethnic empires, which is different again from that for single-party regimes more than six decades old. Second, adjudicate what the episode shows about the method itself: is the collapse evidence that the outside view fails at exactly the moments that matter most, since regime changes are almost by definition breaks with the base rate, or is it evidence that forecasters failed the method by anchoring on the flattering reference class and then reasoning from the inside view? Third, address the deeper problem the case exposes: any episode belongs to indefinitely many reference classes, and history does not label the right one in advance. Does the Soviet case yield any principled procedure for choosing among classes, or does it suggest that reference-class selection is where all the judgment, and all the bias, in forecasting actually hides? Keep the analysis disciplined about the mapping, and end by saying what, if anything, the episode licenses us to conclude about the confident longevity forecasts currently made on behalf of existing states.
