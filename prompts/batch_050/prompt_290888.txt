I am a retired molecular biologist who, after forty years in the laboratory, has spent the past two years auditing philosophy seminars at my local university. Our reading group is currently working through theories of concepts, and each member has been asked to circulate a short "philosophical profile" -- a list of commitments we actually hold -- so that the others can try to take it apart before the meeting. I would rather have my profile stress-tested here first, in writing, so that I arrive knowing where the soft spots are.

Here is roughly where I have landed. (1) For most everyday categories -- birds, furniture, tools -- I think categorization works by similarity to a prototype, which is why a robin is a better bird than a penguin and why borderline cases feel genuinely borderline rather than merely unknown. (2) I think concepts must be compositional: the only way we can understand an unlimited stock of novel phrases like "purple pet fish" is if the meaning of the whole is built up from the meanings of the parts, so the concept PET FISH has to be a function of PET and FISH. (3) Having watched scientific vocabularies evolve, I am convinced that a concept is partly individuated by its role in the theory that embeds it -- the concept of mass in Newtonian mechanics is what it is because of the inferences and laws it participates in, and I suspect something similar holds for lay concepts like HEAT or LIVING THING. (4) At the same time, I want to say that people operating with very different theories can still share a concept and genuinely disagree about the same thing: a child and a chemist can argue about whether some grayish nugget is gold, and Einstein was correcting Newton rather than simply changing the subject. (5) I am also drawn to the idea that we store memories of particular encountered instances -- specific dogs, specific chairs -- and that categorizing a new case is a matter of comparing it to those stored exemplars, and I take it this is simply a rival to the prototype picture in (1), so that at most one of the two can be correct as a general story.

Please audit this set rather than lecture me. For every pair of commitments that genuinely pulls apart, state the tension precisely, rate its severity, and identify the cheapest revision that resolves it along with what that revision costs. Please also tell me which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection -- I would like to know where I am worrying unnecessarily, not only where I should worry. Finally, if a single revision would clean up the largest number of problems at once, name it, though I have deliberately not indicated which commitment I am least attached to, and I would like your judgment about that rather than a guess about mine. Kindly write the analysis in American English.
