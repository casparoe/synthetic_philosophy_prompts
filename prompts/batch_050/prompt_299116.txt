I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about how ordinary people actually make decisions when they genuinely have no idea what the probabilities are -- think picking a career, moving countries, deciding whether to have kids, that kind of thing. While reporting it I noticed that everyone, including me, leans on the proverb "hope for the best, prepare for the worst," and I want a section that takes it seriously instead of just treating it as a cute saying. The thing is, the more I stare at it the more it seems to fall apart. Hoping for the best sounds like it tells you to pick the option with the best possible outcome, and preparing for the worst sounds like it tells you to pick the option whose worst case is least bad, and those two recommendations can point at completely different options. Like, if one job offer could make me rich but might leave me unemployed in six months, and another is stable but capped and boring, "hope for the best" says take the first and "prepare for the worst" says take the second. So which is it, proverb?

Here's what I'd like from you. Unpack the slogan into the distinct precise decision rules it could be recommending. I have a vague sense that "prepare for the worst" maps onto something like maximin -- rank options by their worst outcomes and take the one whose floor is highest -- and that "hope for the best" maps onto maximax, but maybe the proverb is really recommending a mixture, like weight the best and worst cases and ignore the middle, which I think some old decision theorist actually proposed as an optimism-pessimism index. And I keep hearing about minimax regret as a third thing that doesn't fit neatly into either half, since it's about how much you'd kick yourself rather than about good or bad outcomes directly. Lay out these readings (and any others you think the proverb could mean), and for each one say: what exactly the rule is, in what choice situations it diverges from the others, and what its most embarrassing failure case is -- I assume each of these rules has some scenario where following it looks clearly insane. Then the part I actually need for the piece: which reading is probably doing the work when a normal person says the proverb while deliberating, say, about buying earthquake insurance or taking a startup job? My hunch is people smuggle in probabilities the whole time and the "ignorance" framing is partly an illusion, so if you think that's right, argue it, and if it's wrong, tell me why.

One more ask, since this is going in print and I don't want to overstate anything: flag how confident you are in each of the main claims you make -- like whether the formal rules really are the natural precisifications of the proverb halves, and whether the empirical guess about what people mean is supported by anything more solid than intuition -- and at the end, single out the single claim in your answer you're least sure of and say what would change your mind about it.
