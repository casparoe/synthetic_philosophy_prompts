I'm writing a paper for my philosophy elective on a tension I keep noticing. In debate class, the tu quoque move -- answering criticism with "you do it too" -- is taught as a plain fallacy: the speaker's own conduct has no bearing on whether the criticism is true. Outside the classroom, though, the same move is treated as decisive. When a classmate who had been suspended for cheating gave a speech at our honor-code assembly, nobody even engaged with his arguments; the whole room wrote him off on the spot. And notably, the identical criticism from someone with clean hands lands completely differently, so our everyday practice seems to track who is speaking, not just what is said.

What theory of blame does that practice presuppose that the logic-class picture denies? Reconstruct the implicit view -- perhaps blame is a claim to a kind of authority or standing that a hypocrite forfeits, rather than a statement of fact -- and say which rival conceptions of blame (blame as information, as punishment, as the expression of a relationship) the practice rules out, and which it merely happens to fit. Where is the practice underdetermined: must the blamer actually be guilty of the same fault, or is condemning others for what one secretly does enough to lose standing? Finally, what would everyday moral life have to look like if we replaced the implicit theory with the view that blame is nothing more than true accusation?
