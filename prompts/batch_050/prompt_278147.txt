I am taking a seminar on Scanlon's contractualism this term, and for a revision handout I would like a taxonomy of the positions that contractualists and their critics have taken on the aggregation problem: the question of whether, and when, the number of people who stand to gain or lose from an action may count against an individual's complaint, given Scanlon's individualist restriction that a principle must be justifiable to each person separately.

Please present the taxonomy as a decision tree of five to eight branching questions, each node phrased as a question a reader could answer on the basis of concrete cases alone, without already knowing which position it leads to. Build the tree from the logical space first, and only then locate the actual positions within it. Splits worth considering include: whether smaller claims may ever be summed so as to outweigh a larger one; whether a claim counts only if it would survive a pairwise contest against the strongest claim on the other side; whether a claim that is not really at stake in a comparison can nonetheless break a tie; whether there is a threshold of severity below which numbers begin to matter; and whether the numbers matter intrinsically or only via the strength of the individual complaints they comprise.

The leaves should include at minimum: strict non-aggregation in Taurek's style, where each person's claim competes on its own and equal claims are settled by an equal chance; Scanlon's own tie-breaking treatment, with its exclusion of claims that are irrelevant to the comparison at hand; views permitting aggregation only among claims of equal or comparable strength; partial or weighted aggregation, in which sufficiently many smaller claims can jointly outweigh a larger one; and any prominent threshold position. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, mark that leaf as unnamed and say briefly whether the gap exists because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Where the tree cannot separate two named positions, flag that explicitly rather than inventing a distinction.

Before including any branching question, test it against at least one of the following cases and discard it if it does no work: saving one person from death versus saving five strangers stranded elsewhere; saving one person from death versus sparing many people a serious but fully curable harm; rescuing one person where the rescue would impose a grave injury on an uninvolved third party; and a tie between two equally strong claims where a far weaker third claim also hangs in the balance. Each leaf should carry the name of the position or its best-known defender, together with one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. The whole thing should fit on roughly one page.
