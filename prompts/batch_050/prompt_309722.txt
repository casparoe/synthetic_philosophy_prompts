Every country seems to commemorate its past the same way: holidays for battles and independence days, statues of generals and founders, history classes organized around wars, revolutions, and reigns. Nobody has a national holiday for the slow stuff -- the centuries-long draining of marshlands, the gradual rise in literacy, the shift in diet after new crops arrived. A friend of mine half-seriously proposed a public holiday marking the century in which average human height in our country went up by ten centimeters, and everyone at the table laughed, including me, and I couldn't quite say why it was funny.

Here's what bugs me though. When we explain our *own* lives, we almost never talk this way. Ask someone why they're struggling and they'll cite housing prices, deindustrialization, the job market, where they happened to be born -- slow, impersonal structures. We explain the present through structures and the past through people and events. That seems backwards, or at least inconsistent.

So: what theory of historical causation does our commemorative practice actually presuppose? That events and individuals are the real movers of history and slow structures are just background scenery? Or does it presuppose something more modest, like that commemorations aren't explanations at all but social glue, and you can't rally people around a 300-year graph of grain prices? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory, and to be careful about what the practice *strictly* presupposes versus what it merely fits well with -- maybe several rival views of historical causation could license exactly the same holidays and statues, in which case the practice is philosophically underdetermined and my friend's absurd holiday tells us something about us rather than about history.

Also: is the practice internally consistent with how the same societies explain their present? And if you took the slow-structures view seriously as a basis for collective memory, what would commemoration concretely have to look like? Try to come up with at least one or two proposals that aren't just jokes, and say whether their absurdity (if they're still absurd) is a contingent fact about human psychology or something deeper about what commemoration is *for*.
