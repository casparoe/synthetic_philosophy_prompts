Ok so I'm in this philosophy elective at school and we just did Plato's Meno, the part where Socrates is like, why does anyone even care about knowledge when a true opinion gets you to the same place -- his example is basically that a guy who truly believes the road goes to Larissa and a guy who knows it both end up in Larissa. And honestly I thought Socrates was being annoying, but then I tried to figure out what I actually think across a bunch of cases and my answers are all over the place, so I want to know if there's one principle underneath them or if I'm just inconsistent.

Here's my gut on cases. Trivia night: my teammate guesses Canberra is the capital of Australia on a pure hunch, we get the point -- I feel zero difference between that and her actually knowing it. My phone's GPS: it doesn't "know" anything, it's just always right, and I trust it completely, no loss there either. But a doctor who's right about my diagnosis by luck versus one who actually knows? Huge difference, I definately want the second one, even if they'd prescribe the exact same pill. Same with a witness in court who saw the thing versus one who correctly guessed -- feels massive. And then the weird one: if the GPS glitched once I'd probably keep using it, but if my grandma who's driven the route for years got contradicted by the GPS, I'd side with grandma. So knowledge seems... stickier? Like it survives pushback and lucky belief doesn't? But that can't be the whole story because the doctor case matters to me even if both of them are totally confident and neither would ever change their mind. Also, maybe my tentative principle is: knowledge is worth more exactly when being right this once isn't enough -- when you need the belief to hold up under new evidence, or to generalize to new situations, or to justify the answer to someone else. But that seems to break my trivia verdict, since "holds up under new evidence" is also useless there, so knowledge should be worth zero at trivia, and yet I still vaguely feel like knowing things is just... better, even at trivia.

So: is there a single principle that captures all of these, or do I have to drop one of my verdicts (and if so, which one is the most expendable)? And whatever principle you land on -- what does it say about a case I keep thinking about, which is an AI that's always right about medical stuff but doesn't understand anything: is relying on that more like relying on the GPS (fine) or like relying on the lucky doctor (not fine)?
