Last night I had a long and fairly heated argument with a friend about anthropic reasoning — specifically the choice between the self-sampling assumption (SSA) and the self-indication assumption (SIA). My friend is a physicist by training, generally careful, and much better read in this area than I am, but he has a habit of presenting contested ground as settled. Partway through he produced what he called his 'once and for all' statement of the debate, which I reproduce from memory as faithfully as I can:

'There are two rival rules for reasoning about which observer you happen to be. SSA says you should reason as if you were randomly selected from all observers in your reference class who actually exist; SIA says you should reason as if you were randomly selected from all possible observers. The practical upshot is that SIA treats the sheer number of observers a theory predicts as evidence in its favour: between two cosmologies differing only in how many observers they contain, SIA tells you to favour the larger one, in proportion to the number of observers. This is what generates the notorious "presumptuous philosopher" case, due to Bostrom: an SIA reasoner can dismiss, from the armchair and with near-total confidence, any cosmology on which the universe contains only a modest finite number of observers, purely on the grounds that rival cosmologies predict more. Bostrom intended this as a reductio of SIA, and the literature has treated it as fatal — nobody has produced a decent reply. Since SIA is the underlying motivation for the "thirder" position in the Sleeping Beauty problem, that position collapses with it, leaving SSA and the halfer view as the last ones standing. Admittedly SSA requires one to pick a reference class, but that is a minor technical wrinkle rather than a serious objection.'

I am inclined to trust him on the definitions, but the claim that 'nobody has produced a decent reply' set off alarm bells, and I have a vague memory that thirders sometimes argue their case without mentioning SIA at all. Could you audit his summary? Which parts are accurate, which are subtle misrepresentations or overstatements, and what would a defender of SIA (or of thirding) say in response? Most importantly: does his conclusion — that the debate is effectively over and halferism wins by default — actually follow once the summary is corrected? Are there comparably embarrassing consequences on the SSA side that he has conveniently left out?
