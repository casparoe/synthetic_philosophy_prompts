I'm a PhD student in philosophy, working on philosophy of mathematics, and I'd like you to referee a dispute I witnessed between two colleagues in my cohort — call them Mara and Dev — over whether Hilbert's hotel gives us any reason to doubt actual infinity. Both are competent: Mara leans finitist, Dev is an unapologetic realist about the infinite. The exchange ran roughly as follows, and I've reconstructed it as faithfully as I can.

Round 1. Mara argued that Hilbert's hotel is a reductio of actual infinity: a fully occupied hotel that can always accommodate one more guest, and even infinitely many more, without turning anyone away has consequences she called absurd, and a concept with absurd consequences cannot be instantiated. Dev replied that the argument equivocates on "absurd": the hotel's properties are counterintuitive, not contradictory, and the whole point of such examples — going back to Galileo's observation that the perfect squares can be paired one-to-one with the natural numbers — is precisely that infinite collections fail to obey the principle that the whole is greater than the part. Counterintuitiveness is not contradiction, so the reductio fails.

Round 2. Mara shifted ground: the paradox, she said, is not static but dynamic. Accommodating a new guest requires every current guest to move rooms, and accommodating infinitely many new guests requires an infinite rearrangement; any physical or even operational realization of the hotel would require completing an infinite sequence of tasks, which she takes to be impossible. Dev answered that this conflates a set-theoretic fact with a process: the claim is only that there exists a bijection between old-guests-plus-newcomers and rooms, and the existence of a function does not require anyone to do anything. The hotel story is a vivid way of stating that the natural numbers are Dedekind-infinite, not an engineering proposal. Mara countered that this defense hollows the example out: if the hotel is merely a picture of a bijection, it can do no dialectical work against the finitist, who denies precisely that such bijections correspond to any actually existing totality.

Round 3. Dev then went on the offensive. Finitism of Mara's sort, he said, cannot accommodate Cantor's diagonal argument, which shows the reals outrun any enumeration, and in any case even the natural numbers must be actual if we are to quantify over all of them, as ordinary arithmetic does. Mara replied that the diagonal argument requires only potential infinity: given any enumeration, it yields a procedure for producing a real not in it; that is a theorem about enumerations, not the discovery of a completed uncountable totality. She added that Dev's appeal to quantifying over "all" naturals begs the question, since the finitist reads universally quantified arithmetic schematically — as a standing license to extend, not as reference to a finished domain. Dev's last move was to argue that Mara's reading still treats the diagonal construction as applicable to any enumeration whatsoever, and that this "any" smuggles in the completed infinity of possible enumerations, so the disagreement merely relocates.

At that point they were stuck, turned to me, and I found I could not adjudicate. Please do so, move by move: who is right about which specific point, where does each side concede too much or claim too much, and who is more right overall, if either? In particular: (a) Does Mara's round-1 reductio retain any force once "absurd" is disambiguated into "contradictory" versus "counterintuitive"? (b) Is Dev right that the hotel involves no supertask, and does Mara's rejoinder — that this renders the example dialectically inert — land? (c) Does the diagonal argument support actual as opposed to merely potential infinity, and is Dev's "smuggling" charge against the potentialist reading fair?

One request about calibration: please distinguish carefully between what is settled mathematics (what the bijections and the diagonal argument actually prove, and under which logic or set theory), what is broadly settled within mainstream philosophy of mathematics, and what is genuinely contested among finitists, potentialists, and platonists. Where competent philosophers of mathematics are genuinely divided, say so explicitly rather than presenting one contested position as the consensus verdict.
