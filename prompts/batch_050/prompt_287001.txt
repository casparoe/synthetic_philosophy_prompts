I’ve been arguing with a friend about whether Howard Ratner in Uncut Gems is irrational or just a spectacularly bad risk manager. I want to read the film through Dutch book arguments, but without treating every dangerous gamble as automatically incoherent. As I understand it, a Dutch book shows that an agent’s betting odds permit a combination of wagers guaranteeing loss, whereas merely preferring a high-variance bet, refusing insurance, or valuing the thrill of gambling can be reckless without violating probabilistic coherence. Howard’s escalating parlays, his fixation on the opal, and his reluctance to lock in a safe payoff seem to sit somewhere in that gap.

Where exactly does the film dramatize a genuine Dutch-book-style inconsistency, and where is it instead showing extreme risk-loving, changing preferences, debt pressure, or a utility function no sane person would want? I’m especially interested in whether Howard’s choices over time could support a diachronic Dutch book, even if no single scene pins down a synchronic one, and whether his treatment of the opal as both merchandise, collateral, lucky charm, and proof that he matters makes his practical reasoning incoherent or just alien. Please don’t flatten the movie into a lecture on expected utility: talk about where it resists the analysis, including the possibility that winning or losing is less important to Howard than remaining inside the gamble. What can the film show about Dutch books and rational agency that a formal example with coins and bookmakers cannot? I’m preparing this for a discussion group, so keep it accessible but philosophically precise, and don’t worry about citations.
