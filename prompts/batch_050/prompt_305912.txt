I am preparing a single ninety-minute session for first-year politics undergraduates on Marx's concept of alienation, built around this passage from the Economic and Philosophical Manuscripts of 1844:

"It is the same in religion. The more man puts into God, the less he retains in himself. The worker puts his life into the object; but now his life no longer belongs to him but to the object."

Design the teaching plan for the session rather than delivering the lecture itself. Specify the order in which the component ideas must be introduced and what depends on what — in particular, whether and how to separate objectification as such from estrangement before this passage can be read correctly, and where private property enters as a premise rather than a conclusion. Propose one carrying example (a work situation, real or invented) that the whole session builds on, and say what it must contain and must not contain if it is to illuminate the religion analogy rather than collapse it into a claim about job satisfaction. Anticipate the misconceptions first-years predictably bring — that alienation means disliking your job, that it is chiefly about low pay, that Marx is romanticising pre-industrial craftsmanship — and indicate at what point in the sequence each should be headed off, rather than corrected after the fact. Give a check for understanding that would show students can apply the passage's structure to a case they have not seen, such as a social-media content creator, and state what I should deliberately leave out at this level, saying so to the class. Answer in British English, in roughly 900 words.
