For several years I've followed -- and occasionally participated in -- the debate over whether advanced AI poses an existential risk to humanity, and I've become less interested in who is right than in why the debate moves so little. The two sides are familiar: those who think that sufficiently capable systems pursuing misaligned objectives could plausibly disempower or destroy humanity, and those who think this worry rests on speculative extrapolations about intelligence, agency, and takeover scenarios that don't survive scrutiny. What strikes me is that both camps include technically sophisticated people with access to the same information about what current systems do, and yet the exchange keeps cycling through the same arguments about instrumental convergence, deceptive alignment, scaling trends, and historical analogies to other technologies, with almost nobody updating. It doesn't feel like a disagreement that new benchmark results are going to settle.

My working hypothesis, offered tentatively, is that this isn't one disagreement but several stacked on top of each other, and the debaters rarely say which layer they're contesting at any given moment. There's an empirical layer (how will capabilities develop, and how fast), a conceptual layer (what even counts as a goal, an agent, or alignment, and whether these notions apply cleanly to systems trained by gradient descent), and a methodological layer (how much weight to give abstract arguments about systems that don't exist yet versus extrapolation from systems that do). My suspicion is that most public exchanges are framed as layer-one disputes while actually being driven by layer-two and layer-three differences -- which would explain why new empirical results never seem to move anyone: each side folds the same result into its own conceptual scheme and evidentiary standards.

Please diagnose the disagreement along these lines, but commit to a definite diagnosis rather than surveying possibilities: tell me which layer you think is actually doing the work, support this with what each side characteristically argues and how each side responds to the other's best points, and say whether the impasse reflects an ill-posed question, a legitimate difference in standards of evidence about unprecedented things, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for. Then, given your diagnosis, state concretely what would count as progress -- a specific conceptual clarification, a kind of empirical evidence, a formal result, or a reframing of the question -- or argue that nothing would, and why. I'd rather have a committed answer I can push back on than an even-handed menu of options.
