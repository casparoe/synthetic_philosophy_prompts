Question: In his 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics, Friedman argued that a theory should be assessed by the accuracy of its predictions rather than by the descriptive "realism" of its assumptions (the so-called F-twist). Consider two models: (a) a celestial mechanics model that treats planets as point masses concentrated at their centers; (b) a microeconomic model that treats firms "as if" they consciously maximized profit, even though no actual manager runs that calculation. Explain how Friedman would defend each assumption. Then discuss whether the two defenses stand or fall together: is there a relevant difference between the point-mass assumption and the "as if" maximization assumption, or are they unrealistic in exactly the same sense?

Student answer: Friedman argues in "The Methodology of Positive Economics" (1953) that theories are instruments for prediction, and that the descriptive realism of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant to judging it; only predictive accuracy counts. His famous example is the expert billiard player: the player doesn't solve any physics equations, but plays as if he did, and a theory assuming he does predicts his shots well. On that basis Friedman defends profit maximization: firms that fail to act as if they maximized profit get selected out by market competition, so the assumption predicts market outcomes even if no manager actually optimizes.

He would defend the point-mass assumption in exactly the same way: treating a planet as a point mass is literally false, but the model predicts orbits accurately, and that's all that matters. So the two defenses stand or fall together -- both are false descriptions that nonetheless deliver true predictions, and anyone who accepts Friedman's criterion has to treat them as equally legitimate. The critic who coined the term "F-twist," Karl Popper, disagreed, since on his view falsification requires a theory's assumptions to be testable in their own right.

Overall the essay shows that Friedman reduces all theory appraisal to predictive success, so once a model predicts well in its intended domain, its assumptions are thereby vindicated for any other application as well.
