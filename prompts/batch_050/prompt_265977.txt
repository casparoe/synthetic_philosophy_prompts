I'm putting together a handout for a graduate seminar on concepts and categorization, and the centerpiece is the old question that animated Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? The same letter appears as a single-storey scribble in my handwriting, a double-storey serifed form in a book, a pixelated thing on a departure board, and an ornate swash in a wedding invitation, and yet readers sort them all together effortlessly, while confidently excluding forms that differ by a hair. My handout so far surveys six answers, each with its standard trouble: (1) template matching — an 'a' is whatever sufficiently resembles a stored visual prototype — which founders on the fact that no single template covers both the single-storey and double-storey forms; (2) structural or part-based description — an 'a' is a bowl standing in a certain relation to a stem or arch — which fails because the two allographs share no parts, and because describing the parts just re-raises the recognition problem for the parts themselves; (3) the functional or conventional answer — an 'a' is whatever plays the 'a'-role in our writing practices, whatever competent readers take to be one — which is true but arguably circular, since it individuates the letter by the very capacity we're trying to explain; (4) family resemblance — a cluster of overlapping criteria, none necessary or sufficient — which to my taste concedes the game rather than winning it; (5) the historical or genealogical answer — an 'a' is whatever stands in a lineage of copying and gradual deformation descending from earlier letterforms — which explains the unity of the category retrospectively but not our ability to recognize, on first sight, an 'a' in a font no one has ever seen; and (6) the Letter Spirit answer itself — that what all the a's share is an abstract stylistic essence, a conceptual skeleton realized under varying stylistic parameters, and that recognizing it is the same kind of analogy-making that underlies categorization everywhere.

Here is what I need from you: propose a seventh answer that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or weighted average of items already on it — so, for instance, "templates, but historically individuated" doesn't count. Then stress-test your proposal against two cases that I take to be the real crucible for any theory here: first, a novel gridfont in which readers instantly and unanimously accept some unfamiliar shape as an 'a', which any theory must explain without appeal to prior exposure; and second, the context-dependence phenomenon, where the very same stroke can be read as one letter in the middle of one word and a different letter in the middle of another, so that letter-identity appears to be settled partly top-down rather than by the mark alone. A good answer should also make sense of a duality the question hides: a theory of 'a'-ness must explain both why every 'a' counts as an 'a' across all styles and why, within a single style, an 'a' does not collapse into an 'o' or a 'd' — the spirit must be uniform and discriminating at once. Finally, tell me honestly whether your proposal survives your own stress test, or whether it merely relocates the mystery.
