I teach a seminar on ethics and public policy, and for a unit on health-care rationing I pair the standard literature on QALY maximization and the value of a statistical life with Kazuo Ishiguro's novel Never Let Me Go, in which a class of cloned donors is raised to supply organs and is expected to "complete" after a series of donations beginning in early adulthood. I would like you to read the novel through the lens of that literature, in a way I can use to structure the seminar discussion.

Please address the following. First, the donation program is, in effect, a life-year maximization scheme that has solved the usual allocation problem by creating a designated donor class whose years are systematically discounted. How precisely does the novel's arrangement map onto actual features of QALY-based reasoning -- the aggregation of small and large benefits across persons, the fair-innings intuition about who has already had a life, and the distinction between identified and statistical lives? Second, the rumored deferral for couples who can prove they are in love interests me: is it a version of the rule of rescue, an implicit concession that some lives deserve extra years, or merely a humane veneer that leaves the maximizing structure intact -- and what does its falsity, once revealed, show about the function such exceptions serve? Third, the debate between the guardians and the wider society over whether the students "have souls" strikes me as the novel's most philosophically pointed device: the system never denies the donors' personhood, it simply prices their years at zero. Does the novel thereby suggest that moral-status arguments are the wrong battleground against this kind of discounting?

Finally, I want an honest account of where the reading strains. Kathy's narration is flat, resigned, and almost entirely without protest or policy argument; the novel never condemns the system in so many words. Does that refusal to editorialize undermine its use as a critique of cost-effectiveness reasoning, or is it precisely the point -- a depiction of what a statistical discount feels like from inside the discounted class? What, if anything, does the novel add to the QALY debate that the journal literature does not supply?
