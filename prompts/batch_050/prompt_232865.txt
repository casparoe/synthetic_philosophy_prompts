I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on ethics and AI (16 students, one 75-minute session) and want a structured deliberation game that makes the "whose values should aligned AI serve?" problem felt rather than described. The setup I have in mind: the class must act as the governing board of a company about to deploy a single AI assistant to a heterogeneous population, and they must collectively draft a short binding charter specifying what the system may do when users' requests conflict with one another's interests, with third parties, or with the board's own judgment of what is good for the user. I want the activity designed so that students first discover that "just serve the user's values" collapses under cases of adaptive preferences, manipulation, and interpersonal conflict, and then discover that every fallback -- majority vote, a veil-of-ignorance procedure, deference to expert judgment, liberal neutrality between conceptions of the good -- smuggles in a substantive value choice of its own. Please give me concrete role assignments (including a couple of planted roles, e.g., a user whose preferences were formed by the recommender system itself), a small set of staged decision cases the board must rule on, a timeline with rules for how charter clauses get proposed and amended, and a debrief mapping specific moments of the session onto the theoretical options (preference aggregation, perfectionism, neutrality, moral uncertainty). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time -- e.g., the group converging too fast on a vague platitude -- and how to force the disagreement into the open, and be frank about which feature of the real alignment problem (that the system scales to millions of users who never sit in the room) the activity cannot reproduce.
