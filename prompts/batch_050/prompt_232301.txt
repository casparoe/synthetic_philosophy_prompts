The proposition: for a typical consumer, abstaining from industrially farmed chicken for one year reduces expected total vertebrate suffering more than abstaining from industrially raised beef for one year, holding calories roughly constant -- where "total" counts the farmed animals and also the wild animals affected by the displaced land use (feed-crop cultivation for chicken, pasture and feed for beef). My credence is about 0.8, driven mostly by the animals-per-calorie asymmetry; my friend's is about 0.35. Give me your own credence, and decompose the reasoning rather than defending a verdict: assign rough probabilities to each sub-question the answer depends on -- whether the expected effect of a marginal purchase really is proportional to one consumer's share of production, the comparative badness per calorie of chicken versus beef lives, and the sign and size of the land-use terms -- and show how these combine into your final number. On the first sub-question, please engage specifically with the standard lumpy-restocking model (a purchase has a small chance of triggering a discrete production batch whose size exactly offsets that chance, so the expectation comes out proportional) and with one way I think it can fail: if retailers respond to falling demand by shrinking batch sizes rather than batch frequency, the per-purchase expectation could be far below one animal-equivalent. Tell me how much weight you put on that failure mode. Finally, state which single sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to, where a 0.8 and a 0.35 most plausibly diverge, and what empirical finding or argument would move your number the most.
