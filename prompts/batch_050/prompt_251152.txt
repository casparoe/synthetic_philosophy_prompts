I've been re-reading Bagehot's The English Constitution for a seminar I'm running, and in the introduction to the second edition he says this:

"There is a great difficulty in the way of a writer who attempts to sketch a living Constitution—a Constitution that is in actual work and power. The difficulty is that the object is in constant change."

He means 1860s Britain, but I want you to stress-test the passage, and the theory behind it, against an episode he couldn't possibly have anticipated: the night of 23 February 1981 in Spain. Roughly the facts, and tell me only if I'm wildly off on something important: Lt-Colonel Tejero and a couple of hundred Civil Guards stormed the Cortes mid-vote and held parliament hostage at gunpoint; the captain-general in Valencia put tanks on the streets; and the king, Juan Carlos — Franco's appointee, on the throne barely five years, constitutionally commander-in-chief — refused to give the plotters cover, spent the night working the phones to the regional commanders, and appeared on television at about one in the morning in uniform ordering the armed forces to uphold the constitutional order. By morning the coup had collapsed.

Analyse the episode through Bagehot. On his account the monarchy sits in the "dignified" part of the constitution — theatre that secures loyalty whilst the "efficient" parts do the actual governing — and a sovereign who acts politically burns through the very mystery that makes the arrangement work. So what exactly was Juan Carlos doing on Bagehot's map? Was the broadcast the dignified part briefly conscripted into efficient work, or does the distinction itself break down precisely when a constitution is under threat? And does the episode confirm the hunch in the passage I've quoted — that the object wont sit still for its portrait — or does it cut deeper, suggesting that a young monarchy of contested legitimacy can do what a settled one cannot, namely intervene openly in defence of democracy and come out stronger? Be careful to say where Bagehot is simply silent rather than forcing him to speak: I suspect he'd dismiss the whole affair as proof Spain wasn't yet a constitutional monarchy at all in his sense, and that the king's intervention worked only because the rules weren't yet fixed. Would he be right, and what definately settles that question either way?
