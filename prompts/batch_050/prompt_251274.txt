On a philosophy podcast I listen to, the host defended teleosemantics -- roughly the view that what a mental state is *about* is fixed by what it was naturally selected to do -- with this move: natural selection is just a designer without the shop. Your heart is *for* pumping blood the same way a pump in a factory is for moving coolant: someone or something selected it for that role, and that's why we can say it's malfunctioning when it fails. And once you've got functions that can misfire, she said, you get misrepresentation for free: the frog's fly-detector can be wrong about a BB pellet the same way a thermostat can be wrong about the room. So content is naturalized, no spooks, done.

I want an audit of the designer analogy, not a verdict on teleosemantics in general. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the engineer, the blueprint or spec, the intended use, the quality-control inspector who can condemn a unit, and the difference between a broken token and a misdesigned one. Then go through where the mapping breaks -- I'm thinking of stuff like: designers have foresight and selection doesn't; a spec settles what a part is *supposed* to do in cases where selection history is silent or indeterminate (was the fly-detector selected for detecting flies, small dark moving things, or food?); drift and byproducts complicate what counts as having been selected at all; and the "broken token" idea presupposes a standard the lineage itself can't always supply. For each disanalogy, tell me whether it actually threatens the use the host is making of it -- deriving a norm of correctness that grounds misrepresentation -- or whether it's the kind of mismatch every analogy has and teleosemantics can shrug off. Then: does her conclusion survive with the disanalogies on the table, or was the designer picture doing the load-bearing work of sneaking in a standard of correctness that bare selection history doesn't deliver? If there's a better analogy for what selection actually gives you (I keep thinking of apprenticeship, or case law, but I might be off), say what it is and whether it still supports the misrepresentation result.

Also: flag how confident you are in each part of the audit, and at the end single out the single claim in your answer you're least sure about and why.
