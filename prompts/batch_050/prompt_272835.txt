I sit on the grants commitee of a small community foundation in a mid-sized American town, and we have a genuinely hard decision coming up at next Tuesday's meeting. Last year a donor died and left us about $1.8 million, and her will just says the money should go to "the welfare of children" with nothing more specific than that. After months of review we're down to two finalists. Option A is local: free swim lessons plus lifeguard staffing at the town lake where a nine-year-old actually drowned two summers ago, bundled with some after-school programming. It's visible, the town loves it, and it honors what everyone assumes the donor (a lifelong resident) would have wanted. Option B is an evidence-backed charity that funds malaria net distribution and deworming abroad, and their numbers suggest the same money could avert something like a hundred or more deaths, though those estimates mostly come from one evaluation organization that quietly revised its figures downward last year.

The fight on the committee has gotten philosophical, honestly. One member keeps pressing the drowning child analogy: if you'd wade into a pond to save a kid in front of you even if it ruined your shoes and your suit, then mere distance shouldn't matter, and option B isn't even close. Another member pushes back that this is an argument about what individuals owe, and a foundation isn't a person — it has role obligations, donor intent to respect, accountability to the community that built the endowment, and anyway institutional decisions shouldn't hinge on one glossy cost-effectiveness spreadsheet. I keep going back and forth and I need to circulate something before the vote.

Act as a moral philosopher who has spent years consulting for institutional ethics boards and grantmaking bodies. Think this through carefully: first brainstorm the strongest version of each side (steelman both, please, including whether "role obligations" of an institution can legitimately override whatever the drowning child intuition demands of a person), then draft the short written opinion I'll send around. It needs to reccomend a concrete allocation — all-or-nothing or a split, with a timeline for when we'd revisit the decision — engage seriously with the losing side rather than dismissing it, and close by naming the single consideration that, if the facts turned out differently, would flip your recommendation the other way.
