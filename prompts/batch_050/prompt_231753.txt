I spent forty years as a geochemist, retired now, and in the last year or so I've fallen down the AI safety rabbit hole the way you do. The thing I keep chewing on is whether "don't cause side effects" can be made precise for an AI system without first telling it everything we value. So for my own clarity I wrote down what I currently believe about impact measures, and I want you to audit it: tell me which of these pull against each other, how badly, and what the cheapest fixes are. Here's the list:

1. A decent impact measure should be definable in terms of observable, fairly objective features of the world -- stuff like what's been built, broken, moved, burned. It shouldn't require knowing what humans want, because if we knew that well enough to just write it down we wouldn't need the impact measure in the first place.

2. Impact should be measured against a counterfactual: compare what the agent does to what the world would have looked like if it had just sat there and done nothing.

3. Here's a case I'm confident about. Say a dam was going to burst anyway, and the agent quietly reinforces it. That should count as *low* impact -- it kept the world on the track it was already on. A measure that flags that as a big impact is broken.

4. Reversible changes should be penalized less than irreversible ones. Filling a pool is one thing, draining an aquifer is another.

5. The measure must not reward sneaky offsetting -- the agent shouldn't be able to cause a mess and then "cancel it out" by shoving something else around to balance the ledger.

6. And whatever the measure turns out to be, it had better not just be a utility function wearing a trenchcoat. If "low impact" only makes sense relative to a full specification of human values, then the whole concept is redundant and we should drop it and go do value learning instead.

Go through these pairwise, flag the tensions in order of severity, and for each one say what would have to give and what that costs. Also tell me if any apparent tension in there actually dissolves once you look at it properly -- I'm a scientist, I know the feeling of two things that look contradictory until you state them carefully. Don't tell me which commitment you think I'll want to keep; just lay out the options.

Two requests about how you answer. First: this seems like a small and young research area, so please be careful to separate what's basically settled among the people who actually work on this stuff from what's genuinely contested or just one group's pet approach -- don't present someone's preferred formalism as if it were the consensus. Second: put your overall verdict in the very first sentence -- is my set coherent or not, roughly -- then get into the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my list back at me.
