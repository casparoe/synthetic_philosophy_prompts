I recently got appointed to the medical advisory panel that our diocese convenes to look at claimed miraculous cures at a pilgrimage site nearby. The bishop's office sends us maybe eight or ten dossiers a year, we meet once a month, and each case gets about an afternoon. We're mostly doctors, plus one canon lawyer and a hospital chaplain, and our remit is narrow and kind of odd: we're not asked to declare miracles, only to say whether a recovery is, in the phrase the comittee uses, "medically inexplicable given current knowledge." At my first meeting someone invoked Hume's argument about miracles -- roughly, that testimony for a miracle only wins if the testimony being false would be an even bigger miracle than the event it's testifying to -- and said we should just apply that and stop arguing in circles. Fine. I'd like to actually try. Write me a decision procedure we could genuinely run on a dossier: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates with "natural explanation cannot be excluded," and where it has to stop and hand things to a human judgment call because the remaining question isn't a factual one.

The part I most want done carefully is the translation. Tell me which of Hume's distinctions have to survive being turned into a checklist for non-philosophers, and which can be dropped without losing the argument. I'm especially unsure about his line between an event that violates the uniform course of nature and an event that's just vanishingly rare -- spontaneous remission of metastatic cancer is real and documented, so does a remission after a pilgrimage even clear his first hurdle, or does his test only bite for things like a severed limb regrowing? Also flag the places where Hume's weighing-of-testimony logic and modern statistical thinking come apart, because half the panel natively thinks in base rates and p-values and will quietly substitute those for his test if I don't watch them. And say plainly what the procedure cannot do: I suspect "medically inexplicable" and "miraculous" are further apart than the bishop's office likes to pretend, and I want to know whether the procedure can even in principle get us to the second, or only ever to the first.

Then run it on two cases. Case one: a woman with metastatic melanoma, confirmed by biopsy and scans, whose tumors resolved completely within weeks of visiting the shrine; her oncologist of ten years testifies he has never seen anything comparable and can't explain it. Case two: a man whose debilitating chronic back pain disappeared the day of his visit, attested by him, his wife, and his parish priest, with imaging that showed only mild degeneration beforehand. My strong expectation is the procedure certifies the first as inexplicable and not the second. If it doesn't, show me exactly where it diverges from my expectation, which step is doing the work, and whether that step is really Hume's idea or something we've smuggled in from modern medicine. And be honest if the procedure gives an answer I won't like -- e.g., if it turns out the cancer case fails some early step I hadn't thought about.
