I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of history with 18 students and one 80-minute session. We are about to discuss covering-law versus narrative explanation, but I do not want to introduce either label until the final twenty minutes. Design an in-class activity that makes students feel the pull of both models before they are named.

Build the activity around a wholly fictional public inquiry into the collapse of a harbor footbridge in the city of Westmere. Six people were injured, and an inquiry board must explain why the bridge collapsed on that particular day. The evidence should deliberately support two different explanatory impulses:

1. The collapse appears to instantiate a broader pattern: coastal corrosion, repeated maintenance deferrals, understaffed inspections, and exposure to severe weather make failures of this kind increasingly likely.
2. The collapse also depends on a contingent sequence: an inspection report is misrouted, a closure request is delayed because of a festival, a temporary support is removed, and an otherwise survivable storm arrives before the report reaches the responsible official.

Include enough complication to prevent either impulse from winning too easily. For example, the bridge survived a stronger storm the previous year, a nearby pier with worse corrosion remained standing, and the maintenance backlog had existed for years without causing a collapse. Do not rely on technical engineering knowledge; all causally relevant facts should be intelligible from the cards themselves.

Please provide a complete, runnable plan containing:

- A precise setup for 18 students, including group sizes, roles, and a minute-by-minute schedule.
- The full text of 14–18 printable evidence cards. Some cards should state recurring patterns, some particular events, some comparisons or near-misses, and at least two plausible but ultimately misleading facts. Include two cards that are withheld until the middle of the activity.
- A first-round task in which each group prepares a formal finding of no more than 100 words using a limited number of cards. The constraints should force them to choose between foregrounding a repeatable pattern and reconstructing the particular chain of events.
- A structured cross-examination round. Other students should challenge each finding using tests such as whether the cited connection is repeatable, whether the event would still have occurred without a given factor, whether the finding explains why this bridge collapsed on this day, and whether the account merely provides a chronology without identifying explanatory links.
- A simple scoring or credibility-token system that rewards both robust general support and well-identified difference-makers, without building in the assumption that the correct answer must always be a synthesis.
- A mid-activity reveal of the withheld cards, followed by a revision round in which groups must publicly amend or defend their original findings.
- A final vote requiring students to distinguish separately among three questions: Is this statement true? Did it make a causal difference? Does including it make the explanation better? They should not be allowed to answer with a single undifferentiated verdict.

The students should encounter the central difficulty through the rules and challenges rather than through an introductory lecture: a true generalization can still leave the local event unexplained, while an accurate sequence of events can fail to show which steps mattered. At the same time, avoid caricaturing either side; a good narrative should establish causal relevance rather than simply tell a story, and a general pattern can contribute to explaining a particular event rather than merely predicting a class of events.

Finish with a fifteen-minute debrief that maps identifiable moments in the game onto covering-law explanation and narrative explanation, naming both only after the activity. Include the questions I should ask, the answers students are likely to give, and how to connect those answers to issues of causal relevance, explanatory selection, counterfactual dependence, and the difference between explaining why events of a kind occur and explaining why this event occurred.

Also tell me what is likely to go wrong the first time I run it: for example, students treating chronology as causation, treating a statistical pattern as sufficient, arguing about facts that the cards stipulate, or assuming that the best account must mention every cause. Suggest concrete interventions for each problem. Finally, state candidly which features of historical explanation the activity cannot reproduce, especially the absence of genuinely contestable sources and the fact that actual historians must construct both patterns and narratives from incomplete evidence rather than receiving authoritative cards.
