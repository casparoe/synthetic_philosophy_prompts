So I'm a referee in an argument I never asked to be part of. Two friends of mine, Maya and Daniel, were at dinner with me last week and got into it over something our mutual friend said about her boyfriend — she'd told him she loved him because he was funny and kind and brilliant at his job, and he was apparently a bit deflated by that answer. Maya thought the boyfriend was being ridiculous, Daniel thought he was onto something, and it escalated from there. I'll try to reconstruct the actual back-and-forth as fairly as I can.

Maya's opening move was that love has to be grounded in the beloved's actual qualities, because if it isn't, then whatever you love isn't really the person — it's a projection, a placeholder, a warm feeling you happened to staple onto whoever was nearby. Loving someone for no reason, she said, is indistinguishable from loving the idea of them.

Daniel's reply: if love tracks qualities, then it should transfer the moment someone better-qualified shows up. If you love her because she's funny and kind, then someone funnier and kinder is, by your own lights, an upgrade, and staying becomes irrational. But nobody thinks love works like that — "trading up" isn't just frowned upon, it's a betrayal of what love is. So love can't be appraisal of qualities. It's more like the love itself makes the person matter to you, not the other way round.

Maya said that's too fast. The non-transferability is explained by the history: you have reasons to love this person that come from the life you've built together, the shared years, the fact that it's them you went through all that with. That's still a reason, and it doesn't transfer to the funnier stranger, so there's no trading-up problem. Love can be reason-responsive without being a feature-detector.

Daniel pushed back on that one and this is where it got heated: he said the history move is a sunk-cost fallacy dressed up romantically. "I've already invested fifteen years in you" is a terrible reason to love someone — it's the logic of staying in a bad movie because you paid for the ticket. If that's the best the reasons-view can do, it hasn't explained love, it's explained inertia.

Maya's last move: she said Daniel's view has a worse problem on the other side. If love bestows value rather than responding to it, then it should be totally indifferent to what the person becomes — cruelty, betrayal, a complete change of character. But we don't think someone who stops loving an abusive partner has made some kind of error, and we do think there's something pathological about love that would survive absolutely anything. So love must be revisable in light of the person's qualities, which means it was never unconditional bestowal in the first place.

Then they both turned to me, and I genuinely could not call it, because each of them seemed to land a real hit on the other and neither landing felt fatal.
