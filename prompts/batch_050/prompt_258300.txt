I'm a PhD student in philosophy of the social sciences helping to draft this year's comprehensive exam, and I need one essay question on the debate over why some nations are rich — institutions vs. geography vs. culture — answerable in about two hours. Every candidate will know the standard repertoire: the reversal-of-fortune argument against geographic determinism, instrumental-variable strategies using things like settler mortality, and cultural explanations via trust or religious ethics. So the question must not be answerable by summarizing these positions; it should force candidates onto the epistemology underneath them. Concretely, it should push them on (i) what the exclusion restriction of a historical instrument actually amounts to, and why it is untestable when the instrument operated centuries ago and plausibly worked through many channels at once (e.g., settler mortality affecting both institutions and the disease environment and human capital); (ii) Galton's problem — why cross-societal observations are not independent draws, and what that does to regression-based adjudication; and (iii) whether "institutions vs. geography vs. culture" is even a well-posed contrast when each factor plausibly causes the other two. Please provide: the question itself; a model answer of roughly 600–700 words; and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points totaling 30 marks, each with a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates miss it — for instance, treating a robust regression coefficient as settling the causal question, or treating the debate as a horse race over which factor explains "most" of the variance rather than as a question about what is identifiable from a history that runs only once.
