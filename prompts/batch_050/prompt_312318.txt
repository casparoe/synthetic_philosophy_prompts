I edit the "big questions" section of an online philosophy magazine, and I am commissioning a self-assessment feature on the rational structure of love: is love a response to value already present in the beloved, a way of conferring value on the beloved, or something grounded in the relationship rather than in the person at all? Rather than a prose explainer, I want the feature built around a decision tree that sorts readers into positions by how they react to cases, so that someone arrives at their position without ever being asked which label they prefer.

The tree should be constructed from the logical space rather than from a reading list. Begin by stating the two or three diagnostic questions whose possible answers carve up that space (for instance: whether the beloved's qualities give reasons for the love or the love gives the qualities their importance; whether the object of love is the person, the person's valuable nature, or the relationship itself; whether there can be reasons to love or to stop loving at all). Then locate the standard positions within that space, and note any unoccupied combinations of answers with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

The leaves must include: the quality view, on which love appraises the beloved's desirable properties; a Velleman-style appraisal view, on which love responds to the beloved's rational nature rather than to optional excellences; a Frankfurt-style bestowal view, on which the beloved is valuable because loved rather than loved because valuable; a Kolodny-style relationship view; a union view; and a robust-concern view. Where your tree genuinely cannot separate two of these, say so explicitly instead of forcing a branch.

Each branch node must be phrased as a concrete case the reader can react to without knowing any terminology, and the following cases must appear somewhere on the tree: the trading-up case, in which the reader meets someone possessing all of their beloved's virtues plus several more, and is asked whether love provides any reason to stay rather than switch; the transformation case, in which the beloved permanently loses, through illness or injury, precisely the qualities the reader would cite if asked why they love them; the duplicate case, in which an atom-for-atom replica of the beloved is introduced, testing whether love is portable between qualitatively identical persons; the stranger case, in which the reader considers whether one can love, in the relevant sense, a person one has never met, such as a long-correspondence pen pal or a historical figure; and the vicious-beloved case, in which the reader discovers the beloved to be genuinely bad and is asked whether this constitutes a reason to stop loving or is simply beside the point.

At each leaf, give the name of the position, one sentence stating its core claim, and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment — for example, the quality view's implication that trading up is the rational course, the bestowal view's apparent implication that loving any arbitrary object would be equally rational, or the relationship view's difficulty with love at first sight and unrequited love. The finished tree should be usable as published: a reader answers the cases in order, arrives at a leaf, and learns what their pattern of answers commits them to.
