I am drafting a position statement for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics and ethics of virtual worlds, and before I submit it I want an audit of whether my commitments are jointly tenable. Here is where I have landed:

1. Virtual realism about objects. Virtual items -- a ship in EVE Online, an enchanted sword in an MMO, a building in Minecraft -- are real digital objects, not mere pictures or descriptions of objects. They are genuinely owned, and taking one against the owner's will can be genuine theft, not just rudeness.

2. Consent by design. An action that a game's rules and code deliberately permit cannot wrong other players. If the developers of a world allow scamming, espionage, and betrayal within the game, then engaging in those activities is play, not harm, however upset the victims are. Complaints about permitted behavior are complaints about the game's design, not about wrongdoing.

3. Long-term virtual projects matter. Destroying something a person has spent years building inside a virtual world -- a city, a guild, an elaborate curated environment -- is a serious harm, comparable in kind (if not always in degree) to destroying their physical craftwork.

4. The persistence asymmetry. There is a deep difference between deleting a unique virtual object and deleting a digital photograph of a physical object. The photograph is replaceable data pointing at something that still exists; the virtual object is the thing itself, so its deletion is the worse and more fundamental loss.

5. Legal parity. The law should treat the theft of a virtual item with a market value of $1,500 roughly as it treats the theft of a $1,500 bicycle; the medium should not determine whether property law applies.

Here is what I want from you. First, identify which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other and which pairs only appear to. Be careful here: I suspect at least one apparent inconsistency dissolves once you are precise about the type/token distinction or about what "permitted by the code" really amounts to, and I would rather you show the dissolution than flag everything indiscriminately. Second, for the genuine tensions, rank them by severity and say exactly what each one forces: which commitment must be revised or abandoned, and what the cheapest revision costs me. Third, test commitment 2 in particular against 1, 3, and 5 using a case like an in-game scam that the developers explicitly tolerate: can I really call the same act play under one commitment and actionable theft under another, or is there a principled line -- victim consent, developer intent, market value -- that saves the package? Finally, tell me whether the least costly overall repair leaves me with a position still worth defending, or whether the repair guts the view I actually care about.
