I'm in a reading group on the metaphysics of dispositions, and we've been trying to reverse-engineer the implicit theory behind our organizer's verdicts. She classifies ordinary objects as having or lacking dispositions, and so far her calls have been: (1) a plain fragile wine glass, struck, which shatters -- fragile; (2) an identical glass snugly packed in styrofoam, struck, which survives because the packing absorbs the blow -- still fragile, since the packing merely masks the disposition without removing it; (3) an identical glass guarded by a device that would instantly realign its molecular bonds and render it sturdy the moment it is struck -- fragile anyway, because right now it has the internal structure that would do the breaking if only that structure were retained; (4) a sturdy rubber ball wired to a device that would render it brittle the moment it is struck, so that it then breaks -- not fragile, since before the strike it lacks the relevant internal makeup entirely; (5) a fragile glass whose packing is attached to a sensor that would vaporize the packing an instant before any impact -- fragile, and in fact it breaks when struck. We notice her verdicts so far seem compatible with at least two different general rules: one in the spirit of Lewis's reformed conditional analysis, keyed to the object's present intrinsic basis, and one a simple counterfactual test that asks whether the object would manifest the behavior under the stimulus provided nothing is *preventing* it (indifferent to whether the manifestation's causal route runs through the object's own structure or through the surrounding setup).

The new case she'll pose at our next meeting is this: a plexiglass cube, intrinsically sturdy through and through, is ringed by sensors connected to a micro-charge embedded in its surface; upon any strike, the sensors detonate the charge, and the cube shatters. Nothing about the cube's molecular structure changes at any point, and nothing masks or prevents anything -- the apparatus is a pure cause of breaking, never a preventer. Predict her verdict on the cube and say which of the two rules above delivers it and which diverges, giving your best guess as to which rule she is actually running, with reasons drawn from the pattern of her earlier calls (pay attention to what cases 3 and 4 jointly rule out). If you think a third rule is also in the running and consistent with all five verdicts, state it and say what it predicts about the cube. Finally, name the one further test case -- different from the cube -- that would most cleanly discriminate among whichever candidate rules survive this one, and say what each surviving rule would predict about it.
