I'm in the second year of my philosophy degree and currently taking a course on phenomenology, where we've spent several weeks on Husserl's method of the epoché and the phenomenological reduction. Throughout the course, our professor has repeatedly invoked the slogan "back to the things themselves" — the phrase the textbook associates with the founding of phenomenology — and last week it came up in a way that bothered me. During a seminar discussion of Husserl's account of time-consciousness, I suggested that some of his claims about the structure of temporal experience might be checked against experimental results from psychophysics and the cognitive science of time perception, and that conflicts between them would be interesting rather than embarrassing. The professor dismissed this by saying that my suggestion had lost sight of the phenomenological project entirely, and that we needed to go back to the things themselves rather than to the theories constructed about them. Several classmates nodded, but I left the room unable to say what claim had actually been made against me.

The problem is that I can think of many different things this slogan might mean, and they seem to have very different merits. It might be a fairly modest methodological maxim: before theorizing, look carefully at how things actually present themselves in experience. It might be a stronger anti-naturalist thesis: that philosophical inquiry into consciousness must not take for granted any of the ontology or results of the natural sciences, which is presumably how it connects to the epoché. It might be an epistemological claim that only what is directly given in intuition can justify anything, or a quietist claim that philosophical problems dissolve rather than get solved, or a realist claim that we have genuine access to mind-independent essences, or perhaps just a rhetorical device for ending arguments. I'd like these readings — and any others you think are important — laid out as a menu: for each one, state the precise thesis, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or implausible, and explain what it commits one to.

Most importantly, I want to know which of these readings could actually support the use the slogan was put to in my seminar. The methodological-maxim reading seems true but toothless — surely no cognitive scientist denies that you should observe carefully — so it can't obviously rule my suggestion out of bounds. The strong anti-naturalist reading would get the professor his conclusion, but it seems to require an argument that phenomenological descriptions are insulated from empirical correction, and I'm not sure what that argument would be, or whether the epoché itself supplies it. Which precisification of the slogan does the professor's dismissal need, is that precisification defensible, and if the slogan is being used to do argumentative work it can't actually do, what would be a fairer way of stating whatever legitimate point is in the vicinity?
