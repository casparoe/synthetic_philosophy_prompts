So I just spent like three hours arguing with my friend Marta about archives and silence — she's a history grad student and her whole thing is that the gaps in the historical record are mostly *passive*, stuff just didn't get saved, fires happen, paper rots, nobody thought poor people's letters were worth keeping. I kept saying no, the gaps are usually *made*, somebody at some point decides what counts as a document and what counts as trash, and the decision is the violent part, not the flood. Neither of us budged and now I'm annoyed, so I want a short story that works through this.

Write the story set in the records office of a mid-sized city sometime in the 1950s. The main character is a junior clerk whose job includes deciding which incoming papers get an accession number and which get pulped — and here's the bit I want doing the work: the clerk isn't a villain and isn't acting on orders. They've just absorbed, perfectly, the institution's sense of what a *life that generates records* looks like, so the pulping feels to them like tidiness. The city is demolishing a neighborhood soon, and someone from that neighborhood keeps coming in trying to get things *into* the archive before the bulldozers — a rent book, a funeral program, a photograph of people nobody at the office can name. The story should end before the demolition, on some small decision the clerk makes, and it should leave genuinely open whether what just happened was preservation or another kind of erasure.

Keep it under 1200 words, no moralizing narrator, no scene where someone explains the theme out loud. I want to send it to Marta and have it count as my next move in the argument rather than a lecture at her, so it has to be a real story first and a position second.
