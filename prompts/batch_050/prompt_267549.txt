In a seminar on moral psychology we've been circling the question of when a person's settled moral or evaluative attitude counts as a genuine preference that deserves respect, and when it counts as a bias whose deliverances can be discounted. I keep finding that my case-by-case verdicts don't obviously cohere, so I want to lay them out and ask what principle, if any, captures them. First: a colleague prefers tea to coffee because her family drank tea; I say that's a preference, full stop, and nothing about it calls for justification. Second: a man was raised to feel disgust at interracial relationships and now offers elaborate principled arguments against them; I say the arguments are rationalization, the attitude is a bias, and it shouldn't count in any interpersonal calculus. Third: a subsistence farmer in an isolated village sincerely reports contentment and says she wants nothing more for her daughters than the life she has; I say this adaptive preference is distorted by the absence of live alternatives and doesn't settle what's good for her. Fourth: a friend became a vegetarian after reading a book that persuaded him argumentatively but also left him viscerally disgusted by meat, a disgust that now feels to him like part of his conviction; I say his is a genuine moral preference, since he endorses it on reflection even knowing where it came from. Fifth: a person's cilantro aversion is largely genetic; I say that's obviously still a preference, and it would be absurd to urge them to overcome it. Sixth: experimental subjects whose trolley-problem judgments shift depending on the order the cases are presented; I say both judgments are noise and neither counts until stabilized. Seventh: a taste for a brand of whiskey deliberately installed by years of targeted advertising; I lean toward calling it bias, though less confidently than the others.

My tentative principle has been this: an attitude is a bias rather than a preference if the person would disavow it upon learning its actual causal history. But I can already see it strains — the farmer might not disavow her contentment even fully informed, the vegetarian does not disavow despite knowing, and it's unclear why knowledge-of-etiology should be the test rather than, say, counterfactual stability, reflective endorsement, or responsiveness to reasons. So: is there a single account of the preference/bias distinction that delivers all seven of my verdicts, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause each case motivates? If my seven verdicts are jointly inconsistent under any tolerably simple principle, say so plainly and tell me which verdict(s) you'd overturn and why — my suspicion is that the pair (2) and (4), which are structurally similar yet get opposite verdicts from me, is where I'll have to give something up, and likewise that (1) and (3) may not both survive a principle that appeals to social conditioning. Then apply your best repaired account to two cases I haven't listed: a moral conviction that the person knows was produced by a pharmaceutical mood change, and a preference that would be reversed only under reflection conditions the person has good reason never to enter.

Finally, I want an honest self-assessment rather than a confident sweep: indicate how confident you are in the principle you end up defending, and single out the single point in your answer — a case verdict, a clause of the principle, or a prediction about one of my unlisted cases — that you are least sure of, with a sentence on why it wobbles.
