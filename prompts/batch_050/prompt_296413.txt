Contemporary matchmaking platforms treat love as a selection problem: users specify the qualities they value, algorithms rank potential partners by compatibility scores, and the implicit promise is that the better the match, the more reason one has to love this particular person. Philosophers of love distinguish between loving someone in response to their appraised valuable qualities and love as itself bestowing value on the beloved, independent of any ranking of their merits. Reconstruct which view of love's reasons the matchmaking model presupposes, and assess whether it is coherent on its own terms: in particular, can it explain why one should continue loving one's matched partner rather than trading up whenever the algorithm surfaces someone who scores higher, and why a perfect compatibility score should generate love rather than mere admiration or consumer satisfaction? Then consider what a dating service would look like if designed around the rival, bestowal-based view — must it abandon matching altogether, or is a coherent bestowalist matchmaking service possible? Finally, consider whether the actual practice is philosophically underdetermined: perhaps it is compatible with both views, and its real presupposition lies elsewhere, for instance in modeling love as a matter of informed consumer choice.
