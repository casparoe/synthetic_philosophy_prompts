A friend from my philosophy of language reading group cornered me last week with an argument whose conclusion — that most of what we ordinarily say about kinds of things is either false or not truth-evaluable at all — strikes me as absurd, and yet I have been unable to locate the misstep. I would like a diagnosis.

The argument runs as follows. (1) A sentence is true only if it can be assigned determinate truth conditions: some claim about the world whose obtaining would make it true. (2) The truth conditions of any generalization about a kind must take the form of a quantified claim about the kind's members — all of them, most of them, or some of them; there is no fourth option. (3) Consider "ducks lay eggs." It cannot mean all ducks, since males lay no eggs. It cannot mean most ducks, since at any given time the males, the ducklings, and the non-breeding females together plausibly outnumber the egg-layers. That leaves some. (4) But "some ducks lay eggs" cannot be what the sentence means either, because "some ducks do not lay eggs" is just as true, and this would render "ducks lay eggs" and "ducks do not lay eggs" equally true — which they plainly are not. (5) "Mosquitoes carry malaria" makes matters worse: it is obviously true, yet only females of certain species transmit the parasite, a small minority of all mosquitoes; meanwhile "mosquitoes are female," which holds of roughly half, strikes everyone as false. No single quantifier gets both verdicts right, so the failure is not a quirk about ducks. (6) Therefore no quantified paraphrase supplies the truth conditions of generic sentences, and by (1) and (2) such sentences are not straightforwardly true. (7) Yet an enormous share of ordinary and scientific talk consists of generics — "dogs bark," "smoking causes cancer," "bears hibernate." (8) Therefore most of what we say about kinds is false, or at best not assessable as true or false.

I will not accept (8); if anything must go, it is whatever makes (8) follow, and I care most about preserving the plain truth of things like "smoking causes cancer." In your first sentence, tell me which premise or step you reject. Then explain why that step looked inevitable, and what each of the live alternatives — rejecting (1), rejecting (2), or biting a bullet somewhere else — would cost. Keep the whole answer under 350 words.
