I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, and I recently fell down a rabbit hole about pain asymbolia -- patients with certain brain lesions (something to do with the insula?) who still say they feel pain, can describe it and locate it and rate its intensity, but insist it doesn't bother them. They don't pull their hand away, they don't ask for it to stop, some of them apparently grin while reporting a strong pain.

Anyway it got me to a position I currently hold, and I want to stress-test it. The position is: what makes pain *bad* is entirely how it feels, i.e. the unpleasantness, the "ouch this sucks" quality. If you strip that away, whatever sensation remains might be weird or interesting, but nothing bad is happening to the person anymore. On this view the asymbolia patients are fine -- their experience just isn't a misfortune, even if the underlying tissue damage is bad for other reasons. I find this pretty compelling, partly because it maps onto how I think about, say, error handling: the signal isn't the problem, the signal *mattering* is the problem.

So: what would count against this view? I want the strongest defeaters, and I'd like them sorted from "this would actually kill the view" down to "awkward but survivable." Specifically:

- Which features of the asymbolia case itself could undercut my reading of it? E.g., what if it turns out the patients still *are* suffering in some way they can't report, or the lesion is doing something else entirely (like flattening all motivation, not just pain-aversion)? Which of these are things proponents of my view would have to concede as real threats vs. things they'd brush off?
- Are there thought experiments or real cases that pull apart "how it feels" from "how bad it is" in the other direction -- cases where something feels trivially mild but seems genuinely bad for the person, or where we'd say someone was harmed by an experience they didn't mind?
- What is my view committed to about things like morphine, or hypothetical pills that leave the pain-feeling but delete the caring? Would I have to say a doctor who gives such a pill has done everything that matters, morally speaking? Am I ok with that?
- Has the view been defined so loosely ("the badness is in the felt quality") that nothing could count against it? If so, is that actually a defect, or fine for this kind of claim?

One more thing: please be upfront about which parts of this are empirically settled (what asymbolia patients are actually like, what the lesions do) versus which parts are genuinely contested among people who work on this -- both the neuroscience and the philosophy. I don't want a contested interpretation presented as the established consensus, and I don't want to build my whole position on a Wikipedia-level understanding of the syndrome.
