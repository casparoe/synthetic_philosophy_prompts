A friend and I have spent the evening arguing about Malthus, and we have narrowed our disagreement to a single sentence from Chapter III of the first edition of An Essay on the Principle of Population (1798), where, after reviewing hunter-gatherer societies, he writes: "These facts prove the superior power of population to the means of subsistence in nations of hunters; and that this power always shews itself the moment it is left to act with freedom."

Take the Green Revolution of the 1960s and 70s as the test case: high-yield wheat and rice varieties, expanded irrigation, and fertilizer use let food production in much of Asia outpace population growth for decades, even as populations boomed, while fertility rates subsequently fell without the famine and misery the theory seems to require as the check. My friend reads this as a clean falsification: the power of population was "left to act with freedom" and yet the means of subsistence won. I read it as a confirmation deferred: the check arrived anyway, merely in the milder form of voluntary fertility decline, and nothing in the episode shows the "superior power" has been cancelled rather than postponed.

Analyze the episode through the sentence. First, state precisely what the passage commits Malthus to: is it a law about a standing tendency, or a prediction about timing, and what observation would count against each reading? Then say which features of the Green Revolution the passage explains, which it fails to explain, and whether the fertility transition counts as the theory's own mechanism working or as an auxiliary assumption imported to save it. Finally, adjudicate between my friend and me on the philosophy-of-science question: can a long-run theory legitimately be "right but early," and what would distinguish that defense from an unfalsifiable rescue in this case?
