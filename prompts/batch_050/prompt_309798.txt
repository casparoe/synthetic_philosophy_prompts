So I was reading about the hole argument in GR and something bugged me. Einstein worried that if his field equations are generally covariant, then take any solution, hollow out a matter-free region of spacetime (the "hole"), and you can smoothly rearrange the metric inside that hole while leaving everything outside untouched — giving you two solutions that agree everywhere outside the hole but differ inside it. Since the equations can't tell these apart, determinism seems to fail inside the hole. The standard fix (the "point-coincidence" idea) is that spacetime points have no identity apart from the metric and matter fields defined on them, so the two "different" solutions are really the same physical situation described twice.

Now compare that with a much older argument. In his Third Letter to Samuel Clarke (1716, part of the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence), Leibniz argues — paraphrasing here — that if space were an absolute container, then God shifting the entire material universe a few feet to one side, or swapping east and west, would produce a situation differing from ours in no observable respect, so God could have had no sufficient reason to create one rather than the other; Leibniz concludes space is nothing over and above the relations between bodies.

Compare these two arguments properly, please. On the surface both seem to say "these two descriptions differ only by a shift, so they're the same world". But is Leibniz's move really the same as the point-coincidence response? Consider at least: (a) Leibniz's argument leans on the Principle of Sufficient Reason (a premise about God's rational choice), whereas the hole argument is about determinism failing — does that difference in what the arguments are *for* change what the equivalence move has to accomplish? (b) Leibniz shifts *matter within* a fixed space, while the hole argument permutes the *metric field itself* — does that make Einstein's problem harder, easier, or just different? (c) Could a substantivalist accept the point-coincidence response to the hole argument while still rejecting Leibniz's relationalism, or do they stand and fall together?
