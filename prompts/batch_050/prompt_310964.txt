So I teach our department's grad seminar on decision theory, and this week is the infinite-payoffs week, which is always the best and worst week of the semester. We open with Pascal's wager, not the bumper-sticker part but a later bit of the wager fragment in the Pensées, where Pascal answers the objection that the chance of winning might be minuscule. His move, roughly: even if there were infinitely many chances and only one of them favored you, it would still be right to stake something finite, because the prize on the table is infinite and your stake isn't; and since you're forced to play whether you like it or not, refusing to bet isn't caution, it's just forfeiting the one chance you get.

Every year my best student asks why we can't run that same argument with the payoff structure rigged so the expectation doesn't even settle, and this year I finally gave in and built the case. Call it the Altadena game. A demon -- stipulate he's genuinely omnipotent and honest, none of the usual grumbling about whether anyone could actually make this offer -- flips a fair coin until it comes up heads. If the first heads lands on toss n, you win 2^n/n utils if n is odd and lose 2^n/n utils if n is even. Utils, not dollars: he pays directly into your welfare by demonic means, so the standard escape route (money has declining marginal utility, so any real-world version has a boring finite expectation) is boarded up. The expectation terms come out to 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., which equals ln 2 in that order and whatever you'd like in some other order, since the series doesn't converge absolutely. Entry costs one util, and you feel it.

Here's the proposition I want your credence on, as carefully as I can state it: for the Altadena game as described -- rational agent, unbounded utility, a demon who can really deliver -- there is no fact of the matter about what a fair entry fee is, and that's a defect in expected utility theory rather than in the game. My own credence is around 0.6. A colleague puts his under 0.1; he thinks some restriction (he keeps mumbling about only counting absolutely convergent expectations) handles everything, and that the game is just an elaborate way of being confused about infinity.

I don't want a bare number from you, I want the decomposition. Break the proposition into the sub-questions carrying the load -- candidates I'd float: whether unbounded utility is even coherent for a rational agent, whether restricting expectation to the absolutely convergent cases is principled or a patch, whether rearrangement-sensitivity is a genuine pathology or just weirdness we should learn to live with, whether the demon stipulation secretly makes the scenario incoherent and deflates the whole thing. Give each sub-question a number and one line of justification, then show how they combine into your final credence. Then, given my 0.6 and his 0.1, tell me which sub-question my colleague and I are most likely actually disagreeing about -- I suspect it's not the one we argue about over lunch. And flag which term in your own decomposition a single theorem or argument could move the most.

One last thing, and this is where it connects back to the passage. Pascal leans hard on the claim that you're obliged to play -- you can't decline the wager because you're already seated at the table. The mugger on the street at least lets you walk away, and I definately let my students walk away from the demon. If that "forced to play" premise is load-bearing for Pascal, does his argument survive cases like the Altadena game in a way that plain expected-utility maximization doesn't? Fold that in wherever it fits best.
