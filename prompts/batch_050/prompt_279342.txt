Alright, background: I'm a retired condensed matter physicist, got into philosophy about two years ago via a reading group at the library, and Pascal's wager has been bugging me for months because the reasoning looks exactly like the expected-value calculations I used to do all the time, and yet it seems to prove things that can't all be true. I wrote the argument out properly and I can't find the loose screw. Here it is.

(1) When you have to choose under uncertainty, the rational choice is whichever option has the highest expected value -- probability of each outcome times its value, summed up. This is just what we did in the lab deciding whether an experiment was worth running.

(2) Believing in God, if God doesn't exist, costs you something finite. Some Sunday mornings, some foregone pleasures, maybe being wrong. Finite.

(3) If God does exist, believing gets you an infinite payoff, and not believing forfeits it or worse.

(4) Any finite cost set against a positive probability of an infinite payoff gives you infinite expected value. So believing beats not believing, provided the probability of God existing isn't zero.

(5) The probability isn't zero. Nobody's proven God doesn't exist, and you don't get to assign probability zero to something just because you can't measure it. I've seen what happens when people do that with unlikely failure modes.

(6) So rationality requires believing in God.

Now the trouble. (7) Swap in a different god -- say one who damns exactly the people who believe in order to hedge their bets, and rewards everyone who believed for honest reasons or not at all. I see no reason this character gets probability zero either, and steps (2) through (5) go through word for word. (8) But you can't believe in both gods, so the argument now requires two incompatible things. (9) And separately, nobody can just *decide* to believe something at four o'clock on a Tuesday because a calculation told them to. I tested this on myself with the proposition that my old postdoc never messed with his error bars, which I very much wanted to believe, and wanting got me nowhere.

So (10): either expected-value reasoning is useless for the decisions that matter most, or rationality can require the impossible, or something in (1)-(5) is wrong. The first two I won't accept. Which premise goes? I'll warn you that (1) is my bread and butter and I'd rather give up almost anything else, but I want to know what each option actually costs -- if I drop (5), say, what am I commiting myself to about probabilities elsewhere, and if the problem is somewhere in how (4) handles infinities, I want to know why that move looked so innocent. Also tell me which of my premises the professionals fight over, because my reading group has been arguing about (9) for three weeks and I suspect we've got the wrong target.
