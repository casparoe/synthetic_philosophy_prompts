So I somehow ended up on the three-person contributor compensation committee of a mid-sized open source project (a data-processing library, a few thousand GitHub stars, a handful of paid maintainers). About a year ago we set up a bounty system to incentivize grunt work: fixed payments per merged pull request, per closed issue, and a bonus pool that unlocks if the open-issue count drops below 200. I've been reading rationalist blogs long enough that I literally joked about Goodhart's law in the meeting where we approved it, and yet it worked great for six months, so we stopped worrying. Then the weirdness started. Contributors began splitting one real change into five micro-PRs. People opened vague issues ("investigate possible edge case in parser") and then closed them themselves. Two of our most productive contributors quietly started sitting on large, complex PRs from newcomers because reviewing them takes unpaid time, while rubber-stamping each other's typo fixes within the hour. Our issue count is at an all-time low and our backlog of actual hard problems has never been worse. Classic.

At next month's meeting we have to pick a direction, and I got voluntold to write the decision memo beforehand. The options on the table: (1) scrap the bounty system entirely and go back to volunteer labor plus a flat maintainer stipend; (2) keep it but bolt on explicit anti-gaming rules (minimum PR substance, no self-closing issues, review time counts as compensated work, etc.); (3) keep the same budget but replace all metrics with discretionary quarterly awards decided by committee vote. One of my fellow committee members argues the whole episode proves that any quantified incentive in a creative domain gets gamed, so (1) is the only honest answer. The other argues what we saw was mostly a *specification* failure — people optimizing the letter of the metric rather than the spirit — which means better specification (2) is the fix, and that abandoning metrics just trades Goodhart failure for favoritism and vibes-based rewards, which is its own quieter failure mode.

Please write the memo for me. It should: recommend one of the three options (or a hybrid, if you can make it genuinely concrete rather than a mushy compromise); include a short framework distinguishing gaming that merely makes a metric uninformative from gaming that actively damages the underlying goal, because I think that distinction does real work in our case; give the committee a small set of diagnostic checks we can actually run next quarter to tell whether whatever we adopt is failing; and take seriously the strongest argument for whichever option you end up not recommending. Aim for 800-1200 words. Casual register is fine, we're not lawyers.
