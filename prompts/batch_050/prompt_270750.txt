Here's the exam question:

"In the debate over free will and punishment, Gregg Caruso argues that no one is basically deserving of punishment or blame, while Daniel Dennett defends a compatibilist account of moral responsibility. (a) Explain Caruso's argument against basic-desert moral responsibility and the alternative model of criminal justice he proposes. (b) Explain Dennett's grounds for rejecting the skeptic's conclusion. (c) Which account deals better with the practice of criminal punishment as we know it? Defend your answer."

And here's the student's answer:

Caruso argues that because free will is incompatible with both determinism and indeterminism, no one is ever morally responsible for anything they do. His reasoning is that if our actions are determined by factors beyond our control (our genes, our upbringing), we don't deserve blame, and if they're undetermined they're just random, which doesn't help either. Since basic desert requires ultimate origination and no one has that, retributivism -- punishing people because they deserve it -- is unjustified.

As an alternative, Caruso proposes the public health-quarantine model. On this model, we can incapacitate dangerous people the same way we quarantine people with contagious diseases: detention is justified purely by the threat a person poses, not by what they deserve, and so the state may hold them for however long they remain dangerous, without regard to whether the duration of detention is proportionate to their crime.

Dennett, by contrast, is a compatibilist. He thinks the free will worth wanting is compatible with determinism: what matters for responsibility is whether an agent has the capacity to respond to reasons, to be influenced by moral considerations, and to regulate their behavior accordingly. Because most adults have these capacities, Dennett concludes that retributive punishment is justified and that criminals genuinely deserve their punishments in the traditional sense.

Dennett also argues that without a system of responsibility-attribution, we couldn't maintain the moral practices that keep society functioning, so the skeptics' position would be self-undermining if adopted.

In my view, Caruso's account is better, because his argument shows that desert-claims are metaphysically groundless, and the quarantine model achieves everything the criminal justice system needs -- incapacitation, deterrence, rehabilitation -- without relying on a notion of desert that nobody can satisfy. If no one has libertarian free will, it follows that blame and moral anger are never appropriate responses to wrongdoing, and we should treat offenders the way we treat carriers of disease.
