Construct a variant of the Death in Damascus problem where causal decision theory and evidential decision theory stop both just shrugging and actually recommend different actions, and where EDT's recommendation looks borderline self-destructive. Some specs:

- No supernatural beings and no perfect predictors. Replace Death with something realistic -- a hit squad with a good behavioral profiler, a rival firm's pricing algorithm, an admissions model, whatever you like -- that forecasts the agent's choice well but fallibly (put an explicit number on its accuracy, somewhere in the 70-90% range).
- The forecast must be causally independent of the agent's action (already locked in before the agent decides) but evidentially correlated with it (the agent's act is evidence about what was forecast, via the forecaster's accuracy). This should fall out of the scenario, not be stipulated in so many words.
- Make the payoffs asymmetric across the two options, since thats what pries CDT and EDT apart instead of leaving both indifferent.

After the scenario, do the work: compute the expected utility of each action under each theory using your numbers, so the divergence is visible in the arithmetic and not just vibes. Then say which structural feature of the case does the prying apart, and what happens to the verdicts if the forecast is instead made *after* (or in causal response to) the agent's decision -- does the case collapse, and into what?

Finally, two pushbacks to address. First, the EDT defender will say CDT's recommended action is one the agent can foresee will probably get them killed, given the forecaster's track record -- why isn't that a reductio of CDT here? Second: in the original Damascus story the standard escape hatch is randomization. Say whether your agent can avail themselves of it (does flipping a coin break the evidential link?), which of the two theories can actually endorse doing it, and whether the availability of randomization changes which theory comes out looking better overall.
