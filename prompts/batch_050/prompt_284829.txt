I cover science and ideas for a mid-sized magazine, and I'm working up a piece on solar geoengineering -- stratospheric aerosol injection, marine cloud brightening, the whole menu. While reporting it I've noticed I have strong gut verdicts on cases that I can't seem to unify, and I want to stress-test my own instincts before I put anything in print. Here they are. (1) A small island nation facing reef collapse starts brightening clouds over its own waters unilaterally: feels clearly out of bounds. (2) The exact same intervention, same scale, but authorized by a global treaty the islanders helped negotiate: feels fine. (3) A wealthy country running the identical treaty-authorized program to protect its own wheat harvest: feels shadier than (2) even though the governance is the same. (4) A temporary, reversible field experiment that drifts over a neighbor's airspace without consent: wrong, even though it lasts a week. (5) Once a big program is underway, a participant that pulls out abruptly and causes rapid warming (termination shock) seems to me MORE blameworthy than whoever started the program. (6) A program explicitly sold as buying time for emissions cuts is acceptable to me, but the same physical program adopted as a substitute for cutting feels like a moral-hazard disaster -- even though the atmosphere presumably can't tell the difference between the two motives.

So: is there a single principle, or a small set of them, that captures all six verdicts -- something like procedural legitimacy plus motive plus some rule about ongoing commitments -- and can you state it precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it? I'm especially curious where the principle bites on cases I haven't listed: Does it say the islanders in (1) would be justified if they first asked the world and were refused? Does it make intent legally or morally load-bearing in (6), and is that defensible given that intent is hard to observe? And be honest about which of my verdicts a coherent principle can't save -- I suspect (3) and (6) are the wobbly ones, since both seem to rest on judging purposes rather than acts, but tell me if there's a way to keep them without the account collapsing into "it's fine when I approve of the reasons." Please write the answer in American English.
