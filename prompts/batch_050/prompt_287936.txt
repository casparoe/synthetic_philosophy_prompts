I'm in a philosophy of physics seminar this semester and we've been going back and forth about what's "real" in gauge theories. For my final project I want to write a dialogue, but I figured I'd try getting a draft started here first before I revise it.

Write a dialogue (in American English) between two grad students, Maya and Dan, arguing about whether gauge theories contain "surplus structure" and what that means for what physically exists. The setup: they both just read about the Aharonov-Bohm effect, where electrons pick up a phase shift when traveling around a solenoid even though they never touch the magnetic field itself. Maya thinks this shows the vector potential (or something like it) is physically real, since the effect happens in a region where the electric and magnetic fields are zero. Dan thinks the potential is just redundant mathematical scaffolding -- lots of mathematically distinct potentials describe the same physical situation, so none of them can be the real thing -- and he'd rather reformulate everything in terms of gauge-invariant quantities, like the holonomies (phase shifts around closed loops), which he says are the only genuinely physical content.

Some things the dialogue should actually do:

- Both of them should be equally sharp. Dan should know that his holonomy approach has costs (the quantities are nonlocal, defined on loops rather than points, which is weird for a supposedly local physics), and Maya should have to face the underdetermination problem head-on rather than brushing it off.
- They should respond to each other's actual arguments, not just restate their opening positions. Like, if Maya says the effect needs a local cause and Dan points out that holonomies are nonlocal anyway, Maya should have a reply to *that*, not just repeat "locality demands the potential."
- At some point they should grapple with whether their disagreement is empirical at all or just a fight about which formalism "deserves" to be called real -- and it would be great if they at least gesture at what would settle it (or conclude that maybe nothing could, but for an interesting reason, not just shrugging).
- Around 800-1200 words, natural conversational register (they're grad students talking after seminar, not textbook authors). No need for citations or references to specific philosophers -- keep it self-contained.

Thanks!
