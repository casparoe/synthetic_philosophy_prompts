I design the recommendation system for a mid-sized video platform, which means my job is, in effect, to infer what people want from what they do. Lately I have noticed that my working assumptions at the office do not line up with how I judge the people in my actual life, and I would like to know what principle, if any, I am operating with. Here are my verdicts on a set of cases.

One: a user clicks a lurid video, watches nine seconds, closes it, and never touches that channel again. I do not think the click shows he preferred watching it. A friend of mine does the same thing at one in the morning and tells me afterward he wishes he had not; I believe him, and I do not take the click to reveal what he really wants in any sense that matters.

Two: our platform rarely surfaces documentaries, so most users never click one. I do not think we can infer from this that users prefer short clips to documentaries. You cannot read a ranking off a choice when one of the options was effectively never on the menu.

Three: my brother insists his children are the center of his life, but he volunteers for every weekend shift and missed his daughter's recital for a client dinner. I find myself thinking his behavior reveals his actual priorities, whatever he says. I notice this sits uneasily with the first case: in both, avowal and behavior diverge, and in one I side with the avowal while in the other I side with the behavior.

Four: a close friend with depression keeps canceling plans she was plainly looking forward to. I do not think her staying home reveals a preference for solitude; the behavior seems to me to track the illness, not her wants.

Five: a colleague who smokes has quit four times and relapsed four times. My verdict is not that her behavior settles what she prefers, but that the question has no single answer: at some moments her conduct reveals a preference for smoking, at others a preference for being quit, and neither timeslice strikes me as the real one.

Six: a traveler pays seven dollars for a bottle of water at the airport. The economist in me says this reveals she valued the water above seven dollars. I think it reveals something much weaker: she preferred the water to the money given a menu with no drinking fountain in the terminal. Treating the purchase as evidence about how much she values water in general seems wrong to me.

Seven: most of our users never change the default settings, including settings that are plainly worse for them. I do not read this as revealing a preference for the defaults at all, though some of my colleagues treat every unflipped toggle as a revealed endorsement.

Is there a principle that captures all of these, or nearly all? My own attempt runs something like: behavior reveals a preference only among options the agent understood to be available, under conditions where the choice was genuinely hers. But I am not confident it survives my own cases — the one-in-the-morning clicker understood what he was doing, and it was his choice; and I cannot say what my brother failed to understand. So: state the principle, or two or three candidate principles, precisely enough that I could implement it; say which of my verdicts it vindicates and which it forces me to abandon; and apply it to two cases I have not raised — a user who leaves autoplay running for six hours while asleep on the couch, and a voter who chooses the lesser of two candidates he despises. Finally, if the best principle really is mine after repair, tell me what it implies about what my platform ought to be optimizing.
