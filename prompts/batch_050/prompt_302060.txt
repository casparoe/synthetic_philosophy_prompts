I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, and I fell down a wiki hole about the surprise exam paradox (teacher announces there'll be an exam one day next week and the students won't be able to predict which day; the students run backward induction and "prove" it can't happen; then it comes on Wednesday and everyone is suprised anyway). Here are the resolutions I keep running into, so please don't hand me one of these:

1. The announcement is true but can't be *known* by the students — if they knew it, the backward induction would go through and destroy its own truth, so the teacher can sincerely say it but the students can't know it.
2. Self-reference: the announcement formalizes into something liar-adjacent, a diagonal/knower-paradox-style contradiction, so the paradox is a symptom of impredicative self-reference and not really about exams at all.
3. Vagueness/sorites: "unpredictable" is vague, the one-day case is genuinely impossible, and the rest is borderline-case mush.
4. Iterated knowledge failure: the induction needs the students to know that they know that they know... the announcement, to depth n, and that stack of knowledge collapses somewhere.
5. Equivocation on "surprise": "can't deduce the night before" vs "can't deduce from the announcement on Monday morning" are different properties and the paradox swaps them mid-argument.
6. The truncation move: after ruling out Friday, the teacher never actually announced the shortened 4-day version of the puzzle, so re-running the induction on the remaining week uses a premise nobody asserted.
7. Epistemic dynamics: the announcement changes what the students believe, which changes what the teacher can get away with, and backward induction freezes a situation that is actually shifting under the reasoner's feet.

Honestly the whole thing smells like an off-by-one error in the induction to me, but I can't pin down where. Give me one more resolution that is NOT on this list — not a rewording, a special case, or two of these stapled together. Then be straight with me about whether it actually works.

Format, sections in this order: "The pitch" (~100 words, state the resolution cleanly), "Why it's not just 1-7 again" (~150 words, differentiate it wherever there's overlap risk), "Stress test" (~200 words, run it against the 1-day, 2-day, and 5-day cases), "Your verdict" (~50 words, does it survive or not).
