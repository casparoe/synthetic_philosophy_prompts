I'm a novelist and I run a monthly workshop out of my living room for other fiction people, about 8 of us, mostly literary stuff, some crime. Last session we got into a genuinely heated argument about one member's draft where a character has to choose between betraying her sister or letting an innocent man go down for something. Half the room said "it's not a real dilemma, one option is clearly right and she's just a coward," the other half insisted some situations are genuinely impossible -- you do wrong whichever way you go. I realized we were talking past each other because nobody had ever actually *felt* the difference between a hard choice and a trap. So I want to burn one whole session (90 minutes) on a structured activity that makes us feel the strongest argument that real dilemmas exist: the leftover. After you choose, the guilt and the urge to apologize or somehow make it up to the person you wronged doesn't feel irrational the way it should if you'd merely done the painful-but-correct thing.

Design the activity for us. Staged forced choices with small real stakes (snack budget, favors owed, who's stuck hosting next month, whatever works), and some mechanic where the residue actually shows up in play -- I keep picturing tokens for "making amends" that never quite cover what you did, but do something better if you've got it. The rules should make the leftover emerge on its own rather than me lecturing about it afterwards. Then give me the debrief: map specific moments in the game onto the two readings (residue-as-leftover-obligation vs. regret-is-fitting-even-in-clean-cases), tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run this with opinionated writers and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the philosophical issue the game structurally can't reproduce. Whole reply under 500 words, and dont start with a summary of the debate -- just give me the activity.
