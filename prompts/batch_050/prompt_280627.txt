I teach a first-year undergrad moral philosophy class, about 22 students, 75 minute slot, and we're hitting free will and moral responsibility. The problem: every year I present Galen Strawson's Basic Argument (you're responsible only for what flows from how you are, so true responsibility would require having chosen how you are, and that choice would itself have to flow from how you already were, regress regress) and they just nod along like its a homework proof. Nobody feels it. So I want a runnable activity, not a lecture.

I've been sketching a mechanic I call the dossier game: each small group gets a fictional person who did something clearly blameworthy, assigns a blameworthiness score out of 10, then the facilitator hands out "upstream cards" one at a time revealing facts about how that person came to be the way they are (upbringing, a brain thing, whatever), and groups can revise their score after each card. The idea is they start demanding the next card themselves and notice the regress is coming from them, not from me. But I'm stuck on two things: whether to also include what I'm calling "swerve cards" that introduce a genuinely uncaused, random moment into the person's deliberation (I want them to feel that this doesn't rescue responsibility either), and how to structure the scoring so groups actually argue rather than just drift toward zero.

Give me the full design: concrete rules, the card sequence, a timeline for the 75 minutes, the debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the premises of the argument, the ways this will predictably go wrong (e.g. a compatibilist revolt, someone insisting the swerve card fixes everything) and how to handle each, and an honest note on which feature of the Basic Argument the game can't reproduce so I have to supply it myself. Don't name Strawson or the argument until the debrief -- the students shouldn't hear the terminiology beforehand.
