Since retiring from a career in isotope geochemistry I have been attending a philosophy reading group run by our university's extension programme, and this term we have been working through semantic externalism — the thought experiments in which a speaker's environment or community is varied while everything in the speaker's head is held fixed, and one is asked what the speaker's words mean. My friend Derek, a retired classics master with alarmingly confident intuitions, has been pronouncing verdicts all term, and I have been quietly writing them down, because I suspect he is running an implicit theory and I should like to reconstruct it. One caveat from my former life, which you may treat as context rather than as part of the puzzle: in my laboratory we spoke without hesitation of heavy water, D2O, as water, so I am independently doubtful that molecular formula alone fixes what a word means; but Derek has not yet been confronted with that example, and I mention it only so that you know where my own sympathies lie.

Derek's verdicts so far: first, on the standard twin-planet case, where another world's lakes and taps run with a liquid superficially identical to water but chemically quite different from H2O, the twin speaker's word does not mean water — the two speakers mean different things by the same sound. Second, I myself cannot tell an elm from a beech, yet when I say "elm" I mean elm and not beech-or-whatever-it-is; the meaning of my word, Derek says, is secured by the experts of my linguistic community to whom I would defer. Third, a woman who has lived her whole life alone on an island, with no community whatsoever, pans an unfamiliar metal from a stream and coins a word for it; Derek was firm that her word refers to that metal — no community is required. Fourth, the twin planet also contains objects indistinguishable from our pencils, manufactured and used exactly as we manufacture and use ours; those, Derek says, simply are pencils, and "pencil" means the same thing on both planets. Fifth, asked about jade — which, as I happen to know from my mineralogical colleagues, comprises two chemically distinct minerals, jadeite and nephrite, both answering to the name — Derek held that "jade" does not name a single natural kind but rather whatever the jewellers' practice accepts as jade. Now the new case, which I intend to put to him at next week's meeting: an Earth chemist emigrates to the twin planet without knowing that its liquid differs from H2O, settles there, and over forty years becomes fully integrated — she shops, teaches, and sits on the local standards board, using the word "water" exactly as her neighbours do, in constant contact with their liquid and never with Earth's. Does her word "water" now refer to the local liquid, or does it still mean H2O, so that she has been systematically mistaken for four decades about what flows from her own tap?

What I should like from you: first, a prediction of Derek's verdict on the emigrant's case, with reasons drawn from his record rather than from the published literature; second, a reconstruction of at least two distinct rules, each compatible with all five of his verdicts, which diverge on the new case — stating precisely what each rule predicts about it; third, your judgement as to which rule best fits his overall pattern, or an honest declaration that the record underdetermines the choice, in which case I should like you to describe the further case — the sixth verdict I ought to solicit from him — that would discriminate between the two rules. And if, on examination, no single rule accommodates all five verdicts, please say which verdict Derek would have to sacrifice in order to achieve consistency, and why it should be that one.
