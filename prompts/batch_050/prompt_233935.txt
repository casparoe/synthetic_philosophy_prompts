I'm a PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on decision principles behind the veil of ignorance, and I've become convinced that the Rawls–Harsanyi dispute is stuck in a way that neither side's defenders seem to notice. The arguments have been stable for decades: Harsanyi holds that a rational person who assigns equal probability to occupying each social position will maximize expected utility, which yields average utilitarianism; Rawls replies with his three conditions for maximin (grave risks, outcomes below a threshold one cares little about gaining above, and probabilities that are either unknown or not worth estimating), plus the strains-of-commitment point that parties must choose principles they can honor even if they turn out worst-off. Both arguments are sixty-plus years old, both are well understood, and as far as I can tell nobody has switched sides in living memory. The debate just gets re-staged, sometimes with new dressings (prioritarianism, sufficientarianism, risk-weighted expected utility), but the core standoff persists.

Here is my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not a conclusion: the dispute looks like a disagreement within decision theory but is actually two disagreements running on different tracks. Track one is genuinely technical — under ignorance, is Laplacean equiprobability legitimate, and does the separability/linearity structure of expected utility survive when the "states" are persons rather than possible worlds? Track two is about what the original position is for in the first place — a device for deriving principles that actual people could reasonably accept and stably comply with (Rawls's framing), versus a formalization of impartial benevolence for an ideal observer (Harsanyi's framing). If the second track is doing the real work, then the first-track arguments are mostly rationalizations, and technical fixes like introducing ambiguity aversion or dropping the sure-thing principle won't settle anything because they answer a question the parties weren't really asking.

I'd like you to respond as a specialist in political philosophy and decision theory who has watched this literature for years. Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is: verbal, substantive-but-empirical, substantive-but-normative, or two questions being answered under one label. Support the diagnosis with what the two sides actually argue (paraphrase is fine; don't invent citations), including the strongest objections each has lodged against the other. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me concretely what would count as progress: a theorem (e.g., a characterization result distinguishing maximin from expected utility), a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding from psychology or experimental philosophy, or a frank admission that the two devices of representation answer different questions and should be disentangled. Finally, assess my two-track hypothesis directly — does it capture the structure, or am I missing where the real action is?
