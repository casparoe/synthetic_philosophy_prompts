I'm a staff software engineer at a company that's been around long enough to have real history -- two failed rewrites, a founder-era codebase everyone mythologizes, and a graveyard of deprecated services nobody dares touch. The vocab I actually think in: git history, tech debt, deprecation cycles, postmortems, refactor vs rewrite, legacy systems, code archaeology.

Restate Nietzsche's three kinds of history from the second Untimely Meditation -- monumental, antiquarian, critical -- in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. For each one: what it's for, what its characteristic failure mode is, and who in an org embodies it (the founder-lore enthusiast who thinks every problem deserves a heroic rebuild, the person keeping every legacy box alive on life support, the one whose postmortems re-litigate every architectural decision made before they joined). If the unhistorical and the suprahistorical have engineering analogs, give me those too.

Then the part I actually care about: what didn't survive? Nietzsche measures history against life rather than accuracy -- the question is always whether a way of relating to the past helps or hampers the thing that's living now. Does anything in my vocabulary play that role (velocity? team health? the ability to ship at all?), or does the standard get quietly swapped out for something narrower in translation? Take a definite position here: either engineering vocab is missing something real that Nietzsche had, or his talk about serving life was doing less work than it appears. Don't hand me a menu of readings and tell me to pick -- commit and argue for it.

Also, one caution at the end: where does the analogy mislead even where it works?
