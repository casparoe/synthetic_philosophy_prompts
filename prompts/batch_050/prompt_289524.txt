I teach an upper-level seminar on game theory and social institutions, and I'm writing the final exam. Every year I set a question on multipolar traps / Moloch-type dynamics, and every year half the class just regurgitates the Scott Alexander essay with the god-of-failure imagery and the ten examples, which tells me nothing. So this year I want to force actual analysis.

Here's the seed I want the question built around. I keep noticing that some competitive dynamics look structurally identical to races to the bottom but push in the opposite direction: every individual competitor, acting in their own interest, keeps escalating something costly, and the equilibrium is exhausting for everyone, yet the "bad" outcome isn't deregulation or corner-cutting but over-investment in something the participants would all vote to cap (think: college admissions prep, academic publishing arms races, athletes and doping -- though doping cuts both ways, which is itself interesting). I've been calling this a "race to the top that feels like a race to the bottom" and I genuinely dont know if it's the same game with relabeled payoffs or a structurally different game. I want an exam question that makes students settle this, using one fresh case of my choosing and one of their own.

Draft the question (they get 90 minutes, 40 marks, and it's closed-book), then give me:

1. The question (one paragraph, self-contained, no jargon the students haven't seen).
2. A model answer, roughly 600-700 words, that takes a definite position on whether the two dynamics are the same game and defends it.
3. A marking scheme built around six must-hit moves, with marks each summing to 40, and a one-line note per move on the most common way candidates fumble it.
4. A short section on what separates a first-class answer from a merely good one -- the single move that, if a candidate makes it, almost guarantees a first.
