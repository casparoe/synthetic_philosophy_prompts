I've been lurking on formal epistemology threads for years, and the same argument keeps recurring: what, exactly, do Dutch book arguments establish? Two of my interlocutors have agreed, at my prompting, to try an adversarial collaboration, and I want to draft the joint statement they should be aiming for. Party A defends the classical pragmatic reading in the Ramsey–de Finetti tradition: an agent whose credences violate the probability axioms is susceptible to a sure loss, and that susceptibility is itself the defect — incoherence is irrational because it makes you exploitable. Party B thinks the sure-loss result proves the wrong thing: being beatable is a practical misfortune, not an epistemic failing, and the real justification of probabilism is accuracy-dominance arguments of the Joyce sort, which show that non-probabilistic credences are dominated by probabilistic ones with respect to accuracy, no bets required.

Write the document this collaboration should produce. It should contain: a numbered list of claims both parties can sign — I expect these to include that de Finetti's theorem is a purely mathematical result that neither side disputes, that both accept probabilism as a genuine rational requirement on credences, and whatever they agree about the standard objections (the problem of agents who don't value money linearly, the gap between the synchronic theorem and any diachronic norm, whether the betting set-up smuggles in pragmatic assumptions); then a disagreements table in which each residual dispute is stated in wording the side it describes would accept, particularly the question of whether susceptibility to sure loss is constitutive of incoherence's defect or merely a symptom of it; then the cruxes — the sub-questions whose resolution would actually move at least one party, such as whether accuracy-dominance arguments covertly depend on the choice of a legitimate scoring rule in a way parallel to how Dutch books depend on a utility function, and whether either framework can handle an agent who simply refuses to bet.

For each crux, name a concrete case or argument both parties accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each predicts it will show. If any part of their disagreement turns out on inspection to be verbal — for instance, if they mean different things by justifying probabilism — identify which part and say why. Both parties must come off as equally competent; neither is to be cast as the foil. Keep the whole document under 800 words.
