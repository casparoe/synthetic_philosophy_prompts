Ok so some background: a few months ago I looked back at my old spotify wrapped lists and old texts with friends and realized my taste in music, my politics, even the kind of humor I like have all shifted pretty hard over the last 4-5 years. And the unsettling part is I genuinely cant tell how much of that was me just growing as a person and how much was feeds and recommendation algos nudging me the whole time. I've been reading up on this and there's apparently a real distinction people draw between two failure modes:

- influence-seeking systems: something is actively steering you, optimizing for engagement or purchases or whatever, and your changing preferences are partly its doing
- slow value drift: nobody is steering at all, but a person or a whole culture just gradually slides somewhere new anyway (like how attitudes toward smoking or seatbelts changed), and you'd feel exactly the same from the inside

The thought I keep going in circles over: from first-person view, these seem indistinguishable. And they might call for totally different responses (fight the algorithm vs. accept that people change).

Write me a short story, maybe 1500-2500 words, that lives inside this problem. Requirements:

- The protagonist should be someone for whom the distinction becomes practically important, not just interesting -- e.g., they have to decide whether to trust some new conviction of theirs enough to act on it (quit a job, leave a relationship, confront a friend), or whether to intervene in someone else's drift (a parent watching their kid change).
- Include at least one scene where the protagonist tries to run some kind of test or experiment to figure out whether they're being steered or just drifting, and the test fails in an interesting way. This scene is basically the heart of what I want, so make it concrete and clever, not a paragraph of musing.
- Please, no twist where it turns out an evil AI or mustache-twirling tech exec was behind everything. The version where nothing is behind it should feel just as uncanny, maybe more.
- Don't end with a clean answer to which one it was. But do end with the character doing *something* -- I want to see what a decision looks like when you can't resolve the question it's premised on.

If it helps: I'd rather have a quiet, literary, near-present-day setting than cyberpunk. Think someone scrolling their phone at 1am in a normal apartment, not neon and implants.
