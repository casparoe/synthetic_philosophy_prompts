Last month I finished three weeks of jury service on a civil trial that turned almost entirely on testimony: no documents, no recordings, just four witnesses whose accounts conflicted, and many hours in the deliberation room arguing about whom to believe and why. Since then I have been reading about the epistemology of testimony, and I keep returning to a passage from Part I of Section X ("Of Miracles") of David Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding:

"It will be sufficient to observe, that our assurance in any argument of this kind is derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human testimony, and of the usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses. It being a general maxim, that no objects have any discoverable connexion together, and that all the inferences, which we can draw from one to another, are founded merely on our experience of their constant and regular conjunction; it is evident, that we ought not to make an exception to this maxim in favour of human testimony, whose connexion with any event seems, in itself, as little necessary as any other. Were not the memory tenacious to a certain degree; had not men commonly an inclination to truth and a principle of probity; were they not sensible to shame, when detected in a falsehood: Were not these, I say, discovered by experience to be qualities, inherent in human nature, we should never repose the least confidence in human testimony. A man delirious, or noted for falsehood and villany, has no manner of authority with us."

I understand that this passage is usually read as the classic statement of what is now called reductionism about testimony, in opposition to the anti-reductionist view associated with Thomas Reid, but I would like the dispute explained properly rather than in slogan form. In particular, could you address the following?

First, set out what each position actually claims. Reduction to what, exactly — and does the reductionist hold that testimony merely transmits justification originally earned by perception, memory, and induction, or something stronger? And what does the anti-reductionist mean by treating testimony as a basic source, on a par with perception rather than derivative of it?

Second, I am puzzled by the phrase "our observation of the veracity of human testimony." Whose observation? If it means each hearer's own first-hand checks of reports against facts, the basis seems astonishingly thin — I have personally verified only a minuscule fraction of what I believe on others' say-so, and it is hard to see how I could even classify reports into reliable and unreliable kinds without already relying on what others have told me. If instead it means the community's pooled observations, then my access to that pool is itself testimonial, which looks circular. Is this the crux of the anti-reductionist critique (as pressed by later writers such as C. A. J. Coady), and what is the reductionist's best reply — for instance, the move from a global to a merely local reduction?

Third, a worry in the other direction: does the anti-reductionist's default entitlement to accept testimony amount to a license for gullibility? Notice that Hume's own final sentence — the delirious man and the known liar have no authority with us — is something a Reid-style anti-reductionist could surely accept, since the view concerns the default, not indefeasibility. If both sides agree about defeaters, what exactly remains in dispute?

Finally, an application: in our deliberations, my fellow jurors discounted one witness partly because she seemed nervous, and credited another partly because a third party vouched for his character — note that the vouching was itself testimony. How would each side describe what we were doing, and is ordinary legal practice (oaths, cross-examination, corroboration requirements) implicitly committed to one of these views?

I would like an answer of roughly 700 to 900 words, pitched at a reader with some philosophical background but no prior acquaintance with this literature.
