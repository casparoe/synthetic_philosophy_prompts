Here's a thing I keep noticing in conversation and I can't find a word for it. My boss will say "no pressure, but it would be great if this was wrapped up by friday" -- and it works exactly like an instruction, everyone in the room treats it as one, I now have an obligation I didn't have ten seconds ago, but it only works because it isn't officially one. If she said "I instruct you to finish by friday" it would land worse, not better, in our supposedly flat workplace. Same with a friend going "you'd never last a month without coffee, let's be honest." That's functionally a challenge, and I did in fact go a month without coffee to spite him, but the moment I say "nice try, that's a dare and I'm taking it," the whole thing deflates and he can shrug and claim he was just making an observation. So the act seems to succeed only on the condition that nobody names it as the act it is. Which feels backwards: I dimly remember from a linguistics course that for promising, christening, apologizing etc., the hearer has to grasp what you're doing for the doing to come off at all. My cases seem to come off precisely when the hearer *doesn't* grasp it, or at least doesn't let on. I know about the familiar indirect request thing, like "could you open the window" meaning open the window, but that's different, those are *meant* to be recognized as requests. And it's also not just a psychological effect the words have on me, the way flattery makes me like someone -- these feel like real doings (ordering, daring) with real obligations attached, not vibes. Is there an established name for this? I want the closest existing concepts, a clear account of how each one misses the thing I'm pointing at, and, if nothing fits, a coined term with a definition plus a couple of boundary cases (does it still count if the hearer sees through it but plays along? does a threat dressed up as friendly advice qualify?). Please answer as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, with no introduction and no conclusion.
