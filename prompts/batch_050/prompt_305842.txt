I chair the forecasting review panel of a policy research institute. Roughly once a quarter we have to rule on a long-horizon model whose predicted date has come and gone without the predicted outcome — usually scarcity or collapse projections in the spirit of Malthus: a mechanism (a quantity growing geometrically against one growing arithmetically, a fixed resource against rising demand) that implies crisis within a few decades. Whenever we propose retiring such a model, its defenders give the same reply: the mechanism is sound, only the timing was off, because of a factor the model could not reasonably have incorporated — yield improvements, substitution, a demand shift, a policy response. I need a decision procedure the panel can run in a half-day session that produces one of three verdicts (retire, revise, retain) that our board will sign off on. It should give the questions to ask in order, say what counts as an adequate answer to each, and — the hard part — state a defensible line between a legitimate rescue (the missed factor genuinely changes the timing but not the mechanism's implications, and the revised model makes a sharper, sooner prediction than the original) and an immunizing move (each failure is absorbed by an ad hoc addition that makes the model compatible with any future outcome, so the prediction is never really at risk). It should also say how to weigh a model that has survived by successive revisions against a simpler competitor that has needed none, and where the procedure must stop and hand the verdict to judgment because the question is about what a theory even claims rather than about the evidence. Then run it on two cases: a grain-collapse model that failed twice because yields kept rising, with its authors proposing a third revision that folds in a diminishing-returns curve; and a freshwater-scarcity model that failed because its users switched to a substitute input the model treated as fixed, with its authors arguing the mechanism simply transfers to the substitute. At least one of the two should come out as retire despite a plausible-sounding defence. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
