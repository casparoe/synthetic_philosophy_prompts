I recently picked up a popular philosophy book on how philosophers argue -- the sort of thing aimed at curious general readers rather than students -- and one chapter is devoted to Daniel Dennett's notion of an "intuition pump" and his broader views on thought experiments. I've never read Dennett myself, so I can't tell whether the chapter is doing him justice or quietly mangling him. Here is its central paragraph:

"Dennett coined the term 'intuition pump' to describe the whole genre of philosophical thought experiments -- the trolley problems, brain vats, Chinese Rooms and zombie twins that fill lecture halls. His provocative claim was that these devices are not arguments at all but rhetorical machines, carefully engineered to make a predetermined conclusion feel obvious by exploiting the quirks of our imaginations. Because a thought experiment can be rigged simply by choosing which details to mention and which to leave out, Dennett concluded that intuitions elicited in this way carry no evidential weight whatsoever, and that philosophy would make faster progress if it abandoned armchair thought experiments in favour of empirical work. He applied this verdict most famously to Searle's Chinese Room, which he took to be the paradigmatic case of an intuition pump designed to mislead."

On its face this hangs together, and it matches my vague impression of Dennett as a debunker of armchair philosophy, which is part of why I'm suspicious -- it's a little too tidy. A friend who did a philosophy degree insists Dennett was himself a prolific inventor of thought experiments, which seems hard to square with the chapter's claim that he wanted the genre abandoned. So I'd like an accuracy audit. Go through the paragraph claim by claim: which parts would Dennett's readers accept as fair, which are defensible simplifications, and which misrepresent his actual position -- for instance, how he related intuition pumps to thought experiments generally, what he thought the good ones could achieve, and what exactly his objection to the Chinese Room was. Where the chapter distorts him, explain what a more careful statement would look like, and whether the distortions matter for the book's larger conclusion that thought experiments are propaganda rather than philosophy.
