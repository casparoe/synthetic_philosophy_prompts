I run a reading group on AI safety for philosophy postgrads -- about 14 people, mostly new to the technical literature, one 75-minute session. We've just done the basic inner/outer alignment distinction and I can tell it's gone in as vocabulary rather than as a problem. They can say "the learned goal may differ from the specified goal" but they don't feel why this is supposed to be hard to fix, since in their heads there's an obvious right goal and a sufficiently clever training process would just find it.

Design an in-class activity where the participants themselves play the role of a learning system and end up misgeneralising: they're trained on examples where the "true" objective and some proxy are perfectly correlated, they get rewarded on the proxy (or on something ambiguous between the two), and then in a deployment round the correlation breaks and it comes out that different people in the room have internalised different goals from identical training data -- all defensible, none correctable from the data alone. The point I want them to land on viscerally is that the training signal underdetermines the goal, and that this isn't a matter of them being stupid or the training being slapdash.

Give me the full thing: the actual task (rules, materials, example stimuli -- something runnable with index cards or pen and paper, no computers), how you rig the training phase so the ambiguity is there but invisible, how the deployment phase reveals the divergence, and the payoff or scoring scheme so there's a small real stake. Then a debrief that maps each moment of the game onto the concepts: behavioural vs intended goals, the difference between a training failure and a generalisation failure, why more of the same training data wouldn't have helped. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (people will presumably try to guess the trick rather than play straight -- how do you handle that without spoiling it?), and be honest about which features of real goal misgeneralisation the activity can't reproduce -- presumably something to do with the fact that my participants are reasoners who can be told things, whereas the worry is about goals baked in by gradient descent.

Be committed in the design: make definite choices about the task, the stimuli and the timing and give your reasons, don't hand me a menu of three possible games and leave me to pick. And please write it in British English.
