Evolutionary psychologists have long described the human mind as a Swiss army knife: not one general-purpose problem solver, but a bundle of specialized tools — a blade, a corkscrew, a can opener — each shaped for a particular recurring task. On the standard telling, the blades are dedicated psychological mechanisms (a cheater-detection device, a face recognizer, a fear-of-spiders module, a language faculty), the knife's maker is natural selection, and the tasks the tools were built for are the recurring problems of ancestral environments. The analogy is then put to several uses at once: to argue that cognitive science should look for domain-specific modules rather than one general learning engine; to explain modern quirks — our craving for sugar, our phobias of snakes rather than cars — as tools deployed in an environment they were not built for; and to rebut the charge that evolutionary explanations are just-so stories, since a well-designed tool, like a can opener, reveals its function by its fit to the task. A friend of mine, a developmental psychologist, retorts that the mind is less like a Swiss army knife than like the immune system: a flexible, self-organizing system that builds novel responses to novel threats on the fly, so that the knife picture has simply smuggled in the adaptationism it was meant to support. Could you audit the knife analogy properly? Lay out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the knife-maker, the tool's intended function, the fixed inventory of blades — then identify where the mapping breaks: designed tools have known makers and purposes, but selection has neither; tools do not alter each other during development, but neural systems do; a knife's tools are cleanly separable, while putative modules interact and overlap. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for each of the three uses above from those that do not, assess whether my friend's immune-system picture actually escapes the same criticisms or merely relocates them, and say which conclusions, if any, the analogy can carry once the design imagery is stripped away — including whether the just-so-story worry is created by the analogy rather than dissolved by it.
