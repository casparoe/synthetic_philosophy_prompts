I am a novelist, two hundred pages into a multigenerational novel set in postwar Germany and the present day. The plot turns on a claim I have committed to dramatically and now want to stress-test philosophically: that a nation itself — not merely the individuals who acted in its name — can be guilty of a historical wrong, and that this guilt is in some genuine sense inherited by later generations who participated in nothing. On my view, the shame my young protagonist feels about her grandfather's crimes is not a psychological confusion or an inherited misfortune; it is the recognition of a real moral fact, and it grounds real present obligations — to remember, to make amends, to refrain from certain kinds of national self-congratulation. I am working with the strong reading of this position, not the weaker claim that descendants merely inherit burdens or benefit from injustice. I would put it in the neighborhood of Jaspers's insistence that political guilt implicates everyone in a polity, but pushed further: guilt as a property of the collective, persisting across generations, attaching to persons who bear no individual fault.

Act as a professional philosopher with expertise in collective responsibility and the ethics of memory. Think carefully, and reason from general philosophical competence rather than trying to recall particular texts. Before producing any structured output, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming: what kinds of things could in principle count against a position of this kind — metaphysical results about the nature of groups, cases where collective-guilt talk misfires, consequences my position is committed to that may prove unacceptable, ways it might collapse into the weaker beneficiary view it defines itself against.

Then tell me what would count against my position. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which of them a committed defender of collective guilt would herself concede as genuine threats, as opposed to what only critics press. I also want an honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated by qualification until it risks nothing — and if it has, whether that is a vice in a view of this sort. As a novelist I am most moved by phenomenology: cases where the felt texture of inherited shame or pride seems to demand, or to refuse, the collective reading.
