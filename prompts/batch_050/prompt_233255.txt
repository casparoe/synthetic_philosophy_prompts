I am a PhD student in philosophy working on the limits of expected utility theory, and I am hoping you can help me settle a dispute I witnessed between two colleagues at a workshop last week. I will first describe the case precisely, then reconstruct their exchange as faithfully as I can, and finally explain what I am asking for.

The case is a version of what is sometimes called the Pasadena game. A fair coin is tossed repeatedly until it lands heads. If the first heads occurs on toss n, the player receives (or, if negative, must pay) an amount equal to (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n dollars. So with probability 1/2 the game ends after one toss and the player gains $2; with probability 1/4 it ends after two tosses and the player pays $2; with probability 1/8 the player gains $8/3; with probability 1/16 the player pays $4; and so on. The expected value, if one writes it out in the order the tosses occur, is the series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., which converges to ln 2. But the positive terms alone sum to infinity and the negative terms alone sum to minus infinity, so the series is only conditionally convergent: by rearranging its terms, the same sum can be made to come out to any real number whatsoever. On the standard definition, then, the expectation does not exist.

My two colleagues agreed on all of the mathematics. Their disagreement was about what follows for rational choice. Here is the exchange as I remember it.

The first colleague, whom I will call A, opened by saying that the case simply marks a boundary of the theory. Expected utility theory gives verdicts where expectations exist; where they do not, it is silent, and that silence is no more an embarrassment than the fact that arithmetic is silent about five divided by zero. A rational agent, she said, is not required to have a preference over every pair of options, and the correct response to being offered the game at some price is not a mysterious indifference but simply the recognition that the theory delivers no verdict either way.

The second colleague, B, replied that this was evasive. Being offered the game is a genuine choice situation: you must play or decline, and declining at every price is itself a policy, one that looks plainly bad. After all, the player ends up with a positive payoff with probability 2/3 (just sum the probabilities of the odd-numbered tosses), so a policy that refuses the game even when offered at a negative price, i.e., even when the house pays you a dollar to play, seems irrational on its face. A theory whose silence forces such a policy is not modest; it is broken.

A answered that B was conflating two things. The theory's silence does not force the policy of always declining; it merely fails to rule it out. And the 2/3 point, she said, is exactly the kind of reasoning the paradox is designed to punish: it attends to the probability of winning while ignoring the magnitudes, and the magnitudes here are unbounded in both directions. She added that there is at least one principled repair available: if utility is bounded, as diminishing marginal utility of money independently suggests, then the expectation exists and the problem evaporates. So the paradox shows at most that expected utility theory requires bounded utility to be fully general, a constraint many theorists accept on independent grounds.

B then made what struck me as his strongest move. He said that even granting unbounded utility, the game does have a rationally privileged value, namely ln 2. The order in which the terms are summed is not arbitrary: it is fixed by the physical description of the game, toss by toss. Riemann's rearrangement theorem shows only that some artificial reordering of the series yields a different sum; it does not show that the natural ordering yields the wrong one. We might as well say that because the natural numbers can be put into a one-to-one correspondence with the integers in many ways, there is no fact about which set is larger. The expectation is the sum in the order the outcomes are generated, and that sum is ln 2.

A's response was quick. Temporal order, she said, is not value order. Nothing in the setup makes a payoff worth less because it arrives after more tosses; no discount rate has been stipulated. Each outcome contributes to the value of the gamble solely in virtue of its probability and its payoff, and what the rearrangement argument shows is precisely that when both the gains and the losses are infinite, there is no order-independent total of those contributions. To privilege the toss-by-toss ordering is to treat a bookkeeping convention as if it were a fact about value. If ordering really mattered to the worth of a gamble, then two gambles with identical outcome-and-probability structures, differing only in the temporal sequence in which contingencies resolve, could differ in value, and she took that to be absurd.

At that point the session chair cut them off, and they never resolved it. Neither budged, and I found I could not locate a decisive error on either side, which unsettles me because they cannot both be right.

Could you adjudicate their exchange point by point? Specifically, I would like a verdict on each of the following: (1) whether A is right that a rational agent need not have a preference over every pair of options, or whether B is right that the inescapability of the play-or-decline choice makes silence untenable; (2) whether B's probability-2/3-of-winning observation has any real force against a policy of declining, or whether A's magnitude objection fully defuses it; (3) whether the bounded-utility repair genuinely dissolves the paradox or merely changes the subject; and (4) whether B's ln 2 proposal succeeds in identifying a privileged ordering, or whether A's distinction between temporal order and value order defeats it. In each case, please say not just who is closer to the truth but exactly where the losing side's reasoning goes wrong, if it does. Finally, is there something both of them are missing -- an assumption they share that the paradox actually calls into question?
