I’m in high school and our physics teacher went on a tangent about dispositions, finks, and masks, and now I can’t stop thinking about them. As I understand it, a fink is when the test itself makes the thing stop having the disposition, while a mask is when the disposition is still there but something blocks the result. I might be messing that up, but here are my gut verdicts:

1. A crystal glass is packed in custom foam. If I drop the package, the foam absorbs the impact and the glass survives. I still think the glass is fragile.

2. A similar glass has a smart coating built into its surface. Whenever an impact starts, the coating instantly turns rubbery before the force reaches the glass, so it survives. I want to say the coated glass is not fragile anymore.

3. A phone has a separate transparent shield over its screen. The shield hardens whenever something hits it, so the screen never cracks. Weirdly, I still want to say the phone screen is fragile, even though case 2 felt like the whole coated glass stopped being fragile.

4. A capsule contains a toxin in one compartment and an antidote in another. When swallowed, the antidote is always released slightly faster, so nobody gets poisoned. I think the toxin itself is still poisonous, but the capsule as a whole is not dangerous.

5. Another capsule contains the same toxin with a coating that chemically changes it into a harmless substance as soon as it touches stomach fluid. I think this toxin is not poisonous anymore, or at least the capsule definitely isn’t.

6. A firework has perfectly good explosive powder, but its fuse is soaked, so putting a flame to it just chars the fuse without lighting the powder. I still think the firework is explosive. This one bothers me because maybe the wet fuse means the usual trigger never actually happens, rather than masking the explosion.

7. My friend can juggle three balls easily when we’re alone, but whenever someone formally asks her to demonstrate, she gets nervous and drops them immediately. I want to say she can juggle, even though asking her to do it reliably causes her to fail.

8. A car’s engine could physically reach 100 mph, but the car has a built-in limiter that cuts fuel at 80 mph. I think the engine can reach 100, but the car as built cannot. This seems a lot like case 3, except now I’m splitting the part from the whole.

My rough principle is something like: if the trigger changes the thing itself so the basis for the disposition is gone, the disposition disappears; if the thing stays the same and an outside blocker merely prevents the outcome, the disposition is only masked. But this seems too simple. In case 2, the glass itself arguably stays unchanged while the coating changes. In case 3, I split the screen from the phone-plus-shield. In case 7, the anxiety is internal, so I’m not sure whether it is an outside blocker, a fink, or just a different trigger.

What principle, or small set of principles, best captures these verdicts? Please state it precisely enough that I can apply it, and say which cases motivate each part. If saving all eight verdicts requires an overly complicated principle, tell me which verdict you would overturn instead. Also, how should the account handle the fact that we can describe the same test differently, like breaking when dropped versus breaking when enough force reaches the glass? It seems easy to cheat by choosing whatever description gives the answer we wanted.

Finally, what should the principle say about these new cases: red paint that turns gray whenever anyone looks at it and red again when unobserved; a bridge that would collapse under ten tons but has emergency supports that catch it every time; and a sugar cube that instantly repels water on contact so it never dissolves? Please don’t just answer that it depends on context — I want the actual rule and where it bites the bullet. I know this is basicly reflective equilibrium or something, but I’d like the cleaned-up version.
