I run a small decision-theory reading group that grew out of the rationalist blog scene — most attendees are software engineers and a couple of grad students who have read the usual online canon and absorbed the slogan version: violate the axioms and a clever bookie drains your wallet, therefore rationality is expected utility maximization. Our next session is on the coherence arguments themselves (Dutch books, money pumps, representation theorems), and I want to replace that slogan with something sturdier. Nobody in the room has formal philosophy training, but everyone is comfortable with elementary probability and won't flinch at a payoff table.

What I need from you is not the explanation itself but a plan for my ninety minutes. Lay out the order in which the pieces have to be introduced and why — specifically, whether the axioms on preferences should come before or after the betting setup, and what has to be in place before the inference from "exploitable" to "irrational" can even be stated without begging the question. Then settle a choice I'm stuck on: should the carrying example be a money pump run on cyclic preferences, or a Dutch book run on incoherent credences? Pick one, tell me what the example must contain and what it must scrupulously avoid (stakes small enough that risk aversion can't be confused with incoherence, for instance), and explain why the other one fails as the centerpiece even if it works as a footnote.

I also want a misconception map: the errors this particular audience will predictably form, and the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off rather than letting it harden. Candidates I anticipate: that the theorem proves rational agents maximize expected money; that the axioms (especially completeness and transitivity) are just obviously true of any sane person; that the arguments show something about morality rather than about internal consistency; and the savvy contrarian error, popular in our circles, of dismissing the whole thing because real agents can't be forced to take bets — I want that one addressed on its merits, not waved away. Finally: a single question I can pose at the end whose answer would reveal whether the idea actually landed, and a short list of what to deliberately omit at this level (continuity axioms, the von Neumann–Morgenstern versus Savage distinction, St. Petersburg-style infinite payoffs), with your advice on whether I should tell the group I'm omitting them. Please write in American English.
