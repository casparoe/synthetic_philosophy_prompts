I teach philosophy of mind, and after a department colloquium last week two of my collegues got into a genuinely interesting fight over drinks about whether "the brain is a computer" is even a substantive claim. I stayed out of it, partly because I couldn't decide who was winning, and I'd like you to referee for me.

The first, Dan, argued the claim is either trivially true or empty. His line was the old triviality worry: for any physical system and any computation, you can cook up some mapping from the system's states onto the computation's states, so a rock, a wall, whatever "implements" whatever program you like. If that's right, saying the brain computes is saying nothing, because everything computes everything. The second, Rosa, who works with the cog sci people across campus, said this is way too fast. The triviality result only goes through if you allow gerrymandered mappings that carve up physical states however you want, with no regard for causal structure. Actual computational claims in neuroscience aren't like that -- when someone says visual cortex implements edge detection, they're committed to counterfactual-supporting mappings, and the claim is falsifiable: record the neurons, stimulate them, lesion them, and the mapping either holds up or it doesn't. Dan replied that even the respectable version smuggles in semantics. What makes some neural activity a representation *of edges*, rather than just a causal relay in a bigger circuit? Until we have a naturalized account of content, which computation a system implements is fixed by the interpreter's interests, and the triviality worry just comes back at that level instead. Rosa's comeback was that this standard would sink all of biology, not just computationalism. "The heart is a pump" is also interest-relative in the same thin sense -- nobody's confused about what counts as pumping only because nobody's asking. If a functional description earns its keep through prediction and intervention, it's a real claim about the world, and we don't have to solve the content problem first. Dan's last move, before we got kicked out at closing time, was to say fine, but then "the brain is a computer" collapses into "the brain has a functional organization," which literally nobody denies, including dualists. The strong claim computationalists actually want -- that cognition just *is* computation, that a good enough simulation would be a mind -- doesn't follow from the weak reading she's defending. So the claim is either trivial (her version) or undefended (the version that matters).

Please go through the exchange move by move and tell me who's right about what -- not just a final verdict, but where each of them lands a real hit and where each is overreaching. And if there's something both of them are missing (I have a nagging feeling the whole dispute trades on an ambiguity neither named), say what it is.
