Since retiring from a career in geophysics, I have been reading around the question of why societies come apart — Diamond's book on collapse, work on the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya, Rapa Nui — and I keep tripping over the same two figures of speech, which appear to be doing argumentative work rather than merely decorative work.

The first treats a society as an organism: civilisations are said to be born, to mature, to age and to die, so that the end of the Classic Maya or the Bronze Age palace system is a death, and the question becomes what killed them. The second treats a society as a structure: it collapses, like a building or a loaded beam, when stresses exceed what the design can bear, so the question becomes where the fault lines were and what triggered the failure. My colleagues in the sciences reach for the second instinctively; historians seem to favour the first; and both pictures get used, often by the same author in adjacent chapters, to support the conclusion that these societies failed. Yet there is a dissenting view — which I find attractive but cannot yet assess — that the Maya did not die but changed, that the descendants of those polities carried on speaking Mayan languages and farming the same land, and that calling the episode a collapse smuggles in the judgment that a society is only alive while its elite institutions, monumental architecture and writing system are intact.

What I would like is an audit of both analogies. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which analogy, if either, can bear the weight put on it — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. For each analogy, set out the explicit mapping (what in the society corresponds to the organ or the load-bearing member, to metabolism or stress, to death or structural failure), then identify where the mapping breaks. I am particularly interested in what each picture quietly imports that the target lacks: organisms are bounded individuals with a definite lifespan and a clear moment of death, whereas societies have fuzzy boundaries, no natural term, and members who survive the end of their institutions; buildings fail all at once or not at all, whereas a society can shed a layer of hierarchy and continue. Say which of these disanalogies matter for the conclusion each analogy was used to draw, and which are merely incidental. Finally, tell me whether, once the disanalogies are on the table, either analogy can still support the verdict that these societies failed rather than transformed — and if neither can, whether there is a better comparison available, or whether the very concept of collapse is an artefact of a misleading metaphor and the honest vocabulary would be one of redistribution and discontinuity rather than of death and ruin.
