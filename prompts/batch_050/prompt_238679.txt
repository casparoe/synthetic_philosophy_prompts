You are an epistemologist with particular expertise in the structure of justification and the theory of reliable belief-formation. I have a question about a pattern I keep noticing in reasoning about trustworthy sources, and I'd like you to work through it carefully: begin by brainstorming the candidate concepts this pattern might fall under, compare them, and only then commit to your best answer.

Here is the pattern, in three instances.

First, a weather station installs a new thermometer. To validate it, the technicians compare its current readings against the station's logbook of recorded temperatures — a logbook that was itself kept using that same thermometer. After a year of near-perfect agreement, they conclude the instrument is accurate.

Second, a friend assures me her memory is excellent. When I ask how she knows, she lists several occasions on which she remembered something complicated and got it right — occasions she is, of course, recalling by means of the very faculty in question.

Third, a workshop has two pressure gauges. Gauge A was once adjusted to match gauge B; gauge B, some years earlier, had been adjusted to match gauge A. A technician observes that the two now agree closely and concludes that both must be accurate.

What strikes me about these cases is that none of them looks like an ordinary circular argument. Nobody states the conclusion as a premise. The thermometer case arguably involves no argument at all, just a procedure. And the third case contains no single source vouching for itself — only a loop of two sources, each leaning on the other. Yet all three feel defective in the same way, and I lack the vocabulary to say precisely what the defect is.

My questions: Does this pattern have an established name? If several nearby concepts exist, explain each one, say which fits best, and whether my three instances all fall under the same concept or should be split apart. Does the two-device loop differ epistemically from the one-device cases, or is the length of the loop irrelevant? And does repetition change anything — if the thermometer agrees with its own logbook every day for ten years, does the mountain of agreeing data add any genuine support, or is it just more of the same?

If some part of the pattern turns out to be nameless, propose a term for it, with a definition and a few boundary cases. Be advised that I won't be satisfied with a bare label: I want the carving itself — what exactly the defect is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
