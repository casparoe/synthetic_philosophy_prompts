I'm on the ethics committee of a regional hospital, and I need a short written opinion I can circulate before our next meeting. Here's the situation. Our discharge planners have an unwritten practice of treating the nearest adult child as the default caregiver when an elderly patient can't manage alone. That's now blown up in a case: a 79-year-old man with moderate dementia needs daily support at home, and his only daughter, who lives twenty minutes away, flatly refuses. She says he was cruel to her throughout her childhood, that she's had almost no contact with him for thirty years, and that whatever a stranger would owe him is what she owes him. Half the committee thinks family obligation doesn't depend on the relationship being good -- he's her father, full stop -- and that we should keep pressing her to take at least partial responsibility. The other half keeps citing an idea one member picked up from a philosophy paper: that grown children don't owe their parents debts of gratitude for sacrifices made decades ago, only what an ongoing friendship would call for, and where no friendship exists the duty is roughly what you'd owe anyone in need, no more.

The opinion should recommend a concrete policy (do we keep defaulting to the adult child, do we treat estrangement as disqualifying, or something in between?) and say what the discharge team should actually do in this specific case. Please take the strongest argument for the losing side seriously rather than strawmanning it, since whichever way we go, half the committee will need persuading.

End the opinion with a single line starting "Recommendation:" that states the bottom line.
