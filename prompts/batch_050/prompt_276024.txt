For my high school philosophy club's project on how scientists can learn things without doing experiments, I need help designing an original scientific thought experiment built to order, along with a demonstration that the design actually works.

Please construct a thought experiment about the physical world that meets all of the following specifications:

1. It uses only everyday, classical physics — objects like ropes, carts, fluids, pendulums, mirrors, and levers. No relativity, no quantum mechanics, no idealized infinite systems, and nothing a careful high school student could not picture concretely.
2. It must yield a genuine piece of knowledge: the conclusion should be a non-obvious claim about how the world must behave, not merely a definition or a tautology repackaged, and not something we could only learn by actually building the apparatus and measuring.
3. It must isolate exactly one variable. There should be a single feature of the setup whose alteration flips the conclusion, while everything else stays fixed — and you should be able to point to it precisely.
4. It must be novel. Do not use Galileo's falling bodies, Newton's bucket, Einstein's train or elevator, Maxwell's demon, or any other standard example from the literature; the point of the project is to see whether the recipe generalizes to fresh cases.
5. The scenario should be physically possible in principle, even if impractical — no frictionless planes conjured by stipulation without explanation of why the idealization is harmless.

After presenting the scenario, please do the following:

- Give a step-by-step reconstruction of the reasoning, in the spirit of John Norton's claim that thought experiments are just arguments: show that each step is either a background empirical premise we already accept or a piece of pure reasoning, and that no hidden empirical input sneaks in.
- Then test the reconstruction: is there any step that resists being written down as an argument and seems to require a non-inferential grasp of a physical necessity, as James Robert Brown's Platonist account would predict? State honestly which of the two accounts your case fits better, or whether it sits awkwardly between them.
- Identify the single load-bearing feature from specification 3, and describe what happens to the thought experiment if it is removed or replaced — does the conclusion collapse, become trivial, or become empirically testable in an ordinary way?
- Finally, play devil's advocate: explain how a skeptic who thinks thought experiments can only ever repackage what experience already taught us would attack your case, and give the strongest reply you can.

I want the demonstration that the specifications are met to be as rigorous as the scenario itself, since my project is about whether the recipe for generating knowledge from armchairs actually transfers to cases nobody has used before.
