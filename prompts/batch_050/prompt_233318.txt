So I keep coming back to this passage from chapter 3 of Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859):

"We behold the face of nature bright with gladness, we often see superabundance of food; we do not see, or we forget, that the birds which are idly singing round us mostly live on insects or seeds, and are thus constantly destroying life; or we forget how largely these songsters, or their eggs, or their nestlings, are destroyed by birds and beasts of prey; we do not always bear in mind, that though food may be now superabundant, it is not so at all seasons of each recurring year."

What interests me is that Darwin isn't just describing ecology here, he's making an epistemic claim: our ordinary experience of nature is a biased sample, because the deaths and the hungry seasons happen where and when we aren't looking. Here's my question, and I want an actual number, not "it's complicated." Given that bias -- i.e., taking seriously that what we casually observe in nature overrepresents the good moments -- what's your credence that the typical wild vertebrate's life contains more suffering than wellbeing, such that it would have been better for that animal never to have existed?

Decompose it for me rather than giving me a bare number. I figure it has to ride on at least: (a) how much of the "hidden" part Darwin points to actually involves suffering vs. quick, low-distress deaths, (b) whether brief lives ending badly can still be net positive if the middle was fine, i.e. how you weight duration vs. endings, (c) whether most wild animals even have the kind of valenced experience that could add up to a net positive or negative life, and (d) how much Darwin's "we forget" observation should shift us away from the sunny default picture in the first place -- maybe the deaths are visible but the ordinary contentment of fed, safe moments is what's actually undercounted. Give a rough probability and a one-line justification for each piece, say how they combine into your final number, and tell me which sub-question your answer is most sensitive to and what evidence would move it most. Also say somewhere whether you think Darwin himself, in this passage at least, is committed to anything about welfare, or whether he's just doing demography and we're the ones smuggling in the ethics.
