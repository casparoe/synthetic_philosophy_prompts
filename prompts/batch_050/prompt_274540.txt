Question: In Death and the Afterlife, Samuel Scheffler defends what he calls the afterlife conjecture. Explain what the conjecture says, and what it is meant to show about why human extinction would be bad in a way that goes beyond the deaths it involves. Do you think it succeeds?

And here's the student's answer:

Scheffler's afterlife conjecture holds that our capacity to value things rests on an implicit assumption that there will be a collective afterlife -- that other people will continue living after we ourselves die. He argues this assumption matters more to us than our own personal survival, and brings this out with a doomsday scenario: if you knew an asteroid would destroy humanity shortly after your own natural death, much of what you care about now (cancer research, conservation, sustaining institutions and traditions) would lose its point. He makes a similar point with an infertility scenario in which no one can have children anymore and the final generation lives out its days knowing humanity ends with them. So the conjecture shows extinction is bad because it deprives the future generations who would otherwise exist of good lives, a harm over and above the deaths of the people alive when it happens. I think it succeeds, because it establishes that extinction is bad even if no one ever learns its coming -- the badness doesnt depend on anyone's awareness of it.
