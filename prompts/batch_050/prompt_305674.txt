Hi — I teach ancient Greek political thought at a small liberal arts college, and I'm applying for one of our internal summer research grants (about $6k, one summer, expected output is a journal article). I have a half-formed idea I need turned into a proper one-to-two-page proposal. It's about the greatest hits of Athenian democratic failure: the Mytilene decision, the Sicilian expedition, and the trial of Socrates. The textbook story is 'mob rule' — the demos gets swept up by a demagogue or by fear and does something monstrous. But here's what's been bugging me since I last taught Thucydides: the Mytilene case breaks the pattern. The same assembly, composed of basically the same people, voted to massacre an entire allied city one day and then reversed itself the very next morning, with nothing having changed except that the question was debated again with a serious opposing speech. Meanwhile in the Sicily debate the assembly heard a sober warning from Nicias and it backfired — his attempt to scare them off by demanding a huge force turned into a reason to send an even bigger armada. And the Socrates trial wasn't even the assembly at all, it was a jury court, which people conveniently forget. So the crude 'passionate crowd' hypothesis can't be the whole story, because it predicts the wrong result for the most famous case of the bunch.

So the project: figure out what actually distinguishes the cases where Athenian deliberation self-corrected from the ones where it amplified its own errors. Is it time pressure? Whether a dissenting speaker got a genuine platform rather than being mocked off the bema? The type of decision — punishing people already at your mercy, versus committing to a future course of action, versus judging an individual in a quasi-legal setting? Or does the Socrates verdict turn out to be a different failure mode entirely, and if so, does that tell us the 'failures of democracy' framing lumps together things that shouldn't be lumped? In the proposal I need: the question stated tightly enough that it could actually be answered in one summer; why the obvious explanations (mob passion, demagoguery, absence of experts) don't settle it, with the Mytilene reversal doing the heavy lifting there; the method — I'm imagining a structured comparison of the three episodes plus some explicit counterfactual reasoning, e.g. what would have had to be different in the Sicily debate for it to go the way Mytilene did; what a successful deliverable looks like; and, honestly, what would count as the project failing — if the answer comes out as 'each case is sui generis,' is that a result or a non-result? Also flag anything in the plan that secretly requires skills I don't have — I can't learn formal epistemic-democracy modelling in a summer, and I'd rather the proposal admit its limits than overpromise.
