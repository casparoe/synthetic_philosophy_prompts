Last night I had a long and unresolved argument with a friend, following a reading group on classical Chinese thought, about whether the Daoist ideal of wu-wei is a genuine paradox or only an apparent one. My friend holds that the paradox is real and fatal: if wu-wei means acting without deliberate effort, then any attempt to attain it is self-defeating, since striving toward effortlessness is itself effort; worse, the practices recommended for cultivating it seem to install precisely the self-monitoring that spontaneity is supposed to exclude, and the moment one checks whether one has achieved the state, one has lost it. On her view, the ideal is either incoherent or attainable only by accident, which amounts to giving up on it as an ideal. My reply was that skilled performance shows effort can genuinely recede: touch-typing, driving, musical improvisation, and athletic flow all begin as laborious, attention-hungry activities and end up fluent and unselfconscious, and the exemplars in the Zhuangzi are precisely masters of this kind — Butcher Ding, whose blade stays sharp for years because he no longer forces his cuts, is a trained expert, not a man doing nothing. The paradox, I argued, trades on treating trying as a single thing operating at a single level, when in fact effort at one stage can buy effortlessness at a later one. She countered that motor automaticity is not the global unselfconsciousness wu-wei demands, and that the second-order problem — deliberately trying to become the sort of person who does not try — survives everything I said about skill.

What strikes me, on reflection, is that we never really joined issue, and I would like a diagnosis of why this debate stalls rather than a verdict on who is right. My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the disagreement is partly verbal: the two sides individuate trying differently, one treating it as homogeneous across orders of control, the other as stratified so that higher-order effort can be consumed in producing lower-order ease. But I suspect two further fault lines. First, translation: wu-wei is built from a negation and a verb meaning to do or act, and rendering it as non-action manufactures a starker paradox than renderings like effortless or spontaneous action, so part of the dispute may be an artifact of which English word one silently adopts. Second, evidence: my friend's case rests on first-person phenomenology of self-monitoring, mine on third-person psychology of expertise, and we never agreed on which kind of data bears on the question.

So: what kind of disagreement is this? Is it a concealed empirical question about skill acquisition and attention that cognitive science could in principle settle, a clash of starting intuitions about self-reference that neither side can argue the other out of, or two different questions sharing one name? Please engage what each side actually says, as I have reported it, rather than surveying the literature, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction between levels of control, an experiment on expert performance, a formal model of self-defeating goals, or a reframing of the question — or, if you think no progress should be expected, explain what it is about the structure of the dispute that guarantees that.
