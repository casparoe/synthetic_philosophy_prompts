I am a novelist, and for some years I have been turning over Susan Wolf's well-known argument that the ideally moral person — the moral saint, whose every waking hour is organized around the welfare of others — is not an ideal any of us should aspire to realize. Wolf leans partly on taste: the saint lacks the hobbies, the irony, the unapologetic appetites that make a person worth knowing. As a novelist I find this persuasive for a reason Wolf leaves underdeveloped, and I would like an essay that develops it and then tests it to destruction. My hunch is that fiction functions here as a kind of evidence. Perfectly virtuous characters are notoriously hard to write — readers and critics have long observed that the flawed and the vicious carry novels while saints sit inert on the page — and I want to claim that this is not merely a craft problem but a discovery: narrative interest tracks exactly the particularity and inner tension that maximal goodness crowds out, so the novel's cold shoulder to its saints is a datum supporting Wolf. Write an essay on whether maximal moral goodness is desirable, organized around this question: is the unreadability of perfectly virtuous characters genuine evidence that moral sainthood is an unattractive ideal, or does it show only what stories need — conflict, appetite, flaw — and therefore nothing about what lives are worth living? Weigh both sides seriously, including the awkward fact that some beloved characters, like Dorothea Brooke, approach saintliness without narrative death, but reach and defend a verdict, because I intend to stake a character on it.
