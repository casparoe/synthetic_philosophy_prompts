So I've been reading around the debate about whether numbers should count in rescue cases -- Taurek's side of it, the idea that when you can either save five strangers or one stranger but not both, you shouldn't just automatically save the five, because that would be valuing the one person less. Each person, the thought goes, has an equal claim to be saved, so maybe you should flip a coin, or at least it's permissible to save the one. I've actually come around to this view. The aggregative picture where you add up lives like utility points always seemed to me to disrespect the separateness of persons, and Taurek's framing -- that to the person who dies, it doesn't matter how many strangers were saved instead of them -- hits me as basically right.

Here's my problem though: I got here mostly by vibing with the arguments, and I want to stress-test it properly before I commit. So tell me: what would count against the anti-aggregation, numbers-don't-count position? Not the lazy objection "but obviously five deaths are worse than one" -- I want the real exposure map.

A few things I specifically want covered:

1. Cases where the view seems forced into saying something wild. E.g., one person vs. a million -- does the Taurekian really flip the coin there, and is that a defeater or just a bullet? What about smaller stakes: one person loses a finger vs. one person dies, where even Taurekians presumably think you save the life. Once you allow harm magnitudes to matter, is there a principled line that keeps numbers out? Where does the view actually crack?

2. The instability worry: if I give each of the six people an equal 1/6 chance, the group of five gets a 5/6 chance collectively -- so haven't numbers snuck back in through the lottery itself? Is the equal-chances procedure secretly a form of aggregation, and if so, does the position collapse into a weighted-lottery view or something worse?

3. Whether the view even handles ex ante cases -- small risks imposed on many vs. certainty of death for one -- or whether it only makes sense for "each will certainly die unless saved" scenarios. If it can't generalize, is that a fatal scope problem or fine?

4. What the position's own defenders (Taurek himself, or later sympathetic writers) would concede counts against it, vs. what only critics treat as objections. I want the insider's list, not just the standard rebuttal list.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say honestly whether you think the position is well insulated and whether that insulation is a virtue or a sign it's been made unfalsifiable. Oh and tell me which defeater you personally find strongest -- I suspect it's the lottery one but I might be wrong about why.
