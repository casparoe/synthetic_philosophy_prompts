I'm a philosophy professor and, for my sins, chair of our department's graduate admissions committee this year. Five of us read roughly sixty files, and our rule for admitting a candidate is conjunctive: the writing sample has to be strong, the letters have to be strong, and there has to be genuine fit with someone on the faculty willing to supervise. Last cycle we stumbled into what I belatedly realized was a textbook discursive dilemma. On one candidate, a majority said yes to each criterion separately, but when I polled people on the bottom-line question "should we admit?", three of five said no -- because the members who liked the writing sample weren't the ones who liked the letters, so almost nobody individually thought all three conditions held. We ended up admitting her based on the criterion-by-criterion tally, and one colleague is still annoyed, and honestly I'm not sure he was wrong to be. Meanwhile a different candidate failed one criterion by a 2-3 vote even though every single member, on reflection, said they'd be happy to have her in the program, which is the same trap from the other side.

I know the broad strokes of the judgment aggregation literature -- premise-based vs. conclusion-based procedures, why majority voting on connected propositions can go incoherent -- but what I don't have is something I can actually hand to four colleagues who have never heard of any of this and will not read anything longer than a page. So: write me a one-page decision procedure for the committee. It should specify, in order, what we vote on and when (criteria first? bottom line first? both?), how to handle the case where the two tallies disagree, when to defer to the premise-level vote vs. the conclusion-level vote and what principled reason I'd give my annoyed colleague for whichever rule we adopt, what to do about abstentions and members who only skimmed the file, and where the procedure should just stop and force an actual discussion because the disagreement is about what the criteria mean rather than about the candidate. I'm especially interested in whether there's a defensible general rule for premise-vs-conclusion or whether it has to depend on the kind of decision -- we also run essentially the same conjunctive scheme for awarding our one dissertation fellowship, where the stakes and the epistemics feel different, so if the answer is "different procedure for different decisions," say why.

Please show the procedure working on two or three hypothetical candidates, including at least one where it spits out a result I will personally hate (e.g., rejecting someone the whole committee likes, or admitting someone nobody likes), and be honest about which steps are genuine aggregation logic and which are just committee-management judgment calls wearing a procedures's clothing. We're busy people; if the procedure takes more than about ten minutes per borderline file, no one will follow it, so simplicity is a real constraint, not a nicety.
