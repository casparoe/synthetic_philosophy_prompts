After forty years working as an experimental physicist, I have in retirement been reading my way through the philosophy of law, and I have found myself increasingly vexed by the courts' treatment of what I gather is called naked statistical evidence. The standard examples are familiar: the bus company that operates eighty percent of the buses on a given route and is sued on that basis alone when an unidentified bus injures someone; the thousand-seat stadium with five hundred gatecrashers, so that any randomly selected spectator was more likely than not to have entered without paying. My scientific instinct is to say that probability is probability — if my instruments told me a particle had passed through detector A with probability 0.8, I would not hesitate to draw the inference — and I confess my first reaction was that the law's reluctance is simply innumeracy dressed in Latin. A philosopher friend tells me I am wrong and that there is something deeper going on, but when pressed he retreats to gestures about the law needing to treat people as individuals, which I find unsatisfyingly vague.

Please argue, seriously and at length, for the position my friend only gestured at: that there is a genuine epistemic defect in verdicts resting on purely statistical evidence, one that does not reduce to the unreliability of the statistics themselves. To keep the argument honest, stipulate throughout that the numbers are as well-established as anything in my former field — the bus company's market share is exact, the gatecrasher count is known precisely, and in the hardest variant I want you to confront, an environmental contaminant has been traced by a rigorously validated model to one of two factories with probability 0.8, with no further particularized evidence available or possible. Your argument must do three things. First, it must identify what the missing ingredient actually is, rather than merely naming it (saying the evidence lacks individualization only relabels the problem). Second, it must answer the objection that all evidence is ultimately statistical — eyewitnesses err, fingerprint matches have false-positive rates, DNA evidence is reported with likelihood ratios — so that a principled refusal of naked statistics threatens to swallow ordinary proof entirely. Third, it must explain whether the argument cuts differently against civil liability, where the standard is only the preponderance of the evidence, than against criminal conviction, or whether the defect is the same in both.

I do not want a survey of possible views with the verdict left to me. Take a definite position — either defend the courts' instinct as rationally grounded, or concede to me that my physicist's intuition was right all along and that the resistance is superstition — and commit to it through the hardest cases, including the two-factory scenario and the question of what, on your view, actually distinguishes a DNA match from a market share. If your position generates a consequence you find uncomfortable, such as licensing or forbidding liability in some real class of cases like toxic torts, say so plainly rather than smoothing it over.
