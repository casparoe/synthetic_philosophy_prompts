I am a PhD student in political economy, and I recently witnessed a dispute between two colleagues about whether advances in artificial intelligence settle the socialist calculation debate. I would like you to adjudicate their disagreement point by point.

Mara argued that modern computing answers the classic objection in Mises and Hayek. A planning authority, she said, could gather fine-grained data on inventories, production capacity, consumer choices, and shortages; use iterative optimization to generate shadow prices; and continually revise plans in response to observed demand. Lange’s trial-and-error mechanism was impractical with mid-century bureaucracy, but contemporary machine-learning systems can process vastly more information than a central board once could. She added that Mises’s argument proves too much: large corporations already coordinate complex production through internal transfer prices, forecasts, and algorithmic inventory management without holding an internal auction for every resource.

Jonas replied that she was treating prices as if they were merely a compact way of transmitting pre-existing information. On the Hayekian view, market prices also emerge from rivalry among actors with residual claims, and entrepreneurial competition discovers opportunities that no one previously knew to record in a database. If the planner controls property rights, budget constraints, and the criteria of success, then its simulated prices measure responses to constraints the planner has already chosen. They therefore cannot provide the same independent test of whether resources are being shifted from lower- to higher-valued uses.

Mara answered that this conflates economic calculation with the political selection of ends. A socialist system could still permit autonomous worker-managed firms to bid for capital, experiment with products, fail, and be reorganized, while public ownership determined the broad distribution of wealth and investment. Machine learning could support that discovery process rather than replace it. She also questioned whether capitalist profit-and-loss accounting is as epistemically pure as Jonas implied: firms receive subsidies and bailouts, consumers make choices within institutionally shaped option sets, and financial markets often reward control and speculation rather than social usefulness.

Jonas conceded that real capitalist markets are institutionally constructed, but said the relevant distinction is not pure markets versus constructed ones. It is whether unsuccessful projects impose consequences on those who control them. A democratic planning authority would face pressure to rescue strategically important firms, protect employment, and avoid admitting mistakes. Because firms and consumers would anticipate such rescues, their bids and reported preferences would incorporate expectations about political intervention. The planner’s data would then partly reflect its own inability to commit, so better computation would not solve the problem.

Mara’s final response was that soft budget constraints, regulatory capture, and implicit guarantees are endemic under capitalism too. Jonas had therefore identified a general institutional problem, not a decisive objection to economic planning. The real question, she said, is which ownership structure can most credibly combine experimentation, failure, dispersed initiative, and public accountability.

Please assess who is right about each specific claim, distinguishing the logical, empirical, and institutional issues. In particular, say whether artificial intelligence addresses Mises’s calculation objection, Hayek’s knowledge-and-discovery objection, both, or neither; whether Mara’s analogy to planning inside large corporations succeeds; whether Jonas’s residual-claim and commitment arguments depend on private ownership rather than merely effective competition; and what minimal institutions a computational socialist economy would need before this dispute could be settled empirically.
