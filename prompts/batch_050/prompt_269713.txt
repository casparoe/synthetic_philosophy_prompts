So I've been reading ACX for years and one thing that keeps nagging at me is that I work for a mid-size development NGO, and about once a month someone in a meeting -- or a paper we're citing -- explains an outcome by pointing at culture. Countries industrialized because of a Protestant work ethic, this region is poor because of a culture of honor, that program failed because of local attitudes toward time, whatever. Sometimes these feel right, sometimes they feel like just-so stories that soak up whatever evidence shows up. Weber's thesis is the original here and I know the usual back-and-forth (Tawney's reverse-causation complaint, the awkward fact that plenty of Catholic regions got rich, etc.) but I've never seen it turned into something usable.

Please write me a decision procedure I can actually run when a culture-explains-the-outcome claim lands on my desk: the questions to ask in order, what a satisfying answer to each looks like, and where the procedure should just say "this is story-telling, not explanation." Things I want it to handle at minimum: how to tell a values-as-cause claim apart from values-as-justification for something already happening; what would have to be true for the claim to survive the reverse-causation worry; how to deal with the fact that "culture" is usually measured after the fact and unfalsifiably flexible; and when a cultural explanation is legitimately the best one left standing even if it can't be proven. Mark the points where the procedure stops and it's a judgment call. Then run it on two cases: the classic Protestant-ethic-explains-capitalism claim, and a hypothetical where a colleague attributes a failed microfinance rollout to local distrust of formal institutions being a deep cultural trait. Be honest about what a checklist like this can't do.
