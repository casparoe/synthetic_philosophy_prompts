I serve on the grants committee of a small philanthropic foundation that distributes about two million dollars a year, and this funding cycle has deadlocked us. One finalist is a program distributing preventive medical treatments in a poor region: its effects have been measured repeatedly, and we can be confident that funding it would spare a few thousand people a serious illness. The other finalist is a lone researcher pursuing an unconventional line of work that nearly every reviewer believes will fail, but that, in the unlikely event it succeeded, would relieve suffering on a scale orders of magnitude beyond anything the medical program could achieve.

One committee member argues the case is simple arithmetic. Even if the researcher's odds of success are one in ten thousand, he says, multiplying that chance by the size of the payoff yields a figure that dwarfs the medical program's, and declining to fund it amounts to refusing to take small probabilities seriously. The rest of us balk, but our objections feel soft. Funding the proposal feels indistinguishable from throwing the money away. The reviewers also cannot pin down the probability: their estimates range from one in a hundred to one in a hundred thousand, with no stable basis for choosing within that range. And because our funding criteria are public, several of us worry that any rule favoring this proposal will favor every sufficiently grandiose long shot that reaches us, and will teach future applicants that the way to win a grant is to claim the largest imaginable benefit rather than the best-evidenced one. Our colleague replies that "it feels like throwing money away" is not an argument, that our worries about future applicants are an excuse to ignore the people this year's grant could help, and that refusing to multiply small chances by large stakes is innumeracy dressed up as caution.

Who is right here? If the arithmetic is as decisive as my colleague says, what explains the resistance the rest of us feel — is it a cognitive failing on our part, or is something real being left out of the calculation? If the calculation is not decisive, identify exactly where it breaks down: in the instability of the probability estimate, in the way a public rule changes what proposals we receive, in the difference between a one-time choice and a policy applied indefinitely, or somewhere else. Finally, tell me what decision rule the committee should actually adopt — one that neither ignores enormous stakes when they are genuinely on the table nor hands its budget to whoever is willing to promise the most.
