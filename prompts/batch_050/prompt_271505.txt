I am preparing a handout for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the badness of death, and I want a decision tree that lets students locate their own position before we attach any labels to it. Build me a flowchart whose root is the single question of whether death can be bad for the person who dies, and whose leaves are the following positions (glossed here so the tree must sort by commitments, not by name): (i) the Epicurean conclusion that death is no evil to the one who dies; (ii) a comparative deprivationism on which death is bad when, and to the degree that, it cuts short a life that would otherwise have contained goods, with the harm located at no particular time; (iii) a "priorist" deprivationism that insists the harm must be located while the victim is alive; (iv) a "subsequentist" view that locates the harm after death; (v) a symmetry-accepting view that treats prenatal nonexistence and postmortem nonexistence as on a par and concludes neither is bad; and (vi) a symmetry-rejecting view that concedes our asymmetric attitudes but defends them rather than revising them.

Each branch node should be a question a student can answer from a concrete case, without knowing which position it leads to. Use at least these four cases as your diagnostics: a painless, instantaneous, unforeseen death in sleep; a competent adult reduced by brain injury to a contented state without projects or ambitions, who lives on for decades; posthumous events that damage a dead person's reputation or frustrate their projects; and the stretch of time before one's birth, during which one equally did not exist. Where two candidate questions would sort students identically, collapse them and say so.

Then annotate the tree. Mark any combination of answers that no leaf occupies, and for each say briefly whether the gap reflects incoherence, mere unattractiveness, or an unexplored region of logical space. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate by verdicts on cases alone — I suspect at least one such pair exists among the deprivationisms — and explain what further commitment, if any, would drive them apart. Finally, note which single branch node does the most work in excluding the classical Epicurean conclusion, and whether a student could consistently answer that node the Epicurean way while still holding that killing is seriously wrong. Aim for seven to ten branch nodes.
