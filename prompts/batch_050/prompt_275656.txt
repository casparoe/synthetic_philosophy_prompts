I sit on the collections commitee of a small maritime museum, and our whole identity is built around one object: the Petrel, an 1870s pilot cutter we've kept afloat since the 1930s. Problem is, staying afloat for ninety years means conservation. A recent survey found that only about 15% of her current timber is original -- every plank, rib and deck beam has been swapped out at some point, one piece at a time, always to the original plans. The kicker: our predecessors never threw the old wood away. There's a warehouse up the road full of the Petrel's original timbers, catalogued and in decent shape.

Now a donor has offered to fund a project to reassemble that original timber into a full reconstruction (it would need a new internal frame to hold together, but the visible wood would be the 1870s material) and display it in our main hall. The board is split down the middle and it's getting ugly. Half say the ship in the water is obviously still the Petrel -- she's been the Petrel continuously, sailed by crews who called her that, repaired but never destroyed and rebuilt. The other half say a hull containing the actual wood the shipwrights shaped has a claim nothing else can match, and that we've been quietly fooling visitors for years.

This is basically the scenario Hobbes raised in the chapter on identity and difference in De Corpore: he argued that a ship whose planks are gradually replaced stays the same ship, but then pointed out that if someone gathered the old planks and put them back together in the same arrangement, that ship would seem to have an equal claim, and having two ships both be the original one is absurd. He seemed to think the lesson was that identity questions only have answers relative to how we're naming and counting things, not out in the wood itself. I'd like the memo to actually grapple with whether he's right about that, because if he is, then maybe this isn't a metaphysics question at all but a curatorial one about what we're promising when we label something "original."

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting (roughly two pages). It needs a concrete recommendation on the donor's offer -- accept, decline, or accept with modifications -- plus what the label on each vessel should say if we do build the reconstruction, and a line on what we tell the insurers and the regional museum loans network, since both our insurance valuation and a current loan agreement describe the floating Petrel as "the original vessel." Whatever you recommend, give the strongest argument for the side you're rejecting a fair shake, because I have to get at least two votes from that side.
