My novel came out last year and did better than any of us expected — it follows two women who cross into the same country on the same boat on the same night. One is a journalist whose paper got shut down and whose colleagues keep disappearing into prisons; the other is a farmer whose land hasn't grown anything in four years and whose kids were down to one meal a day. Same smuggler, same journey, same beach at the end of it. The journalist gets asylum in about three months. The farmer is still in a detention hotel when the book ends.

Anyway, a refugee resettlement charity read it and asked me to give the keynote at their annual fundraising dinner. Maybe 15-20 minutes. The room is going to be a real mix: donors, immigration lawyers, caseworkers, and people who actually came through the system themselves — including, I'm told, at least one woman whose situation is basically the farmer's, still waiting on a decision.

Here's what I want the speech to genuinely wrestle with, because it's the thing the novel wrestles with and I never resolved it there either: the legal definition of a refugee — well-founded fear of persecution, on account of race, religion, nationality, politics, social group — draws a line that leaves my farmer entirely outside it, and I have never found a principled reason her hunger should count for less than the journalist's fear of a cell. The difference feels like it matters to states, not to the women on the boat. But I also can't stand up there and say the line shouldn't exist, because (a) this charity's entire legal work consists of defending that category and making it hold, and (b) half the people in the room won their cases precisely by proving they fit inside that narrow definition, and telling them the category is arbitrary is a weird way to honor them. And 'let everyone in' is easy for a novelist to say at a podium and costs me nothing — the caseworkers in the room are the ones who'd live with what it does to public support for the whole system.

So: write the speech. It shouldn't be a book talk (I don't want it to feel like an ad), but the two-women structure is obviously available as material. It needs to take the arbitrariness worry seriously — not dismiss it — AND land as something other than an attack on the legal category the audience's lives depend on. And it has to work for the people in the room who've lived this; I definately don't want to lecture them about their own experience. I'd rather the speech sit in the tension honestly than fake a solution, but it still needs to end somewhere that doesn't feel like a shrug, since it's a fundraiser and people came to be moved, not to be told the problem is hard.
