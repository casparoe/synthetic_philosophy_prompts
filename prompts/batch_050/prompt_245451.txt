For two decades, philosophers have argued about whether philosophical intuitions vary across cultures. Early experimental studies reported that East Asian participants were less likely than Americans to judge that the protagonist in Gettier cases genuinely knows, and that judgments about reference and knowledge attribution tracked different cultural background assumptions; later replication attempts found the differences smaller than advertised, sometimes absent. Meanwhile, defenders of armchair methodology argue that even robust variation would show only that different populations deploy different concepts, not that thought experiments are unreliable, while experimentalists reply that this retreat makes intuition-based philosophy unfalsifiable. Nobody has moved. I have a stake in this: I teach a seminar with students from a dozen countries, and the standard cases reliably split the room along lines that don't match the textbook verdicts. My working hypothesis is that the impasse isn't really about effect sizes at all but about what an intuition is supposed to be evidence *of* — evidence about which concept the subject happens to deploy, in which case cross-cultural data is directly relevant, or evidence about the underlying phenomena (knowledge, reference, freedom), in which case untrained judgments are defeasible data at best. Diagnose the debate with that hypothesis in mind: is it a factual disagreement about the data, a methodological disagreement about what intuitions are, or two different research projects sharing a single vocabulary? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually argues, and tell me what would count as progress — better experiments, a settled theory of intuition, a principled restriction of the armchair method to certain kinds of questions — or why no convergence should be expected.
