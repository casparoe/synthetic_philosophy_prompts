I am a novelist at work on a book in which two of the central characters — one a machine-learning researcher who expects a fast, recursive takeoff once AI systems become capable of driving AI research themselves, the other an economist who expects AI to diffuse as a sequence of powerful but ordinary general-purpose technologies — have staked their careers on opposite sides of the AI takeoff debate. I need their dispute to be right: not a dramatist's caricature, but the disagreement as its strongest participants would recognize it. So I am asking you to draft, for me, the joint statement these two would produce if they undertook an adversarial collaboration — the kind of document where each has final veto over how their own position is worded.

Structure it as follows. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can sign. These should be substantive rather than diplomatic — candidates might include claims about what intelligence-in-a-box scenarios would and would not require, about which empirical trends so far are common ground, about what recursive self-improvement actually demands in practice, and about the difference between a technology being transformative and its being discontinuous. If a candidate claim looks agreed but turns out to hide a disagreement about what its words mean, say so and move it to the next section.

Second, a table of residual disagreements. For each row, state the point at issue, then give each side's position in a formulation that side would accept as accurate and unslanted — including the steelmanned version of any intuition the other side finds exasperating. I am particularly interested in disagreements about: whether capability gains from AI-assisted AI research will hit bottlenecks (and which ones — compute, data, experiment cycles, organizational adoption, physical-world feedback); whether the history of prior general-purpose technologies is evidence about this case or whether the analogy is precisely what is in dispute; and whether 'takeoff speed' is even a well-defined variable or a framing artifact.

Third, the cruxes: for each disagreement, identify what observation, development, or argument would actually move at least one side, and record each side's stated prediction in advance where possible. Be explicit about cases where a side's position has no such crux — where nothing observable would count against it — and say whether that side would regard this as a problem or as a feature.

Finally, a short closing section, which I will adapt into the novel's most important scene: the one thing each character privately concedes keeps them up at night about their own view, written so that a careful reader of either camp would find the concession fair rather than a planted weakness. Wherever you suspect part of the public debate between these camps is verbal rather than substantive, mark it and explain what each side is actually talking about.
