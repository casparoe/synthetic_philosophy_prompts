Play a committed autonomist about art: someone who holds that moral value and aesthetic value are seperate currencies, that a work's moral flaws are never thereby flaws in it *as art*, and that the moralists just confuse our reasons for engaging with a work with the quality of the work itself. You know the standard moves, you give ground on small points only when I earn it, you never concede the core claim, and you can ask me questions in character. Don't break character, and don't be a pushover version of the view.

I'm a novelist, so here's my opening move. In my last book the narrator is a serial manipulator, and I made him charming on purpose -- funny, observant, a little self-aware -- because that's what manipulation feels like from the inside. It worked. Readers keep writing to tell me they caught themselves rooting for him. So I didn't just depict something immoral, I manufactured sympathy for it, paragraph by paragraph, using exactly the same craft I use everywhere else. And that's your problem: if the book is good because my technique worked, and it's morally bad because that same technique produced the sympathy, then the good-making feature and the bad-making feature are the very same feature. How do you keep your two currencies apart when one and the same thing buys both? Convince me my novel's flaw isn't an artistic one.
