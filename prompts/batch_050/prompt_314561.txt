ok so i got into a long argument with my brother in law over christmas about whether a central planner could ever run an economy as well as markets do, and afterwards i fell down a youtube rabbit hole and learned this is apparently a famous old fight -- hayek and mises saying the knowledge is scattered among millions of people and prices are how it gets pooled, vs lange saying a planning board could basically imitate the market by adjusting prices by trial and error. what stuck with me is hayek's point that its not even data the planner is missing, its stuff like knowing that the roof leaks only when the wind is from the east, knowledge that lives in peoples heads and situations and maybe cant even be written down. i want a poem about this for my book club (we do one creative thing per month, don't judge me).

Please write me a sestina, so the proper form with the six end-words rotating through six stanzas and the three-line envoi at the end. The twist: the six repeated end-words should each be a piece of local, scattered knowledge -- like a price, a rumor about a tin mine, a farmer's hunch, that kind of thing -- and since a sestina forces those same words to keep coming back in shifting positions and then crams all six into the tiny envoi, i want the form itself to act out the debate: the stanzas are knowledge living dispersed out in the world, and the envoi is the planner trying to hold it all in one room at once. Ideally the envoi should feel either cramped or strangely thin, so the reader can feel whether you think the lange idea works. The poem should be accurate enough about what the actual argument was that someone who knows the debate wouldn't cringe, but no homework vibes, it should still be a real poem.
