I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and last month I attended a small conservation workshop that I can't stop thinking about. The subject was a proposal to use a gene drive on an isolated oceanic island to eliminate an invasive mouse population that is devastating native seabird colonies -- the mice attack chicks, and several species are in steep decline. The drive under discussion would bias inheritance of a female-sterility construct so that the mouse population would crash within a few generations. On the panel afterward, two people I respect enormously got into a disagreement that went several rounds, and I want an outside adjudication of it before I decide how to write the piece.

Dr. A, a conservation geneticist, argued for proceeding. The island is remote, she said, with no land bridge and almost no vessel traffic, so escape is a manageable risk; the alternative -- aerial poison drops -- kills non-target animals, causes real suffering, and has to be repeated; and the drive targets a species that shouldn't be on the island in the first place. Dr. B, a bioethicist who works on environmental interventions, answered that the whole point of a gene drive is that it cannot be recalled: if a few sterilizing mice reach a mainland where mice are native and ecologically important, the drive does not know or care that it has left the island. Dr. A replied that doing nothing is also irreversible -- seabird species lost to predation do not come back -- and that refusing to act is a choice with its own casualties, not a safe default. Dr. B granted that, but said there is a genuine moral and prudential difference between harms we fail to prevent and harms we set in motion with no off-switch: poisoning can be halted next season if the evidence changes, whereas a released drive forecloses every future generation's option to decide differently. Dr. A called that status quo bias dressed up as caution -- it treats whatever happens to exist now as privileged, when the current baseline is itself the product of an earlier irreversible human intervention (we introduced the mice). Dr. B's final move was about standing rather than outcomes: the island's government can consent, but nobody has consented on behalf of the mainland populations, human or otherwise, that would bear the cost if containment fails -- and there is no institution anywhere authorized to grant that consent, which is itself a reason to wait.

Both of them struck me as making serious points, and neither conceded anything. Can you go through the exchange and say who is right at each step -- whether escape risk really is the crux, whether Dr. A's symmetry argument (inaction is irreversible too) actually defeats the act/omission distinction as Dr. B used it, whether the status quo bias charge lands, and whether the consent problem is a decisive objection or merely a practical one? I'd also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing, and who is more right overall -- not just which side has the better rhetoric.
