In my decision theory seminar I present the following case, which I call the silent ledger: the inhabitants of a generation ship must decide whether to divert part of their limited energy budget to reducing suffering in distant, causally inaccessible civilizations whose members are, on the best available theory, evidential twins of the colonists — agents in relevantly similar circumstances running relevantly similar deliberations — where no message has ever passed or ever will pass between the parties. Before my students debate whether paying the ledger is rational, I want a specification first. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of evidential cooperation in large worlds must meet: which phenomena it must explain (how benefit can accrue to anyone in the absence of causal or communicative contact), which distinctions it must respect (evidential versus causal correlation; cooperating with all sufficiently similar agents versus only with those whose deliberations actually track one's own; the large-world ledger versus an ordinary one-shot prisoner's dilemma), and which results it may not contradict (standard verdicts in Newcomb-type problems; the worry that cooperation with everyone is either costless or arbitrarily exploitable). For each desideratum, give the consideration behind it, mark whether it is non-negotiable or the prejudice of a particular camp, and identify any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since that is where I expect the real result to lie. Then, in at most two sentences per view, score updateless decision theory, Hofstadter-style superrationality, and the causal-decision-theoretic refusal to treat the ledger as a decision problem at all against your list.
