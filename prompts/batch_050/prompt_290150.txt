In my aesthetics seminar, a classmate tried to shut down a discussion of forgery with the slogan: if a fake is so good that nobody can tell the difference, then it's just as good as the original. He was using it to argue that museums waste money on provenance research and scientific authentication, since nothing the conservators discover could change what's hanging on the wall. I'd like a careful disambiguation rather than a rebuttal. Lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing: start with the different dimensions of value that "just as good" might range over (visual or aesthetic character, the work's status as a historical artifact, its evidentiary value for understanding an artist's career, its market price, the worth of the artwork itself as opposed to its appearance), and for each one say what the slogan claims on that reading, whether it's a near-platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or just false, and why. Also precisify the antecedent: does "nobody can tell" mean by the naked eye now, by any test available today, or in principle ever, and which version does each reading actually need? Then tell me which of these readings, if any, would genuinely support his practical conclusion that authentication is a waste of money, and which readings he is probably relying on rhetorically while the argument does the work. Flag any reading on which the slogan equivocates between two senses of "good" to get its force.
