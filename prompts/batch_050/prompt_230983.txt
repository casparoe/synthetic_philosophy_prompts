So I'm a PhD student in econ (industrial organization mostly) who's been circling around AI safety stuff on the side, and I keep bumping into this idea people call the alignment tax and the competitiveness constraint -- roughly, that making an AI system behave the way its builders actually want costs something (capability, compute, speed to market), and that in a competitive environment whoever pays more of that cost loses to whoever pays less. Everyone gestures at it but I've never seen anyone say what a real theory of it would even have to look like, and I want to write the IO-angle paper so I need the spec first.

So: write me the desiderata. What would any adequate theory of the alignment tax have to deliver before it counts as more than a metaphor? Some things I'm already confused about, to give you the flavor:

- It seems to matter whether the tax is paid once at training time or continuously at inference time (a refusal mechanism that fires on every query vs. a one-off fine-tuning run). Those have totally different competitive implications, like fixed vs marginal cost. Should a theory be required to decompose the tax along those lines, or is that my economist prejudice talking?
- There are cases where "alignment" seems to be a capability gain rather than a cost -- instruction-following models are just more useful, RLHF'd models won market share. A theory that predicts a positive tax everywhere gets this wrong. But a theory that says the tax can be negative seems to risk saying nothing. How should a theory handle the sign problem?
- Whose tax is it? The lab pays training costs, but the deployer or the user might bear the performance loss, and society eats the externalities either way. Feels like a theory that doesn't specify the incidence of the tax across parties is incomplete, but maybe I'm asking too much.
- There's an ambiguity I can't resolve about what the tax is measured against: the counterfactual best model you could have built with the same resources, or the worst-behaving model a competitor would actually ship? These give wildly different numbers and it matters which one the competitiveness constraint binds on.

Give me the full list of desiderata, with the case or consideration motivating each one, and mark which you think are non-negotiable vs which are demands one camp in particular would make. Also flag any pairs you suspect can't be jointly satisfied -- e.g. I have a vague feeling that wanting the theory to be measureable in practice and wanting it to cover worst-case deceptive alignment (where the "tax" might be deliberately hidden by the model itself) might be in tension, but I can't quite nail it down. If the list itself turns out to be inconsistent that's honestly a result I'd take.
