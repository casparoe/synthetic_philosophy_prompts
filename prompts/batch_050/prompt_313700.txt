I am drafting an essay on Victorian freethought and its modern descendants, and I need a carefully reasoned counterfactual: what would George Eliot have made of New Atheism, had she encountered it fully formed? By New Atheism I mean the program associated with Dawkins, Dennett, Harris, and Hitchens, which I take to rest on four tenets: (1) the metaphysical tenet — there is no God, religious doctrine is false, and the arguments for it fail; (2) the diagnostic tenet — religious belief is, on balance, a net harm in the modern world, productive of violence, oppression, and intellectual dishonesty rather than merely mistaken; (3) the polemical tenet — religion deserves no special deference, ridicule and frank disrespect are legitimate instruments against it, and moderate faith is complicit in extremism because it makes faith-as-such respectable; (4) the practical tenet — a good society should aim at religion's disappearance rather than its transformation, the sooner the better, and moral life needs no replacement structure because secular reason suffices.

To fix the background as I understand it — and please correct me where I have it wrong: Eliot lost her evangelical faith young and in 1842 refused to attend church with her father, the standoff often called her holy war. She translated Strauss's Life of Jesus and Feuerbach's The Essence of Christianity, absorbing the Feuerbachian thesis that theology is displaced anthropology, that the attributes of God are humanity's own projected ideals. In her correspondence she said, in effect, that the moral bearing of her novels rests on the claim that fellowship between human beings does not depend on conceptions of what is not human, and that the idea of God, so far as it has been a high spiritual influence, is the ideal of an entirely human goodness. Yet she was no scoffer: her fiction treats devout figures such as Dinah Morris and Mr. Tryan with unmistakable respect, depicts a religiosity without doctrine in Dorothea Brooke, and she was ambivalent about the positivist Religion of Humanity's attempt to retain liturgy while discarding theology.

Now the questions. First, rank the four tenets by how readily Eliot would have accepted each, justifying the ranking from her writings and life rather than from generic Victorian sentiment. Second, take tenet (2) seriously in light of her Feuerbachianism: if religion is humanity's self-expression rather than an external corruption, then the harms blamed on religion are arguably located in the humans it expresses — but her own fiction, for instance Bulstrode's piety in Middlemarch, seems to grant religion genuine causal power for ill. Say whether the Feuerbachian reply to (2) actually holds, and whether she would have pressed it. Third, on tenet (4): given her view that moral life runs on sympathy, habit, and communal feeling rather than on explicit argument, would she have thought a society can shed religion's communal and ritual forms without eroding the capacities those forms trained — and would that push her toward something like the positivist solution she herself could never quite embrace? Fourth, identify at least one point where the counterfactual is genuinely underdetermined, where her texts pull in opposite directions, and say what additional premise would settle it.

Do not flatten her into either a proto-New Atheist or a crypto-believer; the interesting answer preserves the tension and distinguishes what she would have said from what her commitments merely imply. No citations or page references are needed.

End your response with a single line beginning "Verdict:" followed by one sentence stating which tenet she would most readily accept, which she would most firmly resist, and the single consideration that drives the difference.
