Longtime lurker on LW/ACX here. There's a move I keep seeing in drug policy threads that drives me nuts, and I want someone to actually do the philosophy instead of the usual slapfight. Someone brings up Portugal -- 2001, they decriminalized personal possession of basically everything, swapped criminal charges for these "dissuasion commissions," and poured money into treatment and harm reduction. In the years after, HIV infections among users fell off a cliff, overdose deaths stayed way below European averages, and use rates didn't explode. Then the reply is always: the heroin crisis was already peaking in the late 90s, the policy was a bundle and not just "decriminalization," correlation isn't causation, etc. And then both sides just... stop. Nobody says what would even settle it.

So here's the actual request. Take the Portugal episode and run it through the two main theories of causation: the counterfactual account (roughly Lewis-style: C caused E iff in the nearest world without C, E doesn't happen) and the interventionist account (roughly Woodward: C causes E iff some possible intervention on C would change E, with other stuff held fixed). I want a real case analysis, not a book report. Specifically:

1. What does each theory need to be true for "decriminalization caused the improved outcomes" to come out true? Spell out what the relevant counterfactual world or intervention variable would actually have to look like in this case.
2. My suspicion is that each theory breaks on this case in a different way. For the counterfactual one, the "nearest world where Portugal doesn't decriminalize" seems hopelessly underdetermined -- nearest *how*? Does that world also lack the treatment expansion? For the interventionist one, "decriminalization" doesn't seem like a variable you can cleanly wiggle, since wiggling it in any real implementation drags a dozen other things along with it. Tell me if I'm right about these failure modes, and which theory's failure is worse.
3. Most importantly: commit. Pick one of the two theories as the better tool for this kind of messy real-world policy question, give your reasons, and then give the actual answer that theory delivers on the Portugal case -- yes, no, or "the causal question as posed is malformed and here's the question you should ask instead." Don't just present both and leave me to choose; I've read enough "on the one hand / on the other hand" in these threads to last a lifetime.
4. And run it backwards too: what does the episode show about the theories? Is Portugal the kind of case interventionism was built for and counterfactuals choke on, or vice versa, or is it an anomaly for both?

Feel free to be opinionated and to say where a theory just doesn't speak rather than forcing it to. I'd definately rather have a committed wrong-ish answer with clear reasoning than a hedged survey. End your response with a last line of the form "Verdict: [which theory you pick] / [yes, no, or malformed]" so I can extract it mechanically.
