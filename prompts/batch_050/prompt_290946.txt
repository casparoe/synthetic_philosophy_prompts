Two friends of mine, Dana and Robin, have been arguing for weeks about a mutual acquaintance's decision, and last night they turned to me to settle it. I couldn't, so I'm bringing it here. The facts, which both accept: Mara was caught in a sudden storm on a backcountry ski trail. A passing snowmobiler, Ellis, found her but said he'd been stiffed by stranded strangers before, so he'd only drive her to the lodge if he believed she genuinely intended to wire him $2,000 on arrival; he added that he was an excellent judge of character and would leave her there if he sensed hesitation. Mara sincerely intended to pay, he believed her, and he drove her out. At the lodge, safe, Mara realized nothing bound her: the promise was verbal and unenforceable, Ellis lived far away and knew none of her friends, they'd never meet again, and no one would ever learn whether she paid. She paid anyway. The argument is about whether paying was rational.

Dana's opening move: (1) It was flatly irrational. Rationality evaluates the choice in front of you, and at the lodge the $2,000 bought Mara nothing — the rescue was already in the bank. Paying was at best a generous gift, at worst an expensive superstition about promises. Robin's reply: (2) That standard evaluates acts in isolation when it should evaluate the dispositions that produce them. Only someone genuinely disposed to pay would have passed Ellis's character check, and the disposition that got Mara rescued is the very one that made her pay; you can't keep the benefits of a disposition at the trailhead and disown its costs at the lodge. Dana: (3) Dispositions are background facts, but at the moment of decision the only thing Mara controlled was this act, and this act lost her $2,000. Whatever was true on the trail is causally over; intentions are revisable, and rationality is precisely the freedom to revise them when circumstances change. Robin: (4) The revision isn't actually available to someone in Mara's position, because it reaches backward. If Mara-types reliably decide at the lodge not to pay, then at the trailhead they cannot sincerely intend to pay, Ellis reads exactly that, and they freeze. The only way to be the kind of person who gets rescued is to be the kind of person who pays — so paying at the lodge isn't a loss piled on a finished benefit, it's part of the same package as the rescue. Dana's final move: (5) She concedes that if Mara had foreseen all this calmly in advance, locking in a paying disposition might have been rational. But Mara never did that deliberation; she promised at gunpoint-adjacent, under mortal fear, and promises extracted under that kind of duress arguably don't bind in the first place. So the whole machinery Robin describes never engaged, and the case reduces to a grateful person making an expensive donation she was free to decline.
