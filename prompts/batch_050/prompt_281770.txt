I've settled on a package view of mental causation, and I want to know what it would take to dislodge me. The package: (1) causal closure of the physical holds at the fundamental level; (2) mental properties are not identical to physical properties — I am a non-reductive physicalist; (3) mental causes are nonetheless genuine causes of physical effects, because causation is difference-making in the interventionist sense, and mental variables can figure in invariant generalizations that stably control their effects at the right level of description. On my view the exclusion argument fails because mental and physical descriptions do not name two rival causes competing for one effect; they name causal models at different grains, and the mental-level model is legitimately causal whenever it is the more proportional, stable one. I take the fact that the exclusion argument would otherwise generalize to all special-science causation as a reductio of the argument, not a problem for me.

What would count against this? I want more than the standard "closure plus distinctness implies overdetermination" restatement. Tell me which results, cases, or commitments would genuinely threaten the package: for instance, whether the requirement that interventions on a mental variable must be physically realized reintroduces exclusion at the level of the intervention itself; whether the view vindicates too much causation, licensing gerrymandered coarse-grainings as causal; whether any empirical finding about the failure of mental-level generalizations would count, or whether the view has been qualified into untestability. Distinguish what a committed interventionist would concede as damaging from what they would dismiss as begging the question. The defeaters I would personally find most persuasive are ones where interventionism and the proportionality criterion are made to deliver conflicting verdicts on the same case.

Give the analysis first, then summarize it in a table with these columns: Defeater; Kind (conceptual, formal, or empirical); What would have to be true for it to succeed; Likely defender response (concede or dismiss); Severity (fatal, serious, or merely awkward); Live threat or merely logical possibility.
