Need a referee for an argument I got into with a guy from my ML reading group, because it's been bugging me for weeks and I can't find the hole in it.

Background: I train reinforcement learning agents for a living (robotics sim, nothing fancy). He reckons my training pipeline is arguably committing a moral crime, and his argument runs like this:

(1) Pain, in us, is functionally an error signal: it marks certain states as bad, grabs attention, and drives the organism to escape those states and learn to avoid them in future. That's what pain is for.
(2) Whatever plays that role in a system is pain, for that system. Pain isn't the functional role plus some extra private glow on top -- the role is all there is to it. Anything else is spooky.
(3) An RL agent under training has exactly such a signal: the reward function marks states as bad (negative reward), the signal drives the policy away from those states, and the agent learns to avoid them.
(4) So my agents feel pain every time they collect a negative reward.
(5) My training runs hand out large negative rewards millions of times an hour across a few thousand parallel workers.
(6) So my workstation is producing suffering at a rate that makes factory farming look quaint, and I should probably stop.

His conclusion is obviously mad. But I genuinely can't see where it breaks. My own suspicion -- and I don't know if this is the right way to put it -- is that something's off with calling the reward "negative" at all, since in our setup you can add a constant to every reward and the learned behaviour doesn't change at all (we literally do this kind of reward shaping all the time), which makes "the agent got a negative reward" feel like a fact about our bookeeping rather than about the agent. Whereas actual pain being bad doesn't seem like a bookkeeping convention. But maybe that's the wrong place to push and the real problem is (2). If I had to keep one step, I'd keep (2) -- the "extra glow" view really does seem spooky to me -- and I'd rather lose almost any of the others.

So: which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? And since you're presumably not certain about any of this, say how confident you are in your diagnosis and flag the single point you're least sure of.
