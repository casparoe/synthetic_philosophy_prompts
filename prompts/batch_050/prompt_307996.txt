So I'm a novelist and I've been banging my head against the wall of my current book for weeks, and the wall is made of probability. Here's the setup: my protagonist volunteers for an experiment. On Sunday night they flip a fair coin. Heads: she wakes up on Monday as normal. Tails: she's woken on Monday and again on Tuesday, but each waking her memory of any previous waking is wiped, so every waking feels identical from the inside and she can never tell which day, or which coin outcome, she's in. Fine, that's chapter one. Where I got stuck is this: in the story she's offered a small bet at every waking -- a prize if she correctly guesses the coin landed tails, but she pays a small fee each waking to play. My editor and I have now had two pub arguments about whether she should take it. He says she goes in knowing the coin was fair, nothing new happens when she wakes, so it's 50/50 and the bet is rubbish. I keep saying that in the tails-world she plays twice and pays twice, so from her point of view tails somehow deserves more weight -- as if being awake right now is itself a piece of evidence. Neither of us can say exactly what's wrong with the other's reasoning, which is infuriating but, admittedly, great material.

Then in draft two I made it worse. I changed the setup so that instead of sequential wakings, the tails outcome creates a duplicate of her, a fully real second her living simultaneously in another city, and both of them wake on Monday morning. My editor claims nothing has changed: fair coin, 50/50, end of. I say the parallel version makes my intuition stronger -- of course she should reason as if tails is likelier, there are two of her standing there -- but I honestly can't tell whether the sequential case and the parallel case are the same puzzle in different clothes or genuinely different puzzles, and its doing my head in.

So what I want to know: does this configuration, where a fact about how many times or how many copies of a person exist seems to change what that person should believe, or how they should bet, from the inside, have an established name? I have a vague sense it's a known puzzle but I don't know its proper shape or how it's usually settled. Also, how is it different from ordinary ignorance -- like just not knowing whether it's raining in Leeds, where you assign a probability and get on with life? And if the duplicate version turns out to be a different thing from the memory-wipe version, what's the relationship between them, and does the betting answer actually flip between the two? If some part of this has no established name, coin one for me, define it, and mark its edges, because I need terminology for chapter nine. And please answer in British English.
