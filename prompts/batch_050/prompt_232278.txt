Write a dialogue between two people who've both just reread Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling and are now arguing about a present-day case: a devout parent refuses a routine, life-saving blood transfusion for their kid on the grounds that God has forbidden it, and a court overrules them. One speaker's position: the teleological suspension of the ethical is a category the agent can only ever apply to themselves, in fear and trembling, and from any third-person vantage point this parent is indistinguishable from Abraham — so the court is right to intervene even if, per impossible, God really did speak. The other speaker thinks that verdict quietly gives up on the whole book: if no outsider can ever in principle tell Abraham from a dangerous fanatic, then faith collapses into ethics plus possible delusion, and there has to be some structural mark — the double movement, infinite resignation followed by expecting Isaac back on the strength of the absurd — that differentiates the knight of faith from the fanatic, at least in theory. Requirements: dont strawman either side. The first speaker has to own what their view costs Kierkegaard (is faith then literally unverifiable, and does that make Abraham's silence on the mountain a bug or a feature?), and the second has to actually spell out what their criterion would rule in and out in messy real cases where the claimant isn't a patriarch in Genesis. Both should also grapple with why the tragic hero comparison — Agamemnon, Brutus, sacrificing for a public ethical cause everyone can understand — doesnt settle the dispute either way. Real back-and-forth throughout: each should answer the other's actual last point, not restate their opening. Start with the dialogue itself, no preface, no framing, and no summary at the end.
