In standard RLHF, annotators are shown two responses to the same prompt and say only which is better; a scalar reward model is fit to these ordinal judgments; and the language model is then fine-tuned to maximize expected reward subject to a KL-divergence penalty that keeps it near the original model. Reconstruct the theory of value this pipeline presupposes. The training data license at most an ordering, yet the optimization step treats reward differences as having magnitudes that can be traded off against the KL term; the judgments come from many annotators with heterogeneous values, yet are pooled into a single function; and the penalty treats the pre-trained model's distribution as a default worth staying close to, as if it embodied prior knowledge of the good. Which of these design choices genuinely commit the practitioners to measurable, interpersonally comparable utility, and which could be re-motivated on a purely ordinal or pluralist theory of value? And where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival accounts of value would license exactly the same pipeline?
