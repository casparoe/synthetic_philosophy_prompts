Having recently retired from a career in field biology, I have been reading about animal ethics and am struck by the argument that even a low probability of insect sentience can make the welfare of an enormous insect population morally outweigh a certain harm to a much smaller number of vertebrates. Rather than rehearsing that familiar scale argument, I would like a thought experiment constructed to expose its pressure points. Devise a realistic case in which a conservation trust with a fixed budget must choose between preventing a definite and serious harm to a small vertebrate population, preventing a potentially comparable harm to an enormous insect population, and first funding a limited behavioural and neurobiological pilot study before committing the remainder of the money.

The case must satisfy the following specifications. Among the two immediate interventions, straightforward expected-value reasoning must favour the insects, while a robust-evidence rule that ranks options by the lower bound of a plausible range of estimates must favour the vertebrates. The latter rule may discount uncertainty, but it must not assign insects zero weight merely because their sentience is disputed, nor may it rely on an arbitrary numerical threshold. A sequential value-of-information calculation must then favour the pilot study over both immediate interventions. The study cannot be a fantastic consciousness detector: it may provide evidence that substantially alters a rational credence without settling the question. Make the value of the study depend upon the uncertainty being correlated across the insects, so that evidence concerning the relevant biological mechanism bears on many individuals at once. If each insect instead constituted an independent chance of sentience, the pilot should no longer be able to support such a broad revision.

Present the scenario in one paragraph, with stipulated population sizes, probabilities, costs and welfare magnitudes that are ordinary enough to make the arithmetic transparent. Then show why each of the three decision procedures selects its respective option. Identify precisely which feature of the construction is doing the work, and explain what happens if that feature is removed. Finally, give the strongest objection available to someone who thinks that the case either double-counts uncertainty, exaggerates the evidential reach of the pilot study, or smuggles in an unwarranted assumption about the possible intensity of insect suffering.
