So I've been collecting my gut verdicts on affirmative action type policies and I want to know what principle, if any, is actually driving them. Here goes:

- India's caste reservations in universities and govt jobs: strikes me as justified, at least given the history. Though the "creamy layer" rule (excluding wealthy families within a reserved caste) also feels right to me, like an obvious improvement not a betrayal of the policy.
- Norway's law requiring ~40% women on corporate boards: feels fine, even good. No real discomfort.
- A quota for men in primary school teaching or nursing, where men are heavily underrepresented: feels vaguely wrong, and I can't articulate why this differs from the Norway one. That bugs me.
- Race-based affirmative action in US college admissions (the pre-2023 kind): I'm genuinely torn but lean okay-with-it.
- Legacy admissions: clearly not fine. Worse than race-based AA, even though both are preferences that have nothing to do with academic merit. But I'm not sure my ranking survives scrutiny -- legacies are a small bonus at most schools, race preferences were often larger.
- Athletic recruitment preferences: totally fine, doesn't even register as a fairness problem, even though a recruited squash player with mediocre grades displaces someone just like a legacy does. This one and the legacy one seem hard to hold together.
- Class-based preferences (extra consideration for applicants who grew up poor or are first-gen college): feels the most obviously fine of all of them, to the point where I sometimes think it should just replace the others.
- A hypothetical quota for political conservatives on university humanities faculties, justified on pure viewpoint-diversity grounds: feels bad. But "we want a range of perspectives" is exactly the kind of thing people say to defend racial diversity in admissions, so again, what's the difference?

Is there a single principle that gets most of these -- compensatory justice, anti-subordination, diversity-as-instrumental-good, something else -- or a small set of candidates, each stated precisely enough that I can see which cases motivate which clause? Tell me which of my verdicts each candidate has to give up (I'd bet the athletic/legacy pair is where things break), and what each one says about a couple cases I haven't listed: e.g. age quotas for young people in parliament, and preferential hiring of veterans. Also tell me honestly if the most defensible principle is one that overturns my Norway verdict or my men-in-nursing discomfort rather than saving them.
