I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am preparing an article on why disputes over human enhancement so often become disputes about medicine’s proper boundaries. My readers are more comfortable with health policy than with bioethical theory. Take the familiar position that medicine’s central task is to preserve or restore species-typical functioning: interventions correcting departures from normal function count as therapy, whereas interventions that improve already normal capacities count as enhancement, and publicly funded health systems ordinarily have a stronger obligation to provide the former than the latter. Without relying on citations or attributing the view to particular authors, restate that position as precisely as possible in the vocabulary of health-technology assessment and insurance design: indication, baseline risk, counterfactual benefit, clinical significance, prevention, cost-effectiveness, opportunity cost, externalities, moral hazard, off-label use, and coverage criteria.

The translation should be systematic rather than a list of loose analogies. In particular, distinguish cases in which an intervention repairs an impaired capability from cases in which it purchases a competitive or positional advantage, while making clear that this distinction is an analytical device rather than an established doctrine. Use at least three difficult cases to test the translation—for example, growth hormone for a child who is unusually short but has no diagnosed hormone deficiency, stimulant use by a sleep-deprived surgeon without ADHD, and a genetic intervention that increases resistance to a common disease beyond the level typical of healthy humans.

Conclude with a residue report. Which parts of the therapy/enhancement position translate cleanly into coverage and cost-effectiveness language? Which survive only with distortion, because the policy vocabulary quietly substitutes welfare, fairness, or budget impact for the original idea of normal functioning? And which claims, if any, cannot be expressed in that vocabulary at all? Finally, assess whether the residue exposes a limitation of the policy framework or reveals that the original therapy/enhancement distinction was less determinate than it appeared.
