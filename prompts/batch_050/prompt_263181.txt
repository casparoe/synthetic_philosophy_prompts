So my friend and I got into this massive argument last night about whether it's even coherent to say two career options are "equally good" when one pays more and the other has better work-life balance. She said if you can't rank them they're just equal, and I said no, they could be incomparable or something else, and we went around in circles for like two hours. I teach an upper-level undergrad course on value theory and I want to turn this fight into an exam question, partly so my students can settle it better than we did.

Draft me one essay exam question on incommensurable values and hard choices that a student could answer in 75 minutes. Here's the twist I want in the question itself: describe a case where two options differ on two dimensions, then add a slightly-improved version of one option (say the higher-paying job but with marginally nicer colleagues). The question should force students to explain why the small improvement *doesn't* settle the choice if the original two were incomparable — i.e. they have to actually engage with the logic of the small-improvement argument, not just name-drop incommensurability. It should also make them distinguish "roughly equal/on a par" from "incomparable," because my friend conflated these all night and I want to know whether students can avoid doing that.

I want the response in these sections, in this order:

**The exam question** (about 150 words, written as it would appear on the paper, including the scenario and the marks breakdown)
**Model answer** (about 500-600 words, what a genuinely excellent student writes)
**Marking scheme** (built around five must-hit points, marks for each, totalling 25, plus for each point a one-line note on the typical way students botch it)
**A/B boundary** (a short paragraph naming the specific move that separates an A answer from a B answer — I suspect it's whether they can say what relation "on a par" would have to be to do the work without collapsing back into equality)

Don't make the question answerable by just reciting Raz or Chang summaries. It should be possible to do well without citing anyone, as long as the reasoning about the small-improvement case is airtight.
