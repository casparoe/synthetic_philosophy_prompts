I run my high school's philosophy club podcast, and next month I'm interviewing a guest who argues for strong longtermism: the view that, because future people could exist in enormous numbers, positively shaping the long-term future should be the central moral priority of our time. Most of my clubmates have only encountered this through casual summaries that either make it sound obviously right or obviously sinister, so I want to push past that. Please draft nine to eleven interview questions, in order, that a sharp but respectful high school interviewer could actually ask, with each question grounded in the real pressure points of the debate rather than in caricature.

Concretely, I want the set to include at least one question each on: the claim that the sheer expected number of future lives dominates the moral calculus, and whether that makes longtermism hostage to whatever intervention has the biggest speculative payoff; the problem of cluelessness, meaning whether we can know enough about the far-future effects of our actions for the view to guide anything; how the view depends on contested assumptions in population ethics, like whether bringing additional happy people into existence is genuinely good; whether a fanatical commitment to tiny probabilities of astronomical payoffs follows from the view, and if so whether that's a bug or a feature; and what the position implies for weighing urgent present-day suffering against interventions aimed at risks that may never materialize. For each question, add a follow-up tailored to the reply our guest is most likely to give, so I can keep pressing instead of just moving down the list. If there's a question at the end about what evidence or argument would lead our guest to abandon or significantly qualify the view, all the better.

Since our club includes students with strong opinions on both sides, please keep the questions neutral in tone and phrased so a thoughtful longtermist would recognize them as fair. Where a question touches on a point that people who actually work on this treat as settled, versus one where serious researchers genuinely disagree, flag that distinction in a brief note after the question, and don't let the framing present a contested premise as established consensus. The whole thing should be in American English.
