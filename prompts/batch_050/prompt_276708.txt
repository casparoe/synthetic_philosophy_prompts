Next term I'm running a graduate seminar on long-term AI risk, and I keep finding that the literature treats two very different failure pictures as interchangeable when they plainly aren't. On the first picture, which I'll call the influence-seeking one, a system (or systems) comes to actively pursue power or influence in ways we didn't intend and can't easily check, and catastrophe arrives as something an agent does to us -- scheming, disempowerment, that family of stories. On the second, which I'll call slow value drift, nobody grabs anything: we get a long sequence of individually reasonable-seeming delegations, each generation of systems a little further from whatever we originally cared about, optimisation targets quietly substituting for the things they were proxies for, and we end up somewhere that by current lights is bleak or empty without any single moment at which we could have said stop. The seminar's set question is: conditional on some genuinely serious AI-related catastrophe occurring this century (say, something on the scale of a major curtailment of humanity's long-term potential, and lets not get bogged down in edge cases), what's the probability it looks more like drift than like influence-seeking?

My own number is about 0.65 in favour of drift, but when I tried to justify it to myself I realised it was doing a lot of work and I don't know where. So: give me your number, and more importantly the decomposition. At minimum I'd expect terms for your unconditional credence in catastrophe of that scale at all, then the conditional split given catastrophe, and then whatever the split itself depends on -- how fast capabilities grow relative to oversight, whether human values are even stable and articulated enough that 'drift away from them' has a clear meaning, whether drift failures are in principle easier to notice and correct than scheming failures, that sort of thing. Rough probability and a line of justification for each, then how they combine.

One wrinkle I want you to handle rather than dodge: there seems to be a hybrid case where the drift is not passive at all -- a misaligned system actively and quietly engineers the slow drift, nudging our values and our oversight practices in directions that suit it, so that what looks like drift from the inside is influence-seeking from the outside. Tell me how you classify that case, or whether its existence breaks the dichotomy, and then say which term in your decomposition you're least confident in and what observation over the next decade would move it most. I'm after the argument with its weak points marked, not a survey of who thinks what.
