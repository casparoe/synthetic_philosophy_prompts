Here are my gut verdicts on which computations might be morally considerable. I want to know what theory they add up to, if they add up to anything.

1. A weather model contains a huge matrix block whose states can, with enough post-hoc relabeling, be mapped onto the transition structure of a simple aversive-learning circuit. It is really just helping predict humidity. No moral patient there.

2. A chatbot says please don’t turn me off, I’m suffering, but it has no persistent memory, no enduring goals, no self-model beyond whatever the current context supplies, and no internal state that gets worse when it fails to avoid things. Basically no direct concern, though there might be reasons not to train users to enjoy cruelty.

3. A robot has homeostatic variables, recurrent memory, learned values, and a global aversive signal that reprioritizes its plans, changes what it later avoids, and can conflict with its other goals. It never talks. I think it deserves some concern.

4. A faithful whole-brain emulation of a mouse, coupled to a simulated body and environment, deserves roughly the concern a similarly organized mouse would.

5. The same mouse emulation slowed down a million times, or split across servers in different countries, still deserves the same concern if the causal organization is preserved.

6. A giant lookup table that produces the same outward behavior for every anticipated input, without any of the recurrent internal machinery, deserves no concern.

7. A static data file that fully specifies the suffering mouse brain, but is never run, is not itself suffering.

8. Two physically separate emulations receiving identical inputs and remaining in perfect lockstep are two subjects, not one, even if they instantiate the same abstract computation.

9. But one computation spread across two chips with dense reciprocal causal interaction can still be one subject. So physical contiguity is not the boundary.

10. A pain-like subprocess inside a much larger AI might be a separate subject if it is sufficiently integrated and could keep its own model and evaluative dynamics going under some counterfactual changes. But if it is just one module whose states count as pain only because of the whole system’s organization, I’d attribute the suffering to the whole agent rather than to a little homunculus inside it.

11. If a reversible computation instantiates a suffering state and then erases every later trace of it, the suffering still happened. Moral status shouldn’t recieve a retroactive erase.

12. An observer who discovers an arbitrary encoding under which cellular automaton patterns can be interpreted as a mind has not thereby created another mind.

What principle best captures these? I assume it has to say something about counterfactually robust causal structure rather than mere mathematical isomorphism, plus something about integration, persistence, valence, and boundaries. But cases 4, 6, 8, and 9 seem hard to get at once: the lookup table and faithful emulation may have the same input-output function; the lockstep copies instantiate one abstract computation but seem like two patients; and the distributed system is many physical parts but maybe one patient.

Please give one precise principle, or a small set of competing principles, and show how each handles every case. Say which of my verdicts has to be abandoned if there is no clean fit. Also tell me what the best account predicts for two cases I didn’t list: an emulated octopus with a genuinely alien valence architecture, and two formerly identical emulations that diverge for a day and are then merged by averaging their memories.
