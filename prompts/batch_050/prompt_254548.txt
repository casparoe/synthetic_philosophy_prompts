Write an essay on the relationship between two puzzles about imperceptible increments, and defend a verdict on whether they are one problem or two.

The first is Warren Quinn's self-torturer. A person is fitted with a device whose dial has many settings; advancing the dial by one notch yields a fixed payment and increases the pain the device delivers by an amount too small to be felt as a difference. At the lowest setting the sensation is negligible; at the highest it is agony. Notch by notch, each advance seems worth the money, yet the sequence ends somewhere plainly worse than where it began, and the agent can know this in advance.

The second is Derek Parfit's case of the harmless torturers. Each member of a large group turns a switch that imposes on some victim an increment of pain too small to be perceptible, so that no individual act makes a difference the victim could detect -- yet all the switches together produce severe suffering.

The controversy I want adjudicated is this: must a solution to one puzzle be a solution to the other? There is pressure to say yes. Both turn on a chain of steps each of which is negligible and whose accumulation is not; both seem to show that evaluating options one at a time, by their marginal perceptible effects, goes badly wrong. But there is also pressure to say no. The self-torturer case is intrapersonal and diachronic: it arguably concerns the rational relations between a person and their own future selves, and invites remedies such as resolute choice, precommitment, or refusing to treat each notch as a fresh, separable decision. The harmless torturers case is interpersonal and arguably synchronic: the increments are spread across different agents rather than different times of one agent, and it invites remedies concerning collective action, complicity, and whether an act can be wrong while making no perceptible difference to anyone. A precommitment device solves nothing for Parfit's victim; an agreement among torturers solves nothing for Quinn's agent alone with the dial.

Your essay should do the following. First, state both puzzles precisely enough that the structural parallel, if there is one, is visible rather than asserted. Second, set out at least three candidate diagnoses drawn from the following menu or beyond it: that the cases reveal genuinely rational but intransitive or cyclic preferences; that they show the irrationality of evaluating options separately rather than as whole sequences or patterns; that they are sorites phenomena in disguise, turning on the vagueness of perceptible difference rather than on any defect of practical reason; and that they expose a failure of the principle that an option which dominates its rival step by step must be choiceworthy overall. Third, test each diagnosis against both puzzles, rather than against only the one it fits most naturally. Fourth, reach and justify a conclusion: either that the two puzzles share a single correct solution, or that they diverge at some specific point -- and if they diverge, identify exactly which feature (diachronic agency, interpersonal aggregation, the separability of contributions, or something else) does the work.

Two requests about epistemic conduct. Be explicit about how confident you are in your verdict, and distinguish points you take to be settled from ones where reasonable philosophers split; I would rather have a defended conclusion with flagged weak joints than a uniformly confident facade. And if you think the pairing I have set up rests on a confused premise -- for instance, if I have misdescribed either puzzle, or if the claimed structural parallel is spurious in a way that dissolves the controversy rather than locating it -- say so plainly and explain why, instead of politely writing the essay as requested.
