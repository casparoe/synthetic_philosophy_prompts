I teach a mixed undergrad logic / critical thinking class, about 24 students, mostly second years who can crank through formal proofs fine but have never once asked why modus ponens is ok in the first place. I've got one 75-minute session and I want to run an activity that makes them *feel* the regress from the tortoise-and-Achilles story (the Lewis Carroll one) — the part where you can't justify a rule of inference by writing down more premises, because every premise you add just sits there until the rule does something with it.

My rough sketch: pairs or trios where one student plays the stubborn interlocutor who cheerfully accepts every premise offered but refuses to take the step, and the other student has to get them to the conclusion using only sentences written on index cards, with "but it just follows!" explicitly banned. What I haven't figured out is the rulebook that keeps the stubborn one honest — otherwise they just say no forever and its boring — and some kind of scoring or constraint so the frustration is the engine of the game rather than a bug.

So give me a runnable design: setup and materials, the exact rules for what the tortoise is and isn't allowed to demand, what counts as a win, a mechanism that forces the regress to actually bite within about 20 minutes of play (rather than someone giving up at card three), a second round where roles switch or the rules get tightened, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto the premise/rule distinction — and then onto the harder question of whether deduction can be justified at all to someone who won't grant you any rule. Also: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing collusion to end the game fast, and tortoise players who don't know what a legit objection sounds like), and be honest about which feature of the actual philosophical problem the game can't reproduce, so I know what I have to just say out loud in the debrief. Keep the whole thing under 800 words.
