In the philosophy-of-physics reading group I attend, the dispute over the Born rule in the many-worlds interpretation has been frozen for years, and I want a diagnosis rather than another verdict. One side, following the decision-theoretic program of Deutsch and Wallace, holds that a rational agent who accepts the Everettian description must weight branches by amplitude-squared, and that this follows from axioms about rational preference alone, with no probabilistic postulate added. The other side replies that the project is misconceived from the start: if every outcome occurs on some branch, there is nothing for probabilities to be about, and any purported derivation either smuggles the measure into the axioms or equivocates on what the agent is supposed to be uncertain of. The first side responds with talk of self-locating uncertainty, or else with a caring measure — the claim that rationality constrains how much you should care about your successor branches whether or not anything is genuinely chancy — which the second side dismisses as changing the subject from probability to prudential concern. My hypothesis is that the real disagreement is over the adequacy conditions for counting something as a probability at all: one camp takes a probability to be whatever structure rational action must track, the other insists on a connection to genuine chance or ignorance that Everettian branching cannot supply. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a substantive formal question underneath it — say, about whether the derivation is valid as mathematics, or about what the equivalence of the branches in superposition really commits one to? Tell me what kind of disagreement this is, using what each side actually argues, and what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a theorem, a sharper distinction between uncertainty and concern, or an argument that the question itself should be abandoned.
