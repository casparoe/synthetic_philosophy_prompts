I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a course on ethics and narrative art, and I also serve as the film reviewer for our student newspaper, which means I review roughly one biographical drama or "based on a true story" film per month. In the course we've been circling a question I now can't stop seeing in my reviewing work: what, if anything, do filmmakers owe to the real dead people they dramatize? We've discussed whether the dead can be wronged at all, whether a damaging invention about a deceased person is a harm to them or only to their memory, and whether the epistemic duty of a work that announces itself as true differs from that of one that announces itself as fiction. The theory is stimulating, but it is useless to me at 11 p.m. on a deadline when I have 800 words, no fact-checker, and an editor who signs off on my copy the next morning. My reviews currently say things like "the film plays fast and loose with the facts," which my editor correctly calls a shrug.

Please turn the underlying principle into a decision procedure I can actually run on any biographical drama before I write the review. I want an ordered sequence of questions -- for instance, whether the real person is named or merely thinly disguised; whether the invented element is something the historical record rules out, something it leaves open, or something contested among serious scholars; whether the invention changes the moral reputation of the person (making a decent person look cruel, a guilty one look innocent, a coward brave); whether surviving family or colleagues exist who could be affected; and whether the film signals its inventions or lets them pass as fact. For each question, tell me what counts as a sufficient answer to move on, where the procedure stops with a clear verdict (this film wrongs the dead; this film is within its license), and where the question is one of values rather than facts and must be handed to me and my editor rather than decided by the checklist. Be explicit about which distinctions from the theory had to survive the simplification and which you dropped, and flag where the procedure will misfire.

Then run the procedure on three cases, showing each step: (a) a prestige drama that invents a scene in which a real, deceased supporting figure -- not the protagonist -- betrays a colleague out of jealousy, purely to raise the emotional stakes of the third act; (b) a film that compresses a contested historical episode, resolving an ongoing scholarly dispute in favor of one side without signaling that the dispute exists; and (c) a film that openly reimagines a long-dead public figure's inner life in ways no record could confirm, but signals throughout that it is a speculative portrait. Ensure that at least one run produces a verdict I will not like -- for example, condemning a film it would be easy to admire, or acquitting one it would be satisfying to condemn. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the verdicts about dramatizing the dead that no checklist could settle for a reviewer on a deadline.
