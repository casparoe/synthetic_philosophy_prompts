I'm the methods person for a small policy research shop (6 analysts), and I keep having to referee the same fight: someone digs through a dataset we already have, notices a pattern, builds a story around it, and then presents that very pattern as evidence for the story. I gather this connects to what Bayesian epistemology calls the problem of old evidence -- how (or whether) evidence confirms a hypothesis when the evidence was already in hand, or was even used to construct the hypothesis, before the "test" happened. What I want is a procedure an analyst can run in under an hour, before anything goes to a funder, that decides how much confirmational credit a finding gets given when and how the hypothesis was formed relative to the data. Spell out which questions to ask in what order -- reconstructing what the analyst would have predicted before seeing the data, how expected the pattern is under boring rival hypotheses, whether any data was genuinely held out or could be -- and say what to do when you can't put numbers on likelihoods, which will be most of the time for us. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on two cases: (1) an analyst finds that counties with more public libraries have lower overdose rates, after slicing the data a dozen ways until something came up significant; (2) a new hire proposes a theory that elegantly explains a well-known anomaly in our field that everyone has been staring at for years. I expect the procedure to be much harsher on (1) than my team will like -- if it isn't, tell me where I went wrong in designing it.
