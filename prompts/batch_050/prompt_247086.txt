I recently completed a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and I have come away persuaded by the accuracy-first programme: roughly, the view that epistemic rationality consists in having credences that do as well as possible by the lights of accuracy, where accuracy is understood as closeness to the truth, and where the central arguments -- the dominance argument for probabilism associated with Joyce, and Pettigrew's extensions of this style of reasoning to other norms of credence -- show that familiar requirements like coherence can be vindicated purely in terms of accuracy, without appeal to Dutch books or pragmatic considerations. I find the picture elegant: it promises to explain why the norms of belief have the content they do, rather than merely asserting them, and it seems to give epistemology a unified foundation that rival approaches lack.

Before I build my dissertation around it, though, I want to know what would count against it. Please do not simply list objections from the literature; I want a map of the view's exposure. Which assumptions does the programme most depend on -- the choice of accuracy measure, the legitimacy of dominance reasoning itself, the premise that accuracy is the sole or fundamental epistemic good -- and which of these assumptions would its defenders themselves concede are doing load-bearing work rather than being mere conveniences? If it turned out that different reasonable measures of accuracy vindicated conflicting norms, would that refute the view or merely force a choice within it? If the arguments only establish that incoherent credences are dominated by some coherent alternative but cannot say which, is that a serious limitation or a feature? I am also interested in cases the framework seems unable to describe: agents with imprecise credences, credences in propositions about one's own future rationality, or situations where accuracy and other epistemic desiderata -- informativeness, understanding, the value of true belief in important rather than trivial propositions -- pull apart. Does the view have anything to say about these, or does it quietly redefine accuracy to absorb them, and if the latter, does that render it unfalsifiable in the way critics of ambitious philosophical programmes sometimes allege?

Finally, I would like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an honest verdict on each. Is there an objection that, if sustained, would show the whole approach rests on a mistake -- for instance, that dominance reasoning of this kind proves too much, or that accuracy considerations can never be the fundamental ones because some prior norm must fix what counts as accuracy? And conversely, which objections look dramatic but would leave a committed accuracy-first theorist unmoved, and why? If your assessment is that the view is well insulated, I want to know whether that insulation is earned -- a mark of a theory that has survived scrutiny -- or purchased by qualifications that have quietly narrowed what it was ever claiming.
