"Stephen's argument is that a society can only hold together if its members share a common morality, and that private vice gradually eats away at that shared morality until the bonds of society dissolve. Since nothing is really self-regarding on this view, Mill's harm principle fails and the law is entitled to punish vice as such. This anticipates Devlin almost exactly. Devlin, writing in response to the Wolfenden Report of 1957, argued that an established morality is as necessary to society as good government, that the suppression of vice is the law's business just as the suppression of treason and subversion is, and that what counts as immoral is fixed by the standard of the reasonable man -- the man on the Clapham omnibus -- at the point where tolerance runs out into intolerance, indignation and disgust. Hart replied in Law, Liberty, and Morality that Devlin slides between positive morality (whatever a society happens to believe) and critical morality (the standards we'd use to actually evaluate institutions), that there's no empirical evidence that private immorality dissolves societies, and that a change in a society's morality would be a change of the society, not its destruction.
