I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about the old "what does natural selection actually act on?" fight — genes, whole organisms, families, groups, species. As a sidebar I want a flowchart that sorts a reader into a position without ever using the jargon, because in my experience half the people who say "group selection" mean three different things by it.

Here's the anchor for the piece. In Chapter VII of On the Origin of Species (the Instinct chapter), Darwin writes: "It can be clearly shown that the most wonderful instincts with which we are acquainted, namely, those of the hive-bee and of many ants, could not possibly have been thus acquired." First, tell me in sidebar-friendly terms what problem he's flagging — acquired how, and why are bees and ants supposedly the worst case for it? Later in the chapter he answers himself by suggesting selection can work on the family as well as on the individual (that's my paraphrase, don't quote me on it). I want the flowchart to have to classify that solution: run Darwin's answer down the tree and tell me which leaf it lands on, and whether that's a sensible home for it or a sign the tree is missing something.

The tree itself: build it from the logical space, not from a list of names — start with the diagnostic questions, then the combinations of answers, then place the actual positions. Leaves that must appear: the gene's-eye view, straightforward individual/organism selection, inclusive fitness, modern multilevel selection theory, naive for-the-good-of-the-species thinking (as a trap leaf a reader shouldn't want to reach), and species selection on traits like geographic range. The questions should be concrete cases, not vocabulary tests: sterile worker castes, a gene that cheats its way into more than half of an animal's sperm, cancer inside a body, helping a sibling versus helping an unrelated nestmate, a trait that's bad for the individuals who carry it but lowers their population's risk of dying out, that sort of thing. No question should use the words "fitness" or "level of selection." Ten or eleven questions max, seven leaves max.

Two more things. If two positions on my list agree on every case and differ only in how they keep the books, say so and either merge them or flag them as inseperable — don't draw a fake fork. And if there's an answer combination nobody occupies, note it and say whether it's incoherent, just unpopular, or genuinely unexplored. I'd rather have an honest stump in the chart than a tidy one thats wrong.
