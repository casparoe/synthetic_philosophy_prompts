In the debate over whether psychopaths know that what they do is wrong, the standard moves I already have are: (1) the motivational internalist argument -- genuine moral judgment necessarily motivates, psychopaths are conspicuously unmotivated by their moral pronouncements, so those pronouncements are not genuine moral judgments; (2) the argument from the moral/conventional distinction -- psychopaths tend not to treat moral transgressions as more serious or more authority-independent than conventional ones, suggesting they lack the concept of specifically moral wrongness; (3) the empathy argument -- basic affective empathy is developmentally necessary for acquiring moral concepts, so someone who has never felt it can at best mimic moral talk; and (4) the deflationary reply -- psychopaths pass many explicit moral-reasoning tasks and articulate moral rules fluently, so their deficit is motivational rather than epistemic, and moral "knowledge" should be understood thinly enough that they have it.

Give me one further consideration, for either side, that is genuinely distinct from all four: not a restatement, a special case, or a combination of them. It may be an argument, a piece of conceptual analysis, or a thought experiment, and it may draw on adjacent issues -- such as what it takes to possess a normative concept, or the difference between knowing a rule and grasping its point. Then explain how it bears on the internalism dispute behind (1), and tell me honestly whether you think it holds up.
