Defenders reply that the theory wasn't wrong, only early — the underlying mechanism still operates and has merely been postponed by technology. Can a long-run theory be "right but early"? Explain what separates a legitimate appeal to lag or temporary countervailing forces from an unfalsifiable rescue, and say which side of that line Malthus's defenders fall on.

Student's answer: "Malthus held that population, left unchecked, grows geometrically while food production grows at most arithmetically, so population is always pressed back down to subsistence by famine, disease, and war — the positive checks. What actually happened was two centuries of rising yields and rising wages, so on a naive falsificationist view the theory is simply dead. But naive falsificationism is too simple, because no theory meets the evidence alone: every prediction relies on auxiliary assumptions about technology, institutions, and so forth, and when a prediction fails you can always pin the blame on an auxiliary instead of the core. This means the falsification question is ultimately conventional. Any theory can be kept alive indefinitely by adjusting its auxiliaries, so 'right but early' is exactly as defensible as 'right,' and there is no principled line to draw — at most, some rescues are less elegant than others. It's also worth noting that Malthus made no dated prediction: his figure of population doubling every twenty-five years was a description of unchecked growth, not a forecast, so the claim that 'history falsified him' already overshoots. The demographic transition is sometimes offered as the final nail, but that confuses two things. Falling birth rates are a behavioral response Malthus never considered, since his theory contained only positive checks, and a theory can't be refuted by a mechanism outside its own scope. Overall, Malthus is better read as a conditional theory about what happens when restraint fails, and a conditional isn't falsified when its antecedent doesn't obtain. His defenders' appeal to delay is therefore legitimate, because the theory tells you exactly what would count against it: a world where population keeps growing, technology stalls, and living standards keep rising anyway."
