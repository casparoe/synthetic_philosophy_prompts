My reading group came up with an argument that's supposed to wreck Brandom-style inferentialism and I can't find the soft spot in it. (1) The meaning of a claim is its inferential role: what you can infer it from and what you can infer from it. (2) A claim's inferential role, for a speaker, is fixed by the totality of inferences that speaker is disposed to make using it. (3) No two speakers share all those dispositions -- I infer "that's a dog" partly from its barking, my neighbor infers it from the breed certificate, and each of us endorses some inferences the other would reject. (4) So no two speakers ever attach the same inferential role, and hence the same meaning, to a sentence. (5) Genuine disagreement requires both parties to mean the same thing by the disputed sentence while one affirms and the other denies it. (6) So genuine disagreement between different people is impossible. (7) But it obviously happens constantly. (8) So meaning can't be inferential role after all. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost? If I have to give something up, I'd rather keep (1) and (3) than (6) and (7), but I want to know the price tag either way. Give me a commited verdict -- one premise, defended -- not a menu of options.
