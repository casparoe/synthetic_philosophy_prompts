Two colleagues of mine have been arguing since a seminar last week, and they've asked me to referee. The case that started it: imagine an infallible oracle announces to you the complete objective chance theory of the universe — a specification of the objective probability of every possible history. Call it T. Reading T, you notice that according to T itself, there is a 10% objective chance of a certain bizarre physical fluctuation, and T's own physics says that if this fluctuation occurred, the universe's actual chance distribution would not have been T but a rival theory T′. So T assigns probability 0.1 to a future whose occurrence would mean T was never the true chance theory.

Rowan argued that this exposes a contradiction in the standard principle that your credence in an outcome, given that its objective chance is p, should be p. Since the oracle is infallible, your credence that T is the true chance theory should be 1. But the principle says your credence in the fluctuation, given T, should be 0.1 — and the fluctuation entails that T is false. So the principle demands 10% confidence in something you know to be impossible, and it therefore has to be restricted to chance theories that assign no probability to their own falsity.

Juniper replied that the contradiction is in the case, not the principle: a fluctuation that T itself predicts at 10% is already accounted for by T and so cannot undermine it; no genuine chance distribution can assign positive probability to events that would show it never held. The scenario smuggles in an inconsistency and blames deference for it.

Rowan countered that this gets things backwards: physics does not consult our credence rules, so if a coherent physical theory yields self-undermining chances, we should fix the principle rather than declare the physics impossible.

Juniper answered that her claim constrains what "objective chance" means, not what physics can do — and that Rowan's proposed restriction amounts to the same constraint under another description.

Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about whether the case is coherent, who is right about whether the deference principle needs revision, whether the final exchange shows the disagreement has gone merely verbal, and what, if anything, both are missing.
