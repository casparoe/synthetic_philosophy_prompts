I'm putting together teaching materials for an undergraduate seminar on rational choice, and every year the same thing happens with the Allais problems. I present the two choices in the standard form: first, choose between a guaranteed one million dollars and a lottery giving an 89% chance of one million, a 10% chance of five million, and a 1% chance of nothing; second, choose between a lottery giving an 11% chance of one million (otherwise nothing) and a lottery giving a 10% chance of five million (otherwise nothing). A large majority of my students pick the sure million in the first problem and the 10%-of-five-million gamble in the second, and then, when I show them the substitution argument demonstrating that this pair of choices violates the independence axiom, they split into camps in a way that tracks their verdicts about the cases far better than it tracks which theory they'd endorse if asked in the abstract. Half of them say "then I guess one of my choices was wrong" and half say "then independence must be wrong," and within each half there are further divisions I can never get them to articulate cleanly.

So I'd like a decision tree I can hand out that sorts students into named positions in the debate, starting from their answers to the two problems and proceeding through diagnostic questions they can answer without knowing any labels or technical vocabulary. The leaves must include at least: orthodox expected utility theory holding that the common pattern is simply a mistake to be corrected; a probability-weighting or rank-dependent view on which the certainty of the sure million earns special weight and the pattern is rational; and a regret-theoretic view on which the anticipated anguish of walking away from the gamble with nothing, when the sure thing was on the table, makes the pattern rational for a different reason. Because the last two leaves both vindicate the Allais pattern, the tree needs at least one node that actually separates them -- for instance, a question about whether rational preferences could run in a cycle, or about whether the choice between two gambles should depend only on those two or on what else was available. Other useful node material: whether a compound lottery that reduces by ordinary probability arithmetic to a simple lottery must be treated as the very same gamble; whether getting nothing after turning down a sure million feels different from getting nothing when no sure thing was ever offered; whether risk aversion about money can by itself explain the pattern.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: start from the combinations of answers the diagnostic questions allow, locate the real positions in that space, and explicitly note any unoccupied combinations -- for example, the student who picks the risky option in the first problem but the safer one in the second, the mirror image of the usual pattern -- with a brief diagnosis of whether that combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored in the literature. Keep the tree to roughly seven to ten branch nodes, phrase every node as a plain question about a concrete case or feeling, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and don't let any node secretly presuppose the answer by smuggling in the vocabulary of one of the positions.
