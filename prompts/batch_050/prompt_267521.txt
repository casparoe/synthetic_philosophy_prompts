You are a metaethicist with experience drafting adversarial collaborations between opposed camps, the kind where the whole point is that both parties can sign the final document without feeling misrepresented. I run a graduate reading group that has spent the semester on the internal/external reasons debate. Two of my members -- one a convinced Williams-style internalist who holds that a consideration counts as a reason for an agent only if it could motivate her through a sound deliberative route from her existing motivational set, the other an externalist in roughly Parfit's mold who insists that some reasons apply to agents regardless of what any amount of deliberation from their current motivations would produce -- have argued for months without producing anything cumulative. I want you to write the joint statement they should have produced, and I want you to think carefully before drafting: first brainstorm candidate points of agreement and candidate cruxes, then write the document.

The document should have two parts. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can sign -- for instance, claims about what deliberation can and cannot change in an agent, about the difference between explaining an action and justifying it, and about cases like Williams's man who knowingly drinks what is in fact petrol believing it to be gin, where I suspect both sides agree he has a reason not to drink it and disagree only about the diagnosis. Second, a disagreements section in which each residual dispute is stated in wording each side would accept for its own position, followed by the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it and record each side's prediction about it in advance. Please build in at least these two test cases: an agent whose motivational set contains only self-regarding desires and who is procedurally rational without limit, asked to consider the suffering of a stranger; and an agent who would be motivated by a certain consideration only after a course of deliberation she is psychologically incapable of ever undertaking. I suspect one crux is whether the internalist's sound deliberative route constraint can be specified without either collapsing into the externalist view or letting in obviously bad reasons, and another is whether the externalist's notion of an ideal advisor is doing substantive work or just relabeling the disagreement -- but if your own analysis says the real cruxes lie elsewhere, go with yours.

Finally, I want a committed document, not a neutral menu. Make definite drafting choices: say explicitly which of their long-running disagreements you judge to be verbal rather than substantive and why; rank the cruxes by how much of each side's position actually turns on them; and state, with reasons, which side you predict would be forced to concede more if each crux were resolved against it. If you conclude that one side's formulation is doing more to protect the view from refutation than to capture anything real about reasons, say so plainly rather than papering over it for the sake of balance.
