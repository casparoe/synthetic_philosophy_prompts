I am a clinical psychologist who was recently appointed to the diagnostic-revision working group of our national psychiatric association, and I have run into a problem I hope you can help me solve. Our committee reviews roughly a dozen proposals per cycle for new or revised diagnostic categories, each of which gets about twenty minutes of presentation and discussion before we make a recommendation to the steering committee, which has final sign-off. The members are mostly clinicians and epidemiologists, plus one statistician and two patient advocates; nobody else has any philosophy background. Last cycle we nearly approved a new category until a colleague argued that simply naming the condition would amplify the behavior it purported to describe, and the meeting dissolved into an unstructured argument about whether that was a real risk or science fiction. I keep reaching for Ian Hacking's idea of looping effects — that classifying people changes the people so classified, who then behave in ways that feed back into the classification — but I have no way to make the idea do systematic work in committee, and right now it functions as a rhetorical trump card anyone can play rather than a tool.

Acting as an expert in the philosophy of psychiatry who has also served on classification committees, please write me the screening procedure I actually need. Before drafting it, brainstorm explicitly about the different channels through which classification can feed back on the classified: patients reshaping their self-understanding or symptom presentation around a label, clinicians changing what they look for and find, media amplification, and the incentives a diagnosis unlocks (benefits, accommodations, legal standing, community membership). The procedure itself should be a sequence of questions a reviewer can run in the twenty minutes allotted, with a statement of what counts as an adequate answer to each, clear points at which the procedure halts with a recommendation against the category, points at which it flags the proposal for full committee debate, and points at which it must honestly say the question is one of values rather than facts and hand the decision to a person. Two requirements I insist on: the procedure must distinguish looping that is constitutive but benign — a label giving sufferers an intelligible identity and access to help — from looping that is dangerous, since blocking all looping would paralyze psychiatry entirely; and it must handle the uncomfortable symmetrical case where *refusing* to create a category also has looping effects, because would-be patients and advocacy communities are already organizing around the label in the wild whether we ratify it or not.

Then run the finished procedure on the two proposals currently on our docket: first, a proposal for an adolescent "digital behavioral addiction" category whose proponents present survey data and treatment-demand figures but whose critics argue the behavior pattern only crystallized after media coverage of the concept; and second, a proposal to split an existing, loosely applied personality-disorder diagnosis into two narrower subtypes, where the concern is that the split multiplies identities without adding predictive power. For each, show me the procedure's output question by question, including where it stalls on missing evidence. Finally, stress-test the procedure against my own bias: my instinct is to approve almost any category attached to documented suffering, so walk me through one realistic case of that kind where the procedure recommends rejection, and give me your honest assessment of whether I should trust the procedure over my instinct there — plus a frank closing note on what a checklist of this sort fundamentally cannot do, so that I do not oversell it to the committee.
