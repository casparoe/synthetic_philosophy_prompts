I've been reading LessWrong, Astral Codex Ten, and adjacent forums for well over a decade, and one analytical habit those communities drilled into me is watching for motte-and-bailey maneuvers: a bold, interesting thesis advanced when a writer is on offense, swapped for a modest, defensible one when challenged. I finally sat down with the primary source behind a thesis I had only ever encountered through secondhand summaries, Francis Fukuyama's The End of History and the Last Man (1992). In the Introduction, Fukuyama writes:

"This was not to say that today's stable democracies, like the United States, France, or Switzerland, were not without injustice or serious social problems. But these problems were ones of incomplete implementation of the twin principles of liberty and equality on which modern democracy is founded, rather than of flaws in the principles themselves. While some present-day countries might fail to achieve stable liberal democracy, and others might lapse back into other, more primitive forms of rule like theocracy or military dictatorship, the ideal of liberal democracy could not be improved on."

Here is what troubles me. The famous thesis — that history has ended because liberal democracy is the final form of human government — is bold and, in principle, falsifiable: it predicts convergence on liberal democracy and rules out durable regressions to theocracy or military dictatorship. Yet in this passage Fukuyama explicitly concedes both non-convergence and regression, and retreats to the far weaker claim that the *ideal* of liberal democracy cannot be improved upon. That looks like a textbook motte-and-bailey: the strong, predictive claim does the rhetorical work in public discussion, while the weak, normative claim absorbs the objections.

So my question is this. Which of the two theses does Fukuyama actually defend in the body of the book? Specifically, where does he offer positive argument for the strong, falsifiable version — the one his reputation rests on — as opposed to the weak version conceded in advance here? If it turns out he only ever defends the weak version, then the book's fame rests on a bait-and-switch, and I will file it in my mind alongside the other artifacts whose public reception outran their contents. A pointer to the strongest passage or chapter where he commits to the predictive reading would settle this for me.
