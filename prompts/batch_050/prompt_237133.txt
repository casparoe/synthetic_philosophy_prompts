So I've been going down a rabbit hole on paradoxes lately (no philosophy training, just youtube and wikipedia at 1am) and I have a take I want pressure-tested by having you actually argue for it properly.

My take: Curry's paradox is a way worse problem than the liar paradox, and the standard escapes people use for the liar do nothing against it. Please argue for this.

Here's my understanding, correct me if I've butchered it but argue the case even so. The liar is "this sentence is false" and people have various moves: the sentence is neither true nor false, or it's meaningless, or whatever. Fine, maybe. But Curry's version is a sentence like "if this sentence is true, then the moon is made of cheese." And the killer part as I understand it is the derivation: suppose the sentence is true. Then what it says holds, i.e. if it's true, the moon is cheese. Since we supposed it's true, the moon is cheese. So, discharging the supposition, we've shown: if the sentence is true, the moon is cheese. But that's exactly what the sentence says, so the sentence is true. And then, since it's true, the moon is cheese. And you can swap in literally any conclusion, "2+2=5", "my uncle is the pope", anything.

The reason I think this is worse than the liar: at no point in that derivation did I seem to assume the sentence is true OR false, or that it's meaningful, or that it has any truth value at all. I just used normal conditional reasoning (assume the antecedent, derive, discharge) plus the fact that the sentence says what it says. So the "it's neither true nor false" escape and the "it's meaningless" escape look toothless — you never needed the sentence to have a truth value to run the argument. Which suggests the rot is in the logic itself, and fixing it means giving up some rule of inference that seems completely obvious, which is a way higher price than anything the liar charges.

Please make the strongest version of this argument you can. In particular I want you to handle the two comebacks I keep imagining: (1) "the Curry sentence is just ill-formed / meaningless, so no derivation starting from what it says gets off the ground" — my instinct is this begs the question because the derivation only needs the sentence to mean what it says, not to be true, but spell it out; and (2) "the liar has the same structure really, so they're equally deep problems" — I think that's wrong because the liar at least lets you localise the damage to truth-talk, whereas Curry lets you prove any arbitrary claim about the world, but again, make it precise.

End by saying what you think the argument actually forces on us: which ordinary-seeming logical rule is the cheapest to give up, and does the cost of giving it up support my claim that Curry is the deeper problem, or does it undercut it?
