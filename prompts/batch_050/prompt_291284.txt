In a seminar on professional ethics, a classmate presented an argument that I have been turning over for weeks. Each step looks reasonable, and yet the conclusion seems to prove far too much. I would like to know where, if anywhere, it goes wrong.

Begin with a matched pair of cases. My flatmate asks who finished the milk. In the first case, I answer, "It wasn't me," when in fact it was. In the second, I answer, "The cleaner came through this morning," knowing that the cleaner drank nothing and that I finished the milk myself, but also knowing that my flatmate will take the cleaner to be the culprit. In both cases I intend to produce exactly the same false belief, I foresee it with exactly the same confidence, and I produce it by trading on my flatmate's trust in my testimony. Now consider the argument my classmate built on this pair:

(1) If two acts of deception involve the same intention, the same foresight, and the same outcome, then any moral difference between them must rest on some further morally relevant feature of the acts themselves.
(2) The only difference between my two answers is where the boundary falls between what is literally said and what is merely conveyed.
(3) That boundary is fixed by the linguistic conventions of the community and by the hearer's habits of inference, neither of which is up to the speaker; and a moral distinction between two agents' conduct cannot rest on factors that were never within either agent's control.
(4) Therefore there is no intrinsic moral difference between lying — uttering a falsehood with intent to deceive — and merely misleading — uttering a known truth engineered to induce the same false belief.
(5) If (4) is correct, the same style of argument generalizes: there is no intrinsic moral difference between breaking a promise and deliberately letting someone rely on you without promising, between violating an oath and crafting an oath-compliant deception, between asserting and insinuating in any domain at all.
(6) But (5) is untenable. A courtroom, a contract, and a friendship all depend on precisely the distinctions (5) erases; a theory that treats a perjurer and a witness who testifies in carefully chosen literal truths as moral equals has lost its grip on something real.

My classmate accepts the whole thing and bites the bullet at (6). I cannot. The conclusion seems to prove too much, yet when I press on the premises individually, each one survives my objections. Which step should I reject? In answering, please do three things. First, name the step and explain why it looked so plausible — I want the diagnosis to account for the argument's seductiveness, not merely its failure. Second, price the alternatives: if I reject (3), for instance, I seem committed to the wrongness of my act varying with contingent facts about my language's conventions, which was the very observation that motivated (2); if I reject (1) and allow the mere form of an utterance to do moral work, I seem to make guilt depend on etiquette. Tell me which of these costs is actually payable and which is fatal. Third, if the argument is in fact sound, say so plainly and tell me which downstream distinction — the perjury line, the promise line, or the assertoric line — is most worth salvaging as a merely conventional but still valuable practice, and what, if anything, is lost by conceding it has no deeper basis. If forced to choose, I would rather keep (2) and (3) than (1), but I want to know what I am paying for that preference.
