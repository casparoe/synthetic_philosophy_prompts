I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mathematics — about sixteen students, half philosophy majors and half doubling in mathematics or physics — and I have one 80-minute meeting in which to run an in-class activity on the indispensability argument for mathematical realism before that phrase is ever uttered in the room.

The idea I want the activity to transmit, stated so you need no outside sources: our best scientific theories do not merely use mathematics as convenient notation; in certain cases the mathematics appears to carry the explanatory weight itself, and if a theory's explanatory success confirms it, it confirms the mathematics along with the rest — which commits us to mathematical objects. Build the activity around these two cases, whose empirical details I supply as stipulations so that no one derails into disputing the biology:

(1) Periodical cicadas spend 13 or 17 years underground before emerging in vast synchronized broods; both cycle lengths are prime. The leading evolutionary explanation is that a prime-period cycle minimizes co-occurrence with predators whose own population cycles are periodic: a 12-year brood would collide with a 3-year predator cycle every twelve years, whereas a 13-year brood collides only every thirty-nine. The explanation seems to turn on an arithmetical fact about 13 and 17, not merely to describe the cicadas using numerals.

(2) Honeybees build hexagonal cells, and the explanation is that among all divisions of a plane into equal-area chambers, the hexagonal tiling minimizes perimeter — and hence wax — per cell. This minimality is a theorem of mathematics, conjectured in antiquity and proved only recently. Again the explanatory burden appears to be borne by a mathematical result rather than a biological one.

Design a game for the session with the following architecture. Split the room into two teams with asymmetric rules: a nominalist team, which must explain each phenomenon without incurring any commitment to mathematical objects, and a free team, which may explain however it likes. The crux you must solve is the adjudication rule. "Thirteen cicadas emerged" is plausibly innocent numerical talk; "13 is prime" is plausibly a fact about a number. If the referee penalizes only explicit number-talk, the nominalists win trivially by hiding mathematics in adjectives; if the referee penalizes every implicit mathematical structure, the task is unwinnable and the game proves nothing. Specify the rule precisely, give three or four sample rulings on attempts at the boundary, and arrange the scoring (no money) so that an explanation earns points for genuinely answering the why-question and loses them when the opposing team exposes a mathematical commitment doing real work. Include a second phase — reversed roles, or the free team made to argue that the mathematics is mere bookkeeping — so that the strongest objection to the argument is felt from the inside as well. Give me complete rules, a timeline, materials, and one fully worked example of a nominalist explanation that looks clean but collapses under challenge, showing the collapse step by step.

Then the debrief: map the predictable moments of play onto the components of the argument — the move from mathematics-in-the-description to mathematics-in-the-explanation, the confirmation-of-the-whole-theory step, and the main lines of resistance (mathematics as a representational or inferential device, paraphrase achievable in principle, confirmation not spreading to every part of a confirmed theory). The phrase "indispensability argument" is to be withheld until this point; tell me how to introduce it. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I expect pedantry about wording, the mathematics majors treating the constraint as a trivial translation exercise, and the class settling for the trite conclusion that science needs mathematics — and be candid about which feature of the actual argument the game cannot reproduce and I must supply by lecture.
