I was talking recently with a friend about a pattern we both recognized from our own working lives, and I want to know whether philosophers or social theorists have a settled name for it, and if so, how precisely it has been carved off from its neighbors. Here is the pattern. A speaker does not get interrupted, doubted, or dismissed in the moment -- nothing in the actual exchange is objectionable -- because the speaker has already edited themselves in advance, in light of how past audiences handled what they said. Two concrete instances. A colleague of mine, one of the most technically capable people on her team, has stopped giving detailed explanations in meetings altogether; she watched her careful accounts get met with glazed expressions and then mangled on retelling, so now she pre-compresses everything into one bland, unobjectionable sentence, and the meetings run smoothly and she is widely regarded as a good communicator. Second: a relative of mine dealing with a chronic illness has learned, over years, that describing her symptoms in precise, qualified language leads nowhere, while presenting them in an exaggerated, dramatic register gets her tests and referrals -- so she now automatically translates her experience into the register the system rewards, even though she privately considers the translation a distortion of what's actually true for her. In both cases the testimony that reaches the listener is truncated or reshaped before any listener has done anything, and crucially the speaker often comes out looking fine -- believed, even praised -- while something is still clearly lost.

I know there is a well-known literature on the case where a listener unfairly discounts a speaker's word because of prejudice about who the speaker is -- the harm there is done by the audience, in the moment of uptake. What interests me is that my pattern seems to relocate or even eliminate the audience's act: the damage is done by the speaker to themselves, in anticipation, and in some versions the audience is not even prejudiced -- they are merely limited, or the institutional format merely rewards certain registers, and the speaker's forecast of this may be entirely accurate. So my questions are these. Does the anticipatory self-editing case have an established name of its own, and does the literature treat it as a variety of the better-known discounting case, as a distinct phenomenon, or as downstream of something else (for instance, a gap in the shared conceptual resources available for making oneself understood)? Where exactly is its boundary -- if the speaker's expectation of being misunderstood is well-founded rather than a response to anyone's prejudice, is it still the same phenomenon, and does the verdict change if the editing actually serves the speaker's interests? And if what I'm describing doesn't match anything established cleanly, I'd rather be told that plainly than have a near-miss label pressed onto it.

One more request about the form of the answer: I am not looking for a bare label. Please give me the best-fitting term or terms with enough of the underlying definition that I can see what the phenomenon is taken to consist in, and where its edges are drawn -- in particular, how it differs from the discounting case, from self-censorship in the ordinary prudential sense, and from cases where the speaker simply lacks the concepts to articulate their experience at all. Where the taxonomy in this area is genuinely contested among the people who work in it -- and I gather it is -- please say which parts are settled and which are live disputes, rather than presenting one theorist's preferred carve as the consensus view.
