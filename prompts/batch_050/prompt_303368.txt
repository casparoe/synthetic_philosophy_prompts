I served on a jury last year in a civil case, and afterwards I fell down an internet rabbit hole about what lawyers call naked statistical evidence: the famous puzzles where, for instance, a bus hits a parked car at night, nobody saw which company it belonged to, and it turns out one company runs eighty percent of the buses in town -- or where a thousand people attend a rodeo, only five hundred paid for tickets, and the organizer sues one attendee at random because it is more likely than not that any given attendee snuck in. Every account I have read says the statistical case, even at high numbers, feels like it should lose, while a single mediocre eyewitness at the same reliability somehow feels like enough. I understand the usual explanations -- that verdicts must be tied to this particular defendant, that bare statistics could be produced for any case -- but I want to probe the boundary rather than just accept it.

Here is what I would like from you. Construct a thought experiment that goes against the grain of the standard puzzle: a realistic courtroom scenario (civil or criminal, your choice, but state which standard of proof applies) in which the plaintiff's or prosecution's entire case is naked statistical evidence at roughly gatecrasher-level odds -- say, between eighty and ninety-nine percent -- yet where a fair-minded juror's intuition, including yours, lands in favor of the statistical side winning rather than losing. Constraints: keep the scenario to one paragraph; no futuristic technology, no perfect records or surveillance, no smuggling in individualized evidence under another name (for instance, DNA matches or video footage do not count as naked statistics, so they are off limits); and the statistics must be genuinely base-rate-style, applying to the defendant only because he or she is one member of a large class, exactly as in the bus and rodeo cases. The intuition flip must be generated by features of the case itself, not stipulated.

After the scenario, please do the following: explain why the intuition flips here when it does not in the standard gatecrasher case; identify the single feature of your scenario that is doing the real work, and say what happens to the verdict intuition if that feature is removed; then steelman the objection from someone who holds that purely statistical evidence can never be sufficient on its own -- how would they argue that your case either cheats or proves nothing? Finally, tell me how confident you are that your scenario actually meets the brief, and single out the specific point in your analysis you are least sure about. End with a last line beginning "Verdict:" followed by one sentence stating which feature of the case flips the intuition and whether it survives the objection.
