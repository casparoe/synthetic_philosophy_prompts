The inefficacy objection has been stuck for something like forty years and I want to understand why. The setup: individual contributions to collective harms seem to make no difference — one flight doesn't change the climate, one steak doesn't change how many chickens get raised (the supermarket orders in bulk, so your purchase only matters if you happen to be the customer who pushes it past a reorder threshold), one vote doesn't swing the election. The standard reply is expected value: a tiny chance of being the threshold-crosser, multiplied by the huge stakes when you are, gives your choice the same expected impact as if the harm were divided evenly across everyone. Both moves are ancient, everyone in the literature knows them cold, and noone has budged. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? My best guesses: (1) a hidden empirical question about how markets, power grids, and electorates actually respond at the margin; (2) a normative question about whether a minuscule probability of an enormous effect can ground a genuine moral reason, or whether leaning on it is a Pascal's-mugging-style exploit of our moral concepts; (3) a framing question about whether act-consequentialist accounting even applies to harms that only exist in the aggregate, in which case the expected-value reply just misses the point. Diagnose the stall: is my carve right, where does the impasse really live, and what would count as progress — better economics, a decision-theoretic result, a new distinction — or is this bedrock and nothing would move it?
