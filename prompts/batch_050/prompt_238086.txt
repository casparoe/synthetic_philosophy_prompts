So I've been designing a little strategy board game as a hobby (nothing serious, just something my friends and I play on fridays), and while trying to figure out which moves are "obviously bad" I hit something I can't find a word for. My method was basically: cross out any move that's never better than another move. But my friend pointed out there are two flavors of "never better" — a move that's always strictly worse than some other move, and a move that's never better but sometimes ties. Here's the weird part: when we only crossed out the strictly-worse ones, we seemed to land on the same simplified game no matter what order we crossed them out in. But with the ties-allowed version, the order mattered a ton. I'd cross out one move first and conclude the whole game comes down to a fight between moves A and B; she'd cross out a different one first and end up with A versus C, and in her version B isn't even on the table anymore. We got different "solutions" to the same game purely from the sequence of deletions, each of which looked totally reasonable at the time.

Second thing, possibly related. There's one move in my game that's kind of middling against everything the opponent can do. No single other move beats it in every situation — move X crushes it in some spots and flops in others, move Y is the reverse. But if you flip a coin between X and Y, that random plan does better than the middling move no matter what the opponent plays. My friend says the middling move is therefore trash and should be crossed out too. I say you can't eliminate a move based on a coin flip, because nobody actually plays the coin flip — at the table you play X or you play Y, and neither one alone beats it. Who's right here? Is "loses to a random blend" a real way for a move to be bad, and does it have a name distinct from just "loses to another move"?

I guess my questions are: (1) does the order-dependence thing have an established name, and is it considered a bug in the crossing-out procedure or a genuine fact about the game — are both our "solutions" legit, or is one of us doing it wrong? (2) is there a named distinction between being beaten by one specific move and being beaten only by a blend of moves, and does the standard way of simplifying games actually count the blend? (3) is the order-independence of the strictly-worse version something that always holds, or did we just get lucky in the few examples we tried? Please don't just throw names at me if they exist — I want to understand what's actually going on, what the two cases have in common, and where exactly the crossing-out method breaks down.
