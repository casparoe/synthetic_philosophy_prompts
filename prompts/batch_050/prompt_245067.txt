**Essay question (25 marks):** "If I am sitting now and standing later, I am first bent and then straight. But shape is an intrinsic property, and nothing can be simply bent and simply straight." Set out the problem this raises for persistence over time, and explain how endurantists and perdurantists each respond to it. Which response is more successful?

**Mark scheme:**
- Accurate definitions of endurantism and perdurantism (6 marks)
- Clear statement of the problem of temporary intrinsics, including why intrinsic properties are the sticking point (8 marks)
- Accurate account of each side's response, with the costs of each (8 marks)
- Reasoned verdict (3 marks)

**Student's answer:**

Endurantism is the view that ordinary objects persist by being wholly present at each moment of their existence. When I sit and then stand, the very same, complete object — me — is present at both times. Perdurantism denies this. On that view, objects are extended in time as they are extended in space: just as a road has spatial parts in different places, a persisting object has temporal parts at different times, and the object itself is the whole "worm" made up of all of them.

The problem of temporary intrinsics, pressed by Ted Sider in his work on four-dimensionalism, is this. Some properties are intrinsic: an object has them in virtue of how it is in itself, not in virtue of its relations to anything else. Shape is the standard example. But intrinsic properties seem to be had simpliciter — a thing either is bent or it is not, full stop. If the endurantist is right and the very same object is present when I sit and when I stand, then that one object is simply bent and simply straight, which is a contradiction. The endurantist must therefore treat shape as a relation to a time: I am bent-at-t1 and straight-at-t2. The perdurantist's objection is that this abandons the idea that shape is intrinsic at all, since a relation to a time is not a property a thing has in itself.

The perdurantist instead says that what sits in the chair at t1 is my bent temporal part, and that temporal part is the person at that moment; each stage has its shape simpliciter, so there is no contradiction. The cost is that this commits perdurantists to saying that familiar objects are really four-dimensional worms, which is a heavy revision of common sense.

The endurantist's best reply, then, is to hold that being bent-at-a-time is still close enough to an intrinsic property for ordinary purposes, or else to accept presentism, so that the only properties an object has are the ones it has now.

On balance, the perdurantist response is more successful, because it preserves the intuition that shape is intrinsic rather than explaining it away, and the revision of common sense is a price worth paying for avoiding contradiction.
