I work for a small think tank that advises a national government on regulatory policy, and I've been asked to write an internal explainer on the intellectual foundations of cost-benefit analysis, which is how all major regulations here get justified. The practice, concretely, is this: analysts estimate a regulation's effects across the whole population, convert heterogeneous consequences into a single monetary metric using willingness-to-pay measures (including a monetized value of statistical life for mortality risk reductions), discount future impacts to present value, and then simply sum everything. A regulation passes if total benefits exceed total costs, full stop. Notably, the standard methodology insists that a dollar counts as a dollar regardless of who receives it, so distributional incidence is reported in an annex but does not enter the decision criterion. Meanwhile, the same government's health service allocates its budget using cost per quality-adjusted life year, which is also an additive sum across persons but with a very different numeraire, and transport agencies use yet another one. I've been struck by how much philosophical machinery seems to be smuggled into what is presented to ministers as mere arithmetic.

Here is where I got stuck, and I'd like your help working through it. My colleague claims that the practice rests, knowingly or not, on something like Harsanyi's aggregation theorem: roughly, if each person's preferences over risky prospects satisfy the expected utility axioms, and the social ranking of prospects respects unanimous individual rankings (a Pareto condition), then social welfare must be representable as a weighted sum of individual utilities. On this reading, cost-benefit analysis is what you get when you implement that theorem with willingness-to-pay standing in for utility. But I suspect the fit is imperfect. Expected utility representations are unique only up to positive affine transformations, so the theorem by itself doesn't fix the weights, and a normalization choice that treats everyone symmetrically is an extra ethical commitment, not a theorem output. There is also the familiar critique, associated with Diamond, that evaluating risky policies by ex ante expected utility sums is indifferent to certain kinds of procedural fairness in how risk is distributed across identifiable people, even when the sum comes out the same. And willingness-to-pay is contaminated by ability to pay, so the practice may not even be summing utilities in the theorem's sense.

So my question: what does this practice actually presuppose, philosophically? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of interpersonal aggregation that makes cost-benefit analysis intelligible, and be careful to distinguish three things: (a) what the practice strictly requires in order to be rational at all, (b) what it fits well with but doesn't entail, and (c) what it is simply silent on. In particular: does additive summation across persons presuppose that individual well-being is cardinally measurable and interpersonally comparable, or can the practice be defended on thinner grounds, such as hypothetical compensation or ex ante Pareto improvements behind a veil of ignorance about who one will be? Is the refusal to apply distributional weights a commitment to utilitarianism, or is it equally compatible with a view that takes efficiency and equity to be separate questions to be settled by different instruments (taxation doing the distributive work)? And finally, suppose the government became convinced by prioritarianism, the view that benefits to the worse-off count for more: what concretely would have to change in the methodology, and would the prioritarian be able to justify her own weighting scheme with anything like the formal compulsion Harsanyi's theorem gives the utilitarian, or is she stuck with an intuition where her rival has a theorem?
