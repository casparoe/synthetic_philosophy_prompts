I help moderate a large online hobby forum of about 40,000 members. I have no philosophy training, but I keep running into the same puzzle, and I want help turning it into a rule our team can actually follow.

Our community rules include things like: do not deceive other members, do not dig into anyone's life outside the forum, and do not punish a member without giving them a chance to respond first. The five of us who enforce the rules keep proposing to break one of them in order to reduce breaking of that very rule. Recent examples: one volunteer suggested we run fake bait accounts to catch scammers — deception to cut down on deception. Another suggested we quietly read the private messages of two members we suspect of planning to publish someone's home address — an intrusion to prevent a worse intrusion. A third wanted to publicly ban a serial plagiarist without the usual hearing, to deter copycats — skipping due process in order to protect a norm that depends on due process.

Here is what I cannot sort out on my own. If the point of a rule is to protect people from being deceived, spied on, or condemned unheard, then a small, controlled violation that prevents many larger ones seems to serve the rule's own purpose, and refusing on principle looks like valuing my clean hands over the people the rule exists to protect. On the other hand, once we let ourselves trade rule-breaking against rule-breaking, I cannot see what stops the rules from dissolving: every proposed violation will arrive with a story about the violations it supposedly prevents. Both directions feel like they lose something real, and I suspect the tension runs deeper than a disagreement about consequences.

Please write a decision procedure the five of us can run on any proposal of the form "break rule R to reduce violations of R": the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a firm no, and where it hands the decision back to us because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Whatever the correct resolution of the puzzle is, the procedure should encode it — or encode the leading candidate resolutions, say which one it builds on, and why — rather than pretending the puzzle away. Somewhere in the questions, make sure the procedure asks: whether a rule's point really is just to minimize violations of itself, or whether it also does something else (for instance, giving members something they can appeal to against us); whether the specific person who would be violated retains a legitimate complaint even if total violations go down; and whether it matters that we are the ones charged with enforcing the rules rather than mere bystanders. Then run the procedure on the three cases above, and include at least one case where it delivers an answer my team will not like. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot settle.

Some constraints: these decisions come up roughly weekly; any of the five moderators should be able to run the procedure in under fifteen minutes; and the outcome has to be explainable to the membership in plain language, since people appeal our rulings publicly. Keep the entire response under 850 words.
