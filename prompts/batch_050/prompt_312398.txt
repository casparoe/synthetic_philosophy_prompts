I teach an undergraduate seminar on animal ethics, and every year the same dispute resurfaces when we turn to cross-species welfare comparisons: whether sheer brain size -- not intelligence, not behavioral sophistication, but the physical bulk or neuron count of the organ -- is itself morally relevant. Some of my students defend the practice, common in comparative welfare science, of treating neuron counts as a rough proxy for how much an organism can suffer or flourish; others insist this confuses a fallible evidential shortcut with a genuine moral ground. A colleague and I argued about it over coffee last week and got nowhere, so I would like a thought experiment built to order that can settle, or at least sharply locate, the disagreement.

Construct a thought experiment meeting all of the following specifications. First, it must involve two biologically plausible beings whose brains differ enormously in absolute size and neuron count, while everything plausibly constitutive of welfare -- behavioral repertoire, affective responses, preferences, learning, social bonds -- is made vivid as identical; the verdict I want is that their suffering counts equally, and the case must generate that verdict from ordinary premises rather than assert it (in particular, you may not simply stipulate that their experiences are equally intense; the sameness has to emerge from the details). Second, modify the scenario by changing exactly one variable so that the larger brain begins to look morally relevant again, and explain whether what now matters is genuinely the size of the organ or something that size merely indicates in our world but not in the modified one. Third, describe both scenarios in plain terms a first-year student can follow, with no exotic physics and no science-fictional mind-reading devices. After the scenarios, state what each of two views predicts -- a sentiocentrist view on which only the character of experience matters, and a view on which the quantity of neural realization partially constitutes the weight of welfare -- identify which single feature of the case is doing the work, say what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed, and indicate what evidence or argument would change your own verdict on whether brain size is ever morally relevant in itself rather than as a proxy.

Present the response as exactly five numbered items -- the initial scenario, the verdict on it, the one-variable modification, the diagnosis of the operative feature and its removal, and the conditions under which your conclusion would change -- with each item a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion, and in American English.
