So I'm a novelist (literary fiction, working on book three) and I have a character problem that has turned into a philosophy problem, so bear with me.

The character: a mid-level guy at a firm that processes foreclosure evictions. He doesn't decide anything, he just keeps the paperwork moving. The moral engine of the whole book is that he never causes anything. If he quit tomorrow someone else stamps the same forms by Thursday. And I realised that to write him honestly I need to understand the pure extreme version of his situation, which is probaly why I've been stuck for weeks on the Józefów massacre — the Reserve Police Battalion 101 episode, July 1942. Facts as I have them: about 500 middle-aged Hamburg reservists, ordinary guys, not SS, get sent to this Polish village. Their commander, Trapp, visibly shaken, does something remarkable before the killing starts — he offers the older men the chance to step out, no penalty. Maybe a dozen take it. The rest spend the day shooting around 1,500 of the village's Jews, face to face, in the woods. Men who later asked to be reassigned generally were, without punishment. If I've botched any of that, correct me, but I'm fairly confident it's right.

Here's the ask. Take the philosophical work on complicity — specifically the problem of participating in a wrong you didn't cause, where your own contribution was causally redundant (one shooter fewer saves nobody; the outcome is massively overdetermined) — and run the episode through the main positions. The view that complicity is about intentionally participating in a collective wrong regardless of wether your bit made a difference (roughly what Kutz calls the complicity principle); causal-contribution accounts and whatever fixes they offer for redundancy; and the line between complicity and mere bystanding. Concretely:

1. Under each view, who in the episode is complicit and in what exactly: the men who shot all day, the dozen who stepped out at the start, the ones who shot a few people and then asked to be reassigned, Trapp himself, the drivers back at the trucks? Do the mapping actor by actor, not one gestalt verdict for the battalion.

2. The opt-out is the part that keeps me up. Trapp's offer kills most duress-based excuses. But here's what I can't figure out: can any complicity theory actually separate the man who stepped out from the man who stayed, given that neither changed the body count by a single person? If a theory can't distinguish them, is that a reductio of the theory, or does it just show that complicity was never the right concept for what separates those two men — and if so, what is?

3. Where does the theory misdescribe the episode or go silent? Most of those men, by the accounts, weren't intending the Final Solution; they mostly didn't want to look weak in front of their mates. Is going along out of conformity an intention to participate in the collective act, or does the participatory-intention view need these men to be more committed than they actually were? This matters to me because my character isn't committed to anything either, he's just conformist and tired, and if complicity requires commitment I've been writing the wrong book.

4. Then run it backwards: what does Józefów do to the theories? My hunch is that complicity theory was built for clean little cases — the getaway driver, the lookout — and that the overdetermined mass-participation case breaks something in it. Tell me if the hunch is wrong and the theories handle it fine.

And then the selfish novelist question, which is honestly the point of all this: if the complicity verdict in the extreme case rests on X, whatever you decide X is, does the verdict transfer to my mild case where nobody dies on his desk? Or is complicity a concept that doesn't scale down? Because if it doesn't scale, my novel's premise is in trouble and I'd rather find out at page 80 than page 300.

Last thing: push back if I've set this up wrong. If the premise is confused, if the facts as I gave them won't bear this much weight, or if "run the theories over the episode" can't actually deliver what I'm asking for, say so plainly instead of politely complying. And be honest about where a theory just has nothing to say rather than making it talk.
