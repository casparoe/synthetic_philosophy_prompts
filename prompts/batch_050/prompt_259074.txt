My friend and I spent last night arguing about A Clockwork Orange, and we got stuck on a point I'd like you to untangle using Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities. Her view: once the Ludovico conditioning takes hold, Alex literally cannot choose violence, so his subsequent good behavior is morally hollow and he is responsible for none of it — the state has simply converted a person into a mechanism. My view: Frankfurt cases show that moral responsibility does not require alternative possibilities, so Alex's lack of alternatives does not by itself settle anything against his agency. But I worry my move equivocates: in Frankfurt's cases the intervener never actually intervenes, and the agent's choice unfolds entirely from his own reasons, whereas Alex's alternatives were eliminated in the actual sequence, by design, before he chose anything. Does that difference matter — is Alex a counterexample to my use of Frankfurt, or does the case instead show that my friend is smuggling in a condition stronger than the mere absence of alternatives? And if responsibility here has to be traced back to an earlier free choice, does Alex's consenting to the treatment — in ignorance of what it involved, and only to shorten his sentence — do the required work? Finally, where does the story itself land: the prison chaplain's insistence that goodness without choice is worthless, and the ending in which the conditioning is reversed, seem to take a stand on exactly this question. Please argue it through and take a position rather than split the difference.
