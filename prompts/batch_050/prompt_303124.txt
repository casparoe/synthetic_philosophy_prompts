I am a retired experimental physicist who, late in life, has been reading phenomenology, and I now sit as a lay advisor on the review panel of a rehabilitation engineering group that fits upper-limb prostheses and evaluates assistive devices. The engineers use the word "embodiment" as an honorific — a device earns it if users report liking it — and I have been trying to give the notion some discipline from what I understand of Merleau-Ponty's distinction between the lived body and the body as an object of thought, and of his idea of motor intentionality: the body's prereflective directedness toward tasks, the way a hand "knows" a doorknob without its owner representing the doorknob at all.

What I want is a procedure, not an essay. The panel reviews roughly one device per month; we can require about three hours of observation and testing per user; and our sign-off affects whether a device is prescribed for home use rather than kept in supervised training. Please translate the phenomenological distinction — between a tool one operates by thinking about it and a tool that has been absorbed into one's motor repertoire — into a sequence of tests a clinician without philosophical training can actually run, in a fixed order, with a stated criterion at each step for what observation counts as passing and what counts as failing. I have candidate tests in mind — performance when visual attention is withdrawn, recovery from unexpected perturbations, degradation under a simultaneous cognitive task, whether reach planning recalibrates when the device changes the effective length or mass of the limb — but I do not know which of these track motor intentionality specifically as opposed to ordinary well-practiced skill, and I want that argued rather than assumed. If some candidate test only measures skill, say which and drop it.

The procedure must mark explicitly the points where the philosophy gives out and a clinician's judgment has to take over, and it must state plainly what it cannot settle. Then apply it to three cases: a below-elbow myoelectric prosthesis after six months of daily use; a white cane used by a blind adult with twenty years of practice; and a powered load-handling exoskeleton worn by a warehouse worker who would, in fact, be endangered by treating the machine as part of his body. At least one application should produce a verdict I will find unwelcome — my suspicion is that the cane, the case I took to be the paradigm of incorporation, may come out equivocal, and that the amputee's honest report that the prosthesis still feels foreign may coexist with test results showing it has been absorbed — and if either is so, say so openly and tell me what the panel should do with the discrepancy.

Two closing demands. Where the procedure preserves a phenomenological distinction, name which distinction was kept and why it had to survive the simplification; where you dropped one, tell me what was lost. And address the deepest worry I have: that incorporation may not be an all-or-nothing verdict but a matter of degree and of context, in which case the right deliverable may be a score on several axes rather than a certification. If that is your conclusion, redesign the procedure accordingly rather than forcing the binary.
