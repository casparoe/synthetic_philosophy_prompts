I organize my department's colloquium series, and next term's guest is a philosopher of mathematics whose recent book defends a position she calls inferential essentialism, developed out of a close engagement with Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations. Her central claim goes beyond the standard reading of Lakatos: mathematical concepts are not merely sharpened by the dialectic of conjecture, proof, and refutation but are literally individuated by the proofs in which they figure, so that a change in a theorem's accepted proof structure constitutes a change in what its terms mean. Draft nine interview questions for the Q&A session. I do not want questions that invite her to restate the book's thesis or walk us through the Euler polyhedron case study yet again; I want questions that press where the view is most exposed. Include at least one question on each of the following pressure points, and append to every question a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, so that I am not left flat-footed when she gives the obvious answer:

1. The refutation puzzle. If a proof is deductively valid from its premises, in what sense can a counterexample refute the theorem, as opposed to merely exposing a hidden lemma or a gap between the informal argument and its formalization? Press her on what makes a lemma the guilty party rather than an occasion for quietly restricting the theorem's domain.

2. The unconstrained menu. Monster-barring, exception-barring, monster-adjustment, and lemma incorporation are available in response to any counterexample, and critics charge that nothing in the Lakatosian framework dictates which move is rational. Does she accept that there is no principled stopping rule, and if she claims there is one, what does it consist in — given that mathematics has no empirical content whose increase could adjudicate the choice?

3. Refutation versus change of subject. On her strong individuation thesis, when the proof changes, the theorem's content changes too; so it seems nothing is ever strictly refuted, only replaced by a theorem about something else. How does she distinguish refutation from topic-shift, and how does she avoid the Kuhnian conclusion that successive mathematical frameworks are incommensurable?

4. The status of axioms. Are axioms themselves conjectures, liable to refutation by their consequences, or do they stand outside the dialectic? If the former, what could possibly count as a refutation of an axiom, and does that not collapse her position into the pragmatism about axiomatic choice she elsewhere disavows?

5. Non-surveyable proof. How does the account handle computer-assisted or formally verified proofs that no human being has followed end to end? Does machine verification terminate the dialectic, relocate it into the formalization choices, the specification, and the proof assistant, or reveal that Lakatos conceded too little to the formalists?

6. The Popper analogy. Quasi-empiricism leans on an analogy with falsification in natural science, yet mathematical counterexamples are themselves established by proof, so the refuter seems to need the very certainty the view denies. Is there any genuine analogue of the asymmetry between confirmation and falsification, or is the word quasi-empirical carrying more weight than it can bear?

7. Crucial cases. What, in the mathematics of the last half century, plays the role Lakatos assigned to his counterexamples — can she point to an episode in which a refutation genuinely restructured a concept rather than merely correcting a theorem, and say what marks that difference?

8. Conceptual pluralism. If concepts are proof-generated, there may be several equally legitimate successor concepts where practice once recognized one, as arguably happened with polyhedron. Is the choice among successors rational, conventional, or settled only retrospectively by fruitfulness — and does retrospective vindication drain the view of any guidance it might offer working mathematicians?

9. The normative upshot. If she is right, does the standard textbook format — fixed definitions first, theorems after — systematically misrepresent the epistemology of the discipline, and what would an intellectually honest presentation actually look like?

Keep the tone courteous but adversarial in the way a good Q&A should be: each question should assume she has the standard Lakatosian repertoire at her fingertips and aim past it.
