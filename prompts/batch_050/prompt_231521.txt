I direct the ombuds office for a network of four hospitals, and my office reviews roughly three hundred incident reports and complaints a year. A pattern we keep seeing is that the same event gets recorded differently depending on who reported it: a junior nurse's account of a surgical error gets phrased as "a possible lapse in communication" while a senior surgeon's account of the same event is treated as the authoritative version; a patient's report of post-operative pain is discounted because she has a documented history of anxiety; a complaint from an immigrant staff member whose English is accented is marked "unclear" and effectively shelved. After a difficult case last year, my board asked me to design a standing review procedure so that investigators systematically check whether testimony in a file was given less weight than it deserved because of who the speaker was, rather than because of anything about the testimony itself. I have read enough of the philosophical literature on testimonial injustice to know there is a real distinction between discounting a speaker on the basis of evidence about their reliability and discounting them on the basis of identity prejudice, but my investigators are clinicians and administrators, not philosophers, and I cannot hand them journal articles.

Please write the procedure itself: an ordered sequence of questions or tests an investigator applies to any contested account in a file, designed to flag cases where credibility was likely withdrawn for the wrong reasons. It should include a workable test for distinguishing a warranted credibility judgment (based on track record, inconsistency, or direct counter-evidence) from a prejudiced one, and it should say what to do with the harder structural cases -- for instance, when a complainant seems unable to articulate what happened to them because our institution's categories give them no adequate language for it, or when a report was never written at all because the person learned that reporting gets them nowhere. The procedure must be honest about where it hands the decision back to a human being because no checklist can settle it, and it should be usable by someone with no philosophy background in under an hour per file. I would also like it to say explicitly what kinds of injustice it cannot detect, so that the board doesn't treat a clean file as proof of a fair process.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First: a foreign-trained nurse reports that a surgeon ignored a protocol step; the department head's review notes the nurse's "difficult manner" and closes the complaint. Second: a patient with a psychiatric admission on her chart reports severe post-surgical pain and is recorded as "drug-seeking"; a later scan vindicates her. Third: a well-liked senior physician with an unblemished record is accused of rudeness by a resident, and your procedure, applied honestly, concludes that discounting the resident's account was actually defensible on the evidence -- I include this because I need to show the board that the procedure is not a machine for finding injustice wherever a junior person loses a dispute, and because an answer it gives that I will not like is precisely what will make the board trust it.
