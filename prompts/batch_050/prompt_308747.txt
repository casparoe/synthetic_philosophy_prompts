So this got personal for me this year. My dad had a massive stroke, and in the ICU we ended up in this weird in-between zone where the doctors were talking about brain death testing and also, at one point, about organ donation after circulatory death, where they withdraw support, wait for the heart to stop, wait a few more minutes, and then declare death and take the organs. We were treated amazingly, no complaints, but afterwards I fell down a rabbit hole reading about how doctors and philosophers have been fighting about all this for like forty years and are no closer to agreeing.

As far as I can tell the fight has a few fronts. One camp says whole-brain death is the real deal: if the entire brain is irreversibly gone, the organism is dead, full stop, and the legal definition basically ratified this decades ago. Then there are people who point out that brain-dead bodies on ventilators can fight infections, heal wounds, even gestate pregnancies for months, and they say a body doing all that is pretty obviously a living organism, so the legal definition is a useful fiction at best. Then there are the "higher brain" folks who say what matters is consciousness and the capacity for it, so someone in a permanent vegetative state with a functioning brainstem is the one who's really dead, which most people find horrifying even if they can't say why. And meanwhile the circulatory-death organ donation protocols just sort of... sidestep the whole thing by waiting a few minutes after the heart stops, even though nobody claims the brain is irreversibly damaged in that window, only that nobody's going to try to restart it.

My working theory is that this debate stalled because it's not actually one debate. I think "death" is doing double duty as a biological state and as a moral boundary line (when can we take organs, when can we stop treatment, when does killing become impossible), and the sides are mostly disagreeing about which of those the concept is supposed to track, while pretending they're disagreeing about biology. But I might be wrong -- maybe there's a real empirical or conceptual question buried in there, like what makes something a unified organism rather than a collection of living cells, and the biology genuinely hasn't settled it.
