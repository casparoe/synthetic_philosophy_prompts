I'm a TA for an undergraduate bioethics course, and next month I'm running one of the weekly discussion sections myself: about 18 students, 75 minutes, no prior reading assumed for this session. The topic I want to cover is the authority of advance directives over one's own later self -- the kind of case where a person, while fully competent, signs a document refusing certain treatments should they ever develop severe dementia, and years later is demented, seemingly content, and in need of exactly the refused treatment to go on living. When I've presented this as a plain thought experiment in office hours, students flatten it instantly into either "honor the document, obviously" or "the patient in front of you is who matters," and I can't get them to feel why it's hard. So I want an activity, not a case discussion.

Design me a structured, runnable exercise that makes the students inhabit the problem from the inside before anyone names it. My rough hope is something staged in rounds, where participants first author binding instructions about their own hypothetical future (possibly about something lower-stakes than death, so the room doesn't get heavy too fast), and are then put in a position -- via role assignment, time skips, changed preference profiles, or whatever mechanism you think works -- where honoring their own earlier instruction costs them or someone in front of them something they now visibly want. Small real stakes (points toward a trivial prize, snacks) are fine if they help, but I worry about gamifying a dementia scenario, so judge that. Two design constraints: no lecture or framing up front -- they should not hear "precedent autonomy" or "the earlier self" until the debrief -- and at least one moment where a student is literally holding a document they wrote and begging, in role, for it to be disregarded (or watching someone else do so), so the tug-of-war between the author of the document and the person now present is experienced rather than described.

Alongside the rules and materials, give me a facilitation script for the debrief: which moments in the activity map onto which concepts (precedent autonomy, the distinction between the interests a person takes in their life as a whole and the comforts of their present experience, and the question of whether psychological discontinuity makes the later patient a different authority-relevant self), and in what order to surface them. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first two times I run it -- for instance, students gaming the changed-self role with knowledge their real future self wouldn't have, or the room converging on one side too quickly -- and how to intervene without breaking the no-lecture rule. Finally, be frank with me about which feature of the real dilemma the activity structurally cannot reproduce (I suspect the actual phenomenology of cognitive decline is one), and what I have to supply by narration or follow-up reading instead.
