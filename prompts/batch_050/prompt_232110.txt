For a graduate game theory seminar, I put together the following argument that iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies cannot depend on the order in which strategies are deleted. I am now fairly sure the conclusion is false, but I cannot locate the step where the reasoning breaks. Here is the argument:

(1) Suppose strategy s weakly dominates strategy t for some player: against every profile of opponents' strategies, s does at least as well as t, and against at least one profile s does strictly better. Then no situation can arise in which the player strictly prefers t to s; the player is at worst indifferent between them.

(2) An option that is never strictly preferable to another available option contributes nothing to the chooser: deleting it cannot lower the best payoff the chooser can achieve, since whatever t delivers against any opponent profile, s delivers at least as much.

(3) If deleting a strategy does not worsen the deleter's prospects, the deletion cannot change which strategy profiles are consistent with everyone playing rationally.

(4) Every step in an iterated weak-dominance elimination is a deletion of exactly the kind described in (2), so by (3) each step preserves the set of rationally playable profiles.

(5) Therefore any two complete elimination procedures, run in any order, leave the same remainder: iterated weak-dominance elimination is order-independent.

The counterexample is elementary. Consider the two-player game where player 1 chooses among rows T, M, B and player 2 between columns L and R, with payoffs (player 1, player 2): (T,L) = (1,1); (T,R) = (0,0); (M,L) = (1,1); (M,R) = (2,1); (B,L) = (0,0); (B,R) = (2,1). If T is deleted first (it is weakly dominated by M), then R weakly dominates L for player 2, and the survivors are (M,R) and (B,R), with payoff (2,1). If instead B is deleted first (it is also weakly dominated by M), then L weakly dominates R for player 2, and the survivors are (T,L) and (M,L), with payoff (1,1). The order determines both the surviving strategies and the surviving payoffs, so (5) is false.

What unsettles me is that the identical skeleton of argument, applied to strictly dominated strategies, is the standard and correct proof that iterated strict elimination is order-independent. So the fault must lie somewhere in the gap between weak and strict domination, yet I cannot see where that gap enters the reasoning: each of (1) through (4) looks like a bookkeeping consequence of the definition of weak dominance. If forced to choose, I would most like to keep (1) and (2), since they seem to say no more than what weak dominance means, and I am prepared to give up the uniqueness of the solution if that is the price.

Please identify the step the argument actually fails at, and explain why that step looked valid — in particular, why the strict-dominance analogue of that step is sound while the weak-dominance version is not, using the game above to make the difference concrete. Then tell me what each possible repair costs. The two questions I most want answered are these. First, does the failure show that weak-dominance deletion lacks a purely decision-theoretic justification — that is, can a fully rational player, one who always plays a best response to her beliefs, nonetheless rationally play a weakly dominated strategy? Second, if justifying weak-dominance elimination requires some further assumption about the players beyond rationality and common knowledge of rationality, what exactly is that assumption, and does adopting it restore order-independence or merely license some eliminations while leaving the order-dependence intact?
