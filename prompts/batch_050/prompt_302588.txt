Retired marine biologist here, and over the past year I've gotten sucked into reading philosophy, which my wife finds hilarious. Here's something that's been nagging at me from my working days, and I want the philosophy underneath it dragged into the light.

For most of my career I did experiments involving animals, and the rules we operated under look genuinely strange when you lay them out side by side. Where I worked, if you wanted to so much as take a blood sample from a mouse you needed an approved protocol, a justification for how many animals you'd use, an anesthesia plan, the whole packet. Octopuses get similar protection in a lot of jurisdictions these days despite being mollusks, apparently on grounds of behavioral sophistication. Meanwhile I could grind up thousands of sea urchin embryos or fruit flies with zero paperwork, and as far as I know nobody anywhere has ever filed a protocol for Drosophila. Fish sat in a weird middle zone that shifted depending on the country and the decade. So the practice draws a line: some animals get moral consideration in the form of paperwork and required pain relief, some get none at all, and the line tracks -- roughly -- complexity of behavior and nervous system, which is being used as a proxy for how likely it is that there's something it is like to be that animal.

My question is what this practice actually presupposes, philosophically. It seems to assume that consciousness is spread across the animal kingdom in some graded or probabilistic way, and also that there's a threshold above which you owe the animal consideration. But the threshold is enforced as razor sharp -- full protection for the mouse, none whatsoever for the fly -- which sits oddly with the underlying judgment being both graded and uncertain. Does the rulebook only make sense under one specific theory of how consciousness is distributed, or would several rival theories license the same lines? Is the sharp cutoff just practical convenience pasted over a fuzzy reality, or does it smuggle in a stronger claim nobody's admitted to? And what would the rules have to look like if you took the uncertainty seriously instead, say by weighting protections by how likely each critter is to be sentient, which I gather some philosophers now argue for?

Format-wise, give me the answer in these sections, in this order: "Bottom line" (two or three sentences), "What the rulebook takes for granted" (a couple paragraphs -- the implicit theory, reconstructed as crisply as you can), "Looser fits" (a paragraph or two on other views the same practice would be consistent with, so I can see where it's genuinely underdetermined), "The sharp line problem" (one paragraph on whether the binary treatment is defensible or just convenient), and "Taking the uncertainty seriously" (one paragraph on what would concretely change in the lab). Don't just name-drop theories at me -- actually reconstruct the reasoning, that's the part I'm after.
