I recently started a graduate design studio where nearly every exercise is built around visualization: the instructors tell us to walk through an unbuilt corridor in the mind's eye, rotate a facade to check its proportions, and zoom in on a joint until we can almost feel the material. One of my classmates takes all of this very literally. He argues that mental imagery really is like a picture on an inner screen — a depictive representation with spatial properties, where scanning across the image takes time in proportion to distance and rotating it takes time in proportion to angle — and he concludes that cognition must include genuinely picture-like formats, not just language-like descriptions. A friend from philosophy counters that the picture-in-the-head analogy is a seductive mistake: everything the picture metaphor explains, she says, could equally be explained by propositional representations plus clever task demands, and the feeling of inspecting an image is no more evidence of an inner picture than a progress bar is evidence of a tiny clerk moving files inside the computer.

I find myself genuinely torn, because the picture analogy is doing real argumentative work in our studio: it is used to justify hours of visualization drills, on the grounds that the inner canvas can be trained like an eye. So please audit the analogy of mental imagery as a picture on an inner screen. Respond with exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: first, the explicit correspondence table (what plays the role of the screen, the viewer, the pixels or resolution, distance, rotation, zooming, and occlusion); second, the respects in which the mapping genuinely holds and earns its keep; third, the places where it breaks, distinguishing the disanalogies that matter for the depictive-versus-propositional dispute from those that are merely superficial; fourth, a verdict on whether my classmate's conclusion — that thought must include genuinely depictive representations — survives once the disanalogies are on the table; fifth, a better analogy for mental imagery, if one exists, and what it would license or forbid for my studio's visualization drills.
