I am a PhD candidate in political theory, and the mayor's office in my mid-sized city has asked me to deliver a ten-minute address at a public hearing on a proposal I helped design: replacing the elected neighborhood advisory council with a standing citizens' assembly whose members are drawn by lottery from the civic rolls, serving staggered one-year terms with compensation. Roughly a third of the room will be colleagues from my department who know the Athenian pedigree and the deliberative-polling literature cold; the rest will be residents, many of whom hear selection by lot as unaccountable strangers making decisions about my street. Write the address. It must do three things: make the case that random selection can be more democratic than election rather than a retreat from democracy; answer the accountability objection head-on, without pretending that a mechanism chosen by lot can be punished at the ballot box, which means locating legitimacy somewhere other than the threat of removal; and explain why this body advises rather than decides, in a way that sounds like a principled allocation of authority rather than an admission that the whole idea doesn't quite work. The classical thought that lotteries dissolve faction should carry real structural weight in the argument rather than appear as garnish, and the speech should not condescend to either audience or hide behind the word deliberation whenever a hard question comes up.
