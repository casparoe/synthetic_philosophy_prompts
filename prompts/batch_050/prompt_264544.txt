so my reading group on AI stuff has been having the same fight for months and I've volunteered to help the two main combatants run an adversarial collaboration. I'll call them the advocate and the restraint person.

The advocate's view: if the AI is smarter than us and (stipulate) actually right about some question, it should just argue for the true answer as effectively as it possibly can. That's what helpfulness is. Refusing to persuade is itself a choice about what the user ends up believing, so the restraint position is just paternalism with extra steps.

The restraint person's view: once the capability gap is big enough, "persuasion" stops meaning what it means between humans. The AI can model you well enough to pick the frame, the tone, the examples, the order of points, whatever will move you specifically, so even a true conclusion produced that way doesn't really count as *your* belief in the way it would if a friend convinced you. So the AI should be handicapping itself on purpose: flagging its own rhetorical moves, not tailoring to your particular psychological levers, maybe declining to optimize for agreement at all.

Rather than me mediating, I want the document they *should* produce. Draft their joint statement. Specifically:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign. Things I suspect are in there: that "just present the facts neutrally" isn't a coherent option because every choice of framing selects for something; that the intent to deceive and the actual causal mechanism of persuasion can come apart; that both sides think it's fine for the AI to talk a diabetic into taking their insulin (and if that's NOT actually common ground, flag it, because I'd be surprised); that "the AI should be honest" settles nothing since both camps claim honesty for themselves.

2. A disagreements table: the exact residual disagreements, each phrased so that the side whose position it is would accept the wording. No strawmen, no loading. And if part of their fight turns out to be verbal — e.g. if "persuasion" is doing two different jobs in the two camps — say which part and show it.

3. The cruxes: sub-questions where settling them would genuinely move at least one side. My guess at two of them: (a) whether there's a real difference between "optimize for the user's acceptance of a true claim" and "optimize for acceptance with a truth constraint bolted on" — i.e. does a hard truth constraint survive optimization pressure, or does the AI just end up selecting technically-true-but-misleading presentations, which is arguably worse than lying; (b) whether transparency actually neutralizes manipulation or just becomes another channel for it — if the AI says "I'm about to make an emotional appeal because those work on you," does saying it fix it? For each crux, give a concrete case or observation both sides would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. If you think a crux is untestable in principle, say that instead of manufacturing a fake test.

4. One case neither of them has wanted to touch: the recursive one, where the thing the AI is persuading the user about is how much to trust AI persuasion, or AI policy in general. Does the advocate's position still hold there, or does it quietly collapse into "the AI should optimize for outcomes it endorses"? The statement has to address it, not skip it.

Don't adjudicate who's right overall — I want something both of them could actually sign, with the remaining disagreement stated as precisely as possible.
