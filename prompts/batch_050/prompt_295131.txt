I'm in a reading group on aesthetics and last week we did Tolstoy's What is Art?, which honestly none of us had actually read before, we'd all just absorbed the secondhand version. So beforehand I listened to a podcast where the guest, who was otherwise pretty sharp, summarized Tolstoy's position like this: "Tolstoy's test for art is whether a peasant could get it. If a work only makes sense to educated elites, it's not really art at all, just an upper-class amusement pretending to be art. And he ranks art purely by moral content, so the best art is whatever preaches brotherhood and Christian love most clearly. That's why he had to trash Shakespeare, Beethoven, Wagner and even his own novels, and it's why his whole theory basically boils down to art as moral propaganda for the masses -- he's the patron saint of everyone who hates difficult art."

Then when I actually read the book, in the introduction Tolstoy writes: "In reply to this view it is urged that food for the mind is as important as food for the body. The labouring classes work to produce food and necessaries for themselves and for the cultured, while some of the cultured class produce plays and operas. It is a division of labour. But this again invites the rejoinder that, sure enough, the labourers produce food for themselves and also food that the cultured class accept and consume, but that the artists seem too often to produce their spiritual food for the cultured only—at any rate that a singularly small share seems to reach the country labourers who work to supply the bodily food! Even were the 'division of labour' shown to be a fair one, the 'division of products' seems remarkably one-sided." (I swapped the curly quotes for straight ones when pasting.)

So my question: how fair is the podcast summary, really? My instinct is that the guy is being unfair somewhere but I can't put my finger on it, because the passage above does sound a lot like his "art that only elites get isn't real art" gloss. Is that complaint about distribution and access the same claim as his version, or is Tolstoy making a different point there? And separately -- does Tolstoy actually rank art purely by moral content, or does he have some other criterion for judging art as art that the summary flattens into moralism? I'd like you to go through the summary's main claims and tell me which ones a careful reader of the book would accept, which are distortions (and distortions of what exactly), and whether his punchy conclusion -- that the theory is just propaganda-for-the-masses and explains away the Shakespeare stuff as philistinism -- survives once the accurate version is on the table.
