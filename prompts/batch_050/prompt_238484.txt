A political theorist I know defends a position she calls choice-burden minimalism: making a decision is a psychological cost, comparable to a tax, so whenever two option sets would lead to the same outcome the smaller one is preferable, and the state should design institutions so as to spare citizens avoidable decisions — while never actually closing any option off. Compare this position with the Grand Inquisitor's speech in The Brothers Karamazov, where the Inquisitor holds that people cannot bear the freedom Christ left them and will gratefully hand it over to anyone who relieves them of the burden of choice in exchange for bread and certainty. In your comparison, address whether the two positions diagnose the same human weakness or different ones; whether the Inquisitor would regard the choice-burden minimalist — who steers choices while keeping all options formally open — as too timid to count as a genuine ally; and which of the two gives the more plausible explanation of why people surrender freedom, or fail to surrender it even when they say they want to. Give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion, in British English.
