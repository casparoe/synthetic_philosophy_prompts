I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am writing a long-form piece about an algorithm-review board at a mid-sized financial technology company. The board audits the firm's automated decision tools for fairness, and its rulings are not public, but I have reconstructed its track record from interviews and documents. I want to anticipate how it will rule on a tool currently under review, and I need help reasoning through its precedents.

Act as an expert in the philosophy and theory of algorithmic fairness -- someone fluent in the standard statistical fairness criteria (demographic parity, calibration, equal error rates) and in the well-known impossibility results showing that these criteria cannot generally be jointly satisfied when base rates differ across groups. Think carefully and take your time. Before giving me your prediction, brainstorm every rule the board's track record might encode, then evaluate the candidates against the evidence.

The board's rulings so far:

1. A loan-approval tool whose acceptance rates were equal across demographic groups was approved, even though the tool was not calibrated: among applicants assigned a given risk score, default rates differed by group.
2. A fraud-detection tool that was well calibrated within each group, but flagged qualified applicants from one group at a substantially higher false-positive rate than the other (because the underlying fraud base rates differed), was rejected.
3. A credit-limit tool that was neither calibrated nor error-balanced, but demonstrably outperformed the human loan officers it replaced on every fairness metric the board measured, was approved over internal dissent.
4. A marketing-targeting tool that achieved demographic parity and equal false-positive rates, but had unequal true-positive rates across groups, was approved with a requirement of annual re-audit.
5. A collections-prioritization tool, calibrated within groups and with equal false-positive rates but unequal true-positive rates, was rejected.

The tool now under review is a small-business lending model. The two groups in question have genuinely different default base rates. The model is calibrated within each group: among borrowers assigned the same risk score, realized default rates are the same in both groups. But because the base rates differ, any threshold achieves equal true-positive rates only at the cost of unequal false-positive rates, and vice versa. The model as proposed equalizes the true-positive rates, so its false-positive rate is higher in one group. The vendor argues that calibration is what matters to borrowers, since a score means the same thing for everyone. Advocacy groups argue that the false-positive asymmetry means one group is disproportionately burdened by wrongful rejections.

My questions for you: First, predict the board's ruling, with your confidence and reasoning. Second, identify the two or three rules most consistent with the full track record -- noting where the rulings underdetermine the rule -- and say what each candidate rule predicts about this case, since I suspect they diverge here. Third, identify which past ruling is the closest analog to the present case and explain why. Fourth, describe a hypothetical future case whose ruling would best discriminate among the surviving candidate rules. Where the track record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, say so plainly rather than forcing a prediction, and tell me what principle the board would be committing itself to with each possible ruling.
