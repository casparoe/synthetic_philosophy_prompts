So I never went to college or anything but I got really into the ticking time bomb debate around torture a while back -- the classic thought experiment where there's a bomb somewhere, a guy in custody knows where it is, and you've got an hour. What I keep circling back to is the actual post-9/11 stuff, the CIA enhanced interrogation program, Abu Ghraib, the memos saying certain techniques were fine, all that. Because the ticking bomb scenario is basically what everyone was picturing in their heads when they defended it, right? But from what I can tell the real program looked nothing like the thought experiment. These weren't guys caught mid-plot, there often wasn't any ticking anything, and a lot of the people tortured turned out to have no useful information or to just say whatever would make it stop.

So here's what I want: take the actual post-9/11 torture program as a case and run the ticking time bomb argument over it, hard, in both directions. First direction -- does the thought experiment even apply to what actually happened? Map it carefully. The scenario assumes you know you have the right guy, know there's a bomb, know the torture will work and know it's the only way. How many of those held up in practice? Is there a version of the argument that's honest about uncertainty -- like, you're only 40% sure he knows something -- and does that version still justify anything?

Second direction, and this is the part I actually care about more: what does the episode show about the thought experiment itself? A lot of people (mostly folks arguing about it on forums, which is where I'm getting this from, not journals) say the ticking bomb case is basically a trojan horse -- it gets you to say yes to an impossible perfect scenario and then that yes gets laundered into permission for the messy real version, with standing rules, lawyers writing memos, and torture becoming a routine bureaucratic thing instead of a one-time desperate act. Does the history back that up? And if someone says fine, then keep torture illegal but let the cop who faces a real ticking bomb do it anyway and plead necessity after the fact -- does the post-9/11 program make that position look naive, or is it actually the one lesson the episode teaches?

Also be honest about where I'm wrong. If parts of the program really were closer to the thought experiment than I'm assuming, or if the "it never works" claim is weaker than I think, say so rather than just agreeing with me.
