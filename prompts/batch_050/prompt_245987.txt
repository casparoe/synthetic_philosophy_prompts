So in my philosphy club we got into the absent minded driver problem (the one where a guy is driving home, there are two exits, he cant remember if he's already passed the first one, and the weird thing is that reasoning at the moment seems to give a different answer than planning before the drive). I want to understand what's actually making the paradox work, so here's my ask:

Build me a NEW scenario that has the same basic structure — where what seems rational to plan in advance disagrees with what seems rational to decide in the moment — but with these constraints:

1. No amnesia, no drugs, no sci-fi memory wiping. The forgetfulness has to come from something totally mundane (like being on autopilot, or everything looking the same, or being distracted). A normal person should be able to imagine this actually happening to them.
2. It can't be about driving or exits. Pick a different everyday setting.
3. The stakes should be small and realistic, not life or death.
4. Keep the scenario itself under 150 words.

Then after the scenario, do the breakdown: (a) what the person would decide if they could plan and commit before the forgetting kicks in, and why, (b) what seems best to do at the actual decision moment given what they know then, and why, and (c) point at the exact feature of your case that makes (a) and (b) come apart — like, if you removed that feature, would the disagreement dissappear? Also tell me honestly whether your case is really the same problem as the original absent minded driver or whether you accidentally built something subtly different (like the person actually gets new info, or the probabilities work out differently). I'd rather you flag the difference than pretend its a perfect match.
