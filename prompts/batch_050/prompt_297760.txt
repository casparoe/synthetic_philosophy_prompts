I teach an upper-division political philosophy seminar (16 students, one 80-minute session) in a unit on populism, and I want students to feel, from the inside, why the phrase "the will of the people" is doing more work than it appears to. Design an in-class activity in which the students collectively *are* "the people" deciding something with small but real stakes (I can offer, for instance, a modest choice about an end-of-term event or a small class budget), structured so that across several rounds the "will of the people" keeps coming out differently despite nobody's mind changing.

Concretely, I'd like the design to include:

1. **Rounds with different aggregation procedures.** First a round where a single student, cast in a spokesperson or tribune role, is charged with eliciting views and then announcing what "the people want" — arranged so the announcement inevitably flattens real disagreement into one voice. Then rounds that re-run the same underlying preferences through different decision rules (plurality vote, ranked ballot, open deliberation aiming at consensus, and ideally one round where a designated student controls only the agenda or the exact wording of the question), such that at least once the "will" flips purely because the procedure changed.

2. **Engineered option sets and preference profiles**, so the flip is reliable rather than luck — tell me how many options, roughly what shape of disagreement to seed (e.g., a cyclic structure among three options), and how to elicit preferences privately beforehand so I can check the setup will produce the intended result.

3. **A debrief** that maps specific moments onto the conceptual material: the gap between the mere sum of individual wants and any unified general will; the fact that agenda-setters and spokespeople don't just discover the people's will but partly manufacture it; and the question of who got silently excluded from counting as "the people" at each stage. Students should not hear the word "populism" until the debrief.

4. **Predictable failure modes** the first time I run it — e.g., students treating the tribune role as comedy, apathy from low stakes, collusion, or a round where a genuine majority exists and no flip occurs — with a mitigation for each.

5. **An honest limitation**: which feature of actual populist movements the activity cannot reproduce (I suspect the affective dimension — belonging, betrayal, the construction of an enemy — won't arise in a classroom), and one or two things I can say or add to gesture at it without pretending the game delivered it.

Rules should be complete enough that I could hand them to a colleague to run verbatim.
