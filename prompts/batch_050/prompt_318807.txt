I'm a university student writing a paper for a course on incentives and institutional design, and I want to analyze the Wells Fargo fake-accounts scandal — publicly revealed in 2016, in which employees opened millions of unauthorized deposit and credit-card accounts under aggressive cross-selling targets — through the lens of Goodhart's law and specification gaming.

Use the following working taxonomy of Goodhart failures, proposed by my instructor, so treat it as ours rather than canonical: a metric can fail (1) by decoupling, where pushing the proxy hard destroys its correlation with the underlying goal; (2) by threshold distortion, where effort clusters around the cutoff in ways locally sensible but globally wasteful; (3) by diversion, where resources flow from unmeasured valuable activities to measured ones; and (4) by adversarial fabrication, where actors make the metric read well without even pretending to advance the goal. For each mode, say whether and how it was instantiated at Wells Fargo — and be careful with (1) versus (4), since I think the interesting question is whether the cross-selling metric decayed gradually under pressure or was simply defrauded from the start, and whether that distinction even matters for the theory.

Then run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode show about Goodhart's law itself? In particular, is the scandal really a Goodhart failure at all, given that accounts-per-customer was arguably never a good proxy for customer value to begin with — which suggests a design failure upstream of any gaming? What would a Goodhart-resistant target for a retail bank actually look like, if such a thing exists? Where the theory is silent on a question the case raises, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
