I run a monthly workshop for grad students who are EA-curious but pretty skeptical of the far-future stuff -- about 12 people, and I've got a 2-hour slot. I want an activity that makes them feel the pull of strong longtermism before they've heard the term: specifically not just "future people matter" (they already nod along to that) but the stronger and weirder part, where the sheer number of possible future people makes anything that shifts the long-run trajectory -- extinction, permanent value lock-in, institutional entrenchment -- swamp even very good short-term stuff. I want them to catch themselves making choices that prioritize round-80 over round-3 and then feel slightly vertiginous about it.

Design the whole thing: rules, materials, setup, a timeline I can actually follow. A few constraints:

- No lecture up front and none of the usual vocabulary (no "existential risk," "expected value," "astronomical stakes") until the debrief. They should run into the argument through the incentives, not through me.
- The activity needs a compounding/path-dependence mechanism -- early decisions should multiply or constrain everything downstream -- and some kind of small-probability game-ending event they can choose to hedge against or ignore, so the extinction-risk intuition emerges from their own risk budgeting.
- Real-ish stakes but no money: some point scheme or prize that's just big enough to make them mildly competitive.
- There should be a moment where someone realizes the "obviously right" present-round move is dominated by the boring long-run move, and the room argues about it. Tell me how to engineer that moment without rigging it so hard they feel manipulated.

Then the debrief: map specific moments in the game onto the actual argument (the vastness claim, the persistence/lock-in assumption, tractability), and give me a plan for when it predictably goes wrong -- e.g., a min-maxer who treats it purely as an optimization puzzle, or someone who refuses the premise that the game even keeps going. Also, be honest with me about what the activity structurally can't reproduce. My guess is the big one is that in the game the future rounds are guaranteed and knowable, whereas the real argument leans on highly speculative claims about the deep future -- if you think it's something else, tell me, but either way tell me how to keep the debrief honest about it so the skeptics don't feel like they got conned.
