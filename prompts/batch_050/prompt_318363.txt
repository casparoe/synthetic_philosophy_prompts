Last month I had a long and, by the end, fairly ill-tempered argument with a friend about whether it is ever legitimate to condemn historical figures for views and practices that were unremarkable in their own day. She defended what she called the "man of his time" principle: blame requires that the person could reasonably have known better, and for most people in most periods the better view simply was not available. I argued that the principle proves too much and is usually invoked selectively -- we never hesitate to credit figures for being ahead of their time, so we cannot in consistency exempt them for being of their time. Neither of us could state a rule that said when the defence succeeds and when it fails, and I left the argument suspecting we had both been deciding case by case and rationalizing afterward.

This matters practically, not just theoretically. I sit on the commemorations committee of a large institution, and we review between eight and twelve cases a year: building names, statues, endowed chairs, named scholarships, portraits. Our recommendations go to a board that has been burned twice for being arbitrary -- once for removing a name on what looked like impulse, once for retaining one on what looked like obstinacy. What I want is a reusable decision procedure my committee could actually run on a case, one that operationalizes whatever the serious philosophical thinking about this dispute amounts to. I do not want citations or a reading list; I want the substance, translated into steps. We typically have the facts of a case well documented before we meet, and we can afford about two meetings per case, so the procedure should be designed for a small committee of non-philosophers with a dossier in front of them.

The procedure should address, in order and with explicit guidance on each, at least the following fault lines from my argument -- and should correct me where I have the philosophy wrong rather than simply building my assumptions in:

1. The availability question. What exactly has to be "available" for blame to stick: the bare idea that the practice is wrong, a worked-out alternative, a live social movement, or merely the capacity to have reflected? How should the committee operationalize this -- what evidence in a dossier counts for or against availability?

2. The contemporary-dissent test. I leaned hard on the fact that for almost any historical wrong, some contemporaries saw it clearly. My friend replied that a lone eccentric or a tiny marginal sect proves nothing about what a reasonable person of the period could have seen. Under what conditions does the existence of dissenting contemporaries defeat the "man of his time" defence, and when does it not?

3. The difference between conforming to a bad norm and entrenching it. Some figures merely reflected their era's views; others actively defended the practice against criticism, or expanded it, or suppressed the critics. Should the procedure treat these differently, and how?

4. The asymmetry I raised: if we praise figures for moral insight beyond their time, consistency seems to demand we blame figures who fell short of insights that were available. Is that argument actually sound, or does it conflate two different things? The procedure should take a position, not gesture.

5. The separation question. Judging the person's blameworthiness and deciding whether to continue an honour are different verdicts. The procedure must keep them distinct, say how the first bears on the second, and identify what else the second depends on -- I assume what the honour is *for*, and what removing or retaining it would now communicate, but tell me if the framing is off.

For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer to move on, where the procedure terminates in a recommendation, and where it must stop and hand the question back to the committee as a judgment call that no procedure can settle. Be honest about which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure, in compressed form, on three cases: a founder-type figure who held enslaved people while writing, in his own hand, the arguments against slavery; a wartime leader honoured for one achievement whose record includes a documented catastrophe and openly expressed views about colonized peoples that were contested even then; and a fifteenth-century explorer whose entire historical significance is inseparable from the conquest his voyages initiated. At least one of these should be a case where the procedure yields a verdict you expect me -- as the person who lost patience with the "man of his time" defence -- to dislike, and you should say so explicitly. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do: which recurring disputes on my committee it will not resolve, and where following it faithfully will still leave us exposed to the charge of arbitrariness.
