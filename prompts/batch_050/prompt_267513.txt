I was arguing with my roommate last night about a medieval ethics idea — Abelard's view that sin lies entirely in the consent of the will, not in the outward act itself — and we realized we were both half-remembering it and neither of us could steelman the other side properly. So I'd like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers, Anna and Ben, on this. Anna defends the Abelardian position: the outward deed adds nothing to the moral assessment, so a person who fully consents to murder but is stopped by pure bad luck (say, the gun jams) is exactly as blameworthy as one who succeeds, and someone who performs an outwardly harmful act without consent — forced, coerced, or tragically misinformed — hasn't sinned at all. Ben thinks this is crazy and defends the view that what you actually *do* matters morally, not just what you'd be willing to do; he thinks the world, victims, and the act itself are part of the moral picture, not just inner states of mind.

Don't let it be a one-sided lecture: both should be genuinely sharp, and they should respond to each other's actual arguments. I'd especially like them to clash over two or three concrete test cases — e.g., the thwarted would-be murderer vs. the successful one, the person who does harm while sincerely believing it's good (Anna should have to defend that following one's conscience can be right even when the act is wrong), and maybe a case where two people perform the identical outward action with very different intentions. Let each of them land at least one point that makes the other pause and partially concede something, but they don't need to end in agreement — it's fine if they locate the real crux of their disagreement by the end (e.g., whether moral assessment is fundamentally about the agent's will or about agents-plus-the-world). Around 600-900 words, natural dialogue, no narrator summarizing who's right.
