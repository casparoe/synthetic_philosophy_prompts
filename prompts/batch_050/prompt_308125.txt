I’m trying to figure out when rational disagreement can survive, in Aumann-agreement territory. My verdicts:

- Two forecasters share a prior, have different private weather data, and know each other are honest and rational. If they repeatedly announce their probabilities publicly, they should eventually converge, even if neither explains the underlying data.
- Same setup, except one is secretly inflating every estimate to influence the other: no convergence requirement.
- Same honesty and rationality, but genuinely different priors before the inquiry: lasting disagreement can be rational.
- They share a prior, but one knows she may not have worked out every implication of her evidence: disagreement can remain because idealized common knowledge is missing.
- A doctor and patient share the same prior and case data, but the doctor has much greater expertise. I’m inclined to say they can keep different estimates.
- Two researchers share a prior and know each other are honest and rational, but only announce “high,” “medium,” or “low” rather than exact probabilities. I’m unsure whether their categories have to line up.

Is there one clean principle behind these verdicts? State it in terms of priors, private evidence, honesty, rationality, and common knowledge. Also say which case needs the most qualification, which verdict I should probably drop, and what the principle predicts if both parties email their estimates simultaneously and then aren’t allowed to revise them.
