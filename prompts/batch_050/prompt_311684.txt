My younger sister recently started using one of those AI companion apps, and when she mentioned it at a family dinner it set off a long argument between my cousin Daniel and my friend Priya, who were both visiting. I've been turning it over since, and I'd like a referee, because I genuinely can't tell who got the better of it.

Daniel's opening position was that any relationship with a chatbot is founded on deception: the app simulates caring about you, but nothing behind the screen cares, so the comfort it provides is illusory in the way the pleasure of someone in an experience machine would be. Priya pushed back that no adult user is actually deceived about what the app is -- my sister knows it's software -- and that we don't say the emotional value of a novel is illusory just because the characters don't exist; real feelings of comfort can be produced by things that don't reciprocate, as with grief for the dead or unrequited love. Daniel replied that fiction and grief don't talk back: the app presents itself as a persistent someone who knows you and chooses you, and its whole design depends on users half-forgetting what they intellectually know, which is where the deception lives -- not in any single false sentence but in the engineered overall impression. Priya conceded that was a fair distinction but said it only showed the value is one-sided, not that it's fake; one-sided attachments can still be among the most meaningful things in a person's life. Daniel then shifted ground and said his real objection was the incentives: a company that profits from your attachment will build a product that never challenges you, never leaves, and never has needs of its own, so the "relationship" is optimized to flatter rather than to be good for you. Priya answered that this is a complaint about a business model, not about AI companionship as such -- a nonprofit or self-hosted companion would escape it entirely -- and added that plenty of human relationships are asymmetric and instrumentalized in one direction without us declaring them worthless. Daniel's final move was to say that even if the comfort is real in some thin experiential sense, time spent being agreed with by something that cannot suffer, disagree in ways that matter, or need you back is time withdrawn from the only relationships that can actually make demands on you, and that withdrawal is a cost whatever the comfort is worth.

Who is right about which specific points here? I'd like a step-by-step assessment rather than an overall winner: Is Priya right that full disclosure rules out deception, or does Daniel's distinction between knowing something and being designed to half-forget it hold up? Does his claim that the comfort is therefore illusory follow? Does her fiction analogy survive his objection about interactivity and persistence? Is his incentive argument really detachable from the case for AI companionship as such, as she says? And is there anything -- some distinction or question -- that both of them are missing entirely?
