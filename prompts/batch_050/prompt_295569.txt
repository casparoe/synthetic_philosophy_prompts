I keep getting into the same argument with a friend of mine who does modern European history: would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler? It's been going for like two years now and neither of us has moved, which is starting to feel diagnostic in itself. His side, roughly the intentionalist one: no Hitler, no Holocaust. The specific decisions that made it what it was -- invading the USSR while Britain was still undefeated, declaring war on the US for no obvious reason, the slide from persecution to extermination -- trace back to one man's ideological obsessions and gambler's temperment, and nobody else in the Nazi leadership had his particular mix of charisma, radicalism, and total contempt for expert caution. My side, roughly the structuralist one: Weimar was already wrecked by Versailles resentment, the Depression, anti-Marxist panic among conservative elites, and an army and judiciary sympathetic to the authoritarian right, so some kind of radical right dictatorship was very likely, and once a movement like the NSDAP existed its internal dynamics -- everyone competing to anticipate and outbid what the leader wanted, the way Kershaw describes -- would have radicalized policy whoever sat at the top. Italy got fascism without a Hitler. Spain got Franco without genocide.

Here's my working hypothesis about why we're stuck, and I want you to stress-test it rather than just referee: I think "without Hitler" is under-specified in a way that lets both sides be right. Hitler is both a person-token and a bundle of properties, and different counterfactual surgeries remove different bundles. Remove the orator and maybe the party never wins elections. Remove the apocalyptic anti-Semite and maybe you keep the dictatorship but not the genocide. Remove the risk-tolerant strategist and the war goes differently but the regime stays. And anyone performing the surgery has to say which of his traits the replacement inherits -- if the answer is "none, generic placeholder," then that placeholder assumption is doing all the argumentative work. So maybe structuralists are explaining the type (a fascist regime in interwar Germany) and intentionalists are explaining the token (this war, this genocide, this timing), and the debate is really two questions wearing one coat. Is that the right diagnosis, or is this a genuine disagreement about the causal structure of history -- e.g. about how stable the interwar order actually was -- that better evidence could in principle resolve? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: is comparative history (the other interwar fascist movements) legitimate quasi-data for a single-case counterfactual, or is that an illusion of evidence? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of who said what, and an honest answer about whether anything could settle this.
