Public health agencies routinely allocate resources using QALYs: treatments are ranked by cost per quality-adjusted life year, and a QALY counts the same whether it goes to a young or old patient, a rich or poor one, someone whose baseline health is already good or already terrible. Meanwhile, the same governments' regulatory cost-benefit analyses often weigh costs and benefits by willingness to pay, which effectively counts a benefit to a wealthy person as larger than an identical physical benefit to a poor person. I've been told that welfare economics since Robbins has shown that interpersonal comparisons of well-being are meaningless — that there is no fact of the matter about whether my gain exceeds your loss. Yet these institutions compare and aggregate welfare across persons every day, and treat the results as objective inputs to policy. What exactly must QALY-based allocation presuppose about interpersonal comparability for the practice to make sense? Does it require full cardinal measurability of well-being across persons, or would something weaker — ordinal comparisons of levels or differences, or merely unit comparability — suffice? Which of the standard skeptical arguments (behaviorist, ordinalist, or the claim that there is no common scale) does the practice actually refute, merely evade, or quietly concede? And is the willingness-to-pay methodology in tension with the QALY methodology, or can both rest on one coherent account of interpersonal comparison? Where the literature genuinely disagrees — for instance, on whether the ordinalist challenge was ever successfully answered or simply bypassed — please say so rather than presenting one resolution as settled.
