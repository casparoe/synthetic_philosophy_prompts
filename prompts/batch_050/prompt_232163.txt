I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and I'm putting together a sidebar to go with a feature about scientists who slide from descriptive claims into moral ones (the evolutionary psychologist who says we're "built" for fairness so fairness must be good, the wellness guy citing dopamine studies as proof we should meditate, etc). The sidebar is a sorting quiz: "where do you actually stand on the is-ought gap?" Roughly eight questions, each one a concrete little scenario where someone moves from an is to an ought, with two or three answer options per question, plus a scoring key at the end that maps answer patterns onto positions. The positions I want the key to distinguish: the reductive naturalist (moral facts just are natural facts under another description), the Moorean non-naturalist (goodness is its own simple, indefinable property), the expressivist (the ought-claim was never a fact-claim to begin with, so there's no gap to cross), the error theorist (ought-claims aim at facts but there aren't any), and the neo-Aristotelian naturalist (the gap closes legitimately through notions like function and flourishing -- a lame deer *should* be helped the way a dull knife should be sharpened). If the quiz can't cleanly seperate two of these, say so and explain why the cases blur them, rather than pretending otherwise.

The hinge of the quiz should be a passage I've been chewing on from G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica (1903), ch. 1, §13, which I want to quote in the sidebar's intro: "But whoever will attentively consider with himself what is actually before his mind when he asks the question Is pleasure (or whatever it may be) after all good? can easily satisfy himself that he is not merely wondering whether pleasure is pleasant." Moore's whole test is that for any natural property someone proposes as the definition of good, you can still meaningfully ask whether *that* thing is good, which he takes to show the definition failed. So build one or two questions that run the reader through versions of Moore's experiment themselves (e.g., is the desire to desire something still an open question once you've agreed it's what evolution selected for? does "is this pleasant" feel like the same question as "is this good"?) -- and have the key use their reactions to those as the load-bearing answers. But don't let the quiz just rubber-stamp Moore: at least one question should probe whether the "openness" of the question proves anything about properties, as opposed to just how we talk, since that's where the naturalists push back.

Audience is smart lay readers who have never heard the phrase "naturalistic fallacy," so no question may ask "do you think moral properties are natural properties?" -- every question has to be a case they can react to without knowing any labels. Give each leaf of the scoring key a punchy one-line description plus its most awkward commitment (the thing that position has to swallow that my editors will flag in the comments). And since I know some of your leaves will be thinly populated: if there's a combination of answers that's logically available but nobody really defends, note it in the key and take one sentence on why -- incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Oh and keep the tone of the questions magazine-y, not seminar-y; these run next to a crossword, not in a journal.
