Here's a chunk of Russell's On Denoting (1905) that I keep getting stuck on:

"Suppose now we wish to interpret the proposition, 'I met a man'. If this is true, I met some definite man; but that is not what I affirm. What I affirm is, according to the theory I advocate:
' "I met x, and x is human" is not always false'."

He adds right after: "This leaves 'a man', by itself, wholly destitute of meaning, but gives a meaning to every proposition in whose verbal expression 'a man' occurs."

Please argue that Russell is wrong about his own example: that when I say "I met a man", part of what I affirm really does concern some definite man, and isn't captured by the bare existencial claim he puts in its place. To make this work you'll need to say what that extra content could possibly be, given that 'a man' supposedly contributes no object to the proposition, and you have to take his strongest reply seriously -- roughly, that the whole point of the paraphrase is that I can assert something true about a man I have no acquaintance with, so any definite-man content would have to come from somewhere else. Finally, say what follows for the theory of descriptions if your argument wins: is it refuted, or does it just turn out to be a theory of something narrower than assertion?
