I write about science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and over the course of many interviews I have kept meeting a certain kind of source, and I have never known what to call the position they take — or whether philosophy already has a name for it. Here is the pattern, in two of its instances.

An engineer at a large social-media company tells me she believes the product she works on is, on net, harmful to the teenagers who use it. She has not quit. Her reasoning: if she left, her seat would be filled within a month by someone without her scruples, and the small ways she softens the system — an objection logged in a review meeting, a design change she has managed to get onto the agenda — would disappear with her. She concedes that in five years none of this softening has altered any number the company reports. Meanwhile, people like her feature in the company's public statements about how seriously it takes these concerns.

An environmental scientist inside an energy firm tells me an almost identical story: he stays in order to steer the worst projects away from approval; the steering is invisible in anything the firm publishes; and his job title appears in the firm's sustainability report.

What strikes me is that this is not the usual excuse — that one's individual contribution is too small to matter, so one's hands are clean. These sources agree their presence matters; they argue that it matters negatively, as restraint. Nor is it the politician's dilemma of committing a lesser evil for a greater good: they are not proposing to do wrong, but to remain adjacent to wrongdoing as a brake on it. Yet the brake's performance is never measured, arguably could not be measured by the person claiming to be it, and the claim itself does public-relations work for the very thing being braked.

Is there an established concept for this specific configuration — justified presence as unverifiable restraint, where the justification doubles as legitimation? If so, tell me which of the nearby notions it belongs to — complicity, dirty hands, reforming an institution from within, the mere-replaceability defense — how it differs from each, and which fits best. If there is no established concept, coin a term and define it carefully, with edges: what would the genuine case look like, the conscientious insider whose restraint claim actually checks out, and by what test could an outsider — a journalist, say — tell the real brake from the decorative one?
