In my philosophy of law seminar we have been discussing self-amending rules — Suber's game Nomic, constitutional entrenchment clauses, amendment procedures that can be turned on themselves — and I would like to know what principle, if any, underlies my verdicts about which self-amendments are legitimate. My verdicts: (1) Amending an ordinary rule by following the rule-set's own amendment procedure is unproblematic. (2) Using the amendment rule to amend the amendment rule itself — say, raising the required threshold from a majority to two-thirds — seems legitimate to me. (3) Using the amendment rule to render itself unamendable strikes me as illegitimate, even if every formal step was correctly followed. (4) A rule that simply declares itself irrepealable seems to me toothless rather than genuinely binding. (5) Yet I cannot locate a real difference between (2) and (3): raising the threshold to unanimity entrenches the amendment rule almost as effectively as declaring it unamendable, and I still feel (2) can be perfectly fine. Is there a coherent principle — perhaps about levels of rules, or about the persistence of the amendment power itself — that captures all of these, or must one verdict be abandoned? Please also state what the best principle implies about a case I keep going back and forth on: amending the amendment rule so that it applies only to rules enacted before a fixed date. Keep your answer under 400 words.
