I'm running a second-year phil of law / jurisprudence seminar, 14 students, one 75-minute session, and I want to use a stripped-down game of Nomic to make them feel the paradoxes of legal self-reference before we put a single name on any of them. Design the actual game for me. I want the full initial ruleset written out, 10-12 rules max, in plain english a student can parse at the table, and the ruleset needs to be rigged with at least three deliberate fault lines: (1) an amendment procedure that never says whether it can be used to amend itself, (2) one rule marked 'immutable' that the amendment procedure can plausibly still reach, and (3) a scoring rule that somehow refers to itself (say, points for passing proposals that change how points work) so that mid-game someone tries to repeal it and the group crashes into the question of whether points already scored under it still stand. The win condition should be reachable in under an hour so the game actually ends and somebody feels robbed by how it ended.

Then the debrief: map at least four specific moments that will predictably happen at the table onto the concepts I want to teach afterwards. I'm thinking of the moment the group starts appealing to what the game is 'obviously for' when the text runs out (an unwritten rule of recognition nobody voted on), the fight over whether the immutable rule is really untouchable (entrenchment and eternity clauses), the retroactivity question when the scoring rule dies, and whatever blows up when someone finally turns the amendment rule on itself. Don't lecture them on any of this up front -- the concepts get named only in the debrief. Also tell me what will go wrong the first time I run it: someone will try to win instantly with a self-referential proposal, we'll hit a deadlock where both readings of a rule are defensible, and at least one student will flatly refuse to accept an outcome as 'legal' -- that one's a gift, tell me how to use them. Finally, be honest about the big thing the game can't reproduce: real legal systems have courts whose authority isn't itself up for a vote, so give me a plan for what to say when a student points that out.
