I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on formal epistemology (about 14 students, all comfortable with basic probability but not with the literature on updating rules), and I have one 75-minute session to spend on a puzzle I keep failing to convey with slides: a subject receives a message whose content is just "if A, then B" -- seemingly silent about whether A -- and yet, after updating on it in the most natural way, her confidence in A drops. My students nod along with the arithmetic when I show it on the board and then, in discussion, revert to insisting that a message that doesn't mention whether A can't possibly bear on A. I was complaining about this to a colleague, who said the problem is that they've never had to *be* the one updating. So I want an activity, not an example.

Design a game, runnable in that 75 minutes, that puts the students in the position of the updater. It should use physical materials (cards, envelopes, dice, colored tokens -- whatever works) so that probabilities are things they can literally count afterward, and it should be structured so that each student first commits in writing to how they'd revise their confidence upon receiving a conditional message, then plays many rounds and keeps score against their own prediction. The crucial design constraint: they must discover, from their own tally, that the intuitive update and the standard conditional-probability update come apart -- I do not want to announce the discrepancy. Ideally the game has a second phase in which the message is generated by a visibly different protocol (say, a messenger who was instructed what to report in each contingency, versus a message drawn blindly), so that they feel in their own tally the fact that what matters is not only the content of what was learned but the process by which it came to be uttered. No lecture up front; they should not hear the name of the puzzle, or any philosopher's name, until the debrief.

Then give me the debrief plan: how to map each moment of the game onto the conceptual distinction at stake (the difference between raising the probability of a conditional proposition and imposing a constraint on one's conditional probabilities, and why standard conditionalization on the material conditional is only one of the candidate responses), plus where the standard moves of resistance live -- for instance, the student who insists the correct response is to keep their confidence in A fixed and revise only their conditional judgments, and what that commitment costs. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guesses: students miscounting under time pressure, sample sizes too small for the discrepancy to show, a savvy student accusing the messenger protocol of being rigged) and how to handle each. And be frank with me about which feature of the original puzzle no card game can reproduce -- I suspect it has something to do with the fact that the real subject has to *decide* how to take the message rather than having the protocol imposed on her -- so that I know what I still have to supply in the remaining discussion time.
