Write an essay of roughly 800 to 1,000 words on the following case. Weigh the main considerations on each side, but end with a defended verdict rather than a balanced survey of options.

A renowned director releases a film about Anke Brandt, a hospice nurse who died two years earlier and who, in life, was unknown beyond her small town. The film is admiring on the whole; it revives public interest in her work and portrays her as humane and quietly courageous. One pivotal scene, however, is invented: Anke is shown altering a dying patient's chart so that the patient's estranged son, arriving too late, will believe his mother had forgiven him. Nothing of the kind is recorded of Anke, and the filmmakers concede they have no evidence for it. The screenwriter defends the scene as a compression that expresses a deeper truth about the moral improvisations of end-of-life care, and argues that a drama confined to documented fact would fail to honor what Anke's work was actually like. Anke's daughter objects that the scene will be taken as fact — early screenings suggest most viewers do take it as fact — and that her mother, a stickler for accuracy, would have hated it. Because the dead cannot be defamed, the daughter has no legal remedy. The director offers her a private screening before release and a closing card listing which scenes were invented, but refuses to cut the scene.

Your essay should reach a conclusion about whether the director acts permissibly, and along the way it should address at least the following: whether a dead person can be wronged at all, and if not, whose complaint this really is; whether it matters that Anke was a private person rather than a public figure, and that the invention is compromising rather than flattering; whether the appeal to emotional truth marks a principled boundary between legitimate compression and betrayal, or is merely a license; and whether the offered disclosure card changes the verdict. Do not stop at the case. State the general principle your verdict rests on — some defensible line between dramatization and betrayal — and then test that principle against a hard case of your own construction, for instance an invented but flattering scene about Anke, or a comparably invented scene in a film about a statesman dead for two centuries.

Write your response as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
