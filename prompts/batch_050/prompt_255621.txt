Last weekend I had a long, unresolved argument with a close friend about how far the state may go in overriding parents for the sake of the child's future options. My friend is a committed pluralist: she thinks that unless a child is being physically harmed, the state should leave families alone, and she cites cases like the Amish school exemption in Wisconsin v. Yoder as the right kind of humility. I found myself defending a version of the idea, associated with Joel Feinberg's writing on children's rights, that children have claims against everyone, including their parents, to keep a reasonable range of adult futures open to them -- that an upbringing which forecloses nearly all ways of life except one wrongs the child even when it involves no abuse in the ordinary sense. Neither of us budged. But it made me realize I have no idea how my own principle would actually be administered.

That is what I want your help with. Suppose I sit on a regional child-welfare review board that advises family courts and school districts on intervention requests: a hospital asking to override parental refusal of a non-emergency treatment, a district asking whether to require homeschooled children to meet curricular benchmarks, a caseworker asking whether a community's educational practices warrant scrutiny. I get roughly two such requests a month, each with a one-to-two-page file, and I have about an hour per case. My board's recommendations carry weight but are not binding.

Turn the open-future principle into a decision procedure I can actually run on a file: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure halts with a recommendation (in either direction), and where it must hand the question back to me as a value judgment rather than a finding. The procedure has to respect the distinctions that keep the principle from collapsing into something my friend rightly fears -- a license to override any upbringing that is merely unusual, unpopular, or religious -- so it needs to distinguish at least between closing options and failing to open them, between irreversible and reversible foreclosures, and between harm to the child and mere divergence from majority practice. Wherever one of those distinctions cannot be applied mechanically, say so and say what kind of judgment has to fill the gap.

Structure your response in exactly these sections, in this order: Procedure (the ordered steps, roughly 500-700 words), Where judgment cannot be eliminated (roughly 200 words), Test runs (roughly 500 words total), Failure modes (roughly 150 words), and What my friend would say (roughly 150 words). In Test runs, apply the procedure to three cases: parents declining a cochlear implant for a deaf toddler on the grounds that deafness is an identity rather than a disability; a religious community whose schools teach no science, history, or literature beyond its own texts past age twelve; and parents refusing a low-risk surgery that would correct a condition limiting the child's mobility but not threatening life. At least one of these should be a case where the procedure yields a recommendation I will not like -- flag which one and why. In What my friend would say, give the strongest version of her objection to the procedure as designed, not to the principle in the abstract.
