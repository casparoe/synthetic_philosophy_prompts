I'm drafting a position paper for a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and before I submit it I want an audit of whether the views I defend in it are jointly tenable. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of mind with particular expertise in functionalism, the metaphysics of realization, and the foundations of cognitive science. Work carefully: I suggest you first reconstruct, for each commitment below, the motivation a philosopher would standardly give for it, and only then check the set as a whole — since most of these are near-orthodoxies, the interesting question is whether the standard motivations behind them are compatible with one another, not whether each is defensible in isolation.

Here is where I stand:

(1) Functionalism, full strength. Mental state types — beliefs and desires, but also qualitative states like pain — are individuated by their causal and functional roles rather than by their intrinsic physical constitution. A state is a pain in virtue of occupying the pain-role in a system's overall functional organization, whatever happens to realize that role.

(2) Multiple realizability as the decisive objection to the identity theory. Because pain is realized by different physiological states in humans, octopuses, and plausibly in future silicon systems, no type-identity claim such as pain = C-fiber firing can be correct. I regard this as the strongest argument against the identity theory and treat it as essentially settled.

(3) Physicalism about causation. The physical world is causally closed: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause. At the same time, my pain genuinely causes my wincing and my reaching for aspirin — mental causation is real, and epiphenomenalism about the mental is a non-starter for me.

(4) Antireductionism about psychology. Psychological explanations and generalizations are autonomous: they are not reducible to neuroscience, would not be improved by being replaced with neural descriptions, and capture real causal patterns that the neural level of description misses. Psychology is not a promissory note to be redeemed by future brain science.

(5) A grain constraint on the functional analysis. The functional role definitive of pain must be specified at a level of abstraction that is neither chauvinist (it must count octopus pain, and pain in appropriately organized silicon systems, as pain) nor liberal (it must not count a vast population of people passing slips of paper so as to simulate a brain's functional organization, or a giant lookup-table system, as feeling pain). I take it that some principled intermediate level of functional description exists, even if no one has stated it exactly.

(6) Computational explanation is causal explanation. When cognitive science explains behavior by appeal to computations, it gives a genuinely causal-mechanical explanation, even though computational characterizations abstract away from the details of the implementing hardware.

My questions: Which of these commitments are in genuine tension with one another, and which pairs merely appear to be? Please rank any real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious; for each, state exactly which commitments generate it, what would have to give, and what giving it up would cost the overall position. If a revision restores consistency only by quietly weakening one of my motivations, say so. I would also like you to say how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and to single out the one point in your audit where you are least certain, explaining why.
