Write a research proposal for a one-semester graduate project (final paper of roughly 8,000 words) in metaontology. The working question: under what conditions, if any, is an ontological dispute merely verbal — and can that question be answered in a way that survives the charge that it, too, is merely verbal?

Center the proposal on the following half-formed idea, which I want sharpened rather than replaced. Call a dispute stubbornly symmetric if each party, when asked to state the opposing thesis in their own preferred vocabulary as charitably as possible, produces a sentence they themselves accept as true. Mereological nihilists and universalists about composition arguably stand in this relation: each side can render the other's central claims as trivial truths, differing only in how they talk about arrangements. I conjecture that stubborn symmetry is necessary but not sufficient for a dispute to be merely verbal, because some disputes are symmetric in this way yet continue to generate different downstream judgments — about how to answer counting problems, about parsimony, about which theory is to be preferred. The project would state a precise criterion that supplies the missing condition, perhaps in counterfactual or decision-theoretic terms (what would each side judge differently if the linguistic issue were stipulated away?), and then test the criterion against two case studies: unrestricted composition and the existence of abstract objects.

The proposal should do the following:

1. State the research question so that it has a determinate answer, and define success and failure conditions in advance. For instance: a criterion that classifies every ontological dispute as verbal, or none of them, has failed.
2. Explain why the existing menu of positions — neo-Carnapian deflationism, quantifier-variance approaches, and heavyweight realism about ontology — leaves this question open. Describe positions and arguments rather than citing texts; I will supply all citations myself, so do not include a bibliography, reference list, or reading list.
3. Justify the method: conceptual analysis built around case studies, rather than experimental philosophy or pure exegesis of the literature, and say briefly why those alternatives are being set aside.
4. Address the self-application worry explicitly: what prevents the proposed criterion from becoming one more move in a verbal dispute about the meaning of 'verbal'?
5. Give a candid scope assessment: which components could be cut without gutting the project, and what risks making it unfinishable in a single semester.

The deliverable is the proposal, not the research itself; argue that the project is both worth doing and doable.
