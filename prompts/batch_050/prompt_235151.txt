I run a small research group that has just received funding for a three-year project (three full-time researchers) in the metaphysics of dispositions, with the explicit remit that our results should eventually be usable outside the specialist literature — for instance, in clarifying what pharmacologists, materials scientists, or engineers commit to when they ascribe capacities, fragilities, or propensities to systems. I am writing the project's agenda and would like a defended prioritization of the open questions, not a survey of the literature.

To fix the background: the standard problem cases are familiar to us. A disposition is finkish when the stimulus that would trigger its manifestation instead removes the disposition itself before the manifestation can occur (a fragile glass wrapped in a device that instantly anneals it the moment it is struck). A disposition is masked when the disposition remains but something interferes with the manifestation (a fragile glass wrapped in packing foam, struck, and unbroken). And there are mimics: cases where the characteristic manifestation occurs without the disposition being present at all. We also take seriously a harder, less-discussed class of cases our group has been calling ambiguous interferers: devices where it is genuinely indeterminate, or at least underdetermined by the physics, whether they remove the disposition at the moment of stimulus (fink) or merely block its manifestation (mask) — for example, a chemical that, upon impact, reconfigures the glass's molecular structure in a way that is simultaneously the loss of fragility and the suppression of breaking.

Given that framing, which of the following open questions should we prioritize, and in what order? (a) Whether any conditional or reductive analysis of dispositional ascriptions can survive finks, masks, and mimics, or whether the project should be abandoned in favour of primitivism or a purely functional characterization. (b) Whether the fink/mask distinction marks a real metaphysical difference or merely a difference in levels of description — a question our ambiguous-interferer cases seem to sharpen. (c) Whether every disposition requires a distinct categorical basis, or whether a world of pure powers is coherent. (d) How to individuate multi-track dispositions — whether fragility is one disposition with many stimulus-manifestation pairs or a family. (e) How dispositional ascriptions to distributed systems without a unified intrinsic basis (institutions, ecosystems, large engineered systems) should be treated, given that this is where most of the applied payoff lies.

For your ordering, please state your criteria explicitly — I suggest considering at least how many of the other questions are downstream of each candidate, its tractability for philosophers over three years, and its relevance to our applied remit — and weigh them against one another where they pull apart. Also tell me which question you would deprioritize or drop entirely, and what intermediate finding, within the first year, should make us revise the ranking.
