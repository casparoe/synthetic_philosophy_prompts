So I've been a LessWrong/rationalist-forum reader for like a decade and I finally drafted this essay that's been rattling around in my head about whether "generations" (Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z) are actually real things or just astrology for people with spreadsheets. I'd like comments before I post it. Here's the draft:

Title: Generations are folk species

Demographers have this thing called the age-period-cohort problem. Any outcome you measure in a population can in principle come from three sources: age effects (people get more conservative as they get older, say), period effects (everyone got lonelier in 2020 regardless of age), and cohort effects (people who grew up during the Depression stayed frugal their whole lives). The catch is that these three are perfectly linearly dependent -- age equals period minus cohort -- so no statistical machinery can fully tease them apart from cross-sectional data alone. You always have to make an extra assumption to get identification. Which means every confident claim about "Millennials are like X because they came of age during 9/11" rests on a modeling choice, not on the data itself.

I think this is why the whole generations discourse feels so slippery, and I want to argue we should treat generational labels the way philosophers of biology treat pre-Darwinian species concepts. Before Darwin, species were essences with sharp boundaries and defining traits. After Darwin, they became temporary clusters in a continuous, fuzzy genealogical process, and the "species problem" turned out to be not one problem but like twenty different legitimate ways to carve things. My claim: "Millennial" is a morphospecies. It's a bucket built from a few salient, media-friendly traits (avocado toast, participation trophies), and its boundaries get moved around whenever the narrative needs them to (see: the end-date of the Millennial cohort being revised repeatedly).

The alternative I want to propose is what I'll call imprint-window clustering. There's decent evidence for a "reminiscence bump" -- the events that happen to you roughly between ages 15 and 25 loom disproportionately large in memory and identity for the rest of your life. So instead of drawing arbitrary cutoff years, define cohorts empirically: cluster people by which historical events landed inside their imprint window. Then you get a nice testable prediction. In countries with punctuated history -- Germany around 1945 and 1989, say -- the clustering should be real and tight, and "generations" there are honest-to-goodness natural kinds. In countries with smooth, eventless history, the clustering dissolves into a continuum and every generational label is just cohort smearing: compressing a smooth gradient into hard boxes because our brains want discrete characters.

If that's right, American generations discourse is mostly noise, and the real object-level question is always "is this an age effect, a period effect, or an actual cohort effect?" -- which, recall, is exactly the question the data alone can never fully answer. Uncomfortable, but I think true.
