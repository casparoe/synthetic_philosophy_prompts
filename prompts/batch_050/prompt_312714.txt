Universities and hospitals routinely name buildings after major donors, and fundraisers openly say this works because the recognition is part of what motivates the gift. At the same time, most people I know agree that an anonymous donation is more admirable than one made for the plaque, and several religious traditions treat quiet giving as the highest form of charity. What does this whole practice of publicly honoring generosity presuppose about where the value of a generous act lies? Does it assume that an act's worth depends on the giver's reasons for acting, or only on its effects? If only effects matter, I can't see why we rank the anonymous gift higher; but if motives matter, it seems the institutions are deliberately cultivating a less admirable motive in order to do good, and I don't know whether that's a compromise or an inconsistency. Please reconstruct the view of moral worth the practice appears to commit us to, say whether it could instead be made sense of on the rival view, and tell me what would have to change in how we fundraise and give praise if we took the motive-based view fully seriously.
