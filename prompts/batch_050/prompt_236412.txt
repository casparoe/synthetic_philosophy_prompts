I produce a small interview series for a university philosophy society in which we stage long-form conversations with historical philosophers, played by a scholar who answers in character. Our next episode features Ibn Rushd (Averroes), and the conversation will center on The Decisive Treatise, his argument, framed as a legal judgment, that the revealed Law not only permits but commands the study of philosophy. I want questions that go beyond having him restate the harmony thesis, and I want several of them to turn on a specific passage from the treatise's opening (in George F. Hourani's translation), where the conditional machinery of his argument is laid bare:

"We say: If the activity of 'philosophy' is nothing more than study of existing beings and reflection on them as indications of the Artisan, i.e. inasmuch as they are products of art (for beings only indicate the Artisan through our knowledge of the art in them, and the more perfect this knowledge is, the more perfect the knowledge of the Artisan becomes), and if the Law has encouraged and urged reflection on beings, then it is clear that what this name signifies is either obligatory or recommended by the Law."

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. I would like the sequence to press on at least the following: whether the passage's definition of philosophy smuggles a theological conclusion into a premise — is a natural philosophy that brackets the Artisan altogether still licensed by the Law, or has it forfeited its warrant, so that the harmony is achieved only by redefining philosophy piously; whether the move from "obligatory or recommended" to his considered view, that demonstrative study is obligatory only for those constitutionally capable of it, has the Law commanding something most believers cannot perform, and what that implies about the epistemic standing of the rhetorical and dialectical classes; the later Latin accusation that his position amounts to a doctrine of double truth, and how he can deny it while holding that Scripture bears an interior sense that must be withheld from the majority; who is entitled to settle which verses admit of interpretation, given his own argument that no binding consensus can be established on theoretical questions; and how he answers al-Ghazali's charge that the philosophers' conclusions — the eternity of the world, God's knowledge of universals rather than particulars, the denial of bodily resurrection — constitute not mere error but unbelief. No generic questions about his life, his influences, or what drew him to philosophy. At the end of the sequence, state on a final line, in the form Verdict: Question N, which single question you would expect to give him the most difficulty, with one sentence of justification.
