Years of reading rationalist blogs have left me fond of steelmanning positions I dislike, and I recently constructed the following argument against solar geoengineering research that now worries me because it seems to prove too much. (1) Whenever you make a harmful activity cheaper to engage in, people engage in more of it; this is just the moral hazard mechanism that insurance economics and the Peltzman effect literature take for granted. (2) A credible, well-publicized capacity to inject stratospheric aerosols makes continued fossil fuel emissions cheaper to engage in, since the worst-case warming outcomes are partially insured against. (3) Therefore funding geoengineering research, by building that credibility, increases emissions. (4) Because a deployed system that is ever abruptly stopped produces rapid termination shock, a world that leans on this insurance is exposed to a catastrophe the no-research world avoids entirely. (5) Therefore we should not fund the research. (6) But the form of (1) through (4) is perfectly general: it condemns seatbelts, which encourage faster driving; HIV PrEP, which encourages riskier sex; fire brigades, which reduce incentives to fireproof buildings; and deposit insurance, which encourages bank risk-taking, and in each of those cases I regard the moral hazard objection as a bad reason to abandon the measure. (7) So either geoengineering research is fine, or my ordinary judgments about seatbelts and fire brigades are wrong. I find (5) genuinely tempting and (6) genuinely embarrassing, and I cannot cleanly identify which step does the damage. If forced to choose, I would rather give up the no-research conclusion than my judgments about seatbelts and PrEP, but I want to know what that choice commits me to. Which step do you reject, why did it look plausible, and what exactly does rejecting it cost me? Please give your answer as continuous prose only: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just the way you would write a careful letter.
