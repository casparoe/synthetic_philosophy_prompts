In an aesthetics seminar, a student defends Tolstoy’s theory of art by comparing successful artistic communication to an infection. On this picture, the artist has lived through a feeling; the artwork is the vector; and the audience catches the feeling through perception. Sincerity makes the artist genuinely infected rather than merely displaying symptoms, clarity makes transmission easier, and individuality determines the particular strain being transmitted. The student draws two conclusions. First, the value of an artwork is measured by how reliably and widely it transmits feeling. Second, art that requires specialist training, historical scholarship, or a curator’s explanation cannot be fully successful: one does not need a lecture in order to catch a disease.

Audit the analogy rather than merely accepting or rejecting it. Give an explicit correspondence table covering the pathogen, host, vector, symptoms, immunity, susceptibility, epidemic spread, and public-health intervention, saying what each corresponds to in artistic production and reception. Then distinguish the respects in which the comparison illuminates Tolstoy’s account from the respects in which it distorts it. In particular, address whether an audience must receive the artist’s very feeling, whether recipients are passive hosts or active interpreters, whether artistic form is merely a delivery mechanism, and whether breadth of response is evidence of artistic merit rather than merely evidence of accessibility or fashion.

Use the following cases to test the account:

1. A composer who feels no grief deliberately uses established musical techniques to make large audiences grieve.
2. A sincerely grieving composer produces an opaque work that transmits grief to almost nobody.
3. An installation causes unease through concealed low-frequency vibrations, although viewers mistakenly attribute their unease to its images.
4. A poem initially moves only readers familiar with a suppressed minority culture, but moves a much broader public after its background becomes known.

For each case, say what the infection analogy suggests, what Tolstoy’s criteria of sincerity, clarity, and infectiousness plausibly imply, and whether the two verdicts come apart. Separate disanalogies that matter for the student’s two conclusions from disanalogies that are merely incidental features of disease. Finally, say whether either conclusion survives once those distinctions are made; if the infection analogy needs replacing or supplementing, propose a better comparison and identify what it captures that infection does not.
