You are an expert in the philosophy of language with deep familiarity with mid-twentieth-century analytic philosophy and contemporary theories of mental content. Please think carefully and step by step, and begin by briefly brainstorming the full space of possible objections before selecting and organizing the strongest ones.

Next month I am presenting to a philosophy department's language-and-mind reading group. My thesis: Quine's argument for the indeterminacy of translation, standardly treated as a thesis about radical translation between linguistic communities, is best understood as a general argument about the underdetermination of content by behavioral and dispositional facts — and as such, it transfers essentially intact to contemporary naturalistic theories of mental content. In particular, I will argue that causal-informational and teleological theories of content face a structurally identical problem to the one Quine raises with the field linguist and the rabbit: the non-semantic facts these theories advert to (causal covariation, selection history, normal conditions) no more fix a unique content for a mental symbol than the native's speech dispositions fix a unique translation of his utterance. Just as the linguist's data are compatible with rabbit, undetached rabbit part, and rabbit stage, the naturalist's data are compatible with multiple candidate contents for a given inner representation, and any principled selection among them smuggles in the very semantic facts that were supposed to be naturalized. I conclude that either content naturalism needs a further ingredient it has not yet identified, or we should accept a mild indeterminacy about mental content on a par with Quine's own verdict about translation.

The audience: about a dozen regulars. Two are Quine scholars who know the primary texts and the secondary literature on indeterminacy extremely well and are protective of the claim's distinctiveness — they resist attempts to detach the argument from Quine's behaviorism and his doctrine of the indeterminacy of meaning holism. Three work on teleosemantics and will defend it. Two are sympathetic to informational semantics. The rest are graduate students and generalists. Nobody in the room is a committed meaning skeptic.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me:

1. The objections I will actually receive from this specific audience, phrased the way each faction would phrase them — the Quine scholars' version, the teleosemanticists' version, the informationalists' version — not a generic list. For each, indicate roughly how likely it is to come up and how damaging it would be if unanswered.
2. For each objection: what I should concede immediately and gracefully, what I must hold firm on, and a reply deliverable in about two minutes.
3. A note on where each of my replies is soft — the premise or move a sharp questioner could press on next.
4. The single objection I am hoping nobody asks, why it is dangerous for my thesis, and the best fallback answer I can give if it comes anyway.
5. One or two points where the standard distinction between my target and Quine's actual argument is on my side, which I should deploy early to keep the Quine scholars from framing the whole session as a textual correction exercise.
