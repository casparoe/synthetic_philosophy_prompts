I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and my reading group has been working through self-binding and institutional design. Our organizer rules on whether each proposed precommitment deserves enforcement. Her verdicts so far:

1. A sailor, knowing the sirens' song will overwhelm his judgment, has his crew lash him to the mast with orders to ignore all his later pleas for release — enforce.
2. A patient with recurrent psychosis signs, while lucid, an advance directive authorizing treatment during future episodes even if she refuses at the time — enforce.
3. A man signs a vow, backed by a large forfeit payable to a cause he despises, never to read arguments against his current religion — refuse.
4. A founding generation writes an eternity clause locking its preferred tax-and-spend formula into the constitution, unamendable forever — refuse.

The new case: an eternity clause entrenching not substantive policy but the constitution's own democratic machinery — the amendment thresholds, federal structure, and free-election requirements — declared unamendable in perpetuity.

Predict her verdict on the new case. Her record so far seems equally consistent with at least two rules — roughly, enforce a precommitment only when it binds a single continuing agent against a foreseen temporary distortion of judgment, versus enforce one whenever it protects decision-procedures rather than locking in substantive outcomes. Lay out the candidate rules, say which one better fits the spirit of her earlier verdicts and why, identify which earlier case is the closest analog to the new one, and name the further hypothetical case that would have cleanly separated the rules before this one arose.
