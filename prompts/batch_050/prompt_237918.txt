For a graduate seminar on medieval ethics, write a 1,500–1,800-word dialogue between two equally capable philosophers: Helena, who defends an Abelardian account on which sin or moral fault consists in the will’s consent rather than in the outward act, and Marcus, who defends a carefully qualified act-and-outcome-sensitive account of culpability.

Organize their exchange around three cases:

1. Two clinicians administer the same potentially lethal dose of the same drug. One intends only to relieve pain and reluctantly accepts a foreseen risk of death; the other administers the dose in order to cause death.
2. Two people form the same murderous resolve and carry out the same poisoning attempt, but one victim dies while the other is saved by an unexpected intervention.
3. A person causes serious harm without intending or consciously endorsing it, but only because they culpably ignored an obvious risk or failed to examine an easily correctable false belief.

Helena must distinguish desire, intention, foresight, consent, and bodily action rather than treating them as interchangeable. She should explain why a forbidden desire need not itself be sinful if it is resisted, why outwardly identical acts can differ morally, and why the successful and unsuccessful poisoners may be equally culpable. Marcus must present the strongest version of the opposing view: not the crude claim that outcomes alone determine blame, but the claim that what an agent actually does or brings about can alter culpability even when the prior consent is the same.

The dialogue should contain genuine philosophical movement. Each speaker must answer the other’s strongest objection, make at least one concession or refinement, and avoid merely restating a preferred definition of responsibility. In discussing the third case, Helena should address whether negligence and culpable ignorance can be explained in terms of some earlier or implicit consent; Marcus should explain why his view does not collapse into an objectionable doctrine of moral luck. The exchange need not end in agreement, but it should isolate one precise unresolved question rather than declaring a winner.

Do not include citations, footnotes, or a bibliography.
