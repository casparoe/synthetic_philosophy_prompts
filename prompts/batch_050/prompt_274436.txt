So I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, and I've been reading epistemology stuff on the side. I got stuck on what's apparently called the value problem for knowledge -- the old Plato question of why knowing something is better than merely having a true belief about it. The debate seems completly stuck and I want to understand why.

As far as I can tell the main moves are: (1) knowledge isn't actually more valuable -- if my belief that the prod database is on server B is true, I get everything I practically need whether or not I "know" it; (2) knowledge is more stable, it's tied down by reasons so it survives counter-evidence better; (3) reliabilism says knowledge is true belief from a reliable process, but then there's this "swamping" objection -- if the coffee is already good, the machine being reliable adds nothing to that cup, so reliability adds nothing once the belief is true; and (4) virtue epistemology says knowledge is success *through* ability, like an archer hitting the target by skill rather than luck, and that's valuable in a way lucky success isn't.

Each side seems to have a rehearsed reply to each other side and nobody moves. My outsider hypothesis: this is really a disagreement about whether the extra value is supposed to attach to the belief itself or to the agent/system producing it -- like valuing a correct output vs valuing a well-built pipeline that outputs it. If that's the split, the debate might be two questions under one name.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Are they disagreeing about what "value" means, about which cases count as data, or is it bedrock intuition that cant be argued for? And does the archer analogy do real work or just restate the intuition it's supposed to support? Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress -- a distinction that dissolves it, or an argument that it's permanently stuc and why.
