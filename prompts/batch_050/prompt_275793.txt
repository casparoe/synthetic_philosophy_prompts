You are a philosopher of medicine with a longstanding interest in the concept of disease and the boundaries of psychiatric classification. Think carefully, work through the analogy systematically before reaching any verdict, and feel free to brainstorm before settling on your analysis.

A colleague of mine organises public-awareness programmes for a mental-health charity, and her materials lean heavily on a single analogy: depression is like diabetes. Both, she says, are chronic conditions with a biological basis; both are nobody's fault; both are managed rather than simply snapped out of; and just as a diabetic takes insulin without shame, a depressed person should take antidepressants and attend therapy without shame. She deploys the analogy to argue that stigma is the principal barrier to treatment and that full parity between mental and physical illness is the corrective. A friend of mine who has been reading Thomas Szasz objects. Szasz held that mental illness is a metaphorical extension of bodily illness, and that much of what gets so labelled is better described as problems in living. On his view, the diabetes analogy does not merely convey a fact about depression; it performs a manoeuvre, relocating moral and social conflicts into the supposedly value-free territory of medicine, where they can be settled by experts rather than argued about.

Audit the analogy. Give the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the lesion, of the cause, of the symptom, of the diagnostic test, of the treatment, of prognosis. Then identify where the mapping breaks: which features of diabetes — a detectable physical dysfunction, laboratory criteria independent of anyone's values, treatments whose targets can be specified without controversy — lack a clear counterpart in depression, and which apparent disanalogies do not in fact matter for the campaign's purposes. Say what the analogy quietly imports, in particular whether it smuggles in a verdict about personal responsibility and about the authority of psychiatry that the comparison alone cannot establish, and assess the Szaszian charge that the medical framing relocates moral questions rather than answering them. Finally, state whether the conclusions the analogy is used to support — that stigma rests on a factual error, and that seeking treatment should carry no more shame than taking insulin — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and propose a better analogy if one exists.

Indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and say what evidence or argument would change your verdict. Write your answer in American English.
