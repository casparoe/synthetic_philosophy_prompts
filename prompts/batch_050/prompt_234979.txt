I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who has, rather late in life, taken up game theory as a pastime, working through an introductory textbook the way I once worked through monographs in my own field. I keep running into a wrinkle I do not have a word for, and I would like to know whether the literature does. Consider a player choosing among three plans against an opponent who also has several options. Suppose the third plan is never the worst reply: against each thing the opponent might do, some single rival plan does strictly worse. So by the crude test of "is some other plan always better than this one," the third plan survives. Yet it can happen that a private coin flip — heads take the first plan, tails take the second — outperforms the third plan against every single thing the opponent might do, even though neither coin-face alone does. My textbook calmly permits one to cross out the third plan on this basis, and here is what struck me: once it is gone, further crossings-out become possible that were blocked before, so the final residue of "admissible" plans is smaller, sometimes much smaller, than what one gets if one insists that only a single rival plan may do the eliminating.

I have since noticed two further things. First, the distinction matters empirically for the method, not just in contrived examples: whether or not one allows coin-flip blends to serve as eliminators changes the outcome of iterated elimination in games that look entirely ordinary. Second, my text mentions in passing that in two-player games, the plans that survive this more permissive crossing-out coincide exactly with the plans a player could justify by some coherent (if possibly mistaken) conjecture about the opponent — whereas plans eliminated in this way could not be so justified. My questions, then: does the distinction between being beaten by a single rival plan and being beaten only by a private mixture have an established name, and does the resulting notion of iterated elimination have one? Is the reported coincidence with "plans defensible by some coherent conjecture" a theorem, and does it rest essentially on allowing mixtures, or would some version hold for single-plan elimination too? Finally, the edges of the phenomenon: does crossing out a plan beaten only by a mixture ever remove something that appears in an equilibrium, and does the answer change for the weaker notion where "always at least as good, sometimes better" suffices for elimination, where I gather the order of crossings-out starts to matter? I am not after a label to drop at dinner parties; I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its boundaries lie.
