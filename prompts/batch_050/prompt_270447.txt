My local council is arguing over what to do with a statue of a Victorian doctor who helped found the town hospital, but whose family fortune partly came from the Caribbean slave trade and who also supported some fairly grim ideas about which poor people deserved treatment. A few residents want it removed altogether, others want a longer plaque, and the museum has suggested putting it in a gallery with proper context. What makes it messier is that the hospital still bears his name, and some patients see the statue as celebrating medicine in the town rather than the man himself. I keep realising that I don’t even know what a good theory of commemoration is supposed to decide.

So write me the desiderata for an adequate ethical account of public commemoration, using my town’s dispute as the running example. What must the account distinguish — for example, remembering someone, explaining them, honouring them, benefiting from them, and making present-day people live under their image? Which cases must it handle, such as mixed legacies, changing community membership, uncertain evidence, statues versus street names, and removal versus recontextualisation? Give the motivation behind each requirement, say which are genuinely non-negotiable and which look more like preferences of one camp, and flag any desiderata that may be jointly unsatisfiable. I’m especially interested in whether an account can simultaneously avoid hiding history, avoid continuing endorsement, respect the people most affected, and give communities a fair say. Finish by briefly testing removal, retention with interpretation, relocation to a museum, and regular democratic review against your list — but keep the main emphasis on the criteria rather than the scorecard. Please use British English, and don’t turn it into a bibliography; I want the conceptual spec, not a reading list.
