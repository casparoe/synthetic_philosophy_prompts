I am in Year 12, and my school's philosophy society runs an exercise in which each member records their settled positions on a topic so that the group can hunt for inconsistencies. My topic is the trolley problem and the doctrine of double effect. My positions are as follows:

1. In the standard case, it is permissible — arguably obligatory — to divert the runaway trolley onto the siding, killing one worker to save five.
2. On the footbridge, it is wrong to push the large stranger onto the track, even though doing so would likewise trade one life for five.
3. The doctrine of double effect explains the difference between these verdicts: one may never intend a person's death as a means to one's end, though one may sometimes knowingly cause a death one merely foresees, provided the good achieved is proportionate.
4. In the loop variant, where the siding rejoins the main track and the trolley would circle back to kill the five unless the one worker's body stops it, diverting remains permissible for the same reason: the worker's death is foreseen rather than intended.
5. A doctor may administer pain relief to a terminally ill patient in a dose she knows will hasten death, so long as she aims only at comfort.
6. The moral status of an action should not turn on the agent's private intentions: two agents who do exactly the same thing, foreseeing exactly the same consequences, deserve the same verdict.

Could you audit this set of commitments? Identify which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to, ordered by severity. For each genuine tension, state what the cheapest fix would be and what it would cost; for each merely apparent tension, explain why it dissolves. Finally, if a single revision would resolve the most trouble, tell me which it is — but please let the reasoning come first rather than simply nominating one of my views for abandonment. Please write your answer in British English.
