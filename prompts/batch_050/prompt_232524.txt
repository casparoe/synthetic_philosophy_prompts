I chair the claims review committee of a specialist insurer of artworks, and we must rule on a claim that has deadlocked us. I need a decision memo I can put to a vote.

The case: A sculptor purchased the clay excavated from the demolition site of her childhood home — an irreplaceable material with documented provenance — and modeled it, by hand and without adding or removing any material, into a life-size figure she titled "First House." We insured First House for 1.8 million. The policy names the work, describes it as the clay from the site "modeled into its present form," and provides that on a total loss we pay the insured sum and take possession of whatever remains as salvage.

Seven months into the policy term, a shelving collapse in the studio crushed the figure back into a shapeless mass. No clay was lost. The sculptor has filed a total-loss claim: in her view, First House has been destroyed and she is owed 1.8 million; she also maintains that the clay, being the material of her childhood home, remains hers, so we are entitled to nothing in return. Our adjuster recommends denial. Nothing has ceased to exist, he writes; there is exactly as much clay, with exactly the same provenance, as before, and a thing's matter just is the thing. The sculptor's lawyer replies that the policy insured a statue, not a lump; a shapeless lump is not a statue; therefore the insured object no longer exists and the claim must be paid in full.

The committee is split, and the split maps uncomfortably well onto a long-standing dispute in metaphysics about statues and the lumps of matter that constitute them: whether statue and clay are one thing or two, whether two distinct things can occupy exactly the same place at the same time, and what, if anything, the crushing actually destroyed.

Write the committee's decision memo, 700–900 words, addressed to the committee and suitable for the file. It should:

1. Reach a recommendation — pay the full claim, deny it, or pay it on condition that the clay passes to us as salvage. You may propose a variant, but the bottom line must be something a committee could vote on and a reviewing court could evaluate.
2. Ground the reasoning in both the policy's language and the metaphysics of material constitution. Show how each of the main families of views would cash the case out — one-thing views on which statue and clay are identical, two-thing or coincidence views on which they are distinct objects sharing matter and place, and views on which talk of "the statue" picks out a stage or phase of a longer-lived lump — and explain which approach the committee should lean on, and why, given that we are interpreting an insurance contract rather than doing ontology for its own sake.
3. Give the strongest argument on the losing side a serious hearing. Our legal department expects an appeal, and a memo that prevails by ignoring the best objection will not survive one.
4. Distinguish what is genuinely settled here from what is contested. It is one thing to note that the crushing destroyed no matter and changed the lump's shape; it is another to declare which theory of constitution is correct. Do not present any contested metaphysical position as the consensus of the field, and wherever the recommendation must rest on a contested premise, flag it as an assumption and state how the analysis would change if that premise failed.
