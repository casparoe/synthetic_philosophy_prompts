I'm in a grad seminar on metaethics and we just spent three weeks on expressivism and the Frege--Geach problem, and I noticed something annoying: everyone in the room, prof included, keeps saying things like "sure, but does this view actually solve Frege--Geach?" and nobody ever says what would count as solving it. There's the standard move of pointing at the embedding cases (moral claims inside negations, conditionals, disjunctions, questions), and the modus ponens case with the mixed descriptive/normative premises, but then the discussion always slides into whether some particular proposal "works," and I can't tell what the scorecard even is. I want the scorecard before I read any more proposals.

So: write me the desiderata that any adequate expressivist response to the Frege--Geach problem has to meet. What must it explain (e.g., why moral predicates seem to mean the same thing in asserted and embedded contexts, why the standard inferences come out valid rather than committing some equivocation, how someone can accept a conditional moral claim without accepting its antecedent)? What distinctions can't it collapse -- I'm thinking of things like the difference between expressing an attitude and merely reporting that you have it, or between genuine contradiction between contents and mere "clash" of attitudes? And which surface features of moral discourse does it have to be able to earn back (disagreement, error, talk of moral truth and mind-independence), versus which of those are optional extras that only the quasi-realist camp insists on? For each item on the list, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether you'd consider it negotiable. If you think some pair of desiderata can't be jointly satisfied -- I have a suspicion that full semantic compositionality and the core expressivist claim that moral sentences don't express beliefs pull against each other, but tell me if I'm wrong -- flag it explicitly, because that would itself be a result.

After the list, briefly score the main families of response against it -- Blackburn-style quasi-realism with its higher-order attitude machinery, Gibbard's norm-expressivism, hybrid views that split content into a descriptive and an attitudinal component, and inferentialist approaches -- but keep the weight on the criteria, not the scoring; I mostly want to know which single desideratum eliminates the most contenders and which one is doing the most quiet philosophical work. One more thing: please be explicit about what's basically agreed on all sides here (e.g., what the original problem actually demands at a minimum) versus what's genuinely contested among people who work on this -- like whether minimalist theories of truth dissolve the problem or make it harder -- and don't present a contested verdict as the consensus view. A list or table format is fine.
