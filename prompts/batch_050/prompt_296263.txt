I'm a retired field entomologist (forty years of it, mostly moths) who's recently fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole, and I keep bumping into a claim that strikes me as bizarre but which I can't cleanly refute: that destroying habitat might actually be *good* for wild animals, or at least reduce their total suffering, because it means fewer wild animals get born in the first place.

The part I want explained is the underlying conceptual machinery, not the empirical verdict. As I understand it, the argument runs something like: (1) whether habitat destruction reduces suffering depends not mainly on the deaths it directly causes, but on the counterfactual lives it prevents; (2) whether preventing those lives reduces suffering depends on whether the average wild-animal life is net-positive or net-negative in welfare terms; and (3) there's a line of reasoning -- something to do with reproductive strategy, i.e. species that produce thousands of offspring almost all of whom die young and horribly -- that supposedly pushes the average below zero.

Please explain this machinery properly. In particular:

- Why does the "one-off deaths vs. prevented future lives" distinction matter so much here? A farmer clearing a wood kills the current residents once, but the land then supports nobody indefinitely after -- so how do you even set up that comparison, and does it just reduce to an ordinary population-ethics problem of the "does creating a miserable life count as a harm" variety?
- What exactly could "a life not worth living" mean when applied to, say, a caterpillar that lives three weeks and gets eaten alive by parasitoid larvae? Is there any coherent way to weigh brief stretches of foraging against the manner of dying, or is this precisely where the whole argument becomes unfalsifiable?
- How do the population-dynamics wrinkles feed back into the welfare calcualtion? I'm thinking of density dependence (survivors breeding more once competitors are gone, so the "prevented lives" partly get replaced), ecological succession (cleared land eventually rewilds, so the destruction might just be a welfare *pulse* rather than a permanent reduction), and the difference between r-selected and K-selected species, since the net-suffering argument seems to lean almost entirely on the former.
- And finally: what's the best intuition-pump for why the gut feeling most ecologists have (more habitat = better for animals) could be right about biodiversity and species richness yet wrong at the level of total welfare? I want to understand where exactly those two ways of valuing nature come apart.

I don't want a reading list or citations, I want the concepts laid out clearly enough that I can stress-test the argument myself next time it comes up at my reading group. Bonus points if you identify the load-bearing premise where the whole thing is most likely to snap.
