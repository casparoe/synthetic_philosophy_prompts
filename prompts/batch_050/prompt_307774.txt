Quick background: I'm a machine learning engineer, pretty comfortable with decision theory, proper scoring rules, calibration, selective prediction, that whole toolbox. I have basically zero philosophy training but I've been falling down a rabbit hole on the "norms of assertion" debate — philosophers arguing about what standard a claim has to meet before you're epistemically permitted to flat-out state it. As I understand it the main candidates are: only assert what you know, only assert what's true, only assert what you rationally believe or are justified in believing, plus some fancier variants.

Here's the exercise. Translate that debate into the vocabulary I actually think in. Treat assertion as a system's decision to output a definitive answer instead of hedging or abstaining, and treat each candidate norm as a different output policy or training objective: thresholds on predicted probabilities, cost matrices for wrong answers vs refusals, scoring rules, regret, risk-coverage tradeoffs, whatever fits. Go norm by norm. Which ones have a clean counterpart in this vocabulary, which ones only translate if you fudge something, and which ones have no expression at all?

Then give me the residue report, which is honestly the part I care about. Going in, my guess is that the truth norm is just accuracy maximization, the belief norm is just "act on your posterior," and the knowledge norm is some muddled hybrid the philosophers can't quite motivate. But I suspect a few things won't survive the translation. The factivity of knowledge (knowing p entails p) doesn't obviously correspond to anything in a loss-function vocabulary. Gettier-style luck — being right for the wrong reasons — seems like it might be invisible to any scoring rule, since scoring rules only see probabilities and outcomes. And the philosophers seem to think these norms are constitutive of assertion as a practice, not just instrumentally good policies, and I have no idea what "constitutive" could even mean in my framework. I'm also curious what you do with lottery-type cases: a perfectly calibrated model can be 99.9% confident a claim is true and it still feels wrong to just output it flat-out, which makes me think there's a difference between confidence and whatever assertion needs. For each thing that fails to translate, tell me straight whether that's my vocabulary missing something real or thier disagreement being less substantive than it looks.
