So I work on an alignment team and we've been trying to write an honesty spec for our assistant model, and it's gone badly. Half the team think "deception" needs some kind of inner intentional state, so a model that just outputs false things without any goal behind it can't really deceive anyone. The other half think that's absurd -- if the thing reliably misleads users, who cares what's going on inside? We ended up going in circles because people kept switching between definitions depending on which case we were discussing (sycophancy came up, then hallucinations, then a model behaving differently during evals, and everyone silently picked whatever definition suited them).

Act as a philosopher of language who has also spent real time working on AI evals, and help me out: write a decision tree that takes someone from the starting commitment "I think AI assistants ought to be honest" to a named position on what honesty and deception even are for a system like this. Think carefully before you write -- maybe brainstorm the full space of options first. I'd like the tree built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: figure out which questions genuinely carve the positions apart (candidate nodes: does deception require an intention to produce a false belief? does it require the system to have beliefs of its own at all? is asserting a falsehood worse than merely implying one, à la the lying/misleading distinction? is sycophancy -- telling the user what they want to hear -- a form of dishonesty or just bad calibration? does an omission count? does dishonesty require that the system "knows" better in some functional sense?). Phrase every node as a concrete question or mini-case a person can answer without knowing which position it leads to. Then locate the actual positions in that space as leaves -- things like an Augustinian/Kantian intent-based account, a Bok-style deception-as-misleading view, a purely functional account (any behaviour that systematically induces false beliefs counts), a Gricean norm-violation account, and the deflationary view that "lying" just doesn't apply to current models at all. Note any empty combinations of answers and briefly diagnose why nobody occupies them (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate rather than papering over it.

For the size: roughly 8-10 branching questions and at least 6 leaves, aimed at a reading group of people who know some philosophy but aren't specialists. Please write it in British English. And definately include the actual test cases we'd use in an eval as the node examples where possible, because that's where people on my team flip-flop.
