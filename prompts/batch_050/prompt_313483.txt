I chair the allocations committee of a small family foundation, and we have to decide this years budget, about 4 million, across three programs. Option A is a proven bednet distribution program where we're confident about the impact no matter what happens: roughly 8,000 lives improved-equivalent (we use a crude wellbeing metric, don't worry about the details). Option B is a pandemic preparedness grant that does almost nothing in a normal year but is enormously valuable if a serious outbreak hits the regions we work in, which might happen and might not. Option C is an advocacy org pushing for regulatory changes in agriculture: small chance of a huge systemic win, decent chance of modest gains, and a real chance of total failure plus wasted money that could have gone to A.

Here's the problem. We don't have probabilities we trust for the outbreak scenario or for the advocacy succeeding, and our staff basically refused to put numbers on them beyond "somewhere between unlikely and plausible." At our last meeting the committee split three ways. One member says when we can't assign probabilities we should just pick the option whose worst case is best, which means A, always, full stop. Another says we should pick whichever option minimizes the maximum amount we could end up regretting compared to what we would have done had we known how things would turn out, and she ran a little tabletop exercise suggesting that actually favors B, because if an outbreak hits and we bought bednets we've catastrophically misallocated. The third says this is all overthinking and we should use rough subjective probabilities anyway, since refusing to estimate is just pretending we know less than we do, and on his gut numbers C wins.

I need to circulate a short memo before our next meeting recommending both (a) which decision rule the comittee should adopt for cases like this where probabilities are unavailable, and (b) which option we should actually fund under that rule. The memo has to give an honest treatment of the strongest argument for the rule we don't pick, because two of these members will vote against whatever I write if their view is just dismissed. It should also say something about whether the disagreement between us is really about decision rules at all, or whether the maximin member and the regret member are just weighting the outcomes differently and the rules are window dressing.

Please write it as continuous prose, like a long email I could paste and send -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Around 800 words is fine.
