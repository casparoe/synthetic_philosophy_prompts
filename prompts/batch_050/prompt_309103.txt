I am a novelist, two years into a book about three generations of a family whose members keep sacrificing themselves for the family business, and for one another, in ways that look increasingly ruinous up close. A friend who works as an evolutionary biologist read an early draft and, over dinner, gave me what he called the settled scientific backdrop for my theme. His summary ran roughly as follows: "Richard Dawkins's gene's-eye view resolved the old debate about what natural selection acts on. Selection operates on genes, not on groups and not even really on organisms, so apparent altruism is always genetic self-interest in disguise — a worker bee serving the hive is simply her genes propagating copies of themselves. The older talk of traits evolving for the good of the species turned out to be mathematically incoherent, and group selection was refuted in the 1960s. So whenever you see cooperation or self-sacrifice in nature, the correct translation is that some gene is being selfish."

I was inclined to trust him, since this is his field, and I have built a narrative device on his account: interspersed chapters narrated in a cold, impersonal "gene-level" voice that treats my characters as temporary vehicles and their sacrifices as accounting entries. Before I commit to this, I want to know whether the science underneath is as settled as he made it sound, because a hostile reviewer with a biology background could make the device look naive.

Please act as a philosopher of biology who is also an experienced teacher of humanities students — rigorous about the science, charitable about literary license. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming, as a list, everything in my friend's summary that a specialist might contest, before you write your considered answer. Then go through his claims one by one: which would a careful theorist accept, which conflate things the field keeps apart, which state a perspective as if it were a finding, and does his closing "translation" rule follow from the accurate parts? Note where competent theorists still disagree rather than papering over it. Conclude with practical advice: given the corrected picture, is my gene-level narrator defensible as it stands, and if not, what is the smallest change that would make the device survive a scientifically literate critic without flattening the novel?
