I'm a junior and somehow ended up as the student rep on our district's US history curriculum commitee. Next week I get ten minutes to defend a proposal for how we teach the founding, and then the committee gets to grill me.

My proposal is that we stop arguing over whether 1619 or 1776 was the real founding date and instead start the course with a unit comparing three possible frames: 1619 for slavery and its consequences, 1776 for self-government and the Declaration, and 1865 for the country after emancipation. Students would use many of the same events and primary sources for each frame, then write about what each one explains well and what it pushes into the background. I want to argue that a founding date is not just a neutral fact like the year a building was finished. It is also a choice about what counts as the central story. But I do NOT want to say every frame is equally true, because some choices can clearly distort the facts.

The audience is: two AP US History teachers who are mostly worried about fitting this into the state standards; one parent who thinks any focus besides 1776 means teaching kids to hate America; two students from the Black Student Union who are worried that slavery will get treated like one opinion among others; and a school board member who seems to want a compromise that will make everyone stop emailing her.

Prepare me for the question period. What objections is each person most likely to raise, phrased the way they would actually say it? Which objections are genuinely strong rather than just political, what should I concede immediately, what should I refuse to give up, and where is my proposal weakest? Also tell me the one answer that has to land if this is going to survive, and the question I am really hoping nobody asks.
