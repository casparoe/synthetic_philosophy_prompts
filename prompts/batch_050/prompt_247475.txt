Every couple of years in my phil language seminar we rerun the Lewis–Davidson fight about whether language is a convention, and it goes absolutely nowhere. Lewis thinks a language is sustained by conventions of truthfulness and trust in a population; Davidson and those who follow him reply that convention can't be essential to meaning, since speakers constantly deviate, coin novel uses, get understood despite malapropisms, and coordinate fine without any prior agreement. Both sides cheerfully concede the other's examples and then just restate their thesis. My hunch is they're not really disagreeing about language at all but about what a theory of meaning is supposed to explain — a social regularity versus an individual speaker's competence — so the debate only looks like one debate. But I could be wrong about that. Diagnose the disagreement for me: is it verbal, substantive, or two projects under one label? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually argues rather than slogans, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a sharper definition of convention, a factual claim about how coordination is achieved, or just admitting that 'language is conventional' was never a single thesis in the first place.
