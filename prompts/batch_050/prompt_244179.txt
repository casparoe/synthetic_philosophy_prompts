so our local rationality meetup (the ACX-adjacent one that grew out of an old LW reading group) has this dumb tradition where whoever runs the last session of a reading arc has to close with a poem about the topic. we did a sonnet on Goodhart in the fall, somebody did blank verse on the repugnant conclusion, etc. my turn is coming up and we're finishing a decision theory stretch, and i want to do the Dutch book argument for probabilism. we've been reading Ramsey's "Truth and Probability" (the 1926 essay, posthumously published in 1931) and one thing that surprised me is that Ramsey himself only mentions the book-making point in passing, almost as an afterthought: roughly, that if someone's degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, a clever opponent could offer them a set of bets they'd each accept and they'd lose money no matter how the world turns out. de Finetti made it a theorem later, but the seed is right there.

anyway, i want a villanelle. the two refrains should do the actual philosophical work: the first refrain should capture that each bet looks perfectly acceptable on its own (that's why the agent takes it), the second that the loss is guaranteed before any outcome is known, and they should keep coming back as the bookie adds bet after bet until they finally meet in the closing quatrain, which is when the book is complete and the trap springs. somewhere in the tercets give me a concrete incoherent agent, like someone who's 60% on rain tomorrow and also 60% on no rain, and show how the bets get assembled against them. the thing i really care about: one guy in the group keeps insisting Dutch books only show you shouldn't take bets, or that it's all just pragmatics about gambling, nothing epistemic. the poem should quietly answer him without ever lecturing, i.e. make the reader feel that the loss was already sitting inside the credences themselves, and that the actual weather never gets a vote. keep it to the synchronic argument please, the diachronic/conditionalization stuff is a seperate poem. someone who knows the Dutch book theorem should recognize the construction and not wince, and someone who doesnt should still think it's a good poem about a man being slowly sewn into a bag of his own making.
