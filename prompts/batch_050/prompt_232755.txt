In May 1919, two British expeditions photographed stars near the eclipsed Sun — from Principe and from Sobral — and that November announced that the observed deflection matched Einstein's general-relativistic value of about 1.75 arcseconds rather than the roughly 0.87 arcseconds, about half as much, that Newtonian gravity treating light as corpuscles implies. The plates were noisy, one instrument's data were set aside, and the figure presented sat closer to Einstein's value than the raw measurements strictly warranted — yet general relativity won over most physicists within a few years. Analyse this episode through Popper's falsificationism, in both directions. First, give the theory's reading: what made the deflection prediction risky in Popper's sense, why the eclipse counts as a severe test, and what a strict falsificationist ought to have concluded from data of that quality. Then press the case against the theory: the Duhem–Quine point that the test engaged optical and instrumental auxiliary hypotheses rather than Newtonian or Einsteinian theory alone; whether the data selection amounts to one of the conventionalist stratagems Popper warned against; and the awkward fact that Newtonian gravity also issued a definite numerical prediction, so the episode discriminated between two quantitatively falsifiable theories rather than between bold science and immunised pseudoscience. Close with a verdict: does the community's rapid acceptance of general relativity on imperfect evidence embarrass falsificationism, and is the episode better described in Lakatosian terms of progressive and degenerating research programmes? Give the analysis in roughly 600 words of continuous prose, followed by a table with columns [Feature of the episode | The falsificationist reading | Where the fit breaks down], one row per feature your analysis relied on.
