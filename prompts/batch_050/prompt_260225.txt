Last night I had a long argument with a friend about her preferred account of normativity, and I would like to see it pushed further than the two of us managed to push it.

Her view: an action is right for me if and only if an "ideal advisor" — fully informed about all non-normative facts, perfectly coherent, vividly imaginative, and impartial toward everyone affected — would advise me to do it. She holds that this slips between the horns of the Euthyphro dilemma: rightness is neither prior to the advisor's attitudes (she is no realist) nor hostage to brute approval, because the advisor is defined by her cognitive condition rather than by what she happens to want.

My objection: this transplants the dilemma rather than dissolving it. Either the advisor's advising is right-making, in which case terms like "impartial" and "coherent" are doing unacknowledged normative work and the account is circular; or those terms are purely descriptive, in which case there is no guarantee that what such a creature would advise is right — and if her verdicts are nonetheless reliably right, she must be tracking an independent standard, so realism returns through the back door.

We got stuck at three specific pressure points:

1. Amplification rather than correction. I argued that for someone raised to find a harmless practice disgusting, full information and vivid imagination might intensify the disgust rather than remove it, so idealization can amplify a bias instead of correcting it. My friend replied that such influences should be screened off as contamination — but we could not agree on whether "contamination" can be defined without normative vocabulary.
2. The status of impartiality. She claimed impartiality is a merely structural property — equal weighting of each affected party's interests — specifiable without moral concepts. I claimed that any weighting rule chosen because it treats people as they ought to be treated is already a moral commitment.
3. The analogy with the divine-nature reply. Theists can relocate the standard from God's will to God's nature; my friend claims her advisor's dispositions play that role. I countered that the move works for the theist only because God is supposedly good by necessity, whereas a constructed advisor's dispositions are contingent. She answered that a stipulative construction has its properties by definition, which she takes to be as good as necessity. We could not settle whether definitional necessity suffices here.
