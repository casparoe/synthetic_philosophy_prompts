First, put on two hats at once: someone who actually knows the philosophy of psychiatry — Hacking's looping effects, the natural kinds vs. human kinds distinction, the whole debate about whether diagnoses discover or produce their patients — and someone who's read enough real diagnostic manuals to nail the tone. Don't just wing it. Do a bit of brainstorming first: sketch two or three angles for the satire, then pick the one you think is sharpest and commit to it. I don't want a menu of options, I want you to make the call and tell me in a sentence or two why you went with it.

Then write the piece: an excerpt from a fictional diagnostic manual (DSM-style, call it the 2051 revision or whatever feels right) introducing a newly recognized disorder that the comittee has — knowingly or half-knowingly, your choice, but pick one and stay consistent — designed with the looping effect in mind. That is, the category is built so that being classified under it predictably changes how the classified people behave, and the manual's dry bureaucratic prose keeps quietly tripping over this fact. Around 500–600 words, fully deadpan, in authentic manual-ese: numbered criteria, specifiers, a differential diagnosis section, maybe a short note from the revision committee about "emerging clinical presentations."

The humor has to come from getting the idea right. The loop should actually loop: classification changes the people, the changed people feed back into the criteria, and the manual treats all of this as routine housekeeping. Someone who knows Hacking should laugh because it's fair, not wince because I've (well, you've) flattened the idea into "labels are bad." And it should stay legible — a smart reader who's never heard of the looping effect should still feel something's off and get what's being ironized, without the piece ever winking at them.

After the piece, add a short author's note (5–8 lines): where you took liberties with the looping-effect idea or leaned on a contested reading of it (e.g., whether Hacking thinks looping is a bug, a feature, or just what human kinds do), and where you simplified for comic effect. Be honest about it rather than hedging everything into mush — if your satire only works on a strong reading of Hacking, say so and own it.
