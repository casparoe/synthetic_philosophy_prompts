Exam question (20 points): Mencius and Xunzi famously disagree about human nature, one calling it good and the other calling it bad. State each philosopher's position, explain the key analogies or thought experiments each uses to support it, and then discuss whether their disagreement is a genuine substantive disagreement or whether the two might simply be using the word "nature" differently. Support your discussion with details from the texts.

Student's answer: Mencius holds that human nature is good. His evidence is that every person is born with four incipient moral tendencies, which he calls sprouts: the feeling of compassion, the feeling of shame and dislike, a sense of courtesy and deference, and a sense of right and wrong. His most famous argument is the case of the child about to fall into a well: anyone who suddenly saw this would feel alarm and compassion, and not because they hoped to gain the parents' praise or a reputation among their neighbors. Since this response is spontaneous and unlearned, Mencius concludes that moral feeling is native to us. He also compares human nature to water: just as water always flows downward, human nature always tends toward the good, so people will reliably become good on their own if nothing interferes. Xunzi, by contrast, holds that human nature is bad. He argues that people are born with selfish desires and a love of profit, and that if these are followed without restraint, society collapses into conflict and disorder. Goodness, for Xunzi, is therefore the product of conscious activity rather than nature, just as warped wood must be pressed straight and clay must be shaped by the potter. Because nature is bad, Xunzi concludes that people can only be made good through coercive laws and punishments imposed by sage kings, since nothing within the person could move them toward goodness. On the final question, I think the disagreement is substantive and not merely verbal. Although Mencius and Xunzi do seem to mean somewhat different things by nature, Xunzi flatly denies that the sprouts Mencius describes exist at all, so they cannot both be right about the psychological facts.
