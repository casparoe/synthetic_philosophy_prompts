Earlier this year I had surgery under general anesthesia -- nothing exotic, but the experience of simply vanishing and then, as far as I was concerned, instantly reappearing on a gurney unsettled me more than the operation itself. A friend, tired of hearing me talk about it, told me to rewatch The Prestige with that gap in mind, and I did, and now I can't separate the two things. I would like a reading of Nolan's film through Parfit's teletransportation cases and his claim that what matters in survival is not identity but psychological continuity.

Here is what I want the reading to grapple with specifically. Every night, Angier steps into Tesla's machine, a duplicate appears across the theater, and the man in the machine drops through the trapdoor and drowns in a locked tank. The film's final image -- a warehouse of drowned Angiers -- seems to me to take a position on the metaphysics: something goes wrong each night, and it goes wrong for someone. But on a strictly Parfitian line, nothing of what matters is lost: the man at the curtain call has all of Angier's memories and intentions, and the drowning man is no worse off than someone undergoing an especially irreversible amnesia. I want to know which of these the film actually endorses, or whether it is quietly exploiting the fact that we cannot hold the Parfitian view when the non-surviving body is present on screen, drowning in real time.

I also want the reading to take seriously the contrast with Borden, whose trick is achieved not by multiplying one person but by two people willing to live as one -- shared memories diverging day by day, neither of them identical to the man the audience applauds. Is the film setting up duplication-plus-destruction against division, and does its sympathies -- it clearly finds Borden's arrangement more human, even noble, and Angier's monstrous -- commit it to a view about identity it never argues for? Or is its real point the final line's suggestion that the audience, like Angier, wants to be fooled: that we prefer a reassuring fiction of survival to looking at what continuity actually involves?

I am not asking for a survey of positions I could triangulate myself. Take a stand: say whether the drowned man each night is Angier dying, whether the man at the curtain call is Angier surviving, and whether the film's horror at the tanks is philosophically defensible or just an intuition the film refuses to interrogate. If you think my anesthesia experience is relevantly different from stepping into the machine, say why and commit to where the disanalogy lies. And where the film resists a clean Parfitian reading -- where it is simply doing melodrama rather than metaphysics -- say so plainly rather than forcing the fit.
