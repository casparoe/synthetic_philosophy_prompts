I'm a software engineer by training with no formal philosophy background, though I read cognitive science and philosophy of mind on the side. I volunteer as a host for our local college's philosophy podcast, and next week I'm interviewing the author of a new book defending massive modularity: the thesis that the human mind is composed largely or even entirely of specialized computational modules shaped by natural selection, including the flexible, "central" cognition that Jerry Fodor famously argued resists modular treatment. I've read an advance copy, and I've noticed my guest has a reliable escape hatch. Whenever I raise a case of flexible, context-sensitive, cross-domain reasoning -- planning a career move, understanding a joke, weighing evidence from unrelated fields -- she says some version of "modularity is a claim about computational architecture, not about behavioral rigidity; modules can interact," and then pivots back to tidy examples like face perception and cheater detection. It's not that the reply is wrong; it's that it absorbs every objection without ever being put to work.

I'd like a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that makes this deflection progressively harder to use. The questions should press where the actual debate presses, not on caricatures. Concretely, I want the sequence to cover ground like the following, though you should order and refine it as you see fit: how modules are to be individuated (by domain or by task, and whether the view collapses into positing a micro-module for every ability we happen to have); how a mind built from encapsulated parts handles evolutionarily novel domains like reading, arithmetic, or driving, where selection plausibly had no time to build dedicated machinery (I expect a reply about recycling older circuits, and I'd like a follow-up that tests whether that reply does real explanatory work or just renames the problem); whether moving flexible reasoning into "interacting modules" solves or merely relocates the frame problem -- that is, if no general-purpose system decides what's relevant, which module arbitrates when several modules' outputs bear on the same decision; and what the contrast class for the thesis even is, since a claim that any mind must be modular risks being unfalsifiable rather than true. Where my guest has a standard reply, the question should anticipate it and include the follow-up that blocks the pivot. As an engineer I'm fluent in analogies to software modularity, but the questions should not depend on that analogy, since informational encapsulation in Fodor's sense is stronger than anything we mean by loose coupling in code.

A few structural requests. Open with one or two questions the guest can answer affirmatively and at some length, so the view itself is stated clearly on the record before the pressure starts. Escalate gradually rather than ambushing. Close with a question about what empirical finding -- developmental, neuropsychological, or comparative across species -- could in principle count against massive modularity, so the interview ends on the view's testability rather than on its rhetoric. Keep the language accessible to non-specialist listeners, avoid asking the guest to name or engage specific papers or authors beyond Fodor, and phrase everything the way an interviewer would actually speak it aloud. Please write the response in American English.
