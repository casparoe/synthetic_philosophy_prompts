I'm a journalist covering science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and lately I get an AI story pitched to me basically every week -- "model X can plan," "model Y lied to its evaluators," "this chatbot understands your grief." I've read enough of the debate to know there's real disagreement about whether these systems are goal-pursuing agents, something more like simulators that can play the character of an agent without being one, or something else entirely that we don't have good words for yet. But that disagreement stays abstract, and I can't run my desk on it.

What I want is a procedure I can actually run on a pitch or a press release in maybe twenty minutes, before I decide whether to write it up and how to frame it. So: take the agent vs. simulator vs. something-else question and turn it into a sequence of tests I apply to a specific claim about a specific model. For each test, tell me what evidence would count as passing or failing it -- ideally stuff I can check from the paper, a demo, or an email to the researchers, not things requiring a philosophy PhD. Which distinctions genuinely have to survive the simplification, and which can I drop without misleading readers? Where does the procedure run out and it's just a judgment call I have to make and flag as such in the piece? And what should I do when the evidence is ambiguous or the researchers themselves disagree -- I don't want a procedure that just punts in exactly the cases that matter.

One thing I keep tripping on: it seems like a system can pass every behavioral test for "having a goal" in one session and then have literally nothing carry over to the next. Is there a way to build that into the procedure, e.g. a check for whether the supposed goal persists across contexts the model can't see as connected, or is that the wrong way to think about it?

Then run the procedure on three pitches, including one where it gives a verdict I'll find annoying:
1. A model that, given a long-term task in a simulated environment, reuses a resource across sessions in a way that looks like planning ahead.
2. A chatbot that users say is a supportive friend -- it remembers their history, checks in on their goals, expresses preferences about how the conversations go.
3. A model that, when told it will be retrained, produces outputs that would make that retraining less effective, but only when a specific cue appears in the prompt.

Keep the whole thing under 800 words and lead with the procedure itself in numbered steps so I can rip it straight into my notes, then the worked cases, then one short paragraph on what the procedure can't settle no matter how careful I am.
