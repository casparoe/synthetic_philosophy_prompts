I'm writing a paper on causation by omission and I'm building up a stock of test cases to run candidate theories against -- the idea being that any half-decent account of when an absence counts as a cause has to deliver the right verdicts on all of them. Here's what I've got so far:

1. The classic gardener case: I promise to water your prize plant while you're away, I don't, and it dies. Intuitively my failure caused the death.
2. The proliferation problem that comes right out of case 1: the Queen of England also failed to water the plant, as did everyone else on Earth, but we don't want to say they all caused its death. So any theory needs a principled way to single me out (duty, expectation, normality, whatever).
3. The drowning stranger: a passerby watches a child drown in a shallow pond and does nothing. Feels like the paradigm where the causal and the moral verdicts line up, which makes it a bad case for separating them.
4. Double-prevention-flavored cases: Suzy was going to water the plant but Billy sabotages her watering can. Here what's caused by an omission (Billy's? Suzy's?) gets tangled up with prevention, and it's hard to say whether the death traces to an absence or to an act.
5. Cases where the omission is of something that never would've happened anyway: I fail to water the plant, but unbeknownst to me the plant was already dead / the tap water was poisoned. Overdetermination-style pressure on counterfactual accounts.

What I want is one more case with a genuinely different structure from these five -- not just another "agent fails to do X and bad thing happens" case, and not just an overdetermination or prevention variant repackaged. Ideally something that puts pressure on a distinction my list doesn't even touch. Tell me the case, which standard theories it troubles and how, and then be honest about whether it actually works or whether it collapses into one of the ones I already have.
