I write a magazine column on science and ideas, and last month I attended a closed workshop on priority-setting in global health, where funders and health ministries were debating whether cost-effectiveness methods rest on sound foundations. Two participants -- Mara, a philosopher working on value theory, and Daniel, a health economist -- got into an extended exchange about spectrum arguments and the transitivity of "better than," and I want to write it up fairly. I have my own rendering of who won, but I distrust it. Please adjudicate the exchange below move by move, then give an overall verdict.

For context: the disputants had agreed on a spectrum of outcomes running from (A) one person fully cured of a fatal disease to (Z) ten million people each relieved of a mild tension headache, connected by small steps in which each outcome differs from the last only by a slight change in the number of people benefited and the severity of the condition relieved.

Move 1 (Daniel): Transitivity of "better than" is not optional. If A is better than B, B better than C, yet C better than A, there is no admissible choice among the three, and any agent or institution acting on such judgments can be led through a circle of trades it endorses at every step, paying a small cost each time and ending back where it started -- a money pump. A health ministry must choose, so its betterness judgments had better be transitive, which means one premise of the spectrum argument must be false: somewhere in the chain a step that looks like an improvement is actually a decline, even if we cannot say where.

Move 2 (Mara): The money pump argument equivocates between value and preference. A pump requires an agent disposed to trade according to the judgments; facts about which outcome is better do not by themselves generate trading dispositions, so intransitivity in "better than" is not yet irrationality in anyone. And "we cannot say where the step is" is just epistemicism -- as implausible in axiology as in the sorites about heaps. There is no sharp line at which one fewer person cured turns an improvement into a decline, and positing an unknowable one hides the problem rather than solving it.

Move 3 (Daniel): Set the money pump aside. The deeper point is that "better than" is the relation a ranking reports. If A is better than B and B better than C while C is better than A, then "choose the best available option" has no meaning, and cost-effectiveness analysis -- the ministry's entire toolset -- is not merely difficult but incoherent. Whatever the metaphysics of value, policy requires a choice-worthy ranking, so working transitivity should stand until Mara supplies an alternative decision procedure rather than a critique.

Move 4 (Mara): Two replies. First, the alternative exists: treat adjacent outcomes in the spectrum as on a par, or as indeterminately related, rather than strictly better or worse, and adopt threshold or satisficing rules -- ministries already do this when they refuse to trade certain goods against money at any rate. Second, the inference from "policy needs a ranking" to "value must be transitive" is wishful thinking; the world is not guaranteed to be convenient for our procedures. If our best account of value delivers indeterminacy over fine-grained spectra, the honest response is to revise the decision procedure, not to insist that value conform to it.

Move 5 (Daniel): Parity and indeterminacy do not escape the argument; they relocate it. Suppose each adjacent pair in the spectrum is on a par rather than linked by betterness. Then either parity is transitive, in which case A and Z end up on a par -- which neither of us accepts -- or parity is non-transitive, and the same choice problem returns under a new name. Every repair I have seen either reinstates a sharp boundary somewhere or tolerates a cycle somewhere. A spectrum argument is a sorites, sorites have no comfortable solutions, and my premise-denying strategy is therefore no worse off than Mara's revisionism.

Move 6 (Mara): "All solutions are equally uncomfortable" is a counsel of despair that conveniently preserves Daniel's preferred toolset. The discomfort of indeterminacy is a reason to develop formal machinery for handling it -- and such machinery exists in the vagueness literature and can be imported into value theory -- whereas the discomfort of an unknowable sharp boundary is a reason to reject a posit that does no explanatory work.

Here is what I need from you:

1. An adjudication of each move in turn. For each, state its core claim, the strongest objection available to the other side (including objections the other participant did not actually raise), and a rating of the move as clearly right, mostly right, evenly mixed, mostly mistaken, or clearly mistaken, with a brief justification. Do not let the fact that a move went unanswered in the room count in its favor.
2. An overall verdict on who is more right about the underlying question -- whether "better than" must be transitive for purposes like the ministry's -- and what it would take to settle the residual disagreement.
3. The most important consideration or distinction that neither participant raised (for instance, whether the failure is really intransitivity rather than incomparability or vagueness, and whether a choice rule needs transitive value or merely needs to be defensible), and whether including it would change the verdict.

After your prose analysis, please provide a structured summary as a single table with exactly these columns:

- Move: the move number and speaker.
- Core claim: one sentence.
- Strongest objection: one sentence, whether or not it was voiced in the exchange.
- Rating: one of clearly right, mostly right, evenly mixed, mostly mistaken, clearly mistaken.
- Justification: one sentence.

Follow the table with three short labeled lines: Overall verdict; Consideration both missed; What would settle it.
