Having retired after four decades in experimental chemistry, I have lately been working my way through the decision-theory literature, and Newcomb's problem has occupied me far longer than I care to admit. On a recent evening I rewatched Spielberg's Minority Report (2002), and it struck me that the film is, almost scene for scene, an extended meditation on the same difficulty. The Precrime system predicts murders with such reliability that society is prepared to imprison people for acts they have not yet committed, and Anderton's frantic attempt to evade his own predicted killing looks very much like a cinematic rendering of the two-boxer's reasoning: whatever the oracle says, the future act is surely still open, and one would be a fool to behave as though it were already settled. Yet the plot plainly requires the predictions to be fallible, since the entire notion of a dissenting precog report depends on it, whereas Newcomb's problem is usually stated with a predictor reliable enough to make one-boxing rational but not literally infallible.

I should be grateful for a careful reading of the film against Newcomb's problem. Where does the analogy genuinely illuminate the decision-theoretic issues — the contrast between evidential and causal reasoning, the epistemic status we grant the predictor's track record, the question of whether learning what one has been predicted to do changes what one ought to do — and where does the film smuggle in assumptions that would dissolve or distort the problem as philosophers state it? I have in mind, for instance, the slide from highly reliable prediction to outright determinism, and the fact that Anderton learns the content of the prediction about himself, which the subject in the standard thought experiment does not. I would also welcome your view of the ending: Anderton does, in the end, kill the very man he was predicted to kill, albeit in circumstances he could not have anticipated. Does that vindicate the one-boxer's intuitions, or is it merely dramatic irony dressed up as fatalism? I am drafting an essay for my local philosophy society and want the reading to be faithful in both directions — neither flattening the film into an illustration of a thought experiment nor bending the thought experiment to fit the plot. Please write your answer in British English.
