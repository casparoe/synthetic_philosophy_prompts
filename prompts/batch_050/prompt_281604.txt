I am applying for a one-semester student research grant in philosophy and need a two-page proposal for a project on Pascal's mugging. My half-formed idea is this. Most responses to the mugging locate the problem in the numbers: the probability is too small, the payoff is too large, or both, and the fix is to bound utility, discount tiny probabilities, or refuse to act on them. But it strikes me that the mugging case has a feature these responses ignore, namely that the probability estimate is supplied by the very person who profits if I accept it. A stranger on the street telling me he can avert a cosmic catastrophe is not like a scientist telling me an asteroid might strike: in the first case the probability is generated by an interested party, in the second it is not, and I suspect this difference, rather than anything about magnitudes, is what makes handing over the wallet irrational. If that is right, the standard fixes may be solving the wrong problem, and they may also be too blunt, since they seem to rule out acting on legitimate low-probability high-stakes claims from disinterested sources. Turn this into a proper proposal. The question needs to be stated precisely enough to have a determinate answer; you should explain why the main existing responses leave it open rather than merely asserting that they do; lay out the method I would actually use over a semester (which cases to construct, which objections to test the interested-source distinction against, and how to check whether the distinction is doing real work or just relabeling intuitions); say what a positive result, a negative result, and a genuinely failed project would each look like; and be honest about what would change your mind about the project's viability -- that is, which objection or finding would show the project answers the wrong question, and where the framing is most likely to break down. Do not include citations or a literature review by reference; I will supply those myself, and you may simply refer to positions and arguments in general terms. Present the whole proposal as exactly six numbered sections -- the question, the gap in existing responses, the method, the possible outcomes, the change-my-mind clause, and the scope risks for a single semester -- with each section one short paragraph and nothing before or after the list.
