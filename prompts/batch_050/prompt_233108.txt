After a long argument with a friend about inheritance taxation, we reached an impasse I would like help thinking through. In Book II of Principles of Political Economy, John Stuart Mill draws a distinction (which I paraphrase rather than quote) between the right of bequest — an owner's power to direct where her property goes at death, which he treats as belonging to the very idea of private property — and the right of inheritance, a person's claim to receive wealth they did nothing to earn, which he denies has the same standing. On this basis Mill favored limiting the amount any one person may receive by inheritance, rather than abolishing testamentary freedom outright.

Please explain, first, whether this donor-side/recipient-side asymmetry is actually coherent: can the owner's freedom to give be fully respected while the recipient's take is capped, or does capping the recipient covertly restrict the donor just as much? Second, explain why a per-recipient lifetime cap on inherited wealth might serve equality of opportunity better than the per-estate taxes modern systems typically levy — in particular, how each instrument treats the concentration of wealth across generations, since one large estate split among many heirs differs from the same estate going to a single heir. Third, my friend insisted that once the cap bites, the policy is really about breaking up dynasties rather than equalizing starting points, and that these two aims come apart. Assess whether he is right, and what Mill's framing would predict about where the goals of curbing dynastic wealth and securing fair life chances diverge.
