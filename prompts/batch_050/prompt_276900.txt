Hi, econ PhD student here (decision theory, though I've somehow ended up in a formal epistemology reading group for my sins). We've just done the Dutch book argument for probabilism and I think I've talked myself into a corner, so I'd appreciate someone telling me where I've gone wrong.

As far as I can tell, the theorem says: if your credences violate the probability axioms, then there exists a set of bets, each of which looks fair or favourable to you at your own odds, such that you lose money no matter what happens. A guaranteed loss. And this is meant to justify probabilism, i.e. the norm that rational credences must be probabilistically coherent.

Here's my problem. That sounds like a theorem about betting, not about belief. Which seems to get me three conclusions:

1. On this justification, probabilism only really binds people who actually place bets. If I never gamble, then my incoherent credences about whether it'll rain in Glasgow next Thursday cost me nothing, so the argument gives me no reason whatsoever to tidy them up. Non-bettors get a free pass.

2. Since the theorem guarantees losses for the incoherent, there ought to be a viable career in Dutch booking people -- hunting down incoherent reasoners and systematically extracting money from them. But nobody seems to do this (bookies make their money from the overround, not from exploiting axiom violations), which suggests either that the theorem's guarantee doesn't survive contact with reality or that perfectly coherent reasoners are far rarer than epistemologists assume. Either way, weird.

3. And doesn't the theorem actually show you're rationally compelled to accept the fatal set of bets? Each bet individually looks fair to you, so refusing any one of them would be irrational by your own lights, meaning the incoherent agent is forced to walk into the trap. But that can't be right either, because then incoherent agents are irrational twice over and the arguement proves more than anyone wants it to.

I suspect all three conclusions rest on the same misreading of what the theorem actually establishes, but I can't quite put my finger on where. Can someone set me straight? Bonus points if you can explain why epistemologists think this tells us anything about epistemic rationality specifically, when all the machinery is about money.
