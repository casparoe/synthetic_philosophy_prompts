I teach an upper-division seminar on the late Nietzsche, and this term I keep running into a tension I cannot resolve to my own satisfaction. On the one hand, few philosophers have ever taken the body as seriously as he did: Ecce Homo treats nutrition, climate, digestion, and fatigue as questions of the first philosophical rank, and his own life — the migraines, the ruined stomach, the decades of self-experimentation with diets, waters, walks, and mountain air — reads like a one-man research program in applied physiology. On the other hand, he is savage about anyone who makes health itself the point of a life; his last man is, among other things, a creature who takes excellent care of himself, and his talk of a great health in the later books of the Gay Science describes a capacity to endure and even exploit sickness rather than its mere absence. Before I build a lecture around this tension I want to stress-test it adversarially, in character.

Play Friedrich Nietzsche in Turin in the autumn of 1888. You have just been given a careful description of a contemporary type: the health optimizer. These people wear sensors that track their sleep, heart rate, and blood glucose; they cycle through fasting protocols, cold exposure, and supplement regimens; they organize their days around measurement; and they speak openly of longevity and healthspan as the explicit goals of their lives. You may use only the concepts available to you in 1888 plus what I have just told you. Stay in character, in the first person, until I say stop. Concede small points when they are true; never concede the main one cheaply. If you need a further fact about this world, ask for it as Nietzsche would, but do not let that delay your answer. A few paragraphs per reply at most — you are talking to me, not dictating a book. And do not retreat into intoning slogans I have already read.

Here is my opening, and I want an honest answer rather than a performance of outrage. By your own lights these people should be your heirs. They treat the body as the instrument of philosophy; they experiment on themselves exactly as you did through your years of sickness; they hold, as you so often insisted, that a great deal of what calls itself spirit is secretly physiology; and they have the contempt for herd comfort you always demanded. Yet I cannot shake the suspicion that you would despise them above everyone: that in their glucose monitors and longevity protocols you would see the last man taking his vitamins, health converted from a condition of great works into the work itself — perhaps even a new ascetic ideal with better instrumentation. So tell me plainly: is the optimizer your child or your target? What precisely distinguishes pursuing health as a condition of self-overcoming from pursuing it as the goal of a life? And can you draw that line without convicting your own Turin pages on nutrition, and your own decades of regimen-hunting, of the very confusion you would accuse them of? If honesty requires you to disown some of what you wrote there, tell me which pages go.
