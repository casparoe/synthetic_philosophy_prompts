Write me a pantoum whose subject is Benacerraf's dilemma in the philosophy of mathematics and whose form does part of the arguing.

The dilemma, stated without ornament: a philosophy of mathematics owes us two things at once. First, a semantics. The most natural account takes mathematical sentences at face value, so that 'there are infinitely many primes' has the same shape as 'there are infinitely many stars' and is true just in case certain objects — numbers — exist and stand in certain relations. Second, an epistemology. On any remotely naturalistic picture, knowing about something requires standing in a suitable causal or explanatory relation to it, and abstract objects, being outside space and time and causally inert, enter into no such relations. The two demands therefore defeat each other. Take the face-value semantics seriously and mathematical knowledge becomes unintelligible; make knowledge intelligible by tying mathematical truth to proof, convention, or formal manipulation, and the sentences no longer seem to mean what they appear to mean. Most positions in the field since can be read as choosing which horn to be gored by.

The poem should run to six quatrains, observing the pantoum's rule that the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, and it should close by folding the opening lines back in, so that its final line is its first — the inquiry, like the debate, ending exactly where it began, only better informed about why it cannot rest there.

Use the machinery of recurrence to do philosophical work rather than to decorate:

- Give each horn its own register, so that a reader who knows the dilemma can tell, stanza by stanza, whether the semantics or the epistemology currently has hold of the poem.
- At least two of the recurring lines must change their force when they return: a line that first appears as a straightforward claim about what exists should come back, in the other horn's keeping, as a report of what can be derived, or as a piece of notation we push around, or as a habit of speech — the same words, a different account of what we are doing when we utter them.
- The closing stanza must not resolve the dilemma or award the victory to either side. The pantoum's rules oblige the refrains to meet; let them meet the way the two desiderata meet, in the same small room, unreconciled.

Keep the technical vocabulary to what a poem can carry — primes, proof, truth, cause, the invisibility of numbers. Someone who has never heard of Benacerraf should find a good poem here, and someone who has should find each horn rendered faithfully rather than caricatured.
