Next month I'm giving a talk at a political theory colloquium defending my paper on who should get a say in border policy. My claim, in short: the standard move in the literature is to ask who the demos is before asking what the demos may legitimately decide, but I argue this gets things backwards — for some decisions (I use border enforcement as the case) the decision itself helps constitute the group, so there's no prior "we" to consult, and any principle that pretends otherwise is smuggling in the answer. My positive proposal is that outsiders who will bear the enforcement costs of a border regime should get some weighted say in setting it — not full membership, but a kind of standing to contest, roughly proportional to how much of the policy's coercive weight lands on them. I test it on a made-up case where one country builds a border wall that reroutes a river and floods farmland on the other side.

The room: mostly democratic theorists. I know for sure there'll be one senior person who's spent their career defending the all-affected interests principle and hears every new proposal as a footnote to theirs, a couple of people sympathetic to national self-determination who think states legitimately own their membership rules the way clubs do, a grad student who's going to ask something about future generations no matter what, and a few comparativists who mostly want to know how this cashes out institutionally and will be annoyed by pure ideal theory.

Prep me for the question period. What objections will actually come, roughly in what order, phrased the way these specific people would phrase them? Which ones can I deflect quickly and which have real teeth? What should I just concede up front so I don't look like I'm dodging? And what's the objection I'm secretly hoping nobody raises — i.e., where is my "coercive weight, proportional standing" idea most likely to fall apart under two follow-up questions?
