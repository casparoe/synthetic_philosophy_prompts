I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm writing a long peice pegged to Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences — the one arguing that analogy isn't a rhetorical flourish or a trick poets use but the actual machinery of thought, all the way down. My editor's worry is that the article will end up just being a book summary, since every example I've got in my notes is theirs. So I want one more case or argument for the thesis that I can present as my own analysis rather than paraphrasing them.

Here's what I already have, all straight from the book:

1. Naming itself is analogical: calling this thing a chair means mapping the present situation onto a stock of prior chair-situations, so even the most boring act of categorization is an analogy in action, and categorization and analogy are really one thing seen at different scales.

2. Category boundaries creep by analogy until the extensions go invisible — the "foot" of a mountain, a virus "infecting" a computer, a country's economic "heartbeat." Dead metaphors are fossilized analogies, and the fact that we can't hear them anymore is evidence of how deep the machinery runs, not how marginal it is.

3. Speech errors and blends as windows into the engine: when someone fuses two idioms mid-sentence or comes out with the wrong word from the right conceptual neighborhood, you're catching the analogical retrieval system in the act.

4. Deep episode-to-episode analogies: those "this is another one of *those*" moments where a current mess instantly summons a superficially unrelated mess from years ago, and you know which prior situation governs the new one.

5. Einstein as the star exhibit: his breakthroughs came from analogies (equating free fall with weightlessness, extending patterns from earlier physics into new domains), and his great late-career failure came from an analogical commitment too — to continuity and determinism — blinding him on quantum mechanics. Genius as high-quality analogy-making, not a separate faculty.

What I need is a sixth item that is genuinely not on this list: not a rewording, special case, or mashup of 1–5. It should support the *strong* version of the claim (analogy as the engine of cognition, not just one tool among many), and it should be something I can describe in two paragraphs to readers with zero background. Also tell me how it relates to the items above — does it reinforce one of them or plug a gap the book left open? And give me your honest take on whether it survives the obvious skeptic's move, namely that whatever phenomenon I describe is just associative pattern matching that we're choosing to *call* analogy.
