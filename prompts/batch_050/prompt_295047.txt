I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and next month I am giving a talk at an interdisciplinary university seminar on minds and machines. The audience is mostly philosophers of mind (several sympathetic to enactivist and embodied approaches) plus two computer scientists who work on programming-language theory. I would like you to act as a veteran philosopher who knows this literature well and has sat through many such talks: think carefully about how this particular audience will react before drafting anything, and feel free to brainstorm freely before settling on the final prep sheet.

My thesis is that Escher's Drawing Hands is a cheat as a picture of self-reference: in any actual causal system, what looks like a tangled hierarchy always bottoms out in an inviolate level that does the tangling but cannot itself be tangled. My argument sketch: in every case of apparent code modifying itself (self-hosting compilers, reflection APIs, a neural network updating its own weights, genetic self-replication), what gets modified functions as data relative to some machinery that remains untouched — an interpreter, hardware, physics — so the hierarchy is never truly tangled, only folded; and the drawing itself concedes this, since both hands are drawn by Escher. I further claim this has a practical upshot my fellow engineers ignore: proposals for AI systems that could "rewrite themselves without residue" are confused in principle, not merely difficult in practice.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they would do to the talk, each phrased in the register the person raising it would use (I expect the enactivists and the programming-language theorists to come at me from opposite directions, and I want to be able to tell a fatal objection from a terminological quibble). For each, give me a two-minute reply, note where the reply is weakest, and say what I should concede outright versus hold firm on. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
