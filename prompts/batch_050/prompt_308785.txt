Exam question: In A Treatise of Human Nature (III.i.2), Hume writes: "We do not infer a character to be virtuous, because it pleases: But in feeling that it pleases after such a particular manner, we in effect feel that it is virtuous." Explain what this claim means and what it implies about what moral knowledge consists in. Then, drawing on the experimental literature on psychopathy (in particular studies of the moral/conventional distinction), assess the following inference: psychopaths characteristically lack the normal aversive response to others' distress; therefore they lack moral knowledge in Hume's sense; therefore the psychopathy literature is an empirical vindication of sentimentalism against moral rationalism.

Student's answer:

Hume's claim is that moral judgment is a matter of feeling rather than reasoning. When we call a character virtuous we do not first observe that it pleases us and then infer its virtue; the distinctive pleasure just is the approbation. So for Hume, moral knowledge is not propositional knowledge of facts or relations discoverable by reason — it is constituted by having the appropriate sentiment. Blair's 1995 studies of psychopathic offenders found that they fail the moral/conventional distinction: where ordinary people treat moral transgressions (e.g., hitting someone) as wrong independent of any authority's say-so, and as more serious than conventional transgressions (e.g., talking out of turn), psychopaths treat moral rules as authority-dependent, the way they treat traffic rules. This shows psychopaths have no moral concepts at all: they literally cannot tell right from wrong, which is precisely the deficit the M'Naghten standard for legal insanity identifies. Since psychopaths lack normal empathic responsiveness and, with it, moral knowledge, Hume's prediction is confirmed: remove the sentiment and moral judgment disappears. Nichols (2002) reads the same data as showing that psychopaths do make genuine moral judgments but are simply not motivated by them, and argues that this vindicates motivational externalism and hence moral rationalism. But Blair's data show the deficit is cognitive rather than merely motivational, so the rationalist has no reply, and the psychopathy literature settles the debate in favor of sentimentalism.
