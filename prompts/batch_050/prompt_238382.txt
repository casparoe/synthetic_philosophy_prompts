I sit on the board of a regional water utility, and I would like your advice on a decision we are facing. Our reservoir system was sized in the 1960s, and we must decide now whether to commit roughly two billion dollars to expanding it, because the permitting and construction lead time is about fifteen years. The case for expansion turns entirely on what regional precipitation will look like from around 2050 onward.

We hired a consulting firm, which ran our watershed through the projections of about thirty leading climate models. Their report emphasizes a striking consensus: nearly all the models agree that average temperatures in our region will rise by two to three degrees Celsius by mid-century. On precipitation, however, the models spread out considerably — a majority project modestly drier summers, but a substantial minority project wetter ones, and the extremes differ by enough to flip our cost-benefit analysis in either direction.

The consultants argue that the temperature consensus demonstrates that the models are fundamentally sound, and that we should therefore take the center of gravity of the precipitation projections — a moderate drying trend — as the best available estimate and design the expansion around it. They add that the models have all successfully reproduced the global climate of the twentieth century when run retrospectively.

A few things trouble me, though I struggle to articulate exactly why. I happen to know that many of these thirty models are not truly separate efforts: several share major components or even substantial portions of computer code, they were all calibrated against the same historical record, and they embody broadly similar assumptions about how clouds, aerosols, and soil moisture behave. I also suspect that reproducing the last century may be less impressive than it sounds, given that the models were developed and adjusted with knowledge of that same century.

So here are my questions. How much evidential weight should agreement across models carry when the models are not genuinely independent of one another — and is there a principled difference between convergence produced by many independent lines of inquiry and convergence produced by shared assumptions? Given that the models agree on a variable that does not drive our decision but disagree on the one that does, how should we regard the consultants' recommendation to plan around the middle of the projection range? And more generally: what would it take for model projections to earn our trust in a decision of this magnitude — and if they cannot fully earn it, is there still a rational way to proceed?
