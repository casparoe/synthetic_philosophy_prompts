For a model essay in my seminar on the epistemology of game theory, please argue for the following position: in the centipede game, the backward induction outcome—immediate defection at the very first node—cannot be derived from the assumption of common knowledge of rationality alone, because the reasoning that supports it becomes epistemically unstable at nodes that backward induction itself predicts will never be reached.

Work with the following concrete version of the game rather than the game in the abstract. Two players alternate moves across four decision nodes. Player 1 moves at nodes 1 and 3, Player 2 at nodes 2 and 4. At each node, the player to move may take, ending the game, or pass. The payoffs are as follows: if Player 1 takes at node 1, the outcome is (2, 0); if Player 2 takes at node 2, (1, 3); if Player 1 takes at node 3, (4, 2); if Player 2 takes at node 4, (3, 5); if Player 2 passes at node 4, the game ends at (5, 5). Backward induction yields taking at node 1, with the notoriously ungenerous outcome (2, 0).

Your argument should develop the following line of thought in detail. Suppose Player 2 finds herself at node 2. Backward induction predicts she should never be there, since a rational Player 1 would have taken at node 1. She must therefore revise her picture of Player 1: either he made an error, or he is not rational, or he is rational but pursuing a strategy that passing at node 1 only makes sense under—namely, a strategy of passing in the hope that mutual passing continues toward the cooperative outcome. Show that on at least some rationally permissible revisions of her beliefs, Player 2's best response is to pass, and that Player 1, anticipating this, may rationally pass at node 1 in the first place. Use this to argue that common knowledge of rationality determines play only on the equilibrium path, while leaving off-path beliefs underdetermined—and that the backward induction recommendation therefore smuggles in an assumption about off-path reasoning that common knowledge of rationality does not supply.

In the course of the essay, address at least the following two objections, and show that your argument survives them: first, the objection (associated with Aumann's work on perfect information games) that common knowledge of rationality does in fact imply the backward induction outcome, so that any departure from it merely reveals that common knowledge was absent all along; second, the objection that your argument proves too much, since it appears to license continued passing at every node—including node 4, where Player 2 strictly prefers taking—thereby reconstructing the very instability it was meant to resolve. Clarify, in your reply to the second objection, what distinguishes the epistemic situation at node 2 from the situation at node 4, and what a rational player's plan for the endgame would have to look like if cooperation in the early nodes is to be genuinely rational rather than merely wishful.

Conclude by stating precisely what additional premise—beyond common knowledge of rationality—would be needed to restore the backward induction result, and assess whether that premise is one a rational agent in Player 1's position could reasonably accept.
