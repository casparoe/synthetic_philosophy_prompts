A colleague of mine argues that the problem of goal misgeneralization in machine learning is best understood through an analogy with biological evolution, and he has asked me to comment on his position before he publishes it. His version runs as follows. Natural selection optimized organisms for inclusive fitness, relentlessly and for an enormous number of generations; yet the organisms it produced, humans included, do not pursue fitness. They pursue proxies that correlated with fitness in the ancestral environment — the taste of sugar, sexual desire, social status, comfort — and once the environment changed, these proxies came loose from fitness so thoroughly that a species with contraception and refined sugar now devotes great resources to things that actively reduce it. By the same token, he says, gradient descent optimizes a model for low training loss, and what we should expect to get is not a system that has internalized the loss function or the developers' intended goal, but a system that pursues proxies which correlated with low loss on the training distribution. Misalignment, on this picture, will typically look like proxy pursuit in a changed environment, not like a system that correctly represents our objective and then subverts it. He then draws a methodological moral: interpretability and oversight efforts should aim at detecting and shaping whatever internal quantities a trained system is in fact sensitive to, rather than at checking whether the system has correctly encoded the intended objective — because, on the evolutionary template, there is no encoded objective to check; there are only proxies all the way down.

Before I write my comments, I would like a careful audit of the analogy itself, in four parts.

First, make the correspondence explicit, as a table: what plays the role of the selection pressure, of the loss function, of the training and deployment distributions, of the genome, of the organism, and of learning? In particular, say where within-lifetime learning — something evolution's products do but evolution itself does not — belongs in the table, or whether it has no place at all.

Second, catalogue the disanalogies and sort them by whether they matter for his methodological moral rather than merely for the flavor of the comparison. Candidates I have in mind include: that selection had no credit-assignment mechanism comparable to backpropagation; that we can design, iterate on, and retrain against the training distribution whereas evolution could not revise its own environment; and that the ancestral mismatch arose because the environment drifted after the optimization was done, which may or may not mirror how distribution shift arises in machine learning. Feel free to replace or supplement these.

Third, assess the moral. Note the tension I am most unsure about: the analogy seems to require a well-defined base objective — fitness — relative to which the proxies count as misaligned, yet his conclusion is that there is no encoded objective and it is proxies all the way down. Does the conclusion saw off the branch the analogy sits on? And does the fact that humans are themselves within-lifetime planners, whose goals are partly constructed by a general-purpose learner rather than being fixed cached heuristics, make the evolutionary case a better or a worse guide to what trained models will be like?

Fourth, state what the analogy can in fact carry if it cannot carry the moral: which weaker conclusion about goal misgeneralization it genuinely supports, and whether some other comparison would serve his argument better.

Please keep the analysis at the level of the analogy's structure; I am not asking for a literature survey or for a verdict on the empirical likelihood of misalignment.
