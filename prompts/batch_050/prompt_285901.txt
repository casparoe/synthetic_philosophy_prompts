Before I read any more proposed criteria for separating legitimate historiography from pseudohistory, I want the specification first. Write the desiderata: what any adequate demarcation criterion between history and pseudohistory must deliver, with the case or consideration motivating each requirement.

Use Atlantis, ancient-astronaut readings of ancient monuments, and Fomenko's "new chronology" as the test cases the criterion must classify correctly -- but note that classifying them correctly is the easy part, since almost any criterion manages that. The harder requirements I want you to address include at least the following, and you should add any I have missed:

- The criterion must not demarcate by conclusion. Revisionist theses that sounded outlandish have sometimes won (an amateur famously cracked Linear B; once-fringe re-datings and attributions have become orthodoxy), so "disagrees with the scholarly consensus" cannot itself be the marker -- yet the criterion also cannot reduce to "anything consensus rejects is potentially legitimate," which would let everything in.
- It must distinguish being wrong from being pseudohistory. Competent historians make errors; pseudohistory is presumably a defect of method or of epistemic conduct, not of accuracy. Say what that defect is, and make sure the definition does not accidentally convict ordinary mistaken scholarship.
- It must handle the mimicry problem: pseudohistory typically imitates the surface forms of scholarship (citations, footnotes, debates about evidence, accusations of establishment bias). A criterion stated in terms of surface features will fail; one stated in terms of what is done with evidence must be precise about the difference between selective use of evidence (which all historians are accused of) and something worse.
- It must explain why "just check the claims against the evidence" is not a sufficient answer for a non-specialist, given that pseudohistories construct internally elaborate evidential cases and that most people cannot independently evaluate, say, arguments from linguistic comparison or radiocarbon dating.
- It must say whether motive matters. Pseudohistories cluster around nationalist, religious, and commercial projects in a way that errors in mainstream historiography arguably do not. Is that clustering diagnostic, constitutive, or merely a contingent correlation the criterion should ignore?
- It must respect the distinction between a pseudohistorical claim and a pseudohistorical practice: can a true conclusion be reached pseudohistorically, and does the criterion classify the claim, the method, or the community?

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration behind it, flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied (in particular, examine whether a purely method-based criterion can survive the mimicry and non-specialist problems without smuggling in sociological or motive-based elements), and identify which single requirement excludes the most candidate criteria you have seen in the wild. If the main families of proposals -- falsificationist, virtue- or conduct-based, sociological/community-based -- each violate a different desideratum, say so; that is the part I most want to see. Keep the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on a scorecard.

End with a last line beginning "Bottom line:" containing a one-sentence statement of which desideratum does the most work and why.
