in my political philosophy seminar the prof keeps leaning on the "wisdom of crowds" line: Condorcet's jury theorem basically shows that majority voting tracks the truth the same way averaging a crowd's guesses at the weight of an ox at a county fair lands dead on the true weight. each person's error is just random noise, noise cancels in the aggregate, so a big enough electorate is a near-perfect truth machine as long as each voter is even slightly better than a coin flip. he uses this to argue that epistemic worries about democracy (voter ignorance etc.) are overblown. i want to push back but i want to do it properly: how far does the analogy actually hold? walk me through what maps onto what -- the guess = the vote? the scales = the ballot count? the ox's true weight = the correct policy or candidate? -- and then where the mapping breaks. guesses at a fair are independent of each other in a way voters' beliefs obviously aren't, and the ox has one true weight whereas "the best policy" might not be that kind of fact at all. which of these disanalogies actually matter for his conclusion and which are just cosmetic? does the pro-democracy conclusion survive once you price them in, and if the averaging picture is the wrong model of voting, what's a better one?
