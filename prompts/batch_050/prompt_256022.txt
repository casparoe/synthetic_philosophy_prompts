I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the origins of inequality (18 students, one 110-minute session, no laptops). Design an in-class simulation in which students experience the agricultural revolution as a trap rather than a triumph. The simulation should have three phases with simple token-based rules I can run with poker chips and index cards: (1) a foraging phase in which returns are modest but roughly equal, effort costs are low, and a visible share of each round's time is left unspent, so that "affluence as leisure" shows up on the score sheet rather than in my lecture; (2) an adoption phase in which farming is introduced as an optional technique with a genuine but small per-round calorie edge that grows as foraging returns decline, where adoption is individually rational at the moment each student chooses it, but where the rules quietly make reversion to foraging progressively more expensive and eventually impossible; (3) a consolidation phase in which stored harvests become raidable, some students end up as guards or managers extracting a share, and the group's Gini coefficient and per-capita calories both get measurably worse even as total output rises.

The crucial design constraint: the transition must be driven by relative advantage, not preference. I want the debrief to reveal that farming came to dominate the room without a single participant ever preferring the outcome it produced -- that a strategy can displace a better one because it out-reproduces or out-accumulates it, not because anyone chose it. Build in a mechanic (e.g., farming households drawing extra "offspring" chips that dilute foraging territory) that forces this.

Provide: full rules and payoffs, a round-by-round timeline fitting the 110 minutes, a facilitator script limited to procedural announcements (no editorializing mid-game), and a debrief plan that maps specific moments onto the "original affluent society" thesis, the ratchet/lock-in mechanism, and the argument that only storable, countable, confiscable surpluses generate durable hierarchy. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (e.g., students gaming the store-raiding rules or refusing to adopt), and state frankly which features of the real transition -- disease burden, nutritional decline, generational timescale -- a single-session game cannot reproduce, and what I must supply in discussion instead.
