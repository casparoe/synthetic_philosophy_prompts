I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm fact-checking a manuscript for a popular book on paradoxes before I agree to blurb it. The author is a competent science writer, not a logician. Here is his summary of Curry's paradox and Yablo's paradox, and I want to know how much of it I can let stand.

He writes: "Curry's paradox is a beefed-up version of the liar. The liar sentence says of itself that it is false, and reasoning about it lands you in a contradiction. Curry's sentence instead says, 'If this sentence is true, then everything is true,' and from it you can derive absolutely any claim you like — that the moon is made of cheese — using nothing but the ordinary rules of logic. So where the liar merely breaks consistency, Curry produces triviality: every sentence comes out true. The standard diagnosis of these paradoxes has always been that self-reference is the culprit, and the logician Stephen Yablo closed off that escape route by constructing an infinite list of sentences, each of which says only that all the sentences after it in the list are untrue. No sentence in the list refers to itself, yet the same contradiction arises. Yablo's construction therefore settled the matter: self-reference was never essential, and logicians now agree that the only options left are to restrict the rules governing the concept of truth or to revise logic itself."

I have no training in logic, but I've interviewed enough philosophers to be suspicious of any sentence containing the phrase "now agree." Finally, does his closing claim — that the remaining options are restricting truth or revising logic — actually follow from the parts of his summary that survive scrutiny, or is he foreclosing options that specialists still take seriously?
