I chair the grants jury for a small city arts fund -- we hand out about 40 micro-grants a year (couple thousand dollars each) to local artists, and the jury is five volunteers, none of us with any real training in aesthetics. Every cycle we end up in the same fight: half the applications are openly message-driven work (a mural series about housing displacement, a play about a historic lynching in our county, a zine about trans youth), and we have no shared standard for judging them. Some jurors treat "it's propaganda" as an automatic knock against a piece; others treat the message as basically the whole point and get annoyed when anyone asks whether the thing is, well, good. In a reading group I'm in we just covered W. E. B. Du Bois's 1926 essay "Criteria of Negro Art" from The Crisis, and I think his position is more interesting than the "all art is propaganda" line everyone quotes at each other. There's a bit where he describes loud American tourists swamping a quiet Scottish lake and says of them: "They struck a note not evil but wrong. They carried, perhaps, a sense of strength and accomplishment, but their hearts had no conception of the beauty which pervaded this holy place." That stuck with me, because it suggests his real complaint is never about art having an agenda -- it's about which vision of life the agenda serves, and whether the artist can actually see beauty at all.

So here's what I want: turn Du Bois's actual position in that essay into a working procedure our jurors can follow when scoring message-driven applications -- an ordered set of questions or tests we ask of each piece, with the distinctions that have to survive (e.g., what separates propaganda Du Bois would defend from work that "strikes a note not evil but wrong"), plus what the procedure should tell us to do with a technically beautiful but completely apolitical submission, and with an on-message piece that's shoddy or tawdry. Show the procedure working on two or three made-up applications, including one where it gives an answer our jury will hate (funding something we find politically distasteful, or rejecting something we're all cheering for), and be explicit about the points where the checklist runs out and a juror just has to judge. Cap the whole thing at 900 words.
