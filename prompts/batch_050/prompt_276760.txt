Before I compare any proposals about how a solver — human or artificial — induces a concept from a handful of labeled figures, as in Bongard-style problems where six positive and six negative instances must be separated by a rule the solver can then state, I want the standard of adequacy fixed in advance rather than reverse-engineered from whichever model happens to be on the table. Write the desiderata that any theory of few-shot concept induction must meet. The intended use is modeling competent adult human solvers, not maximizing benchmark accuracy, and you should treat adequacy as relative to that purpose throughout.

Your list must respond, at minimum, to the following four problem classes, and each desideratum should be motivated by a concrete case of one of them rather than asserted in the abstract:

1. The apparent disjunction. The positives are exactly the figures that are either large-and-striped or small-and-dotted. A theory must explain when a solver legitimately concludes that the disjunctive rule is the rule, versus continuing to search for a hidden simple one — and it must do so without simply decreeing that disjunctions never count, since sometimes they are the intended answer.

2. The enumerative cheat. The concept "exactly these six figures, by identity" fits the positives perfectly and excludes the negatives perfectly. A theory must explain why this is not a successful induction, and the explanation may not rest on merely stipulating that memorization is disallowed, since the memorized description is, extensionally speaking, a rule.

3. The nested concept. In one problem the solver induces the rule "triangles outnumber circles"; in a later problem that very relation figures as a primitive feature inside a more complex rule. A theory must say how a previously induced concept becomes part of the vocabulary available for subsequent induction, or explain why it needn't.

4. The contested parse. One figure can be read either as a slightly open triangle or as a quadrilateral, and which reading is correct depends on which concept is correct — yet which concept is correct seems to depend on the parse. A theory must address concepts that bear on how the figures are described in the first place, rather than operating only over a fixed, pre-given feature list.

For each desideratum: give the motivating case, explain why it is a genuine requirement rather than a prejudice of one research camp, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference tied to a particular theoretical orientation. Then flag any subsets of your list that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — say, a strong bias toward simple hypotheses together with full sensitivity to every observed detail — since an inconsistent specification is itself an interesting result. Identify the single desideratum that excludes the most currently popular approaches, and say why.

Finally, and briefly, score three broad families against the list: Bayesian program induction over a fixed hypothesis grammar, feed-forward pattern matchers trained on many problems, and exemplar- or similarity-based models. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than the scorecard; the criteria are where the thinking is.
