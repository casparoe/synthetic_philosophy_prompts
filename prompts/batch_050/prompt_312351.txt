I'm taking a graduate seminar on economic culture, and we've just spent two weeks on Max Weber's Protestant ethic thesis — the claim that certain strands of Calvinist religiosity, with their doctrines of predestination and worldly asceticism, inadvertently cultivated the habits of disciplined labor, systematic saving, and rational reinvestment that Weber called the spirit of modern capitalism. Our professor keeps stressing that Weber wasn't making a crude causal claim that Protestantism single-handedly produced capitalism, but something subtler: an elective affinity between a set of values and an economic order. I want to stress-test that framework by running it against a case Weber never could have considered: the explosive industrialization of South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Singapore between roughly 1960 and 1990.

Here's the puzzle I keep circling. In the 1980s and 90s, a number of scholars and political figures — Lee Kuan Yew most prominently — explained the East Asian growth miracle in explicitly Weberian terms, arguing that Confucian values (emphasis on education, family discipline, thrift, deference to authority, group loyalty over individualism) played the same functional role in East Asia that the Protestant ethic allegedly played in northern Europe. But this immediately generates an awkward problem: Weber himself studied Chinese religion and concluded that Confucianism was poorly suited to generating modern capitalism, since it taught harmonious adjustment to the existing world rather than the restless, tension-driven mastery of it that he attributed to the Puritans. And there's a brute timing problem too — these societies were Confucian for centuries during which they were poor, and the values supposedly causing the boom didn't change around 1960, though the growth rates certainly did. Meanwhile, the competing explanations on offer are decidedly non-cultural: export-oriented state policy, land reform, heavy investment in primary education, Cold War American aid and market access, and authoritarian governments willing and able to suppress consumption in favor of investment.

So here is what I'd like you to do. First, construct the strongest Weberian cultural explanation of the East Asian miracle that the evidence can bear: what exactly would the mechanism be, at the level of individual motivation and economic behavior, and which features of the episode does it genuinely illuminate better than the institutional and geopolitical accounts? Second, say where the cultural explanation breaks or goes silent — what it cannot explain about the timing, about the variation among culturally similar countries, and about the fact that some of the same values were earlier blamed for East Asia's supposed backwardness (thrift became excessive saving, deference became stifled entrepreneurship, depending on which decade's commentary you read). Third, use the episode to evaluate Weber's underlying methodology: given that Confucian values were invoked to explain both stagnation and growth, is explaining economic outcomes with values even falsifiable, and what would it take to show that culture is a genuine cause rather than a post hoc narrative pasted onto outcomes that institutions and geopolitics actually produced? If you think Weber's original thesis survives this test better than the Confucian-ethic version, or vice versa, I'd like to hear that too, along with what distinguishes the two cases methodologically.
