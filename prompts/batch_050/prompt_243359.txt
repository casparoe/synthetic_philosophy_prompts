ESSAY DRAFT:

The paradox, as I reconstruct it: assume every truth is knowable in principle (if p, then possibly someone knows p). Take any truth q that nobody happens to know. Then the conjunction "q and nobody knows q" is itself a truth, so by the principle it's knowable. But knowing that conjunction would require knowing q while it's still true that nobody knows q, which is impossible — knowing q falsifies the second conjunct. So some truth is unknowable, and pushing the argument further apparently shows every truth is actually known, which is absurd. The standard escape routes are to restrict the knowability principle to a tamer class of truths (Tennant's "Cartesian" restriction), to reformulate it with an actuality operator so that truths only have to be knowable-as-they-actually-are (Edgington), or to just shrug and accept that some truths are unknowable (Williamson).

My idea: there's a natural class of truths where unknowability has nothing to do with idealism or with our cognitive limits — what I want to call "labile truths," truths that can't survive being known. I'd split them in two. Type one is self-effacing truths: "the surprise party is at seven," "there's an unexploited pricing error in this market." These are knowable, but only at the cost of being destroyed by the knowing — once known and acted on, they stop being true. Type two is ignorance-dependent truths: the worldly content itself includes the ignorance, like "this bridge has a flaw no inspector has found." These look strictly unknowable, because finding the flaw is exactly what makes the claim false.

Thesis: type-two labile truths give a third reading of Fitch. The paradox is usually taken to force a choice between anti-realism (truth must be epistemically constrained) and swallowing brute unknowable truths. But if the truths that resist knowledge are labile ones, then unknowability comes from the world's sensitivity to inquiry rather than from anything idealist or mysterious. Some facts are facts about unexamined corners, and examining a corner changes it. Inquiry is an intervention, not a window, and unknowability is just what intervention-sensitivity looks like from the inside.

I admit a worry: maybe type-two labile truths are just Fitch conjunctions in disguise, since "flaw and nobody found it" has the same shape as "q and nobody knows q." If so, my third reading collapses into a diagnosis of why the second conjunct happens to hold so often. I suspect the disguise charge fails — labile truths would still be unknowable in a world of perfect logicians, which isn't obviously true of Fitch's conjunction — but I haven't nailed that argument down.
