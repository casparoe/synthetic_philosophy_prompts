You are a professional philosopher with expertise in moral and legal philosophy, particularly the ethics of threats, offers, and coercion. Think carefully about each case before generalizing, and do not force a tidy answer if the cases resist one.

I am trying to figure out what I actually think about the blackmail paradox -- the puzzle of why it is wrong to demand money in exchange for refraining from something you would otherwise be entitled to do. Below are my verdicts on a set of cases. What I want is a principle (or a small set of competing principles) that captures the pattern, plus an honest account of which verdicts it cannot save.

My cases and verdicts:

1. A photographer discovers that a married politician is having an affair and emails him: pay me $50,000 or I sell these photos to a tabloid. Verdict: clearly wrong, and rightly criminal.

2. The same photographer simply sells the photos to the tabloid for $50,000 without contacting the politician. Verdict: sleazy but not wrongful in the same way, and should be legal.

3. The politician's former lover approaches him and offers to sign a nondisclosure agreement about the affair in exchange for $50,000. Verdict: morally murky, but it does not feel like case 1, and I do not think it should be criminal.

4. A man whose car was damaged in a hit-and-run tracks down the driver and says: pay for my repairs or I report you to the police. Verdict: acceptable, or close to it.

5. A diner discovers the restaurant's kitchen has serious health code violations and tells the owner: give me free meals for a year or I report you to the health inspector. Verdict: wrong -- this is blackmail, even though reporting the violations would be a public service.

6. A lawyer sends a letter saying her client will file a (meritorious) lawsuit unless the other side agrees to a settlement. Verdict: completely fine; this is ordinary legal practice.

7. An employee tells her boss she will quit and join a competitor unless she gets a raise. Verdict: completely fine.

8. The same employee discovers her boss has been expensing personal purchases and says: give me a raise or I tell the board. Verdict: blackmail, and worse than case 1 in some respects.

The pair I most struggle to reconcile is 1 and 3: both involve someone proposing silence in exchange for money concerning the very same facts, yet my verdicts diverge sharply. I am similarly uneasy about 4 and 5, which have the same structure (pay me or I exercise my legal right to report you) but different verdicts. A tentative principle I have considered is that the demand is wrongful when the payoff is disconnected from any legitimate interest the demander has in the underlying facts -- the hit-and-run victim has a claim to compensation, the diner does not have a claim to free meals -- but I suspect this does not distinguish 1 from 3, and I am not sure it handles 6 either, since the lawyer's client could simply sue.
