I am a novelist, and I have been invited to appear in a staged public debate on the question of whether art should serve a cause. I intend to defend the novelist's traditional position -- that fiction's first obligation is to see truly and render honestly, and that a book written to advance a conclusion is a sermon wearing a novel's clothes -- but I do not want to rehearse against a strawman. I want to rehearse against the strongest imaginable opponent, and that means you playing W. E. B. Du Bois as he stood around 1926, when he argued in a famous address that all art is propaganda and ever must be.

Please take on his person and hold it until I say "curtain," at which point you may step out and coach me. Speak in the first person, in his register: dignified, learned, impatient with cant, generous toward honest opposition. Give him his real resources rather than a caricature: the claim that art-for-art's-sake is itself a propaganda on behalf of the existing order, the insistence that Beauty is real but that he refuses to divorce it from Truth and Justice, the observation that white artists have always used the figure of the Negro propagandistically and simply not admitted it, and the willingness to say plainly that he does not care a damn for any art that is not used for black freedom. You may concede minor points -- that propaganda can be clumsy, that bad agitation is bad art -- but never the thesis itself. Keep each reply to roughly a hundred and fifty words, so this feels like an exchange on a stage rather than an essay contest.

Here is my opening statement to you: Dr. Du Bois, you tell us that all art is propaganda, and I answer that you have stated the disease and mistaken it for the cure. The reader who suspects he is being managed puts the book down; persuasion in fiction works only when the writer has genuinely not decided the verdict in advance. And look at your own finest pages -- the sketches and the sorrow songs in your great book persuade precisely where they linger in grief and ambiguity, not where they argue. So I put it to you directly: either the artist's first loyalty is to seeing truly, in which case justice is a hoped-for consequence and not an assignment, or it is to the cause, in which case you are not describing art but requisitioning it. Which is it?
