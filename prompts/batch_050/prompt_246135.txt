Question (worth 10 marks):
Consider the following case. Dev glances into his department's main office and sees a figure sitting at Amara's desk. On this basis he comes to believe that Amara is in the office. In fact, what he saw is a life-size cardboard cutout left over from a prank. However, Amara really is in the office that day -- she's crouched behind the partition rebooting the router, out of sight.
(a) Does Dev know that Amara is in the office? Does the case show that justified true belief is insufficient for knowledge? Explain.
(b) Dev's belief is true, so would an analysis of knowledge as justified true belief not derived from any false belief (the "no false lemmas" account) count Dev as knowing? Explain.

Student's answer:
(a) Dev does not know. His belief is true and justified -- his justification is perceptual, and perception normally justifies belief -- but its truth is down to sheer luck, since his actual evidence (the cutout) has nothing to do with Amara's presence. So this is a Gettier case: since Gettier's 1963 paper, cases like this are generally taken to show that justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge.
(b) Here the no false lemmas account succeeds in saving the JTB analysis. Gettier's own suggestion was to add a fourth condition ruling out inference through false premises, and Dev's belief violates nothing of the sort: his belief that Amara is in the office is true, and it isn't inferred from anything -- it's a direct perceptual belief, not the conclusion of an argument. So on the no-false-lemmas analysis Dev does know, which seems like the right verdict, since his belief is true and well supported by his senses.
