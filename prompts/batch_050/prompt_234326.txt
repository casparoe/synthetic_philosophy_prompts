I'm prepping a discussion unit for a philosophy-and-literature reading group (we're mostly amateurs but a pretty sharp crowd) and our next book is Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. I want to frame the discussion around self-deception, and specifically around whether Stevens the butler actually deceives himself or whether something else is going on. Here's the thing that got me onto this: I was reading Joseph Butler's sermon "Upon Self-Deceit" (from his Fifteen Sermons, 1726) and there's a bit where Butler argues, roughly, that self-deceit doesn't work like a con you run on yourself in one shot -- it's more that people steer their attention, dwelling on the considerations that flatter them and letting the damning ones slide past half-noticed, so that by the time they judge themselves the evidence has already been rigged. Butler seems to think this is how you get around the obvious paradox, namely that to deceive yourself you'd have to both know the truth (to hide it) and not know it (to be deceived), which looks impossible.

So my question: read Stevens through Butler's picture. The novel gives us a narrator who constantly remembers things in slightly off ways -- the business with his father's death while he keeps serving at the conference, the way he reframes Lord Darlington's antisemitism and the firing of the Jewish maids, the whole "dignity" story he tells himself, and those slippery moments where he says he doesn't recall something or distances himself from Miss Kenton's letter. Does Stevens fit Butler's attention-steering model, or is he better described by the more deflationary modern view (I'm thinking of Mele-style accounts) where there's no real "deceiving" at all, just motivationally biased belief formation -- in which case the paradox dissolves but maybe something important about Stevens gets lost? And push on the hard cases: near the end, on the pier, Stevens seems to finally see his life clearly. If he was self-deceived, what exactly changed, and can a Butler-style account explain the piercing of the illusion as easily as the maintaining of it?

Also, to be fair to the book: where does it resist the philosophical reading? A colleague of mine insists Stevens isn't deceived at all, just a reliable reporter of a life built on genuinely held (if awful) values, and that we readers impose the self-deception story because it's more comfortable than admitting someone could clear-headedly choose devotion to Darlington. I'd like you to take that objection seriously and say which scenes settle it one way or the other, if any do. We're meeting in three weeks and I want a handout's worth of argument, not plot summary -- assume everyone has read it.
