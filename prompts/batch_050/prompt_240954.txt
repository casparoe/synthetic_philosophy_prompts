Here is the answer i'm grading:

"Radical interpretation is Davidson's thought experiment about a field linguist who encounters a community speaking a completely unknown language and has to work out what their words mean starting from nothing. The question it answers is how meaning can be recovered from evidence available to an outside observer. The difficulty is that you cannot find out what a speaker believes without knowing what their sentences mean, but you cannot find out what their sentences mean without knowing what they believe, so belief and meaning have to be recovered together. The evidence available is just which utterances the speaker produces in which observable situations, for instance that they utter gavagai-style sentences when a rabbit runs past. To get from this thin behavioural evidence to meaning, Davidson brings in the principle of charity: the interpreter assumes the speaker's beliefs are mostly true and that their utterances are mostly expressions of those beliefs, wich breaks the circle, since the interpreter can guess the speaker's beliefs from the situation and then assign meanings that make those beliefs come out true. The truth theory enters because for Davidson, to understand a sentence is to know the conditions under which it would be true. A Tarski-style theory of truth for the language yields, for every sentence, a T-sentence like 'My neighbour owns a parrot' is true if and only if my neighbour owns a parrot, and Davidson's claim is that knowing a theory that entails all of these theorems amounts to knowing the meanings of all the sentences of the language. Like Tarski, Davidson defines truth for the language in a compositional way, which is how a finite theory can cover infinitely many sentences. One objection is that charity is too strong: whole communities seem capable of sharing massive false beliefs, for example about religion or superstition, so assuming most beliefs are true looks like it builds agreement in by fiat. Davidson's reply is that massive error is not really intelligible, since what a belief is about is fixed by what normally causes it, so a creature whose beliefs about the world were mostly false could not even be credited with having beliefs about that world in the first place. So the objection fails."
