So I'm TAing a phil of mind course this semester and last week a student cornered me after section with an argument that she swears shows functionalism "proves too much," and the annoying thing is I can't figure out where it breaks, if it even does. Here's her setup, cleaned up a bit. (1) What makes a state a pain, or a belief that it's raining, is the causal role it plays -- what typically causes it, what it causes in turn -- not the physical stuff realizing it. That's just functionalism, and honestly it seems like the only game in town given that we take octopus pain and (maybe) future AI minds seriously. (2) So any system whose parts are causally organized in the right way has the relevant mental states, no matter what the parts are made of. Silicon, neurons, whatever. (3) But then take the entire population of a big country, give everyone a two-way radio and a card of instructions, and have each person mimic one neuron -- relaying signals to exactly the people their neuron's outputs would go to, firing exactly when their neuron would. For, say, ten minutes, this crowd perfectly mirrors the causal organization of my brain while I'm tasting coffee. (4) By (1) and (2), there's a second subject of experience during those ten minutes, tasting coffee right alongside me, floating on top of a hundred million people who each feel nothing unusual. (5) That seems absurd on its face, but fine, maybe we bite the bullet. (6) Here's where she says it tips over: if the crowd counts because it matches my causal organization under some scheme of interpretation, then nothing principled stops us from finding mind-shaped organization in way weirder systems -- an anthill, the global shipping network, the fan inside my laptop -- because for any complex enough system there's some way of carving it into states and transitions that lines up with some functional description. (7) So functionalism either crowns a nation-sized ghost or slides into a panpsychism where minds are everywhere you bother to look, and both options are ridiculous, so functionalism is false.

The part that gets me is that every step looks like something I'd tell my own students. I definitely don't want to give up multiple realizability -- if I have to choose, I'd rather accept the crowd-mind than say mental states depend on carbon specifically, that feels like biological chauvinism. But the slide to anthills and laptop fans feels like it has to be cheating somewhere, I just can't name where. So: which step do you actually reject? If the problem is in the move from the crowd to the anthill, spell out exactly what the crowd has that the anthill lacks, because "the people are following instructions" feels too cheap -- neurons are arguably just following instructions too. And if the problem is earlier, tell me what I'm paying for rejecting it, because I suspect each exit costs something I'd rather not spend.
