My research group has spent the whole term arguing about where delusions come from, and it's basically condensed into two people: a one-factor theorist and a two-factor theorist. For context if you need it: the one-factor view says an anomalous experience or aberrant prediction error is basically enough to explain a delusion -- given weird enough data, forming a strange hypothesis is just what a normal reasoner does, and ordinary biases (jumping to conclusions, self-serving reasoning, that sort of thing) explain why it never gets corrected. The two-factor view says that can't be the whole story, because plenty of people have the relevant anomalous experience (the odd sense of unfamiliarity toward a loved one after certain brain injuries, or comparable experiences from drugs or sensory loss) without ever becoming delusional, so there must be a second deficit, in belief evaluation or hypothesis rejection, that explains why the explanation gets adopted and sticks around.

I want you to draft the document these two would produce if someone forced them into an adversarial collaboration. That means: the claims both of them can actually sign (about what delusions are like, about the role of anomalous experience, about what any adequate theory has to explain); the points where they still disagree, stated precisely enough that each would accept the wording of their own side; the cruxes -- the sub-questions where settling them would genuinely move at least one of them -- and for each crux a case, patient population, or kind of evidence that both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I suspect one crux is whether the first factor ever really occurs without the delusion following, and another is whether there's a measurable reasoning deficit in delusional patients that goes beyond the biases healthy people show, but check me on that and find others.

Two extra things. First, I'd like the document to address whether their disagreement is at least partly verbal -- e.g., whether "second deficit" and "extreme end of normal bias" might be the same thing described two ways -- and if so, what would make it non-verbal. Second, both camps tend to just assume the delusion is a belief: have them say explicitly whether their theory is commited to that, and what it would do to each view if delusions turned out to be some in-between state (closer to imaginings or acceptances) rather than full beliefs.

One format note: please write the whole thing as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables -- like a long letter summarizing where the collaboration landed, including the recorded predictions.
