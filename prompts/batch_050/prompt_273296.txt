I'm preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on animal ethics and population ethics, and I want a decision tree that sorts students by their position on what I'll call the scale argument for insect moral importance. The argument, roughly: the number of insects alive at any moment is vastly larger than the number of vertebrates (estimates on the order of 10^18 individuals are commonly given; you may treat the exact figure as stipulated rather than precise), so even if the probability that insects have valenced experience is low, their aggregate expected welfare might dwarf that of all vertebrates combined, and insects could become the dominant object of moral concern.

Build the tree as follows. Start from the shared premise that the empirical facts just described are not in dispute, so that all disagreement is normative or about consciousness. Each branch node should be a single diagnostic question, phrased so that someone can answer it without knowing which position it sorts them toward -- a concrete case or a bare claim, not jargon. The questions should probe the points where the positions genuinely come apart, such as: whether moral weight attaches to expected welfare (probability of sentience multiplied by welfare if sentient) or to welfare discounted or thresholded by uncertainty; whether extremely low-probability, astronomically high-stakes claims trigger a "fanaticism" objection to expected-value reasoning; whether it is valenced experience specifically, rather than nociception or self-awareness, that grounds moral status; whether plausible differences in the intensity or range of insect versus mammal welfare change the arithmetic; whether welfare can legitimately be summed across organisms at all; and whether deep uncertainty about insect consciousness licenses a precautionary duty not to harm, a positive duty to promote insect welfare, or neither.

The leaves should include at least these positions: the expected-value prioritizer who concludes that insect welfare dominates the moral calculus; the view that weights welfare by confidence in sentience and so lands on vertebrate priority; the precautionary minimalist who accepts duties not to harm insects but denies positive duties toward them; the anti-aggregationist who denies that welfare sums across individuals in the way the argument requires; the rejecter of expected-value maximization on fanaticism grounds; and the view that insect welfare ranges or sentience probabilities are too small for the scale argument to get off the ground. Give each leaf a name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Note any coherent combination of answers that no one seems to occupy, and say briefly whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot really separate.

Please be explicit about what is settled versus contested: distinguish claims that are genuinely disputed among people working in this area (for instance, where the live fault lines in the fanaticism debate lie) from anything you present as the standard or majority view, and do not dress up a contested stance as consensus.

Use at most eight branch questions, and keep the entire tree, including leaf annotations, to 800 words or fewer.
