I'm a third-year student in a seminar on the philosophy of history, and partly because of the course I've been asked to give the main student address at a ceremony my university is holding next month. The occasion is the re-dedication of our oldest building, named since 1891 for the industrialist whose donation founded the university. A two-year historical commission has just published its findings: his fortune rested in part on investments that profited from coerced labor, and — this is the finding that has made the ceremony genuinely fraught — his private diaries show that he knew the abolitionist and labor arguments of his own day, corresponded with critics, and explicitly reasoned his way past them. The university is keeping the name but adding a permanent exhibit and a new plaque. The audience will be genuinely mixed: descendants of the coerced laborers, some of whom campaigned for the name's removal; history faculty, several of whom sat on the commission and disagree sharply among themselves about presentism; donors; and my classmates.

Here is where the seminar has made the task harder rather than easier. Both stock moves seem to me intellectually dishonest in this specific case. The first — he was a man of his time, and we cannot judge the past by standards it couldn't have held — is gutted by the diaries: the standards were available to him, articulated to him personally, and declined. But the second — we simply judge him by timeless moral standards — concedes the very thing we've litigated all term, namely whether the judging 'we' is anything other than our own time talking, and whether the confidence of 2025 differs in kind or only in content from the confidence of 1891. So the speech has to do real philosophical work, not gesture at it. I need roughly seven minutes of remarks that take an actual position on whether historians and communities can escape the standards of their own time, and then build the ceremony's logic out of that position: what the plaque can honestly say, what the exhibit is for, and what we owe the people in the front row. Note that neither keeping nor removing the name follows automatically from either side of the presentism question, so the speech has to argue for the coherence of the whole package rather than treat one element as a corollary of the other.

Please write the speech. It should be deliverable by a student — not a disguised lecture with 'ladies and gentlemen' stapled to the front — and it needs to survive the scrutiny of the historians in the room without losing the people who came for a ceremony rather than a philosophy talk. I'd rather it commit to a view, even a controversial one, than split the difference. And if you think the diaries finding doesn't dissolve the man-of-his-time defense as cleanly as I've suggested — because a person can be surrounded by an argument without it being part of the standards he could actually inhabit — then the speech should reckon with that objection openly instead of hiding from it.
