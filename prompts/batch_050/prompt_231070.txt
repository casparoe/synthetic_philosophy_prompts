I am a PhD student working in applied ethics, and I keep getting stuck on a cluster of intuitions about mutually beneficial transactions that feel exploitative. Rather than ask for the literature, I want to do the exercise properly: here are my verdicts on cases, and I would like you to find what principle, if any, captures them.

Case 1: A factory owner in a low-income country pays garment workers $1.50 an hour. The workers' next-best option is subsistence farming, which pays less and is harder, so they take the jobs voluntarily and are genuinely better off. The owner could easily pay three times as much and still turn a healthy profit, but offers the lowest wage the local labor market will bear. My verdict: clearly wrong, and the fact that everyone benefits does not excuse it.

Case 2: A tourist in the same country haggles a street vendor down from $3 to $1 for a hat, purely for the sport of it. The vendor happily accepts $1; it is a normal price locally. My verdict: fine, or at worst mildly tasteless. I notice, though, that in both cases a wealthy party is using a favorable bargaining position to capture nearly all the surplus of the transaction, so I am not sure what separates them.

Case 3: A hardware store triples the price of plywood the day before a hurricane. Customers pay because the alternative is boarded-up nothing. My verdict: clearly wrong, roughly as wrong as Case 1.

Case 4: A well-off retiree pays the neighbor's teenager $5 to mow an enormous lawn, well below the going rate, and the teenager is thrilled because $5 is real money to her. My verdict: completely fine.

Case 5: A clinical trial pays participants $500 for an uncomfortable, slightly risky study. The payment is calibrated to be generous relative to local wages, so poor students sign up in droves while the wealthy would never consider it. My verdict: I genuinely cannot settle this one. It feels like Cases 1 and 3 in structure, but I resist condemning it.

My tentative principle is that a transaction is wrongfully exploitative when one party takes unfair advantage of another's constrained circumstances, even if the transaction is consensual and mutually beneficial. But unfair seems to do all the work, and I cannot say when it applies: it convicts the factory owner and the hardware store, but it seems to convict the tourist too, and I do not want to convict the tourist. I have also considered a competing idea: that a transaction cannot wrong someone if the alternative was not interacting with them at all, since the interaction leaves them no worse off. That saves the tourist and the teenager, but it forces me to acquit the factory owner, which I refuse to do.

Please do the following: state precisely the best principle (or two or three candidates) that captures as many of my verdicts as possible; identify which case motivates each clause of the principle; say what the principle implies about cases I have not mentioned, such as paid surrogacy and unpaid internships; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts, if any, the best principle cannot accommodate, and whether the defect lies in the principle or in the verdict.
