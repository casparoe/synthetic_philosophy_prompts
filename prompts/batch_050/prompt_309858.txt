I am a PhD candidate in political theory, and my dissertation prospectus defends a framework for thinking about our obligations to future generations in the climate context. Before I submit it, I would like an audit of whether the positions I have staked out are jointly coherent. My commitments, as stated in the prospectus, are these:

(1) Pure time discounting is morally illegitimate. The date at which a life is lived has no bearing on how much that life matters, so the well-being of a person in 2300 counts for exactly as much as the well-being of a person alive today.

(2) The non-identity problem shows that the people who will exist in the distant future owe their existence to whatever policies we in fact choose. Because different climate policies would bring different people into existence, those future people cannot complain that our emissions harmed them or made them worse off than they would otherwise have been. Any sound obligation regarding the distant future must therefore be stated impersonally -- in terms of making the world better or worse -- rather than in terms of what we owe to particular future individuals.

(3) Framing climate change as an injustice perpetrated by the present generation against future generations is both morally accurate and politically essential, and I defend this framing against critics who find it merely rhetorical.

(4) Given standard expected-value reasoning, reducing existential risks should be the dominant priority of long-term-oriented policy, and this conclusion holds even where the probability reductions we can purchase are very small, because the value at stake is so large.

(5) Justice gives priority to the claims of the worst-off people alive now. It would be wrong to impose significant sacrifices on the global poor of the present generation -- for instance by pricing them out of energy access -- in order to secure merely speculative benefits for people who do not yet exist.

My sense is that these sit comfortably together, but several examiners at my prospectus defense looked uneasy, and none could articulate why. Please tell me: which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, and how severe is each tension? Which apparent tensions dissolve once the positions are stated carefully? For each genuine tension, identify what I would have to give up or qualify, and rank the tensions by how costly the cheapest repair would be. I would rather revise the prospectus now than defend an inconsistent package at the viva, but I also do not want to abandon a commitment on the basis of a merely apparent conflict.
