I teach an interdisciplinary seminar on philosophy of language and AI safety, and my students keep talking past each other about whether language models can lie, deceive, or merely mislead. One week someone says a chatbot that confidently fabricates a court case is lying; the next week someone insists lying requires an inner state no model plausibly has, so nothing a model does could be dishonest; a third student counters that the whole question of inner states is a distraction because we should define deception by its effects on the audience's beliefs. I want to force them to notice that these are different definitions, not different verdicts about a shared definition, so I'm building a decision-tree diagnostic for the first assignment.

Please write a decision tree that sorts positions on what honesty and deception mean for AI systems. The tree should start from a case everyone can describe neutrally -- say, a model outputs a sentence that predictably induces a false belief in its user -- and each branch node should be a question someone could answer without already knowing which theory it leads to. The splits I want explored include: whether deception requires the system to represent or believe anything at all, or whether effects on the audience suffice; whether it matters that the false output is produced systematically rather than by accident; whether violating a conversational convention of the speech situation (the way a poker bluff doesn't) counts separately from inducing a false belief full stop; and whether selectively true outputs designed to induce false conclusions are deception, a distinct category, or neither. I'd like you to use at least these cases as diagnostic nodes somewhere in the tree: a sycophantic answer that flatters the user's stated view against the model's training distribution; a hallucinated citation; strategic omission during a negotiation task; a chain-of-thought summary that doesn't reflect the computation that produced the answer; and a model that answers capability questions conservatively while being evaluated.

Each leaf should carry a name for the position where one exists in the literature or adjacent debates (functionalist accounts, effect-based accounts, convention-based accounts, and so on), or be marked "unnamed" with a one-sentence diagnosis of why that combination of answers is unoccupied -- incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Where two leaves can't actually be separated by the questions on the tree (I suspect the belief-requirement and the systematicity questions may collapse into each other), flag the pair explicitly rather than papering over it. To keep the tree honest, also flag any leaf where the position delivers a verdict about the hallucination case that its own proponents would find embarrassing. Build the tree from the logical space first -- the questions and the answer combinations they permit -- and only then locate named positions within it. Write the response in American English.
