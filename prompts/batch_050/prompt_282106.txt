I'm in a graduate epistemology seminar and we've just finished the unit on Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge -- the idea that S knows that p only if, roughly, S wouldn't believe p if it were false and would believe it if it were true, across the relevant nearby possibilities. I thought I understood the payoff: the theory is supposed to let me keep my ordinary knowledge (that I have hands, that I'm sitting in a seminar room) while still taking the brain-in-a-vat hypothesis seriously. But then our professor said, almost in passing, that tracking theorists achieve this by rejecting epistemic closure, and several people in the room nodded as if this were obviously a cost worth paying. I've been stuck on this for days. Closure -- the principle that if I know p, and I know that p entails q, then I know q -- strikes me as just an application of deductive reasoning to things I know, i.e., basically modus ponens applied to knowledge. Rejecting it seems less like a philosophical position and more like refusing to follow an argument where it leads.

So my question: how can Nozick simply deny a rule of logic in order to escape the skeptic? If the answer to skepticism is that we're allowed to pick and choose which valid inferences to accept, hasn't the skeptic effectively won, since the whole force of the skeptical argument was that it proceeds by unimpeachable steps? I want to understand what the tracking theorist's principled reason is for treating closure as optional -- something beyond the fact that denying it is convenient -- and whether there's a way to keep the anti-skeptical benefits of tracking while remaining, so to speak, logically law-abiding. I'd also like to know whether the closure denial is doing all the work in the theory, or whether the tracking conditions themselves still contribute something independently.
