You are a philosopher who has spent years working on the strategic dynamics of advanced AI systems, with a serious background in decision theory and game theory. Think carefully before you write: brainstorm the candidate requirements first, then commit to a list. I want considered judgment, not a survey of talking points.

Context. In discussions of AI alignment, people invoke what is usually called the strategy-stealing assumption: roughly, the claim that for any strategy a misaligned AI system might pursue to gain influence over the future, an aligned system could execute a modified version of that strategy — one that secures comparable influence while remaining aligned — and thereby prevent the misaligned system from gaining an advantage merely by being unscrupulous. The assumption does load-bearing work. If it holds, alignment need not put a system at a competitive disadvantage. If it fails, alignment is a handicap, and safety-conscious developers lose races by construction.

What strikes me is that invocations of the assumption are almost never accompanied by a specification of what it would take for it to be true. Before I evaluate any argument for or against it, I want the specification.

Task. Write the desiderata an adequate account of strategy-stealing must meet — the conditions under which the aligned system can in fact copy the strategy, minus the bad parts. For each desideratum, give the consideration or case that motivates it, and state whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp. Your list should at minimum address the following:

- Individuation. What counts as one strategy, finely enough that stealing is well-defined but coarsely enough that the aligned modification still counts as the same strategy rather than a different, possibly weaker one.
- Presupposed resources. What capabilities or assets the copier must possess that the original strategy quietly assumes, and what follows when it lacks them.
- The epistemic problem. How the copier identifies which strategy to steal, given that a misaligned system has every reason to conceal its strategy, and may benefit precisely from its illegibility.
- Symmetry. Whatever mechanism lets the aligned system steal from the misaligned one appears to let the misaligned system steal from the aligned one. An account must say whether this symmetry holds, whether it must be broken, and what could break it.
- Temporal structure. Stealing implies a lag between the innovator and the copier. In domains with first-mover advantages or winner-take-all dynamics, the lag itself may be decisive. An account must say when the lag is survivable.

Then: (a) identify any subset of these desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say what that implies for the assumption itself — this is the part I care about most; (b) name the single desideratum that excludes the most naive versions of the assumption; (c) in one short paragraph only, sketch how the assumption fares against your own list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, not on the scorecard.

To fix the purpose: I want to know whether a developer could rationally commit to alignment on the grounds that it forfeits little or no competitiveness — not whether strategy-stealing is a reassuring story we can tell regulators.
