I am a novelist, and I want to interrogate the assumptions built into my own craft. When I write fiction — especially historical fiction — I operate under conventions I have absorbed without ever examining. Chief among them: a character's consequential act must be prepared for. If a mild-mannered clerk is to betray his friend in chapter thirty, I am expected to have seeded the betrayal earlier — a humiliation in childhood, a debt, a pattern of small compromises — so that when the act comes, the reader feels it was, if not predictable, then intelligible. Editors flag unmotivated actions as flaws. Reviewers call them implausible. The implicit standard is that a person is explained by the shape of their story: to understand why someone did something is to see how it grew out of what came before.

Meanwhile, when I read actual history for research, I encounter a rival picture: historians (or at least the more theory-minded among them) sometimes claim that events are genuinely explained only when subsumed under general patterns — that revolutions happen when fiscal crisis, elite fragmentation, and mass immiseration coincide, and that citing these conditions is what makes an account explanatory rather than merely evocative. On that view, my novelistic backstory-seeding looks like a decorative substitute for explanation, trading on the reader's feeling of understanding rather than the real thing.

What view of explanation does my craft presuppose? Does the novelist's demand that actions be "earned" by prior narrative commit me to the thesis that narrative is a genuine mode of explanation — or is the practice merely a psychological concession to readers, compatible with the view that only law-involving explanation counts? Which philosophical positions on historical explanation would my conventions quietly rule out, and which would they fit equally well? Is there an inconsistency in the fact that I also rely, constantly, on generalizations about human nature — that grief works this way, that ambition works that way — and if so, does my practice smuggle in the very covering laws it seems to bypass? Finally: if the covering-law theorists are right that narrative understanding is an illusion, what, concretely, would have to change about how novels are written — and would the result still be a novel?
