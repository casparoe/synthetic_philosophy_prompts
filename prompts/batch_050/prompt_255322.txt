At dinner last night, two old friends of mine — I'll call them Anna and Ben — fell into an argument about the word "genocide" that consumed nearly three hours, and I came home unable to decide who had the better of it. I mostly held my tongue, so I'd like a verdict from someone uninvolved. The occasion was a conflict currently in the news, which Anna calls a genocide and Ben calls a mass atrocity that has not been shown to be one; but please don't adjudicate the news event itself, since I've deliberately omitted which it is. Anna's position was that the term has a precise legal meaning — a demonstrated intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial or religious group as such — and that applying it where that intent hasn't been established cheapens it. Three of her points stuck with me. First, the word's moral force depends on its rarity and precision: once it covers every war with heavy civilian casualties, it covers nothing. Second, the label does practical work only when it is defensible in a courtroom, and accusations that collapse under legal scrutiny hand the perpetrators a propaganda victory. Third, nothing is lost morally by calling something a crime against humanity instead — the dead are just as dead — so the insistence on the g-word is about status rather than substance.

Ben conceded the legal definition but attacked from two directions. Historically, he argued, the lawyer Lemkin who coined the word during the Second World War had a much broader conception in mind, encompassing the destruction of a group's culture and institutions, and the narrower treaty definition we inherited was the product of postwar bargaining — certain powers insisting that political groups be excluded and that cultural destruction be dropped — so treating that definition as the word's true meaning, rather than a politically negotiated residue, is a mistake. His second point concerned timing: specific intent is something courts establish years after the fact, which means that on Anna's standard the word can only ever be applied once it is too late to serve the purpose for which it was coined, namely sounding the alarm in time. A word that arrives only after the victims are dead, he said, is a memorial, not a warning. Anna replied that Ben was really complaining that criminal law has standards of proof, and that alarm-language unconstrained by evidence is how the word gets weaponized by every side in every war. Ben's final move was to say that Anna's position quietly constructs a hierarchy of atrocity — one magic category at the top, everything else beneath it — so that victims of a mass killing that fails the intent test are told, in effect, that their suffering is second-class. Anna answered that the hierarchy exists in any case and the word merely marks it.

Can you adjudicate this, point by point? I don't need to be told that it is complicated. I want to know: who is right about whether the word's force depends on its rarity; who is right about whether the negotiated origins of the treaty definition should weaken its authority over current usage; whether Ben's late-arrival problem is a genuine defect of the legal definition or merely a feature of evidentiary standards generally, as Anna claimed; and whether declining to call a mass atrocity a genocide really does demote its victims, or whether that conflates a legal classification with a moral ranking. If both of them are missing some distinction that would dissolve part of the dispute — say, between the word's different jobs — I'd like that identified as well.
