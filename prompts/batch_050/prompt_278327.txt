I'm organizing a long-running seminar on wild animal welfare, and two of our regulars have agreed to do an adversarial collaboration instead of their usual arguing-past-each-other. I need you to draft the joint statement they'd both actually sign. You are a professional philosopher who's also read a decent amount of ecology and population biology -- think carefully, and maybe brainstorm the possible points of agreement and disagreement for yourself before you write the final document.

The two parties:

1. A hedonic utilitarian working on wild animal suffering who suspects that habitat destruction is, on net, a good thing. Her argument: most wild animals are r-selected (huge numbers of offspring, almost all of whom die young and horribly), so most wild lives are probably not worth living, and fewer animals = less suffering. Paving a meadow or draining a wetland looks, to her, like it might prevent more misery than it causes.

2. A conservationist who thinks protecting and restoring habitat is obviously good. He's not a deep ecologist or anything -- he's open to welfarist arguments in principle -- but he thinks the above reasoning is reckless, empirically shaky, and probably wrong even on its own terms.

The document should have three parts:

- A numbered list of claims both can sign (e.g. about the scale and nature of wild animal suffering, about which empirical questions the moral conclusion actually depends on, about what each side is NOT claiming -- no strawmen in the agreement section). Don't let it get vacuous: each claim should be something a careless version of one side might have denied.
- A disagreements section, where each residual disagreement is stated as a single precise question, with each side's answer phrased in words that side would accept. Please try hard to seperate genuine disagreements from ones that are really about words, and say which is which.
- A cruxes section: for each crux, the observation, study, or thought experiment that both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance (e.g. what population-dynamics evidence after habitat destruction would move whom, what role insect sentience plays, whether "net negative lives" is even the right frame vs. experienced quality per hour, how each side weights irreversibility and our massive uncertainty).

Two constraints: neither party is allowed to lean on intrinsic value of species or ecosystems as a conversation-stopper (he's agreed to argue on roughly welfarist turf for the purposes of the document), and the utilitarian has to engage seriously with the possibility that habitat loss just concentrates and intensifies suffering rather than reducing headcount. Aim for the actual cruxes, not the slogans each side puts on posters.
