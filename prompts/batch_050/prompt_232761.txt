In my philosophy of mind seminar last week, a classmate defended dualism with a now-familiar argument: we can conceive of a being physically identical to a conscious person but lacking any inner experience; whatever is conceivable is possible; therefore such beings are possible, and so consciousness is something over and above the physical. When I pressed back that we can also seem to conceive of water existing without being H2O, and that scenario nonetheless isn't genuinely possible, he answered that the water case is only *apparently* conceivable, whereas his case is genuinely conceivable -- and then, when I asked what the difference was, he said I could "just tell" if I thought about it hard enough. I left the seminar sure that "whatever is conceivable is possible" is doing too much work here, but unable to say exactly which versions of it are doing which work.

Here is what I'd like. Lay out, as an organized menu, the distinct precise theses that the slogan "whatever is conceivable is possible" could be expressing. Please disambiguate at least both halves of it: what "conceivable" might mean (mere imaginability, absence of detectable contradiction, coherent positive conception of a scenario, conceivability that survives ideal rational reflection) and what "possible" might mean (epistemic possibility, conceptual or logical possibility, metaphysical possibility, physical or nomological possibility). For each thesis on the menu, tell me: is it a platitude, a defensible but substantive claim, or straightforwardly false? Then, and this is the part I most need, tell me which of these readings would actually support my classmate's inference from the conceivability of his scenario to the falsity of physicalism -- and which reading his retreat to "apparently conceivable" presupposes, and what it costs him (for instance, whether the stronger notion of conceivability that survives the water counterexample still lets him establish that his own scenario is conceivable in that sense, rather than just assuming it).

Finally, please be careful to separate what is broadly settled among people who work on this from what remains genuinely contested. I know, for instance, that the naive form of the slogan is widely thought to have been undermined by standard cases of necessary truths that can be denied without obvious contradiction, and that the survival of some refined conceivability-to-possibility link is a live dispute -- but I'd rather you flag which parts of the terrain are consensus and which are open battlegrounds than present one philosopher's favored refinement as the received view. I'll be bringing this to the seminar when we revisit the argument next week, so organize it as a list I can work through point by point.
