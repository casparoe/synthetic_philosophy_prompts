The question:

Larry Temkin's spectrum arguments purport to show that "all-things-considered better than" can be intransitive. (a) Reconstruct the structure of a spectrum argument, making explicit the premises about adjacent cases and the comparison of the two endpoints. (b) Explain why intransitivity of "better than" would be a significant result for axiology and for rational choice. (c) Present and evaluate two distinct responses available to someone who wants to preserve transitivity. Your answer should make clear which premise each response rejects and what it costs.

The student's answer:

A spectrum argument considers a long sequence of outcomes, each differing from its neighbor in only one or two respects. Take Temkin's example: outcome A is two years of excruciating torture, and we proceed through a spectrum of outcomes, each slightly shorter or slightly less intense than the one before, until we reach Z, a mild hangnail. Premise 1 states that for each adjacent pair, the earlier outcome is worse than the later one: A is worse than B, B is worse than C, and so on. Each such judgment seems compelling because the difference between adjacent outcomes is tiny relative to the overall badness involved. But transitivity would then deliver the conclusion that A is worse than Z — that two years of agony are better than a hangnail — which is clearly false. So we have an inconsistent triad: the pairwise judgments, the endpoint judgment, and transitivity. Temkin's own verdict is that transitivity is the premise to give up: "better than" is not transitive, because the pairwise comparisons are more secure than any abstract structural principle.

If transitivity fails, the consequences are serious. First, money-pump arguments show that anyone with intransitive preferences can be turned into a pump for money, so intransitive value orderings are logically incoherent and cannot describe any possible rational agent. Second, Parfit's mere addition paradox in Reasons and Persons is essentially the same result as the spectrum argument — both are arguments by Temkin that transitivity of "better than" fails — and so the Repugnant Conclusion literature already presupposes intransitivity.

A defender of transitivity has at least two options. The first is to deny Premise 1 by appealing to threshold effects: somewhere in the spectrum there is an adjacent pair where the relation reverses or breaks, even if we cannot identify where. The cost is that every individual pairwise judgment looks just as reasonable as every other, so the defender must insist that one of a huge number of seemingly identical judgments is false. The second option is to reject the completeness of "better than": adjacent outcomes are only roughly comparable, and the relation "roughly as good as" is transitive across the whole spectrum, which blocks the argument from pairwise comparisons to the endpoint comparison. The cost is that this treats "equally good" as a relation that can chain indefinitely, which is a strong assumption about the precision of value.

Overall, I find the threshold response more promising, because the completeness response relies on transitivity of a relation that is obviously transitive, whereas thresholds merely require us to accept some indeterminacy about where comparisons reverse.
