I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and last month I attended a small workshop on democratic theory where two political theorists got into an extended argument during a panel. The occasion was a hypothetical case the organizers had set: a coastal province of a midsized democracy, call it Illyra, announces a referendum on independence, and the question on the table is simply who should be entitled to vote in it. I'd like to use the exchange as the spine of a piece, but first I want it adjudicated on the merits.

The two panelists -- I'll call them Asha and Benedikt -- went roughly five rounds:

1. Asha opened by saying the franchise should go to all current residents of the province, full stop. That's how real secession referendums are typically run, residence is administratively clean, and it tracks the intuition that a people living on a territory should determine its own future.

2. Benedikt replied that this is question-begging in a literal sense: letting the province define its own electorate hands the secessionist side the power to choose the body most likely to say yes. The decision doesn't just affect Illyrans -- the rest of the country loses territory, coastline, tax base, perhaps strategic assets. On the principle that all affected interests deserve a voice, the whole country should vote.

3. Asha countered that the all-affected-interests principle proves too much: my country's elections affect my trading partners and my neighbors, yet nobody thinks foreigners should vote in them; taken seriously, the principle dissolves every political border it was meant to justify. Worse, letting the whole country vote gives the majority a permanent structural veto over any minority's self-determination, which empties the idea of self-determination of content -- the point of such claims is precisely that the province is a distinct demos, not a dissenting minority inside someone else's.

4. Benedikt answered that this appeal to the province as a distinct demos is circular, and that this circularity is the whole problem: any democratic procedure for settling who belongs to the demos already presupposes a demos to run it, so the boundary question can never be settled democratically -- it can only be settled by a substantive principle. His candidate: those who will be subject to the coercive laws of the resulting state get the vote. On that basis, current mainlanders don't vote in the Illyran referendum (they'll live under their own state's laws, just in a smaller state), but neither do members of the Illyran diaspora who left years ago and will never return. His positive proposal was a nested procedure: the province votes on whether it wants independence, and the whole country votes on whether to accept the terms -- secession as something closer to a negotiated divorce than a unilateral declaration.

5. Asha replied that the subjected-to-coercion principle has the same circularity he accused her of: whether someone will be subject to the new state's laws depends on where the border is drawn, which is the very thing under dispute, so the principle can't deliver a verdict without smuggling the answer in. And the nested procedure, she said, is the majority veto from round three wearing formal dress. She closed by noting that essentially no existing state acquired its present boundaries through any procedure resembling his -- they came from history, war, and accident -- so demanding procedural legitimacy from would-be secessionists alone is a double standard that entrenches the status quo.

Both are serious people making real points, and I can't tell who gets the better of it at each step. Please go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate: state each side's claim in its strongest form, say who is right about that specific point and why (or that the point is genuinely tied), and note what both are missing -- including, if it matters, whether the two are even arguing about the same question (a principle for just boundaries, versus a workable procedure for unjust situations). I'm a bystander here and haven't indicated to either of them which way I lean; keep the adjudication even-handed.

After the prose adjudication, please give me a compact summary table with exactly these columns: Move (the round number and a five-to-ten-word label); Speaker; Core claim; Strongest form (the claim repaired of any weakness in how it was actually put); Verdict (who holds the better position on this point, or "tied," with the reason in one sentence); What's missing (the consideration both parties overlook at this step, or "none").
