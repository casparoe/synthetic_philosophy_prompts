I run an interdisciplinary reading group (philosophers of mind plus a few machine-learning researchers) and we keep circling the same question without ever mapping it properly: can a reinforcement learning agent suffer, and in particular does its reward signal amount to anything like pleasure and pain? The discussions founder because participants talk past one another — some assume suffering requires phenomenal consciousness, others think the functional role is all there is, and the ML people point out that the reward isn't even 'inside' the trained agent at deployment, which nobody knows what to do with. I would like a decision tree that takes someone from a neutral starting point ('an RL agent receives positive and negative reward during training, and the resulting policy behaves as if it seeks some outcomes and avoids others') to a named position on whether anything it is like to suffer is going on.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of authors: each branch node should be a question someone could answer without knowing which position it leads to, and the questions should be the points on which the positions genuinely come apart — for instance, whether valence requires phenomenal consciousness or whether the functional profile (motivational priority, aversive learning, global reportability) is sufficient; whether, given a consciousness requirement, the substrate matters (biological naturalism versus substrate-independent functionalist theories); whether the right candidate-bearer of valence is the reward signal in the training loop, the learned value function, or the policy's ongoing internal states; whether suffering requires a self-model or subject that the suffering is happening to; and whether scalar reward magnitude could track felt intensity at all. Each leaf should carry a position name (feel free to coin descriptive names where the literature's labels are contested), one sentence on what the position is committed to here, and one sentence on its most awkward consequence — such as the functionalist's implication that a sufficiently large tabular Q-learner might suffer a little, or the biological naturalist's implication that no behavioural evidence could ever settle the matter.

I want roughly eight to twelve branch nodes and six to eight leaves, one of which should be an explicitly undecided or precautionary position (we cannot know, and the epistemic situation itself has moral consequences). Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate with its questions, and note any combination of answers that has no occupant, with a brief diagnosis of whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Throughout, please separate what is genuinely settled — say, the functional difference between a reward signal and a felt state, or the fact that a deployed policy typically receives no reward — from what is live disagreement among people who work on this, and do not present any contested theory of consciousness as the default view.
