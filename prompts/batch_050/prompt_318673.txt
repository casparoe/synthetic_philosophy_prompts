I'm a philosophy prof who's been teaching philosophy of mind for years, and I'll admit I've become half-persuaded by constitutive panpsychism — the version where little pockets of micro-experience combine to constitute the big unified experience I'm having right now. What I want is a map of what would actually count against it, beyond the standard combination problem everyone recites. Which specific failures would be decisive — say, if it turns out the view can't state what the subjects of micro-experience even are without smuggling in a macro-level subject, or if it collapses into panqualityism or illusionism when pressed? Are there empirical results that would genuinely threaten it (say, neuroscience showing that phenomenal unity tracks something with no microphysical analogue, or that the structure of experience mismatches the brain's physical structure), or is the view so insulated by qualifications that nothing could count? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one, tell me whether panpsychists themselves would concede it as a real threat or dismiss it as a misunderstanding. Also say whether the insulatedness, if you find it, is itself a problem or just what metaphysics looks like. Wrap up with a last line of the form \answer{...} naming the single defeater you think is most likely to be fatal, or "none — the view is unfalsifiable" if that's your honest verdict.
