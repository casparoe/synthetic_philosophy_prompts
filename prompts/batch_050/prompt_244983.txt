Exam question: Daniel Dennett urges us to ask which varieties of free will are "worth wanting" rather than asking whether we have free will simpliciter. Explain what this reframing amounts to, describe one variety of free will Dennett thinks we should want and why, and assess whether securing that variety gives us everything we intuitively thought free will was supposed to deliver.

Student's answer: The traditional free will problem asks whether determinism is true and whether we possess contra-causal freedom, and it treats the whole debate as hostage to that metaphysical question. Dennett's reframing is to stop asking about freedom in the abstract and instead identify the specific capacities that matter to us -- the ones that make moral responsibility, deliberation, and blame sensible -- and then ask which of those capacities we actually have and whether determinism threatens them. Most of them, he argues, survive determinism just fine, so the traditional problem largely dissolves.

One variety worth wanting is rational self-control: the capacity to govern one's conduct in light of reasons rather than being pushed around by impulse or external manipulation. This matters because it is what makes holding someone responsible more than a mere institutional convention -- a person who can recognize reasons, weigh them, and act on them is the sort of creature to whom praise and blame can intelligibly be addressed. Dennett also connects this to avoidability: what we care about is that people can notice harms coming and steer around them, and that capacity doesn't require that they could have done otherwise in exactly the same circumstances. On this point Dennett leans on Frankfurt's cases, which show that moral responsibility requires alternative possibilities: an agent is only responsible if she could have avoided doing what she did, which is why freedom from external constraint is the relevant notion. This also fits with van Inwagen's Consequence Argument, which was designed to show that determinism is compatible with the ability to do otherwise, since the past and the laws leave room for rational deliberation.

Because capacities like self-control and avoidability are both worth wanting and compatible with determinism, Dennett's reframing secures everything we intuitively wanted from free will, and the traditional problem can be set aside as solved.
