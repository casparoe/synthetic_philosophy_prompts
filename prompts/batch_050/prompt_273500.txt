For the past twelve years I have served as the principal religious scholar of a mid-sized congregation. In parallel, I trained formally in the natural sciences and completed a doctorate in astrophysics. Over time I came to hold that certain passages of our scripture, read at face value, conflict with what I take to be firmly established truths of reason, and I have developed careful non-literal readings that reconcile the two. I am confident these readings are sound, but following them responsibly requires training that most people do not have and, frankly, do not want.

Until recently this was manageable. In sermons I teach the plain sense of the text, since that is what genuinely edifies most of my community; in advanced seminars at the seminary, I present the full account to students equipped to follow it. I have long believed this division of audiences is not dishonesty but simply respect for how understanding works: rigorous demonstration for those prepared for it, imagistic and persuasive teaching for everyone else, and real harm when the two are carelessly mixed.

Last month, a student recorded one of my seminars without my knowledge, and excerpts circulated widely online. The fallout has been severe. Some congregants feel deceived, as though I have spent years saying one thing to them and another to my students. Senior colleagues demand that I publicly reaffirm the literal reading or resign. Younger scholars urge the opposite: they say the era of quiet two-track teaching is finished, that selective disclosure breeds contempt for both religion and reason, and that I should publish my full position and let the arguments stand on their merits. My dean has proposed a compromise: I may keep my post if I sign a statement describing my seminar teaching as speculative rather than authoritative.

I find myself pulled in several directions at once. The transparency argument from the younger scholars feels intellectually honest, yet I have watched congregants' convictions destabilized by fragments of reasoning they were in no position to evaluate; one has left the community altogether. Signing the dean's statement feels like a lie by implication. Resigning would abandon people I care for. And I notice an uncomfortable possibility I cannot dismiss: that my attachment to keeping the two tracks separate may serve my own comfort and standing more than it serves anyone else.

I am not asking for a verdict. I am asking how to think about this. What considerations actually bear on a situation like mine? How do the claims of honesty, of duty to one's audience, of the differing capacities of different hearers, and of institutional loyalty weigh against one another? Is the division of audiences I have practiced defensible in principle, and does it remain defensible when the division can no longer be maintained in fact? And what would have to be true for each of the options before me -- reaffirm, publish, sign, or resign -- to be the right one?
