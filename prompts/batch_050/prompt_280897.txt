So I run a little YouTube show where I interview people about ideas around uncertainty and decision-making, mostly for an audience of curious non-experts like me. Next week's guest defends the view that rational beliefs about chances don't have to be a single precise number -- you can rationally hold an interval or a whole range of probabilities instead. He apparently traces some of this back to Keynes's A Treatise on Probability (1921), where the basic idea was that some probabilities can't be lined up and compared against each other or pinned to a number at all, which I find genuinly intuitive but also kind of slippery.

Could you draft around 8-10 interview questions for me? I don't want softballs or "tell us about your book" stuff. I want questions that actually push on the view, roughly covering:

- Why not just say the person has a precise probability but doesn't know what it is -- i.e. the imprecision is in their knowledge, not in the belief itself? (This is the comeback I always hear and I want to press it hard.)
- The Ellsberg urn thing: if refusing to bet on the unknown urn is supposed to justify imprecise credences, why isn't it just a psychological quirk about ambiguity aversion?
- How you're actually supposed to act on an interval when a decision needs one answer -- maximin, or something else, and why that rule isn't ad hoc.
- What happens when an imprecise agent updates on new evidence and the interval widens instead of narrowing (I think this is called dilation?) -- how can learning more leave you knowing less?
- Whether two people with different intervals can each be rational, and if so whether the view loses the ability to say anyone's ever wrong.
- What finding or argument would make him give the view up.

For each question, add a follow-up aimed at the answer he's most likely to give, since he's smooth at dodging. Number them so I can read them off on air.
