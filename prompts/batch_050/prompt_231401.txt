Two friends of mine got into an extended argument about the following thought experiment. A perfectly reliable predictor flips a fair coin. If it lands tails, the predictor gives you $1,000 -- but only if she predicted that you would pay her $100 in the event that it landed heads. If it lands heads, she asks you for $100. The coin lands heads, you are asked for the $100, and you will never face this situation again. My friend Daniel says you should pay: before the flip, you would have wanted to be the kind of person who pays, since that policy has higher expected value across the two branches, and learning the outcome shouldn't change what the right policy is. My friend Sofia says this is a confusion: the $1,000 was never going to exist in the branch you're actually in, so paying is just destroying $100 in the actual world for the sake of a world you know didn't happen, and "you would have wanted to pre-commit" only shows pre-commitment is valuable when it can still influence the predictor -- which here it no longer can. Daniel replied that her reasoning collapses the difference between this case and so-called XOR blackmail, where paying a blackmailer is clearly correct even in the branch where the threatened disaster isn't occurring, and that any principle delivering the right answer there has to deliver his answer here. Sofia answered that the cases differ precisely because in XOR blackmail the bad outcome really obtains in some branch and your payment is evidence about which branch you're in, whereas in the coin case you already know everything relevant, so no evidential or policy-based consideration can reach the counterfactual branch. Finally, Daniel conceded one thing that Sofia claims sinks his view: if the coin had been biased 1000-to-1 in favor of heads, paying would be absurd, so his position depends on how probable the counterfactual branch was -- and once that's admitted, the dispute is no longer about principle but about drawing an arbitrary line. They asked me to adjudicate and I couldn't. Please go through the exchange point by point: state who is right about each distinct claim (including whether the analogy to XOR blackmail holds and whether the biased-coin concession is fatal), identify anything both are missing, and give an overall verdict.
