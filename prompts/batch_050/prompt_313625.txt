My dad ended every Brexit argument at Christmas dinner the same way for years: 'the people have spoken, end of.' I've since heard the identical move in totally different fights -- a friend defending a California ballot prop nobody can amend, a Swiss colleague about some initiative that won with 50.1%. What bugs me is I'm sure the sentence is doing different work in each case but I can never put my finger on it in the moment.

Can you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that 'the people have spoken' or 'respect the will of the people' could be making? I'm thinking of candidates like: a majority preference exists and this vote revealed it; the vote is binding regardless of what anyone wants now; majorities are more likely to be right than elites; the result settles the *question* even if not the *policy*; losing voters consented to be outvoted by participating. For each one, tell me whether it's basically a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or actually false -- and which readings people who use the phrase as an argument-stopper probably need.

Also: which of these readings, if any, survive the fact that the ballot usually bundles a bunch of options into one question? Like, 'leave' covered everything from Norway-style single market stuff to crashing out with no deal, and I keep suspecting there was never a single thing 'the people' asked for, in which case 'the people have spoken' might be literally contentless rather than just overconfident. Am I onto something there or is that too cute?
