So I've been reading about the medieval thinker Peter Abelard and his take on ethics, and as I understand it his big idea goes roughly like this: sin consists entirely in consenting to what you know to be wrong — that is, in deliberately accepting the wrongdoing in your mind — and not in the outward act itself, not in the desire or temptation you feel beforehand, and not even in whether you actually manage to pull the act off. So on his view, someone who fully consents to commit a murder but gets stopped by sheer luck is just as sinful as someone who goes through with it, while someone who feels a powerful urge to do wrong but never consents to it hasn't sinned at all. Apparently even the person who does the deed while inwardly refusing to consent (say, under coercion) comes out clean on this picture, which seems pretty radical.

Please describe the most important implications of this "sin is in the consent" view for how a modern legal system ought to treat: (1) attempted versus completed crimes, (2) strict-liability offences where no intent is required at all, and (3) people who form a settled intention to commit a crime but never act on it. For each, say whether you think the legal system should actually move in the Abelardian direction or whether there's a good reason the law can't follow ethics all the way there — I'm especially curious where you think the analogy between sin and crime breaks down, since sin presumably isn't bothered about detterence or evidence but the law has to be.
