So I'm a science/ideas journalist and I've somehow agreed to give the closing talk at a small interdisciplinary workshop on nature-nurture debates. My thesis is deliberately middle-of-the-road and therefore probably annoying to everyone there: I argue that the heritability estimates behavioral genetics produces for complex social traits (educational attainment, income, wellbeing, that kind of thing) are technically legitimate science, but that the number itself is communicationally toxic — it should basically never appear in a headline or lede, because nearly every use a general audience makes of a heritability figure is a use the underlying math doesn't license. I'm NOT claiming the field is rotten or that twin studies are pseudoscience, and I'm NOT claiming heritability means "genetic destiny" — my whole point is that the technically correct reading (a population-specific proportion of variance, silent about individuals, silent about between-group gaps, changeable with the environment) is so counterintuitive that honest reporting of the number reliably misinforms anyway.

Here's the audience I have to survive: two behavioral geneticists who do twin/family and GWAS work and are sick of journalists calling them eugenicists; one population geneticist who is openly skeptical that human behavioral genetics delivers anything robust; two sociologists of science who study how genetic claims get laundered into policy and think I'm being naive about "honest reporting"; and a philosopher of biology moderating who will definitely ask a clarifying question that is actually a trap.

Build me a prep sheet, not a pep talk: the objections I'll actually get from THIS crowd (not from twitter), roughly ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that specific person would phrase it — the behavioral geneticists will push on missing heritability and on whether I'm holding their field to a standard no other science meets; the pop gen person will say my concession that the science is "legitimate" is doing all the work and I can't have it; the sociologists will say the problem isn't comprehension it's incentives. For each objection give me a two-minute reply, what I should just concede up front versus hold the line on, and a note on where my reply is softest. Also: my talk leans on the standard illustration that a trait can be highly heritable and yet wholly environmentally fixable (I use the variation-in-soil-quality plant example, roughly), plus the point that high within-group heritability licenses nothing about group differences — tell me where those two load-bearing moves can be attacked by someone who actually knows the variance decomposition literature. And tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks, which I suspect involves me admitting there's some trait for which I'd accept a heritability headline.
