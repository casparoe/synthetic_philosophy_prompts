A colleague of mine has lately taken to citing Ayn Rand's slogan "the virtue of selfishness." Most recently, he used it to wave off a suggestion that he might occasionally do small favors for people he expects nothing from — covering a shift for a coworker, say, or helping a neighbor move — as if the slogan settled, all by itself, that such acts are at best morally optional and at worst a kind of vice. I have not read Rand, but I suspect the phrase is doing more work than any single idea could bear.

Please spell out the different precise theses this slogan could be expressing, laid out as a list I could bring to our next conversation. I would like the readings to range from the near-trivial (for instance, that acting in one's own interest is not automatically wrong) through the substantive (for instance, a normative claim that rational concern for one's own life and flourishing is the proper standard of moral value) to the radical (for instance, that benefiting others without expectation of return is positively immoral). For each reading, indicate: whether it is trivial, substantive-but-defensible, or plausibly false; how it trades on ambiguities in the word "selfishness" (e.g., the difference between having one's own welfare as a motive, exploiting others, and merely declining to place others' interests above one's own); and whether it is the sort of thing Rand herself likely meant, given that she is often said to be redefining the term against its ordinary usage. Most importantly, for each reading, state whether it would actually support my colleague's use of the slogan — the inference from "selfishness is a virtue" to "uncompensated favors are morally suspect" — or whether that inference requires smuggling in a stronger claim than the reading licenses.

I am not asking for an overview of Objectivist ethics or for sources; I am asking for a disambiguation of one piece of language, with verdicts.
