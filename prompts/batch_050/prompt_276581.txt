In the last few decades, many police departments and courts have converged on a standard package of safeguards for eyewitness identifications. The officer administering a lineup is kept blind to which person in it is the suspect. The witness is told explicitly that the perpetrator may not be present. Photographs are shown one at a time rather than side by side. The witness's confidence is recorded immediately after any identification and before anyone in the room reacts. No feedback of any kind is given afterward. Identifications made without these safeguards are treated as less reliable, and in some jurisdictions they are excluded from evidence altogether. I want to know what theory of the mind this package presupposes. Take seriously the difference between two pictures. On the first, what a witness expects, hopes, or later comes to believe can reach back and alter what the witness actually perceived or what got stored in memory in the first place. On the second, perception is sealed off from belief and desire, and everything the safeguards guard against happens strictly downstream, in memory, judgment, confidence, and testimony. Which measures in the package make sense only on the first picture, and which are fully explained by the second? Does the package presuppose one coherent view, or does it hedge between the two? Consider also that every safeguard polices what happens after the crime, never the encoding at the scene itself: does that presuppose that the original perception is a fixed baseline the procedure can protect, and what follows if it is not? Finally, the package distrusts top-down influence on witnesses yet deliberately uses it, instructing witnesses what to assume about the lineup's contents. Is that an inconsistency in the implicit theory, or can it be made sense of?
