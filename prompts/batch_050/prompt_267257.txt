A friend of mine who's doing a PhD on 19th century ethics gave me this summary of Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason: 'In the Methods Sidgwick tests three methods of ethics -- egoism, intuitionism, and utilitarianism -- and the "dualism of practical reason" is his name for the fact that all three give conflicting verdicts, so practical reason ends up contradicting itself. He argues intuitionism is really just unsystematized common sense that needs utilitarianism to organize it, wich narrows the fight down to egoism vs. utilitarianism. But reason can't adjudicate between those two without theological assumptions about the universe being morally governed, and since Sidgwick was an agnostic, he concluded that morality has no rational claim on the agent unless it happens to line up with self-interest.' How fair is this? He works on this period so I'm inclined to trust him, but that ending struck me as stronger than what little I've read of Sidgwick. Tell me which parts a Sidgwick scholar would accept, which parts are conflations or overstatements, and whether his follow-up claim -- that Sidgwick basically admits utilitarianism is indefensible as a moral theory -- follows from whatever turns out to be right in the summary.
