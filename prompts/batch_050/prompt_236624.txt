I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing an explainer about why intelligent people persistently disagree. A researcher I interviewed summarised Aumann’s agreement theorem for me as follows: Aumann proved that two rational and honest people cannot disagree indefinitely. Once they exchange their estimates and know one another’s estimates, their opinions must coincide, because Bayesians who begin with the same prior and possess the same evidence will update in the same way. Hence, if a dispute about a forecast, a policy or a price carries on after both sides have heard each other, at least one participant must be irrational, dishonest, or secretly better informed. Some economists therefore think that, if you regard another person’s judgement as reliable, you should simply move all the way to their position rather than splitting the difference. The result also helps explain why sustained disagreement between rational traders should be impossible unless someone is withholding private information.

How fair is this as a summary of the theorem and its significance? Please audit it sentence by sentence, distinguishing what a careful decision theorist or economist would accept from what they would qualify or reject. In particular, explain the roles of the common-prior assumption, common knowledge rather than merely shared information or mutual awareness, Bayesian rationality, and knowledge of each other’s posterior probabilities. I am especially interested in whether the theorem describes a conversation converging over time, whether it licenses the claim that continued real-world disagreement shows irrationality or dishonesty, whether respecting another person’s judgement requires adopting their view wholesale, and whether anything in the theorem supports the claims about markets and concealed information. A simple numerical or everyday example would be helpful, but please avoid turning the answer into a general survey of Bayesianism. Please write in British English.
