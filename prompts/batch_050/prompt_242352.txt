The gist as I got it: every physical event already has a fully sufficient physical cause, my intention to raise my hand is either literally identical to some brain state or it's something extra, and if it's extra then either it does no causal work or everything it does is also being done by neurons anyway, which would make the whole world weirdly overdetermined. So the mental gets squeezed out.

Here's what I want: restate that whole argument in the vocabulary I actually think in -- the author, the draft, the characters, what happens "in the story" versus what I type, plot logic versus the words on the page. When I say things like the character wouldn't let me kill her off, or the plot demanded the brother come back, is that mental-causation talk in disguise? Does the author play the role of the physical base, or of the mind, or neither?

Then give me the residue report: what translates cleanly, what you had to fudge, what has no counterpart at all. I especially want to know whether causal closure has any real analogue in fiction, and whether the author standing *outside* the story's causal order (while my mind does not stand outside my brain) breaks the translation in a deep way or a boring one. And be honest about the verdict: from inside the fiction vocabulary, does the philosophers' problem look smaller than they think, or bigger?
