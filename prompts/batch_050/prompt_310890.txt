There is a small but persistent debate at the intersection of wild-animal welfare ethics and conservation that, as far as I can tell, has not advanced in years: does habitat destruction — clearing forest, draining wetlands, paving grassland — reduce or increase total wild-animal suffering? One side argues roughly as follows. Most wild animals are r-selected: they produce enormous numbers of offspring, nearly all of whom die young from starvation, predation, disease, or exposure. Invertebrates plausibly outnumber vertebrates on any given hectare by many orders of magnitude, so the welfare ledger of an ecosystem is dominated by vast numbers of very short, very harsh lives. If the average such life is not worth living, then habitat destruction, by preventing the births that habitat would have supported, plausibly reduces suffering in expectation — an uncomfortable result, but one the argument seems to force. The other side — most conservationists, and many animal ethicists — treats habitat destruction as a paradigm case of harming animals, and resists the conclusion either by disputing the grim empirical picture, by rejecting the aggregation, or by insisting that species, ecosystems, or naturalness itself carry value that the welfare arithmetic ignores. What strikes me is that both sides largely agree on the natural history, and yet nobody moves.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that this is not one disagreement but at least three stacked on top of each other and rarely disentangled: an empirical disagreement about the sign of average wild-animal welfare; a disagreement in population axiology about what follows even if wild lives are net-negative, since totalist, critical-level, and person-affecting views deliver different verdicts on preventing the existence of bad lives; and a deontic disagreement about whether destroying habitat harms the animals who would have existed, or merely declines to bring them into existence — a distinction that matters if harms and omitted creations have different moral weight. A fourth possibility is that the whole thing is a proxy war between welfarist and conservationist value systems, with the welfare biology serving as cover.
