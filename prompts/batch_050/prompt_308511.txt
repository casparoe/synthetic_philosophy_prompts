Okay so I've been reading Han Feizi lately and I keep thinking about the Wells Fargo fake-accounts scandal as a test case for the whole rule-by-incentives vs rule-by-virtue fight, and I want someone to actually run the analysis instead of me half-doing it in my head.

The facts as I understand them (stipulate these, and only push back if I've gotten something major wrong): up through 2016, Wells Fargo retail bankers worked under brutal cross-selling quotas, descending from an internal initiative called Going for Gr-Eight, i.e. eight financial products per household. To hit the numbers, employees opened roughly 1.5 million checking/savings accounts and over half a million credit cards without customer consent — later reviews pushed the total up toward 3.5 million. When it broke publicly in 2016 the bank paid a $185 million fine, it came out that about 5,300 mostly low-level employees had been fired over the years for the same practices, CEO John Stumpf resigned with some clawbacks, and in 2020 the bank settled with the DOJ and SEC for $3 billion. Some employees had raised concerns internally before all this and got ignored or pushed out.

The theory, briefly so we're on the same page: Han Feizi holds that most people are self-interested and there will never be enough genuinely virtuous people to staff a large organization, so the ruler's job is institutional design — clear public standards (fa) and the "two handles" of reward and punishment, calibrated so that doing your actual job is what pays. He also insists on checking performance against what's claimed, and on the law not sparing the high-ranking. The rival Confucian line: rules get gamed, so select and cultivate people of good character and lead by moral example.

What I want answered:

- On the Legalist reading, was this an incentive failure or an incentive *success*? The quotas produced exactly the behavior being measured — the behavior just happened to be fraud. Does Han Feizi have the resources to say what went wrong (bad standard? missing punishment handle for executives? no verification of claims against actuality?), or does the case show his framework can't distinguish good metrics from gameable ones?
- Suppose it's 2010 and each camp gets to redesign the bank. What's the Legalist fix, what's the Confucian fix, and which one actually prevents 2016?
- The bank's real response — thousands of front-line firings, some executive clawbacks years later — which theory does that pattern vindicate, if either?
- The other direction: plenty of employees knew it was wrong and did it under quota pressure, and the whole thing surfaced partly because some insiders objected on conscience grounds with no incentive to do so (and several got punished for it). Does that support Han Feizi (character is no protection, incentives rule teh day) or embarrass him (his system produced the fraud and virtue is what exposed it)?
