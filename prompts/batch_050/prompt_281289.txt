So I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm fact-checking a draft essay by a colleague who's writing about how hospitals and governments should allocate scarce resources -- vaccines, ICU beds, disaster relief, that kind of thing. In one section he invokes the philosopher John Taurek, and I want a second pair of eyes on whether his summary is fair before it goes to print, because I've half-read Taurek's famous paper years ago and something about the paragraph bugs me, but I cant put my finger on it.

Here's the paragraph, verbatim from his draft:

"In 1977, the philosopher John Taurek dropped a bomb on the way we think about emergencies. His argument: numbers don't count. If you can save either one stranger or five strangers, Taurek held, you have no obligation whatsoever to save the five -- saving one is every bit as good, morally speaking. His reasoning was that each person can only lose their own life; the five don't collectively suffer some super-loss five times worse than one death, because there's no group-mind that experiences it. So whoever you save, the outcome is equally bad, and the moral thing to do is to flip a coin to give everyone an equal chance. Taurek himself admitted he'd save his own friend over five strangers, which shows he knew his position was really just about personal preference, not morality. The upshot for policy: triage rules that direct resources toward saving the most lives rest on a philosophical mistake, and a government that saves the many over the few is acting no better than one that saves the few."

My editor likes the drama of it. My worry is that it makes Taurek sound like he's proved triage is *wrong*, when I vaguely remember his claim being narrower -- something about what we're required to do rather than what's better or worse, and something about the coin flip not quite being his actual prescription.

So: audit the paragraph for me. Which parts are a fair statement of what Taurek actually argued in "Should the Numbers Count?", which parts are the standard misreading that even philosophers complain about, and which parts are my colleague's own gloss dressed up as Taurek's view? In particular: does Taurek say saving the greater number is *impermissible*, or something subtler? Is the coin-flip thing his view or a reconstruction? Does the friend example undermine him the way my colleague implies, or did Taurek build that in deliberately? And does the policy upshot at the end actually follow from anything Taurek argued?

One more thing: please be careful to separate what's genuinely settled among philosophers who work on this (e.g. what Taurek said, what the standard objections are) from what's still live and contested (like whether his argument succeeds, or whether aggregation can be defended). I don't want my fact-check to swap my colleague's overstatement for an equal and opposite one.
