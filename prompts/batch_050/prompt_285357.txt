So my partner and I have been doing this thing for a few years now and I want to understand what we're actually committed to by doing it. I'm a vegetarian for animal-welfare reasons and I think the best use of my spare money is donating to animal welfare charities. My partner thinks that's mostly a waste and that global health charities (bednets, deworming, that kind of thing) do far more good per dollar. A while back we hit on an arrangement: instead of each of us donating to our own preferred charity, I donate to her global health charities and she donates an equal amount to my animal ones. The idea being that I care way more about the animal money going through than she cares about blocking it, and vice versa, so we both get more of what we morally care about than if we'd each just given to our own thing. People tell me this is called moral trade and that philosophers are into it, and honestly it felt like a clever hack at first, but the more I think about it the weirder it seems.

Here's my question: what does this practice actually presupose about morality, philosophically? Like, it seems to assume my moral reasons and hers are somehow measurable on a common scale, or at least tradeable -- that "more of what I value" plus "more of what she values" adds up to something both of us should want. Does that commit us to thinking moral value is one kind of stuff that can be aggregated across people with totally different views? Also: she's now funding something she believes is a misallocation of scarce resources, and I'm funding something I think is less good than what I'd have picked. If either of us is right that the other person's charity is actively bad (not just suboptimal), does the trade fall apart, or does it still work?

It might help to compare with cases that feel different to me. Vote trading between candidates strikes a lot of people as corrupt or at least icky, and I share the intuition but can't say why. And nobody would say a utilitarian and a Kantian should "trade" on lying -- I'll lie for you this once if you'll break a promise for me later. So where does our donation swap sit relative to those? Reconstruct what theory of morality (or of moral disagreement, or cooperation under moral uncertainty) makes our arrangement look rational and above-board, which theories would condemn it, and whether the practice is actually tied to one theory or compatible with several. If some parts of what we're doing only make sense on a view I'd reject, tell me that too.
