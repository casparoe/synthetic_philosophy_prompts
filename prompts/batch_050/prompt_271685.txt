So I'm writing a piece for our ideas vertical about pooled giving funds -- the kind where donors with totally different values (one cares about malaria, one about animal welfare, one about existential risk) throw money into one pot and let a committee allocate it. The pitch these funds make is that they can find grants that every donor would endorse as an improvement over their own preferred allocation, because of trading across uncertainty: donor A thinks AI risk is probably nothing but isn't sure, so she's happy to trade some of "her" share of the pot for more of the malaria budget, and symmetrically for donor B. Everyone walks away thinking they got a better deal than splitting the pot by contribution share.

I want to pressure-test that pitch properly before I write it up, so: write me the desiderata for a theory of when a reallocation of resources counts as a genuine improvement for all parties under moral and empirical uncertainty. Not "a theory of the good" -- specifically the weaker thing these funds are implicitly relying on: a criterion for deals that every stakeholder can accept as better by their own lights, whatever their lights happen to be.

The list should cover, at minimum, and with the case or consideration motivating each requirement:

- Which baseline the improvement is measured against (the contribution-proportional split? the status quo? what each party could get unilaterally?) and why the choice of baseline does or doesn't smuggle in a substantive fairness judgment
- Whether the criterion has to be ex ante (each party's expected value goes up given their own beliefs) or ex post (it actually goes up), and what happens to the fund's pitch when the two come apart
- How it handles a party whose values are fanatical in structure -- tiny credence in an astronomically large payoff -- since under expected value that party will rationally demand nearly the whole pot every time
- Whether it must respect distinctions like uncertainty about facts vs uncertainty about values, and whether those even come apart for real donors
- What it does with cluelessness: a party who has no well-formed probability at all over some outcomes, not just a low one
- Whether the deals it licenses have to be robust to parties updating their beliefs later, or whether buyer's remorse retroactively voids the improvement

For each one, say whether you think it's a genuine constraint any such theory must meet or a preference of one camp, and flag any subset of your desiderata that looks jointly unsatisfiable -- if the pitch these funds make requires both ex ante improvement by everyone's lights and stability against the fanaticism problem, and those two can't be had together, that's basically my lede, so don't bury it. Then, briefly: how do (a) a straight ex-ante expected-value criterion, (b) a Nash-bargaining-style solution over the parties' utility functions, and (c) a rule that just requires unanimity on rankings without any inter-party comparison, each fare against the list?

Keep the weight on the requirements themselves. I'll do the colorful magazine stuff myself.
