I am marking the final examination for my upper-level undergraduate seminar on the epistemology of knowledge, and I would like assistance with one script. The question on the paper reads as follows:

"State Timothy Williamson's argument against the luminosity of conditions such as feeling cold — his 'margin for error' argument — as precisely as you can. Define luminosity; state the margin-for-error principle; and show how the contradiction is derived. Then explain what, if anything, the argument establishes concerning the KK principle, the thesis that if one knows that p, then one knows that one knows that p."

Here is the candidate's answer, reproduced in full:

"Williamson defines a condition as luminous when, if it obtains, one knows that it obtains: luminous conditions are transparent to the subject. His target is the traditional idea that our own mental states are luminous in this sense. His argument that they are not runs as follows.

Imagine that one feels cold at dawn (call this moment α_0) and does not feel cold at noon (α_n), with one's feelings changing imperceptibly over the interval, so that consecutive moments α_i and α_{i+1} are subjectively indiscriminable. The argument's key epistemic premise is the margin-for-error principle: if at α_i one knows a proposition, then that proposition must remain true at α_{i+1}. A belief that is true at α_i but false at the indiscriminable α_{i+1} would be true only by luck, and lucky true belief is not knowledge.

Assume for reductio that the condition of feeling cold is luminous. At α_0 one feels cold, so one knows that one feels cold. By the margin-for-error principle, one still feels cold at α_1. Iterating the two steps across the whole series, one feels cold at α_n, contradicting the stipulation. So feeling cold is not luminous, and because nothing in the argument was specific to that condition, Williamson concludes that no non-trivial condition is luminous.

The argument is, in effect, a refutation of the KK principle. The iterative step works only because knowing that one feels cold carries with it knowing that one knows that one feels cold: it is the repeated application of KK at each link of the series that propagates knowledge of one's coldness from dawn to noon and thereby generates the contradiction. Williamson thus shows that one can know that p without knowing that one knows that p — as with the unconfident examinee, who produces the correct answer while doubting that she knows it.

The upshot is significant but should not be overstated. The argument does not show that we never know our own mental states; on most occasions when we feel cold we know it perfectly well. What it shows is that such knowledge is not guaranteed by the obtaining of the state itself."
