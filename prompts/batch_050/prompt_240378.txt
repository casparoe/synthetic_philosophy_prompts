I host a long-running interview podcast about how people make decisions under uncertainty. I have no academic training — I came to these questions through years of playing poker semi-seriously and then running a small business — but I read a great deal and prepare carefully for guests. My next guest is the author of a recent book defending a position she calls geometric rationality: roughly, the view that the uniquely correct norm for choosing under uncertainty is to maximize the geometric growth rate of one's wealth — the Kelly criterion elevated from a betting heuristic into a general theory of rational choice. Her core argument is asymptotic: an agent who maximizes expected log wealth will, with probability one, eventually end up richer than an agent following any essentially different strategy, and rationality just is whatever wins in the long run.

I am broadly sympathetic, but I have been turning over a handful of pressure points on my own, and I want the conversation to build through them in sequence rather than wander. Please write me eight to ten interview questions, in order. For each, give me the question itself in a form I can ask aloud, the reply you expect her to give, and the follow-up I should have ready when she gives it. Here are the pressure points I care about, which the sequence should cover:

First, the one-shot problem. The long-run argument only has force if decisions repeat, but the decision weighing on me most — whether to sell my business — will happen exactly once. If she says that life is itself a sequence of bets, I want to press her on how one is supposed to individuate the sequence: what makes two decisions instances of "the same bet," and does the answer do any work beyond being whatever makes her theorem apply?

Second, the collapse worry. Maximizing expected log wealth yields precisely the Kelly bet, so her theory looks suspiciously like ordinary expected utility theory with a log utility function stapled on. The usual defense is that log is not one taste among others, because it is the only function whose maximization tracks the time-average growth rate. But preferring the time average to the ensemble average seems itself to be a taste — the lottery player simply prefers the ensemble. I want her to explain why that preference is irrational rather than merely unusual, without quietly appealing to the long-run argument, which would be circular.

Third, the estimation problem. Every serious practitioner I have met bets fractional Kelly because they distrust their own probability estimates, and she concedes this in the book. But then the norm delivers verdicts only for agents who know the true probabilities, which none of us do. If she replies that fractional Kelly is simply what the norm outputs once parameter uncertainty is folded in, I want to press her: at that point the geometric framing seems to be doing no work at all, and we are back to plain Bayesian expected utility with extra steps.

Fourth, the pooling problem. Two agents who pool their bankrolls can face a positive pooled growth rate on bets each would be required to decline alone. So whether a bet counts as rational flips depending on one's social arrangements, which is a strange property for a theory of individual rationality. I want her to say whether the theory quietly recommends collective bankrolls, and if so, why the obligation stops at pairs rather than extending to the largest pool one can join.

Fifth, the numéraire problem. The theory instructs us to maximize the growth of wealth, but the absorbing states that genuinely frighten me are not financial — ruin in the literal sense is not losing a bankroll. If she retreats to "maximize the growth of whatever you value," the entire apparatus becomes a redescription of ordinary utility maximization, and the book's central claim evaporates. I want to make her choose between those horns on air.

Please close the sequence with a final question that forces her to show the theory has bite rather than merely hindsight: ask her to name a real decision from her own life where geometric reasoning overruled a positive-arithmetic-expectation choice she would otherwise have made — and be ready to note it if she cannot.
