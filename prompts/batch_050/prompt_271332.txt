I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and my professional instincts have been shaped less by any philosophy seminar than by years of managing sources: people who will speak on the record, people who will only talk on background, people who communicate through intermediaries, and people who, for reasons good or bad, refuse to be identified at all. My newsroom has thick conventions about which of these arrangements can ever justify publishing a claim, and about what a reader is entitled to conclude when an allegedly crucial source stays invisible.

I would like you to use that vocabulary to restate, as faithfully as you can, the philosophical argument from divine hiddenness associated with J. L. Schellenberg and the standard replies to it. In outline, the argument runs that a perfectly loving God would make his existence evident to every creature capable of a personal relationship with him, that the existence of sincere, non-resistant nonbelievers shows this evidence is not in fact available, and that the best explanation is therefore that no such God exists. Please translate each of its premises into the terms of sourcing practice: treat God as a source who supposedly wishes to be known and related to, treat belief in his existence as something like a finding a newsroom might publish, and treat the nonbeliever as the reporter or reader trying to verify the story. Then translate the main replies one by one: the free-will reply (evidence so strong it would coerce belief would destroy morally significant freedom), the soul-making reply (a world of ambiguity is better for moral development), and the reply that the evidence is available to anyone who seeks it in the right spirit. In each case, find the closest analogue in editorial practice — for instance, the rules about anonymous sourcing, about off-the-record briefings, about what 'no comment' permits a journalist to infer, or about whether a source who only speaks to the already-sympathetic can ground a public claim.

The part I care most about is the residue report. For every element of the debate, classify the translation as clean, distorted, or impossible, and explain your classification. I suspect some things will not survive: the notion of a personal relationship as the argument's central good, the distinction between resistant and non-resistant nonbelief, and the idea that a being's love could require a particular kind of evidence-giving. But I want you to test my suspicions rather than confirm them. Conclude by telling me whether whatever fails to translate points to a limitation of the journalist's vocabulary — evidence that sourcing norms were built for a narrower class of cases — or whether it instead exposes the philosophical argument as trading on notions that lose their precision when pinned down.
