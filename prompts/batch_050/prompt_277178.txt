I'm giving a twenty-minute paper at an interdisciplinary aesthetics event next month and I want to be ready for the question period, because I suspect it'll be hostile. My thesis: if two objects are perceptually indistinguishable — down to whatever descrimination the best-trained eye and ordinary connoisseurship can manage — then they carry the same aesthetic value, and the premium we put on the original is snobbery, fetishism or market logic dressed up as aesthetics. My main move is a Goodman-style one: what feels like a perceptual difference between original and copy is usually just knowledge about origin leaking in, and stripped of that knowledge the supposed difference evaporates. I also take a swing at the idea that van Meegeren's Vermeers got uglier the day they were exposed.

The awkward part is the audience: roughly half art historians and curators, a quarter conservation scientists, a quarter philosophers of art. Prepare me for the Q&A. Give me the objections I'll actually face, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it does, phrased the way each constituency would actually phrase it — the curators will come at me with authenticity and provenance talk, the conservators with material history and what analysis under the surface shows, the philosophers with Dutton and the performance-view of artworks. For each objection: a two-or-three sentence reply, a note on where that reply is weakest, and what I should just concede upfront rather than fight. And flag the one question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks.
