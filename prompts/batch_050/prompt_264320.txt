"In Fear and Trembling (1843), what does the pseudonymous author Johannes de silentio mean by a teleological suspension of the ethical? Explain how Abraham's willingness to sacrifice Isaac is supposed to differ from Agamemnon's sacrifice of Iphigenia, and explain the difference between the knight of infinite resignation and the knight of faith."

And here's the student's answer:

"In Fear and Trembling Kierkegaard introduces the idea of a teleological suspension of the ethical. The ethical is the universal, meaning the sphere of duties and norms that apply to everyone equally, which is why de silentio more or less equates it with Hegelian Sittlichkeit. Normally the highest a human being can do is express their life in the universal. Abraham's case is paradoxical because a father's duty is to love his son, and from the ethical standpoint killing Isaac is simply murder. But God commands it, so Abraham relates absolutely to the absolute (God) and only relatively to the universal. The suspension is 'teleological' because the ethical is suspended for the sake of a higher end, the absolute relation to God, rather than abolished -- although while the suspension lasts, the ethical simply does not apply to Abraham at all.

This is what separates Abraham from Agamemnon. Agamemnon also sacrifices his child for a higher purpose, but that purpose -- the good of the Greek army and the state -- is itself located within the universal, so his deed remains ethically intelligible and he is a tragic hero whom we can weep for and understand. Abraham has no justification available in universal terms, which is why he can neither be understood nor spoken about in the language of ethics.

Finally, the knight of infinite resignation makes the movement of giving up what is dearest to him in the finite world -- he renounces Isaac (or in the other example de silentio gives, the princess) and reconciles himself to the loss for all eternity. The knight of faith makes this same movement but then a second one: by virtue of the absurd, he believes he will get Isaac back anyway, as a reward for his obedience, even though by all human calculation he shouldn't. This double movement is faith, and de silentio admits he himself can only make the first movement and not the second. This shows that faith is the highest passion a human being can have, and that Kierkegaard endorses a divine command theory of ethics, where whatever God commands is automatically right."
