I'm trying to work out when compromising with someone whose values conflict with mine is actually good — a genuine gain from moral trade — and when it's just both of us doing something wrong. Here are my gut verdicts on some cases.

1. My neighbor and I each planned to donate $1,000: I to an anti-malaria charity she considers a distraction from local needs, she to a local animal shelter I consider low-priority. We agree to each give $500 to both. This seems fine, maybe even admirable.

2. A stranger at a town meeting proposes: I'll vote for your library funding measure if you vote for my parking ordinance. Neither of us thinks the other's measure is bad, just not worth our limited political attention. We shake on it. Seems fine.

3. A legislator votes for a bill she believes is mildly unjust — say, an unnecessary subsidy — in exchange for a colleague's vote on a bill she believes prevents serious harm. This seems fine; it's just politics.

4. Two judges in different jurisdictions agree: I'll convict this probably-innocent defendant if you convict yours, since each of us cares more about convicting our own defendant than acquitting the other's. This seems obviously appalling, and yet structurally it looks like case 2 and case 3: each party does something they judge wrong in exchange for the other doing likewise, and both prefer the package.

5. A couple disagrees about how religiously to raise their child — one wants full religious schooling, the other none. Compromise A: half the child's education in each mode. Compromise B: a uniform "moderately religious" upbringing throughout, which both regard as a muddled worst-of-both-worlds. I have the intuition that A is a genuine compromise and B is not, even though B is in some sense the same midpoint — but I'm not confident in this.

6. An employer believes, on what she takes to be moral grounds, that employees shouldn't discuss salaries. An employee believes transparency is a matter of justice. They agree that salaries will be posted for new hires only. I find myself wanting to say this is not a compromise between values at all but a mere strategic standoff, and I can't articulate why it differs from case 1.

7. I resolve to indulge a value my future self will hold but I currently reject — eating meat now because I predict I'll later think vegetarianism was wrong, say. This strikes me as nothing like the two-person cases at all, despite the superficial similarity of "conceding something to an opposing evaluative standpoint in exchange for a concession."

Is there a principle — about what makes some of these genuine mutually beneficial moral compromises and others mere coordinated wrongdoing or non-compromises — that captures most of these verdicts? I'd like it stated precisely enough to see which cases motivate which of its clauses. Please also say what it implies about case 5 (where I waver), whether it must treat cases 2 and 4 differently and by what feature, and which of my verdicts, if any, a cleaner and simpler principle would have to overturn — I'd rather know the cost of consistency than be told everything fits.
