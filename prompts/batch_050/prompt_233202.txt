You are a scholar of Renaissance political thought with particular expertise in Machiavelli. Think carefully, and begin with a short brainstorm of candidate scenarios before committing to one.

In the Discourses on Livy (Book III, Chapter 9), Machiavelli argues, in effect, that a man prospers when his natural mode of proceeding matches the conditions of his time, and fails when the two diverge: Fabius Maximus's caution suited Rome while Hannibal was at full strength, Scipio's impetuosity suited the changed circumstances that followed, and neither could have traded temperaments, because men cannot act against what nature inclines them toward, nor abandon a path along which they have long been successful. This sits uneasily with the famous advice in The Prince, Chapter 25, that it is better to be impetuous than cautious.

Construct a thought experiment meeting all of these specifications:

- Two leaders of equal virtù — equal skill, courage, and judgment — one cautious by temperament, one impetuous, placed in circumstances such that the cautious leader succeeds and the impetuous one fails.
- The impetuous leader's failure must not stem from bad luck in the crude sense (an accident, an illness, an unforeseeable misfortune); it must fall out of the mismatch between his mode of proceeding and the character of the times, in Machiavelli's sense of that mismatch.
- Neither leader may change character mid-scenario, and the case should dramatize why Machiavelli thinks they could not.
- Realistic setting — electoral politics, military command, or corporate leadership, not a fable — and the scenario itself confined to a single paragraph.

After the scenario: (1) state what the framework of the Discourses chapter predicts about each leader, and why; (2) say whether your case refutes the Chapter 25 advice, or instead exposes a condition on it that Machiavelli himself acknowledges; (3) identify the single feature of your scenario that is doing the work, and explain what happens to the case if that feature is removed; and (4) sketch how a defender of the impetuous leader — one who insists he displayed no less virtù than his rival — would attack the construction, and whether that attack succeeds.
