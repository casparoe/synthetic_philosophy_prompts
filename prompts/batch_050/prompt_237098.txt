Draft: "Why a threshold, not a lexicon"

Negative utilitarianism in its naive form says: minimize suffering, full stop. Everyone knows the two knock-down moves against it. First, the pinprick argument: if suffering is all that matters lexically, then any world with even one tiny headache is worse than a world full of ecstatic joy, so the view implies absurd tradeoffs. Second, the world-destruction argument: the best outcome is no sentient beings at all, so the view seems to favor painless omnicide.

My essay argues that a lexical threshold version escapes both. The idea: suffering below a certain intensity threshold is commensurable with pleasure -- an hour of mild discomfort can be outweighed by enough joy -- but suffering above the threshold (think torture, the worst stages of certain illnesses) is lexically prior: no amount of pleasure, however large or however widely distributed, compensates for even a moment of it. This blocks the pinprick argument (small pains are tradeable) and softens the world-destruction argument (since most of life is sub-threshold, creating happy lives isn't worthless).

Then my main section, which is the part I actually care about: I argue the threshold view doesn't eliminate the commensurability question, it relocates it. The classic objection to negative utilitarianism is that pleasure and pain are phenomenologically incommensurable -- there's no common unit, so talk of "outweighing" is confused. But the threshold view still needs sub-threshold pains to be comparable to each other and to pleasures, and more interestingly, it needs a principled answer to: what determines where the threshold sits? I propose the threshold should be defined functionally rather than phenomenologically -- roughly, pain counts as above-threshold when it commandeers the whole of attention and makes tradeoffs from the victim's own perspective impossible, i.e. when the sufferer would not rationally accept it for any compensation. This, I argue, makes the threshold non-arbitrary without needing a hedonistic exchange rate. I also introduce a distinction I haven't seen elsewhere between "threshold as cliff" (pain jumps from tradeable to untradeable at a single intensity point) and "threshold as fog" (a grey zone where pain is partially tradeable with steeply worsening exchange rates), and I argue the fog version is psychologically more realistic but risks collapsing back into plain commensurability under iterated tradeoffs -- many fog-zone pains, each "barely" outweighed, could stack into something no one would accept. I call this the compounding problem and it's where the draft currently ends, with me admitting I don't have a solution.
