I spent forty years as a population geneticist, and since retiring I have been reading moral and political philosophy in a self-directed way, with more enthusiasm than discipline. I have also, in retirement, acquired a small collection of committee seats -- a research foundation's grant panel, a college governing body -- and it is there that I first noticed an argumentative pattern that I now seem to see everywhere in policy writing. I would like to know whether it has an established name, and, more importantly, exactly what it is and is not.

The pattern, abstractly: a decision is defended on the ground that everyone concerned is at least as well off under it as they would otherwise have been, and some are better off -- yet the set of people who count as "concerned" is itself fixed by the decision, so that the unanimity is, in a sense, manufactured rather than discovered.

Two instances. First, our foundation moved funding from investigator-led basic grants toward mission-directed programmes. At the five-year review, every group in the portfolio was doing at least as well as it would have done with no funding, and most better; the report described the reform as an improvement for all concerned, with no losers. But under the old scheme the portfolio would have contained partly different groups. Several laboratories that would have flourished were simply never constituted; their prospective members went to industry. The claim that nobody was made worse off is true only because the people with grounds for complaint were, by the decision itself, never assembled into the constituency that the review polled.

Second, a college changed its admissions policy and later justified the change by surveying enrolled students, all of whom had benefited from or been unaffected by it. The students who would have been admitted under the old policy and thrived were not students at all, and so appeared in no survey.

Now, what this is not -- or so I believe, and I would like to be corrected. It is not, as I understand it, the non-identity problem, which I take to be the puzzle of whether an outcome can be wrong when the people it disadvantages owe their existence to the choice. In my cases the outcomes may be perfectly good; what seems to me defective is the *justification* offered, which would be available for good and bad reforms alike. Nor does it seem to be ordinary consultation bias, since the exclusion is not sloppy sampling but is produced by the decision under review. I am used to distrusting analyses conditioned on post-treatment selection -- judging a therapy by its survivors -- and I would like to know whether this is merely survivorship bias in institutional dress or whether there is a distinctively normative residue once one asks whose welfare the comparison ought to count.

My questions: Does this pattern of justification have a name or a literature of its own, distinct from the evaluative puzzles about identity-affecting choices? How does it stand to the economist's notion of an improvement that leaves no one worse off, and to what I dimly recall seeing called a "safe" such improvement -- one endorsed from every plausible evaluative standpoint? Does the endogeneity of the constituency undermine that safety, or does the worry depend on holding a person-affecting view that an impersonal evaluation would dissolve? And what are the boundary cases: does the pattern persist when the constituency changes in size but not in identity, or when the would-be losers exist under both options but possess standing to be consulted under only one? If nothing established fits, I would rather have a well-carved definition, distinguished from the neighbouring concepts, than a bare label.
