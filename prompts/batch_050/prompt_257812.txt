i've gotten a bit obsessed with the tetralemma lately — the madhyamaka habit of taking some claim and running it through all four corners: it is, it is not, it both is and is not, it neither is nor is not — and then refusing the whole set. i want a poem that actually performs this instead of just being about it.

here's the setup: someone finds a childhood photo of themself and asks, in effect, am i the same person as that kid? give me four stanzas, one per lemma, and each stanza should genuinely inhabit its position from the inside rather than describing it from outside. stanza one argues continuity (the scar on the knee that's still there, the same stubborn set of the jaw). stanza two argues difference (the cells all turned over, the memories mostly gone or rebuilt so many times theyre more like paintings of memories). stanza three takes both at once without collapsing into either. stanza four takes neither, and it should feel genuinely vertiginous, not like a cop-out. then a short coda — a couplet or a single line — that refuses all four horns the way nagarjuna does: not a fifth synthesis, not "it's a mystery," but the recognition that the question was leaning on a self that was never findable in the first place. the photo keeps asking and there's no one the question is about.

the formal constraint, which is the whole point: one refrain line that recurs in each of the four stanzas, bent each time to fit its lemma, so the same words take on a different valence under each corner. the coda must drop the refrain entirely. the silence where the refrain should be is the payload — the form itself should go quiet at exactly the moment the positions run out, so the structure enacts the exhaustion of the four views rather than announcing it.

someone who knows the tetralemma should be able to tell which stanza is which horn without any labels, and someone who's never heard of madhyamaka should still read it as a good poem about an old photograph. somewhere in the 20-28 line range, no fixed form otherwise. and please dont title it anything like "the four corners of the self" — give it a title as plain as the photo.
