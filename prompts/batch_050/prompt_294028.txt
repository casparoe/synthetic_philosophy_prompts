The criminal law of many jurisdictions recognizes a defense of duress: the clerk who opens the vault at gunpoint, or the driver coerced into carrying contraband across a border, is excused on the ground that she could not fairly be expected to have acted otherwise. Yet the standard courts apply is not whether this defendant, with her particular fears and history, could have resisted; it is whether a person of ordinary firmness in her situation would have yielded. Meanwhile, the same legal order convicts people of criminal attempts whose success was impossible from the start — the would-be murderer who fires into a corpse he believes to be a sleeping man. What conception of responsibility does this cluster of practices presuppose about the relation between blame and alternative possibilities? Does the duress defense, as actually structured, presuppose the principle that responsibility requires alternatives, or does the ordinary-firmness standard already replace that principle with something else — a norm of reasonable resistance, or a test of what the agent acted for? If Frankfurt-style cases are sound, do they leave the defense intact, force its re-grounding, or abolish it? And is the combination of excusing the coerced while punishing the impossible attempter coherent under a single theory, or does the law presuppose two different views of what excuses and what incriminates? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with.
