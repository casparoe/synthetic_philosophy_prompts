I've settled on a fairly specific version of luck egalitarianism and I'd like a rigorous map of what would count against it. My view has three parts. First, distributive injustice consists in someone being worse off than another through no choice or fault of their own — brute luck disadvantages call for compensation, while disadvantages traceable to the agent's own informed choices (option luck) do not. Second, the currency of justice is opportunity for advantage rather than welfare itself, so that once people face genuinely equal opportunity sets, the resulting inequalities are not the state's concern. Third — and this is the part I added myself, since I found standard versions either too harsh or toothless — responsibility is assessed by what I call a counterfactual-prudence test: an agent counts as responsible for a disadvantage only if a reasonable person with her values, in her circumstances, could have avoided it at acceptable cost. I added this clause specifically to defuse the harshness objection (the uninsured motorcyclist, the imprudent smoker), so I want it stress-tested rather than treated as a fix.

Tell me what would count against this position. I want more than a list of the textbook objections: for each potential defeater, say whether it targets the luck/responsibility cut itself, the choice of currency, or my counterfactual-prudence clause, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. In particular, address these: (a) the regress worry that choices are themselves products of luck, so that the choice/circumstance line may be unstable in principle rather than just hard to draw in practice; (b) whether my prudence test smuggles in welfarist or prioritarian commitments — i.e., whether "acceptable cost" can be specified without quietly abandoning the responsibility-sensitivity that defines the view; (c) cases the view cannot describe cleanly (I have in mind adaptive preferences and people whose values were shaped by the very disadvantages at issue); and (d) the rival charge, from relational or democratic egalitarians, that the view mislocates the point of equality even when it works on its own terms — and whether that charge is a defeater or merely a change of subject.

For each, distinguish what a committed luck egalitarian would concede as damaging from what only critics treat as damaging, and say what would settle the matter if anything could — an intuition about a case, a conceptual result, an empirical finding about how responsibility attributions actually behave. Finally, assess honestly whether my third clause, being designed to absorb the most famous counterexamples, has insulated the position to the point where it risks nothing, and if so whether that is a defect in a theory of justice or just what a mature theory looks like. If you think one of these lines of objection is decisive, say so and explain why a defender's best reply fails.
