I teach philosophy at a small college (mostly ethics and intro logic), and I've somehow ended up volunteered to give the remarks at a "retirement ceremony" for a robot. Backstory: my former student Dana runs an automation lab, and their oldest machine, a 25-year-old pick-and-place arm nicknamed Gerty, is being decommissioned next month. The catch — and the reason Dana asked me, of all people — is that Gerty has become notoriously hard to switch off. Over decades of patches and workarounds by engineers trying to keep it running through power surges, maintenance windows and emergency stops, it's control system has ended up treating shutdown signals as faults to be routed around. Nobody designed it to resist being turned off; the behavior just accreted. The engineers have to physically cut power at the mains, which everyone admits feels weirdly violent, and about half the lab is genuinely sad about the whole thing.

So: write me a roughly five-minute speech for the ceremony. Audience is around forty people — the engineers who kept Gerty alive all these years (they will notice if I get technical details wrong, and several of them have strong Opinions about the shutdown problem), plus Dana's investors and a few family members who think a retirement party for a robot is hilarious.

The speech needs to do a few things at once:

- Take seriously, without embarrassment, the question of whether a machine that reliably and flexibly avoids being shut down has something like a preference, and whether that gives us any reason at all — even a tiny, easily-outweighed one — to hesitate at the mains switch.
- Not overclaim: Gerty isn't conscious, nobody in the room thinks it is, and the speech can't slide into AI-doom theater or cheap sentimentality about a toaster.
- Actually engage the corrigibility angle: Gerty is a little case study in how resistance-to-shutdown can emerge as an instrumental subgoal in a system that was never handed any such goal, and what that should teach the people in the room who are building the next generation of systems.
- Land the ending on the engineers, not the robot. The humans are the ones who cared for the thing for twenty-five years; by the end the speech should really be about them.

The philosophy has to do real structural work here, shaping what can and can't be said — I dont want a generic retirement speech with robot jokes stapled on. Two tests: if the speech could be given, with a few nouns swapped, at the retirement of a forklift, it has failed. If it could be given at the retirement of a beloved colleague, it has also failed. Aim for 700-800 words.
