I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a column about psychopathy and criminal responsibility. Before I commit to a line I want to check whether my own working assumptions actually hang together. Roughly where I've landed:

(1) I'm sympathetic to sentimentalism about moral judgment: to genuinely judge that something is wrong -- as opposed to just knowing it's classified as wrong -- you have to feel something, some aversion or guilt-potential. Purely intellectual sorting doesn't count as moral judgment.

(2) Psychopaths know right from wrong. Their manipulativeness seems to prove it: you can't push people's moral buttons without knowing where the buttons are.

(3) I take seriously the studies where psychopaths blur the moral/conventional distinction -- e.g. saying that if the authority figure gave permission, hitting another kid would be fine, treating "don't hit" more like "don't wear pyjamas to school."

(4) Blame and punishment are only fair when the person knew what they were doing was wrong. Not knowing it was wrong excuses, at least partly.

(5) Psychopathic offenders should get roughly normal sentences, not excuses or diminished responsiblity.

So: is this a coherent package? Go through it and tell me which commitments pull against each other, ordered by how serious the tension is, and for each one say what I'd have to give up to fix it. Also flag any pair that *looks* tense but actually isn't once you think about it. Don't just tell me it's complicated -- I want to know which single plank is cheapest to drop and what dropping it would cost the column.
