Exam question (answer in roughly 400 words): "Explain the role of the veil of ignorance in Rawls's argument for his two principles of justice. Why does Rawls think parties in the original position would reject utilitarianism, and what does the maximin rule of choice have to do with this?"

Student answer:

"Rawls's argument begins from the original position, in which rational parties choose principles of justice behind a veil of ignorance. The veil strips the parties of all knowledge: they do not know their class, talents, sex, conception of the good, or indeed anything at all about the society they will live in, since such knowledge could bias the choice toward one group or another. The point of this device is to model impartiality: because no one can tailor principles to their own advantage, whatever is chosen is fair by construction.

Because the parties know nothing, they cannot attach probabilities to the positions they might end up occupying, and the choice determines the whole course of their lives. Rawls therefore holds that rational choice under these conditions is governed by the maximin rule: one should choose as if one were certain to end up in the worst position and maximize the minimum outcome. Rawls grounds this rule in the parties' risk aversion: no rational person facing so momentous a choice would gamble on ending up well off. Maximin, he argues, leads directly to his two principles.

The first principle guarantees equal basic liberties for all. The second principle permits social and economic inequalities only where they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged — the lowest income group — and attaches offices to fair equality of opportunity. Of these two components of the second principle, the difference principle takes priority: inequalities must first be arranged to benefit the worst off, and equality of opportunity operates within that constraint.

It is here that utilitarianism is ruled out. Average utilitarianism would allow the position of the least advantaged to be sacrificed whenever doing so raised the average, so it fails to take seriously the distinction between persons. A party reasoning by maximin would never accept such a principle, since it might turn out to be among those sacrificed, and the strains of commitment would in any case make the choice unstable: no one who later found themselves badly off could sincerely affirm principles they had agreed to only on a probabilistic bet. Equal basic liberties likewise could not be traded away for economic gains, as utilitarianism in principle permits."
