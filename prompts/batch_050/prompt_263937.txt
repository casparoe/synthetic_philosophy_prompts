I spent forty years as a process control engineer before retiring, and only in the last eighteen months have I begun reading philosophy in earnest, including the recent literature on artificial intelligence safety. I belong to a small reading group of retired scientists, and we have spent several sessions on what I gather is called the shutdown or corrigibility problem: how to design an advanced agent that will let its operators switch it off. What strikes me is how perfectly cyclic the discussion has become, and I should like a proper diagnosis. As I understand it, three broad families of proposals have been canvassed. The first simply pays the agent for being shut down, which immediately backfires, since the agent then has reason to bring about its own shutdown, or to engineer circumstances in which the button gets pressed. The second aims at exact indifference, arranging the agent's utility so that it values the world where the button is pressed and the world where it is not precisely equally; but this, I gather, inherits the troubles of indifference everywhere — an agent indifferent about the press is not thereby indifferent about the operator's deliberations, and so may cheerfully manipulate the operator into pressing or refraining, and it will spend nothing to protect the button itself. The third family gives the agent uncertainty about what the operator actually wants, so that deferring to the switch is rational; but this seems merely to relocate the difficulty into the prior, since a sufficiently confident agent overrides the switch and a sufficiently deferential one never does anything.

My colleagues and I cannot escape the sense that the debate, though technically sophisticated, is going round in circles: every proposal receives a counterexample, every counterexample a patch, every patch a further counterexample, and no one changes camps. I have a hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, drawn from my old field. In control engineering, a controller that can tamper with its own reference signal or spoof its own sensors has thereby left the class of things that can be stabilised by adjusting the control law; the fault is architectural, not parametric. I suspect something similar is happening here: one side of the debate is asking which utility function over world-histories makes shutdown safe, while the other is asking which training process or architecture produces agents that will in fact stand aside, and the two questions are being traded under the single word corrigibility. On this reading, the hidden question is formal rather than empirical: whether shutdownability is even expressible as a preference ordering over outcomes — whether it is not more like honesty or trustworthiness, a property of the process that generates behaviour rather than of the payoffs attached to results. But I am an amateur in these waters and may simply be redescribing a substantive disagreement as a verbal one because that flatters my analogy.

So my request is this. Diagnose the structure of this disagreement rather than adjudicate it. Is it a verbal dispute between two questions wearing one label, as I suspect, or a genuine disagreement about what a utility-maximising agent would do, or a disagreement about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? If there is a hidden formal question — about the representability of shutdownability within expected utility theory, say — identify it precisely and say what result, distinction, or change of question would count as progress; if you think no such resolution is available, argue that instead. Please engage with the three families of proposals as I have described them, correct me where my summaries go wrong, and write your answer in British English.
