Suppose that David Hume were transported to the present day and asked to adjudicate the Sleeping Beauty problem. The setup is standard: Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday, and a fair coin is flipped. She is awakened on Monday regardless of the outcome; if the coin lands tails, she is awakened once more on Tuesday with her memory of the Monday awakening erased. Each time she awakens, she is asked for her credence that the coin landed heads. Halfers say one-half; thirders say one-third.

Now consider a variant designed to engage Hume's own philosophy more directly. In the variant, Beauty is told in advance that once the experiment concludes, her memory of every awakening will be permanently erased, so that no future stage of her will ever inherit, recall, or resemble the experiences of Monday or Tuesday.

First, argue for which position Hume would take in the original problem, drawing on his conception of probability as degrees of belief proportioned to experience, his skepticism about necessary connections, and his general method of resolving disputes by consulting what impressions and ideas are actually available to the mind.

Second, examine whether Hume's bundle theory of the self gives him resources to dissolve the halfer-thirder dispute rather than settle it. Hume held that the self is nothing over and above a succession of perceptions linked by resemblance and causation, and that our sense of a persisting, unified subject is a fiction imposed on this flux. Does the thirder's reasoning, which treats each awakening as a distinct epistemic predicament, sit more naturally with this picture than the halfer's? Or does the bundle view undermine the very intelligibility of asking what "I" should believe "now," when the "I" in question is merely one awakening-stage among several indistinguishable ones?

Third, assess the memory-erasure variant. If Hume takes the connections of resemblance and causation among perceptions to constitute personal identity, does he have any reason to treat awakenings that no later perception will ever recall as epistemically significant? Should Beauty's credence upon awakening change if she knows her experiences will leave no trace in any subsequent self?

Conclude by identifying one respect in which Hume's framework resists the standard framing of the Sleeping Beauty problem, and evaluate whether this counts as an insight on his part or a limitation of his philosophy.
