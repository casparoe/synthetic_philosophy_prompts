Several times in the past year I have heard "the brain is a prediction machine" used to settle an argument, and I have started to suspect it settles different arguments in different mouths. The two occasions that stick with me: first, a machine learning colleague argued that since brains predict, predictive architectures in AI are not merely engineering conveniences but literally what cortex is doing, so neuroscience has nothing further to teach AI; second, in a philosophy of mind seminar the same slogan was deployed to conclude that perception is a kind of controlled hallucination, that we never encounter the world as it is, and so naive realism is simply dead. In both cases the slogan ended the discussion, which is precisely what made me suspicious of it.

Please treat the slogan as a piece of language to be disambiguated rather than as a theory to be explained. Lay out, as an annotated list I can take away, the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, ordered roughly from the blandest to the most radical. For each: state the thesis precisely enough that one could say what would count against it; give a verdict -- near-platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive but genuinely contested, or overreach -- and indicate which of my two disputants, if either, could actually extract their conclusion from that reading. The candidate readings I have in mind include at least the following: brains exploit statistical regularities and prior expectations in perception; top-down signals carry predictions while bottom-up signals carry only residual errors; perception is inference under an internal generative model; action, not only perception, serves to make predictions come true; everything the brain does minimizes a single underlying quantity; and the strongest claim, that there is nothing to cognition beyond prediction at all. Feel free to split or refine these if my taxonomy is off.

Then answer the two further questions that actually matter to me. First, which reading are people who deploy this slogan to end arguments most plausibly trading on, and is there a recurring pattern of equivocation -- establishing the bland reading and spending the radical one? Second, on your own assessment, which readings survive contact with the empirical and theoretical criticisms you are aware of, and which readings would my two disputants have to retreat to in order to keep something defensible, even if that retreat costs them their original conclusion?

Finally, an epistemic request: for each of your verdicts on the contested readings, say explicitly what would change your mind -- which experimental result, theoretical argument, or conceptual clarification would push you to a different verdict -- and identify the step in your own reasoning where you have the least confidence.
