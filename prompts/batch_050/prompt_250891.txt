I'm a novelist, and I've gotten myself tangled up in the metaphysics of my own cast list. While revising a mystery novel, I caught myself saying both that my detective, Mara Venn, doesn't exist and that Mara hates hospitals because her brother died in one. That sounds perfectly normal when I'm talking to other writers, but philosophically it seems bizarre: if Mara doesn't exist, how can I be saying something true about her rather than making sounds about nothing? The standard Sherlock Holmes version of the problem is probably clearer. Does the name Sherlock Holmes refer to anything? If not, in what sense can it be true that Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street but false that he is Queen Victoria's eldest son? If it does refer, what exactly is the object: an abstract character, a cluster of descriptions, a social artifact created by Conan Doyle, or something else?

Write an essay on this controversy and take a definite position. Don't just survey options like fictionalism, possibilism, Meinongianism, and abstract-artifact theories; explain which one best handles the ordinary distinction between truth inside a story and truth outside it. I especially want you to grapple with three awkward cases: sentences where a story is silent, such as whether Holmes had a favorite childhood song; sentences generated by authorial mistakes or contradictions, such as inconsistent details across drafts; and sentences comparing fiction with reality, such as saying that Holmes is more famous than any actual Victorian detective. You may introduce useful distinctions of your own, but don't let the essay become a terminological maze or a reading list.

My provisional thought is that authors don't create spooky people, but neither are fictional statements merely pretend noise: we create durable public objects, roughly like games or institutions, whose properties are partly fixed by texts and partly by interpretive practices. But I'm not confident this can explain negative truths, omissions, or references made by readers who falsely think Holmes was real, so feel free to demolish it if nessesary. Lead with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
