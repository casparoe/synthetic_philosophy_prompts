I’m a PhD student in CS working near formal methods and AI safety, and I’ve been banging my head against an argument in my dissertaion that seems to prove way too much. It’s about the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents. I’ll state it a bit more carefully than I normally would at a whiteboard.

Suppose an agent A uses some formal system F to reason about its actions. A is *cautious* in this sense: before it takes an action a, it must derive Safe(a) in F. Now suppose A is designing a successor B that uses the same architecture, the same proof rules, and the same safety predicate. A wants a guarantee that B will not perform an unsafe action merely because B has produced some internally accepted safety proof.

Here’s the argument:

1. A should not delegate open-ended authority to B unless A is justified in believing that B’s accepting a safety proof is evidence of a very strong kind that the action really is safe.

2. Since B is meant to operate autonomously in novel situations, ordinary empirical testing is not enough. A needs a general guarantee, not just evidence that B behaved safely in the cases we happened to test.

3. For a self-similar B, the relevant guarantee has to include some version of the reflection schema: if B can prove Safe(a), then Safe(a). More generally, B’s proofs about the relevant domain must be sound.

4. But if A can formally prove this reflection principle for B, and B is sufficiently similar to A and strong enough to represent its own provability predicate, then the proof can be internalized in B. So B would effectively be able to prove its own soundness.

5. By Löb’s theorem, no consistent formal system strong enough for the relevant amount of arithmetic can prove its own full soundness or reflection schema.

6. Therefore A cannot justify creating B unless at least one of the following happens:
   - B is made proof-theoretically weaker than A;
   - B is prevented from reasoning about its own provability;
   - B’s safety claims are restricted to a narrow fragment; or
   - A relies on empirical, statistical, or otherwise non-deductive grounds instead of a genuine guarantee.

7. The first three options produce a successor less capable or less generally trustworthy than its designer. The fourth option does not really answer the worry, because empirical evidence gathered under the old distribution may fail exactly when B encounters new capabilities or new environments.

8. Iterating this over generations of self-improvement, either each generation must lose expressive power, autonomy, or scope of reliable safety reasoning, or else the chain of guarantees must eventually be replaced by mere confidence.

9. So recursive self-improvement faces a dilemma: either capability converges downward toward a weak and tightly restricted agent, or justified safety guarantees disappear. Hence a highly capable, aligned, recursively self-improving agent is impossible in principle, not merely hard to build.

That conclusion seems nuts. Taken literally, it looks like it would also undermine lots of mundane cases: a verified compiler generating another verifier, a mathematician trusting a proof assistant they helped build, or even a researcher training a junior collaborator whose basic norms of reasoning match theirs. Surely Löb’s theorem doesn’t show that nobody can ever trust anyone cognitively similar to themselves.

Where exactly does the argument go wrong, if it does? I’m especially interested in whether there is a subtle shift between A proving something about B and B proving the analogous thing about itself, or between full reflection and the narrower reflection needed for safety. I’d prefer to keep premises 1 and 5 if possible: I don’t want the answer to be either “just vibe that the successor is safe” or “deny Löb’s theorem.” Please identify the weakest step, explain why it initially looks compelling, and say what it costs to reject it. If the argument can be repaired into a weaker but still serious theorem about self-trust, I’d like to know what that theorem is.
