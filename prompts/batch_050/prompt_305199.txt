Alright, so here's my situation: I spent fourty years as an experimental physicist (spectroscopy, mostly) and since retiring I've gotten really into philosophy, and I keep banging my head against the question of how we can compare wellbeing *across people*. When a hospital board weighs giving one patient a costly treatment against giving ten others cheaper ones, or when policymakers trade off one group's suffering against another's gain, everyone just talks as if units of welfare can be added up between heads, and nobody seems bothered by it the way I'd be bothered if someone added a Fahrenheit reading to a Celsius one without conversion. I've read a bit about the old ordinalist skepticism (Robbins and that crowd) and about Harsanyi-style thought experiments where you imagine not knowing who you'll be, but I don't want to pick a side yet. First I want the spec.

So: act as a philosopher of economics with a strong background in measurement theory, and think this through carefully -- maybe brainstorm a few candidate requirements to yourself before settling on the final list. Write the desiderata that any adequate account (or method, or theory) of interpersonal comparisons of wellbeing must meet. For each requirement: state it, give the case or consideration that motivates it (e.g. the fact that some comparisons seem obviously meaningful even to skeptics, like a death versus a stubbed toe; the problem of expensive tastes; adaptive preferences where someone lowers their sights to cope; whether the account has to work with only ordinal information or gets to assume cardinal utilities; whether it must survive being plugged into a social welfare function without generating nonsense; cross-species comparisons if you think those matter). Then tell me which requirements you take to be non-negotiable versus which ones smell like the prejudice of a particular camp, and -- this is the part I care most about -- whether some subset of the desiderata looks jointly unsatisfiable, the way Bell-type results show certain intuitive demands on a physical theory can't all hold at once. If the impossibility is the real finding, say so plainly.

Briefly at the end, score the main contenders against the list (ordinalist skepticism, extended-sympathy/impartial-observer approaches, revealed-preference or willingness-to-pay methods, capability-type approaches), but keep this short; the criteria are the deliverable, not the horse race.

Format: lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- i.e., your overall verdict on wether a workable theory of interpersonal comparison is even possible and what the single hardest requirement is -- then go straight into the list, no preamble, no restating my question back at me. A table for the scorecard at the end is fine.
