I'm putting together a two-year research group of five philosophy PhD students, all with solid formal training in proof theory and modal logic but no deep set theory beyond the basics. Our focus is the paradoxes that appear not to rely on direct self-reference or on negation: Curry's paradox, which generates triviality from the truth schema plus a seemingly harmless conditional, and Yablo's infinite liar sequence. We've deliberately set the plain liar aside, partly because it is overworked and partly because we suspect the diagnosis of paradox changes once negation and self-reference drop out. I need to fix the agenda now, and what I want from you is a defended ordering, not a survey.

Here is my current map of the live questions. Push back on it if you think it is wrong or incomplete:

1. Whether Yablo's sequence genuinely avoids circularity. One line of objection (associated with Priest) holds that the sequence is circular in disguise, because each sentence quantifies uniformly over all the later ones, so the construction rests on a kind of infinitary fixed point; a counter-line points to the fact that the sequence is satisfiable yet omega-inconsistent, suggesting it is a structurally different phenomenon from the liar. The real question is whether this dispute is substantive or merely a fight over the word circular, and what actually hangs on it for a general theory of paradox.

2. What Curry shows about where a solution must locate the fault. Because Curry needs no negation, views that blame explosion or the behavior of negation (paraconsistent and dialetheist approaches) seem to misdiagnose the problem. The open issue is whether every adequate response must restrict either contraction or the detachment behavior of the conditional used in the truth schema, and which restriction exacts the lower cost.

3. The validity-Curry problem: the argument that an analogous paradox can be built for a logic's own validity predicate, so that even non-contractive logics are threatened once they try to state their own consequence relation internally. Whether this blocks structural-rule solutions wholesale or merely forces careful formulations of them.

4. The structural kinship between Curry and Löb's theorem in provability logic, and whether the modal analysis yields a general lesson about which "paradoxical" reasoning is really just self-application of an operator under the wrong assumptions.

Which three of these (or others I have missed) should anchor the two years, and in what order? Defend the ranking explicitly against these criteria: how many of the other questions are downstream of a given one; tractability for people with proof-theoretic tools rather than heavy model theory; neglectedness relative to the liar literature, where the marginal student adds least; and what specific result or development in the next few years would move each item up or down the list. I'd rather have a ranking I can argue with than a hedged overview.
