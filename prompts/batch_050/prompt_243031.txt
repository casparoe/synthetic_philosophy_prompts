Two friends of mine (neither is a philosopher; one is an SRE) got into an arguement over whether self-deception is even possible. The example was an engineer who sees one scary-looking production error, decides the monitor must be flaky, doesn’t open the logs, and later sincerely says he believed the service was healthy.

Here’s roughly how it went:

1. Maya said self-deception can’t literally happen. To deceive someone, the deceiver has to know the truth while getting the other person to believe something false. But if the same person is both deceiver and deceived, they supposedly both know and don’t know the same thing at the same time, which sounds contradictory.

2. Priya said that treats the mind like one tidy database row. A person can have enough awareness of a problem to avoid checking it, while not consciously accepting the conclusion. Like the engineer may think “looking at the logs could ruin my weekend” without ever thinking “the service is down.” So one part of him steers his attention while another part forms the comforting belief.

3. Maya replied that “one part steers another part” is just a metaphor. If no single person both knows the truth and believes the falsehood, then nothing literally got deceived. Maybe the engineer was negligent, avoidant, biased, or willfully ignorant, but calling it self-deception just makes ordinary motivated reasoning sound more mysterious than it is.

4. Priya said deception doesn’t always require the deceiver to already possess the exact truth. If I hide your map before you figure out where we are, I can deceive you about our location without myself knowing the exact coordinates. Likewise, the engineer can intentionally interfere with his own evidence-gathering so his future self ends up with a false belief.

5. Maya said the map example still has two separate agents at two separate times. In the one-person case, either he knows enough at the start to count as knowing the truth, in which case he isn’t fooled, or he doesn’t, in which case avoidance isn’t deception. She thinks “future self” language hides the contradiction rather than solving it.

6. Priya said Maya is demanding an all-or-nothing notion of knowledge. The engineer can suspect the service is broken, know that investigating would probably confirm it, and still not believe the specific proposition “the service is broken.” His avoidance is guided by a suspicion, while his eventual sincere belief is false. That seems enough to make it self-deception rather than mere carelessness.

7. Maya’s last move was that if suspicion-guided avoidance is enough, then almost every biased search becomes self-deception: reading only news sources you agree with, not stepping on a scale while dieting, ignoring code-review comments, etc. She said Priya needs a principled line between self-deception and just being intellectually lazy.

Can you adjudicate the exchange move by move? For each numbered move, say whether it’s strong, weak, or mistaken, and why. Also say what definition of self-deception would make each person’s position come out true, whether the disagreement is partly just about definitions, and whether there’s a useful software-engineering analogy that doesn’t falsely assume the brain has cleanly separate modules.
