I spent most of my working life running a small engineering firm in the Midlands, and since retiring I have been reading, slowly and without guidance, some of the books I never had time for. One of them is Marx's 1859 Preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, which I picked up because it is short. I want to ask about a pattern I have noticed for years, in business and in local life, which I suspect the Preface touches on but may not exactly name.

The pattern is this. An organisation or a community makes some change, and the reason it gives itself for the change is always moral or principled, yet when you look at the timing, the change follows the money almost exactly. Three instances. My own firm moved to a four-and-a-half-day week in the late nineties and we told ourselves, sincerely I think, that we believed in our workers' dignity and family life; what had actually happened was that a new machine let us do the same work in less time, and the dignity came afterwards, as the story we told about a decision already made. A village near me fiercely opposed a supermarket on grounds of community and character for fifteen years, then quietly stopped opposing it within eighteen months of the mill closing, and now speaks of the supermarket as a source of local pride. And I notice the same shape in national life: rules and courtesies I grew up with are remembered as having been abandoned because society became enlightened, when by all appearances they became expensive first and immoral second. In every case the people involved are not lying, or not simply lying: they come to believe the principled account, and would pass a lie detector on it.

In the Preface, Marx writes: "Just as one does not judge an individual by what he thinks about himself, so one cannot judge such a period of transformation by its consciousness, but, on the contrary, this consciousness must be explained from the contradictions of material life, from the conflict existing between the social forces of production and the relations of production." I take the first half of that to be very close to my pattern. But the second half seems to claim more: not merely that a society's account of itself is unreliable, but that it can be explained, and explained from something specific. So my questions are these. First, does my pattern — the principled self-description that arrives after the material change and is then sincerely held — have an established name, and in whose work? I have heard the word "ideology" used for anything of this sort, but it seems to be used so broadly that it would cover deliberate propaganda and simple hypocrisy too, and my pattern is neither: the sincerity is essential to it. Second, is what I am describing the same thing Marx means in that sentence, or a narrower and tamer thing? He is talking about whole periods of transformation; I am talking about firms, villages and habits. Does his claim survive being scaled down to those, or does something get lost? Third, if there is no name that fits exactly, I would like one proposed, with a definition that marks the edges: how it differs from after-the-fact excuse-making by an individual, from cynical public relations, and from the cases where the principle genuinely came first and dragged the economics behind it. I am not after a single label so much as a clear account of what the thing is and what it is not.
