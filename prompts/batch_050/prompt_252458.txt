My roommate designs typefaces and we've been arguing for weeks about which shapes actually count as the letter 'a'. Her rulings so far: the double-storey a in a book face, obviously yes. The single-storey circle-plus-stick a that kids write, yes. A cursive a with a huge swoopy ascender, yes. A capital A — "still an a, just wearing a suit." A perfectly drawn a reflected in a mirror, no. An a rotated 180 degrees, no. A Greek alpha sitting in a physics paper — "no, that's an alpha, whatever it happens to look like." An a whose bowl doesn't quite close at the top, like in a stencil font, yes. An a where the stem and bowl don't even touch each other, she hesitated but said yes. And a scribble that looks exactly like an a but was made by someone who insists they were writing a fast 'd' — she says its a d, intention wins.

Next up she wants to rule on two weirder specimens: a piece of driftwood whose grain forms a flawless double-storey a, totally untouched by anyone, and a pet shop logo where the a is drawn as a sleeping cat curled into a ring that doesn't quite close, tail sticking out as the stem.

Which way does she go on each? I'm less interested in the verdicts than in what rule she's actually running. A structural rule (enclosed counter plus stem on the right, roughly) fits most of the list but strains at the capital A. A recognizability rule (whatever fluent readers would read as 'a') fits other parts. And an etiological rule (it's an a iff it was produced as a token of the a-grapheme, by someone or some process aiming at it) explains the d-scribble and maybe the alpha, but seems to imply the driftwood a isn't an a at all, which I'm not sure she'll stomach. So: lay out which rules fit her track record, where each lands on the driftwood and the cat, give me your best prediction and why, and then describe one further test case that would cleanly seperate the two strongest candidate rules.
