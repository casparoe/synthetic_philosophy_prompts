You are a philosopher specializing in formal epistemology and the philosophy of action, with long experience mediating stalled disputes. Before writing anything, think carefully and step by step about what the two positions described below actually commit their holders to, brainstorm a list of candidate cruxes (more than you will ultimately use), and only then draft the deliverable.

The dispute: whether a rational agent can assign probabilities to her own presently deliberated actions. On one side, the Deliberational Incompatibilist holds that while genuinely deliberating about whether to do A, an agent cannot rationally hold any credence about whether she will do A: deliberating means treating the outcome as settled by one's reasons rather than by one's character or history, and to entertain "I probably won't do it even if I judge it best" mid-deliberation is to stop deliberating and start spectating on oneself. On the other side, the Self-Prediction Compatibilist holds that agents can and often rationally must assign probabilities to their own future acts, including acts they are currently deliberating about: an agent who refuses to learn from her own track record (the serial resolution-breaker deliberating about whether to quit smoking for the eleventh time) is practically irrational, and the felt "openness" of deliberation is a perspective one occupies, not a constraint on one's credences.

Draft the joint statement these two could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. The document must contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — about what deliberation is for, about the difference between predicting one's act before deliberation begins and predicting it mid-deliberation, about cases where self-prediction is obviously legitimate (e.g., foreseeable weakness of will) and where it is obviously undermining (e.g., being handed one's own psychological profile mid-decision) — that both parties could honestly endorse, in wording neither would want to amend.

2. A disagreements table stating the residual disagreements precisely, each disagreement phrased as a single thesis with two columns, where each column formulates that side's own position in language that side would accept.

3. A cruxes section identifying the two to four sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one side to revise. For each crux, specify a concrete case, thought experiment, or argument that both sides agree bears on it — candidate material you should consider includes: whether an agent can rationally take a bet on the outcome of her own ongoing deliberation; the smoker who predicts her own relapse while genuinely trying to quit; whether "What shall I do?" and "What will I do?" can be entertained simultaneously without one collapsing into the other; and whether an outsider's reliable prediction of the agent's choice differs in kind from the agent's own. For each crux, record in advance what each side predicts the correct treatment of the case will show.

4. A final short section in which you state, in your own mediating voice, whether any part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive — for instance, whether the two sides mean different things by "deliberating" — and if so, say which part and why, in a way both parties would recognize as fair.

Do not cite specific books, papers, or authors; refer to the positions and arguments descriptively. The tone should be that of a document both parties would actually sign: precise, unslanted, and unwilling to paper over genuine disagreement with vague compromise language.
