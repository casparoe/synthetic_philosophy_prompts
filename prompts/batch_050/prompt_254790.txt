I am a novelist, and next month I've been invited to present the method behind my counterfactual novel at a university history department's colloquium. The novel imagines that a single minor, well-documented incident — a courier delayed by a flooded road — causes one pivotal political assassination to fail, and traces the consequences over thirty years. My method, which I mean to defend, rests on two self-imposed rules: the point of divergence must be the smallest physically plausible change I can devise, and every downstream development must be driven only by mechanisms already visible in the actual historical record, never by invented forces or personalities. My deeper claim is that this discipline shows the event was contingent, not inevitable — that the familiar outcome was one branch among several, not the working-out of structural necessity.

The audience will be mostly social and economic historians of the longue durée persuasion, professionally suspicious of counterfactuals as parlor games and of great-man narrative generally; one cliometrician who may be friendlier but will press on rigor; and a few diplomatic and narrative historians I suspect are quietly sympathetic. Prepare me for the question period: the objections I will actually get from this audience, phrased as they would phrase them, ordered by how damaging they are, with a two-minute reply to each and a note on where the reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front to lower the room's temperature, whether my minimal-divergence rule smuggles in a contestable view about what causes really matter, and which question I should be hoping nobody asks.
