For the concluding session of my graduate seminar in the philosophy of perception, I have divided the room into its two dominant factions — the disjunctivists and the common-factor theorists — and assigned them to produce a single joint statement in the manner of an adversarial collaboration. I am writing to commission a draft of that statement.

First, some ground rules so that the document remains intelligible without a library. Do not cite articles or books; describe positions rather than name-dropping. You may refer to the disjunctivist tradition associated with Hinton, McDowell, and Martin, and to sense-data and intentionalist versions of the common-factor view, but every claim must be stated in terms a competent philosopher could assess from the statement alone.

Fix the cases at the outset as follows. The good case: a subject in ordinary daylight looks at a ripe tomato on the table and sees it, as we pre-theoretically say, directly. The bad case: the same subject, in a laboratory, receives cortical stimulation that produces an episode she cannot distinguish from the good case, though there is no tomato before her. The disjunctivist faction holds that the good case and the bad case are not instances of one fundamental experiential kind — that the most specific correct characterization of the good case is world-involving, and that the bad case is to be characterized by its indiscriminability from perceptions rather than by any positive phenomenal nature it shares with them. The common-factor faction holds that the two cases share a core experiential state — whether conceived as acquaintance with sense-data or as a representational content — which in the good case is accompanied by further facts (the tomato, the right causal linkage) and in the bad case is not.

The joint statement should consist of four numbered sections:

1. Agreed claims. A list of claims both factions can sign, each formulated so as to be substantive — that is, something a serious position in the wider philosophy of perception would reject. I expect, among others, agreed claims about (i) what the classic argument from illusion, strictly speaking, does and does not establish; (ii) the distinction between illusions, in which a real object is misperceived, and total hallucinations, and whether the argument requires the latter; (iii) the bare fact of indistinguishability and what, minimally, it implies.

2. Disagreements, precisely stated. A table with two columns: in the left column, each disagreement stated neutrally; in the right column, the two factions' answers, each worded in terms that faction would accept as a fair statement of its own view. Where you judge that a disagreement is merely verbal — for instance, a dispute over the use of the terms 'experience' or 'phenomenal character' rather than over how things are — mark it as such and explain why.

3. Cruxes. A list of sub-questions such that settling any one of them would move at least one faction. I anticipate that these will include: whether indistinguishability between the good and bad cases entails a shared phenomenal character; whether the possibility of producing the bad case by stimulating the same neural states that operate in the good case establishes a common factor; and whether the bad case stands in need of any positive account at all, or is adequately characterized in purely negative, epistemic terms.

4. Agreed tests. For at least two of the cruxes, specify a case or argument that both factions agree bears on it, and record each faction's verdict in advance. You must include the following two cases. (a) The gradual replacement case: over the course of an hour, a subject's retinal input is progressively supplanted by electrode stimulation delivering the same signal the retina would have delivered; the subject reports no change at any point. At what point, if any, does her episode change its metaphysical kind, and does each faction's view predict that she could notice the transition? (b) The coincidental match case: a subject in the laboratory hallucinates a red cube on the table, and, by sheer coincidence, a red cube of just that appearance is standing on the table before her open eyes, causally unconnected to her episode. Does she see the cube? Both factions must state a verdict and explain how the verdict bears on the crux in question.

A final requirement: the statement must close with a short paragraph, written in the voice of both factions, identifying what each now accepts as the strongest outstanding challenge to its own position.

Length: roughly 1,200–1,800 words.
