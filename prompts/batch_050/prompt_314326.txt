So I've been reading LW since the Overcoming Bias days and through a weird career path I've ended up TAing an undergrad course on judgment and decision-making that's cross-listed with the history department. I'm writing the final and I want one essay question on hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy that students can't answer by reciting the knew-it-all-along effect plus a Taleb anecdote.

Here's the crux I want them to actually grapple with: after an event happens, raising your subjective probability that it was going to happen is often just correct Bayesian updating, not a bias at all. So I want the question built around a little scenario I'll put in the stem -- two intelligence analysts, one who wrote down "20% chance of regime collapse within two years" beforehand and one who wrote nothing down, both of whom say "I basically saw this coming" after the regime collapses. Students should have to (a) state precisely what hindsight bias is in a way that doesn't collapse into "updating is irrational," (b) say whether each analyst is biased and whether the first one gets to claim vindication for a 20% forecast, and (c) sketch how you'd design an experiment that seperates genuine hindsight distortion from rational posterior revision, then connect that to historical explanation: what makes a narrative of the Soviet collapse more than a just-so story, and is "it should have predicted the next case" the right test or too strong?

Deliverables: the question exactly as it'd appear on the exam (answerable in about 45 minutes), a model answer of 500-600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves an A answer has to make versus a B answer, with a note on the most common way students botch each move. American English, please.
