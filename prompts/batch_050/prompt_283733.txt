I'm prepping for my undergrad environmental philosophy seminar next term and we're doing a two-week unit on whether the shift to agriculture was, on balance, a disaster for the humans who lived through it. My students always come in having absorbed one of two pop takes -- either the "original affluent society" line where foragers worked three hours a day and lounged around the rest of the time, or the progress-is-obvious line where farming gave us surplus, cities, and eventually penicillin, so case closed. I want to scramble both of those before we get to the readings.

So: write me a decision tree that starts a student at the commitment "it makes sense to ask whether adopting agriculture was good or bad for the people who adopted it" and ends them at a named (or nameable) position. The branch questions should force the real cruxes, not the textbook summaries. A few things I want the tree to actually engage: whether the comparison should be judged by the welfare of the transition generation or their descendants ten generations out; whether skeletal and disease evidence (shorter stature, enamel defects, crowding epidemics) settles anything or just shows foragers died differently; whether inequality is intrinsic to grain agriculture or a contingent political outcome; whether the irreversibility matters -- i.e., once your population grows past what foraging can support, "it was a mistake" becomes advice nobody can act on; and whether leisure-time affluence should count against caloric reliability in the first place. Leaves should include at minimum: a Diamond-style "worst mistake" view, a strict progressivist view, an incommensurability view (the question dissolves once you look closely), a "mistake for the many, boon for the few" distributional view, and a trap view where the transition was individually rational and collectively ruinous with no one to blame. Every branch question has to be phrased so a student can answer it without knowing which leaf it leads to, and flag any two leaves your tree genuinely can't separate.

Format-wise: give me the whole tree as a numbered list of exactly fourteen items -- each item is one branch question or one leaf, a sentence or two each, with leaf items noting the position's most awkward commitment in a few extra words. No intro paragraph, no closing remarks, just the fourteen items.
