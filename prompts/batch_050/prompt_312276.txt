A friend who reads decision theory summarized Good’s theorem on the value of information for me as follows: if additional evidence is free, a rational agent can never be worse off in expectation for obtaining it before choosing; therefore declining information is irrational, and costly information should be bought whenever its expected benefit exceeds its price. I have no formal background in decision theory, but this sounds as though it may turn a carefully bounded theorem into a general rule about curiosity and inquiry. Which parts of the summary are fair, and which are overstatements? Please explain the theorem’s actual assumptions—such as fixed options, beliefs, and utilities; Bayesian updating; no delay or acquisition cost; and the ability to act differently after observing the evidence—and distinguish the expected value of information before seeing it from regret after seeing an unwelcome result. Finally, consider cases in which information causes anxiety, changes incentives, creates strategic exposure, or consumes attention: are these genuine exceptions, or situations in which the theorem’s assumptions no longer hold?
