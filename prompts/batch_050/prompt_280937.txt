Okay so I need help refereeing an argument that broke out in my writing group last night, because it maps directly onto a problem in the novel I'm drafting and I keep going back and forth on it. Quick background: I write literary fiction, and my current manuscript has a character — a pretty selfish hedge fund guy — who does something genuinely decent in act three that costs him a lot. My critique partner, Maya, told me the turn is unearned because nothing in the first two acts gives him the motive for it. At drinks afterward this blew up into a real fight between her and another member of the group, Tom, about something they kept calling internal versus external reasons. Maya did a philosophy degree years ago, Tom didn't but held his own. Maya's opening position: a person only has a reason to do something if it connects, even through some long chain of deliberation, to something they already want or care about. So a man who cares only about his own comfort has no reason to help a drowning stranger, and saying "but you ought to" is just making noise at him — like insisting he has a reason to grow feathers. Applied to my book: if I haven't planted the seed of the motive in acts one and two, no speech from another character can supply it in act three, and the turn will always read as authorial puppetry.

Tom pushed back hard. First, he said that consequence is nuts — it means someone who doesn't care about other people has no reason not to cheat or lie or worse, so morality ends up hostage to whatever desires people happen to walk around with, and we couldn't even blame the indifferent person, since blame seems to presuppose they had a reason to do better. Second, he said people obviously do change: religious conversions, becoming a parent, surviving something awful — people get confronted with reasons that weren't in them before. Maya answered both. She said conversions actually prove her point, because what happens in a conversion is that the person acquires new cares — through imagination, experience, attention — and the reasons follow the new cares; nobody gets argued into caring by the sheer existence of some free-floating external reason. And she said the blame objection backfires: if a so-called reason couldn't move the person even after flawless deliberation starting from where they actually are, then calling it a reason is a name without a lever. Tom's last move was that on her view the word "reason" loses all its critical bite and just means "whatever you're already disposed to do," and Maya's last move was that the bite survives, because you can show people that what they already want, properly thought through, commits them to way more than they realize. Then they got stuck and both turned to me, and I genuninely could not say who won.

So two things. First, adjudicate it point by point: who's right about the drowning stranger case, who's right about whether blame requires that the blamed person actually had a reason, whether conversions are evidence for Maya's side or Tom's, and what — if anything — both of them are missing or talking past each other on. Second, the selfish application: given the correct verdict, is Maya right that my act-three turn needs a motive planted earlier, or is there a philosophically respectable way for a character to be moved by something that wasn't already in them? And please be careful about the state of the actual debate here — flag which parts are more or less settled (what the standard positions are, what the standard objections to each are) and which parts are genuinely live among people who work on this stuff, rather than presenting one side as the consensus. Also don't name-drop readings at me — I want the arguments themselves, not a syllabus.
