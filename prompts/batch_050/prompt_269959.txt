So I got into an argument with my brother in law over thanksgiving and it's been bugging me for weeks. I have no philosophy training or anything, I just read alot and watch youtube essays, so sorry if this is a well known thing with a name. He says causation is at bottom a matter of "if the cause hadn't happened, the effect wouldn't have happened" — like when we say the match caused the fire, the cash value of that is: no match, no fire. Fine. But then he pressed this line on me and I couldn't find the hole in it, even though the conclusion is clearly nuts:

(1) To say that A caused B is (at least centrally) to say that if A hadn't happened, B wouldn't have happened.
(2) But for pretty much any real-world event, there's some backup waiting in the wings that would have produced the same effect anyway. If Suzy hadn't thrown her rock at the window, Billy's rock would have smashed it half a second later. If I hadn't left the stove on, the ancient wiring in my kitchen would probably have started a fire sooner or later anyway. The world is thick with backup plans — spare fuses, second shooters, diseases that would've gotten you if this one hadn't.
(3) So for pretty much any pair of events, it's false that the effect wouldn't have happened without the "cause."
(4) So almost nothing ever actually causes anything. Genuine causation is incredibly rare, maybe limited to freak cases where no backup was available.
(5) But that's obviously false. The match did cause the fire, Suzy's rock did break the window.

Now (4) has to be wrong, but I can't tell which step to refuse. My brother in law thinks the fix is to give up (1) and replace it with something like: A causes B when wiggling A is a way of controlling whether B happens — not what *would* have happened without A, but what *does* happen when you intervene on A. He says this handles the backup cases because actually grabbing Suzy's arm does change whether the window breaks right then, even though Billy exists. Maybe, but it seems to me this just moves the bump in the rug: couldn't you rerun the whole argument about interventions? (Billy's arm is a "backup intervention" for Suzy's, or whatever.) And worse, does tying causation to wiggling things make causation weirdly about *us* — did nothing cause anything before hands existed?

Which step should I actually give up, and what does giving it up cost me? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) than the brother in law's wiggling idea, since (1) feels like it captures why causes matter for responsibility and explanation, but I want to know what I'm signing up for either way.
