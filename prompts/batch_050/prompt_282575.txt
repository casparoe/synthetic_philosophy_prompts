I am preparing a position statement for a graduate seminar in philosophy of biology and would like a consistency check before I circulate it. My considered views are as follows:

(1) The tautology objection to 'survival of the fittest' is serious, and the propensity interpretation answers it: fitness is a dispositional property, an organism's probabilistic propensity to contribute offspring to the next generation, rather than its actual reproductive output.

(2) Differences in fitness, so understood, causally explain why heritable traits spread through populations over time.

(3) I am persuaded by the statisticalist critique: natural selection is not a force acting on populations but a statistical pattern that emerges from countless individual-level causal histories of birth, death, mating, and accident, so talk of selection 'doing' anything is at best a convenient shorthand.

(4) Because fitness is a propensity rather than an outcome, two organisms with identical realized offspring counts may nonetheless differ in fitness, and two organisms with identical genotypes may differ in realized offspring number purely by luck, without any fitness difference between them.

(5) Natural selection explains why advantageous traits become common but is entirely silent about where those traits come from; the arrival of novelty is the business of mutation and recombination, not of selection.

(6) An organism's fitness is a property it carries with it: the same individual, moved from one environment to another, takes its fitness along, just as it takes its height.

Please tell me which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. For each genuine tension, state which commitment is cheapest to revise and what that revision would cost me; for each merely apparent tension, show briefly why it dissolves on inspection. Order the genuine tensions by severity, and conclude by identifying the single revision that would make the overall set maximally coherent.
