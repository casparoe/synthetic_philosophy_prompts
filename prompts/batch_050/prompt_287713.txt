So I run the philosophy night at my local pub (long story) and last week this bloke corners me and tells me, dead serious, that a white horse is not a horse. I laughed it off, but apparently it's a real thing -- Gongsun Long, the School of Names, an entire ancient Chinese dialogue where he argues for it while some opponent keeps objecting and losing. Worse, I promised everyone we'd do it as next month's topic, and I honestly can't tell whether it's a pun, a logical sleight of hand, or actual philosophy of language. Before I commit to any reading in front of the regulars, I want the spec first: what does any adequate interpretation of the white horse argument have to deliver? For instance it presumably has to explain why the conclusion sounds like an obvious sophism while the dialogue and later Chinese logicians treat it as worth taking seriously, it has to take a stand on whether 'white horse' picks out a kind, a subset of horses, or some compound concept, and it has to make the opponent's objections inside the dialogue come out non-stupid rather than just declaring him the loser. Give me exactly seven numbered items, no intro, no conclusion, a sentence or two each: items 1-6 one desideratum apiece, each with the bit of the dialogue or the consideration that motivates it and a note on whether you'd call it negotiable or load-bearing, and item 7 a quick scorecard on the three readings I keep bumping into (it's mere wordplay and the joke's on the opponent; it's about naming kinds versus naming individuals; it's about compound predicates carving the world differently than simple ones) -- which desiderata each reading satisfies and where each falls down. And definately flag any pair of your own desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied, because that's half the fun. British English please, and casual enough that I can nick your phrasing at the pub.
