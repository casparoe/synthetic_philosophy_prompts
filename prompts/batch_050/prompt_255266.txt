Last year, at 54, I was diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's disease. While I retain full decision-making capacity, I have been drafting an advance directive — a so-called Ulysses contract — instructing my future caregivers to disregard certain requests my later self may make: requests to leave residential care, to refuse particular treatments, or to discontinue the directive itself. I have come to accept what is sometimes called the precedent autonomy view: that the considered directives of my competent present self should bind my future self even when that future self sincerely protests and, indeed, even when he appears content with the life I would have denied him.

I would like you to tell me what would count against this position. Not a survey of familiar objections, but a map of what the view actually risks.

1. Which possible findings or cases would genuinely undermine it rather than merely discomfort it? For instance: if people with moderate dementia reliably report higher wellbeing than healthy people predict for them, is that a defeater for precedent autonomy or only a puzzle about affective forecasting? If my future self no longer remembers signing the directive and no longer endorses the values behind it, at what point does he become, in effect, someone else whose interests my signature should not override?

2. What does the view presuppose about personal identity, the authority of earlier selves over later ones, and the relationship between narrative values and moment-to-moment welfare — and in which of those neighboring debates does it stand or fall? If the best account of persistence turns out to make my demented future self only weakly continuous with me, does that strengthen the directive (I am protecting a vulnerable stranger from encroachments he cannot foresee) or void it (the signer is not the patient)?

3. What would defenders of precedent autonomy themselves concede as counting against it — as opposed to what critics who prioritize the demented person's current experiential interests say about it? I want the view's internal standards, not only the opposition's talking points.

4. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me honestly whether the position has been padded with qualifications — the directive applies only when the patient's current preferences are not genuine, settled, or minimally competent — until it risks nothing at all, and whether that is a strength or an evasion.

For what it is worth, the defeater I would personally find most persuasive is one showing that the view rests on a confusion about whom the directive is for: that it treats my future self as raw material for my present self's life-story rather than as the person the contract purports to protect. But I would like the full map regardless. Where relevant, please compare the psychiatric Ulysses contract (say, for bipolar disorder, where the patient typically recovers and is retroactively glad to have been bound) with the dementia case, where there is no recovery — I suspect the analogy between them is doing more work in this debate than it should.
