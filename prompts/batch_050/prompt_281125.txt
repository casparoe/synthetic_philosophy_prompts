I sit on the board of a small-town historical society, and at nearly every meeting about how much commemoration our town needs, one member deploys the same analogy: a community that neglects its history is like a person with amnesia. Without a past, she says, it no longer knows who it is, cannot learn from its errors, and drifts into repeating them. She uses this to argue for more archival funding, more monuments, and mandatory local history in the schools, and anyone who hesitates is treated as if they were advocating collective memory loss as a lifestyle. I happen to be reading Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, on the use and disadvantage of history for life, where he distinguishes monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes of relating to the past and argues that the capacity to forget is as vital to a person or a culture as memory, since nothing new can be done under the full weight of everything that has been. That essay has made me suspect the analogy is doing more argumentative work than it is entitled to, and I would like a proper audit of it rather than a summary of Nietzsche.

So: first, give me the explicit correspondence table. What in the community plays the role of the patient's memory, of the patient's self, of the injury, of the clinician? What maps onto remembering, onto forgetting, onto repetition of mistakes? Second, lay out the disanalogies and grade them by how much damage they do to my colleague's policy conclusion rather than by how clever they are. Some candidate worries: personal memory is autobiographical and partly constitutive of who the person is, whereas a community's "memory" is a curated representation assembled later by particular people with particular interests; forgetting in a patient is an affliction, while forgetting between groups is sometimes the price of reconciliation and peace; a person is a single subject of experience, while a community contains many parties whose pasts conflict; and the claim that a memoryless community repeats its errors seems to assume that history offers transferable lessons in a way that needs defending rather than assuming. Third, tell me whether any part of her conclusion survives once the disanalogies are priced in -- for instance, whether the analogy supports antiquarian preservation but says nothing about monuments or school curricula, or whether Nietzsche's three modes let it be rehabilitated in a weaker form. Finally, if the amnesia analogy is beyond repair for her purposes, propose a better one she could honestly use, and say what weaker conclusion it would actually license.
