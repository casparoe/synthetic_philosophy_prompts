I'm a novelist and I'm stuck on a chapter. Setup: my main character runs a sort of salon for resurrected dead writers (dont ask, its a long book) and one of the regulars is cranky late-period Tolstoy, the version who'd already disowned his own novels. In the chapter I can't crack, she shows him a bestselling contemporary novel that everyone agrees is devastatingly moving -- readers say it saved marriages, reconciled estranged brothers, the works. The catch, which most readers don't know: it was written by an AI, trained by scraping basically every published novel ever without permission or payment, so there's a live argument that the book is stolen goods. And obviously the AI felt nothing while writing it. There's no author whose feelings could have poured into the reader.

So to write him properly I need you to work out what Tolstoy would actually say, going off his views in What Is Art? and his late moralism in general. Specifically:

1. Does this thing count as art at all on his view? His test seems to be that art transmits feeling from the artist into the audience and infects them with it. Here there's no feeling at the source, yet the audience genuinely feels things. Does he call it counterfeit art, which I gather he considered worse than merely bad art? Or does he grudgingly admit that if people are infected, it's art, full stop?

2. His other big criterion is moral: good art fosters the brotherhood of man, bad art divides or corrupts. Say the novel genuinely makes its readers kinder. Does the fact that it was built on mass theft poison it for him, or does he judge the work by its effects on people and shrug about the training data? Would he treat the theft differently from the case where a human artist is personally immoral, and why?

3. The twist I'm most unsure about: what does he make of readers who loved the book and then felt cheated and hollow when they found out no human wrote it? Nothing in the text changed. On his view, is that reaction irrational squeamishness, or does it show the infection was somehow fake all along -- like they were responding to an imitation of feeling rather than feeling itself?

Give me his actual verdict on each question, with the reasoning in his terms (infection, sincerity, the moral criterion, all that), and flag the point where you think the prediction gets genuinely dicey -- where a different plausible reading of Tolstoy flips the answer and I get to pick whichever serves the chapter.
