My reading group spent two entire meetings talking past each other about Warren Quinn's self-torturer puzzle and I want to force a truce. Quick version of the case, in case it's fuzzy: someone has a device fitted to them with settings numbered 0 to 1000. Each notch up raises the pain level by an increment too small to consciously notice, and pays out $10,000 per advance, but the catch is you can never turn it back down. At every single step, advancing looks obviously worth it -- an imperceptible tick of pain for ten grand -- yet after enough steps the person ends up in agony they would pay everything they earned to escape.

The room split into two camps. Team Transitivity says rational preference has to be transitive, full stop, on pain of money-pump style exploitation, so the puzzle's real culprit must be something else: vague pain comparisons, confusing imperceptible differences with no difference, or treating what is really one long sequence as a stack of independent choices. Team Local Rationality says each advance genuinely is an improvement given the person's preferences at that moment, and the puzzle instead shows that stepwise rational choice can lead somewhere terrible, or even that "better than, all things considered" can be intransitive across a spectrum of barely-distinguishable steps (Temkin-flavored). Please draft the joint statement these two camps could actually sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration -- not an adjudication, not a dialogue, a document both would put their names on.

Format it as exactly five numbered sections, no introduction or conclusion: (1) the claims both sides accept about the case itself and what makes it puzzling; (2) the claims both accept about what any adequate theory of rational choice owes us here; (3) the remaining disagreements, each phrased carefully enough that the side holding it would endorse the wording of its own position, no strawmen; (4) the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp; (5) for each crux, one case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about how that case should come out recorded in advance. If any disagreement turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say so inside section 3 and explain why, and keep each section tight -- a few sentences or a short list, not an essay.
