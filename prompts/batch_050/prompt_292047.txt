At a negotiation workshop I attended last month, the instructor closed the final session by announcing that "tit for tat always wins," citing Axelrod's computer tournaments as proof that in any repeated interaction the optimal policy is to open cooperatively and thereafter simply mirror whatever the other party did on the previous move. Since then I have heard the same slogan invoked to justify diplomatic retaliation, matching a competitor's price cuts, and even replying to colleagues' emails only as promptly as they reply to mine.

I suspect this one sentence is doing the work of several distinct claims, some trivial, some substantive, and some simply false, and I would like them separated. Please enumerate the precise theses that "tit for tat (always) wins" could be expressing, ranging from narrow mathematical statements about the specific tournament setup (a round-robin among a particular pool of submitted strategies, a fixed payoff matrix, a probabilistic shadow of the future, no misperception of moves) to broad normative advice about how to live. For each reading, assess what it would take for it to be true, whether it is in fact a platitude, a defensible substantive claim, or false, and whether it would actually license the instructor's inference that one should play tit for tat in real negotiations. In particular, please address the places where I suspect the slogan's confidence outruns the evidence: I dimly remember that tit for tat performs badly when players occasionally misread each other's moves, that its status as a tournament winner is not the same as being an evolutionarily stable strategy, and that it won without ever outscoring the opponent sitting across from it in any single pairing.
