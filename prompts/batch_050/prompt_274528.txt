I am a PhD student in philosophy with a minor field in logic, and I serve as the organizer of our department's reading group on philosophy of computation. Last week, during a session on whether human mathematical cognition could in principle be modeled by a formal system, one participant closed down a long exchange by declaring that Gödel had proved there are true mathematical statements that can never be proved, and that it followed immediately that no machine could replicate human mathematical understanding. Several people nodded as if the matter were settled; I was not convinced the slogan was doing legitimate work.

Here is what I would like. Take the slogan, in its commonly repeated form, that Gödel proved there are true statements that can never be proved, and treat it as a piece of language rather than a theorem statement. First, lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims this sentence could be expressing. I have in mind, at a minimum, the need to disambiguate along several axes: provability within a particular formal system versus provability by any system whatsoever versus provability by us; truth in the standard model of arithmetic versus truth in some absolute, model-independent sense; and claims properly drawn from the first incompleteness theorem versus claims that would require the second. Where a reading quietly drops a necessary qualification -- for instance, the consistency (or stronger) assumption the theorems actually require -- please flag that explicitly.

Second, for each reading on the menu, give a verdict: which are near-platitudinous restatements of the theorems, which are substantive and defensible, and which are simply false or not licensed by anything Gödel proved. Where the verdict turns on a subtlety a careful grader of this material would insist on -- such as the fact that the unprovable sentence of one system is routinely provable in another, or the difference between a sentence being undecidable in a system and being unknowable in principle -- say so.

Third, and most important to me: identify which reading, if any, would actually support the use the slogan was put to in the reading group, namely the inference from incompleteness to the conclusion that human mathematical understanding outruns any machine. If no reading supports it as it stands, say which additional premises would have to be added -- concerning, for example, the soundness of idealized human arithmetical reasoning or the assumption that a mind, if mechanical, would correspond to a single consistent formal system -- and assess how contentious those premises are, in the manner of the Lucas–Penrose debate but without needing to adjudicate that debate wholesale.

I intend to bring the menu and verdicts to the next session, so please format the answer as a list of readings with a short assessment of each, followed by a paragraph on the dialectical question. Precision about what the theorems say matters more to me than completeness of historical detail; please do not pad the answer with references I would need to chase down.
