EXAM QUESTION

Explain the hole argument as Einstein deployed it in the period 1913–1914, when he had the framework of general relativity but not yet its final field equations. What conclusion did Einstein draw from the argument, and why did he take that conclusion to follow? Then reconstruct the standard contemporary diagnosis of where the argument's apparent threat to determinism arises, and explain how the standard response — sometimes called Leibniz equivalence — is supposed to dissolve the threat. Does that response solve the problem, or does it merely restate a metaphysical preference in different vocabulary? Give a reason for your verdict.

STUDENT'S ANSWER

The hole argument runs as follows. Suppose the gravitational field equations are generally covariant, meaning that if a metric g on a manifold M is a solution, then so is any metric obtained from g by a smooth transformation. Consider a "hole": a finite region of spacetime containing no matter. Take a diffeomorphism that is the identity everywhere outside the hole but differs from the identity inside it, and use it to drag the metric around. The original metric and the dragged metric then agree everywhere outside and on the boundary of the hole but disagree inside it. Since both are solutions of the same generally covariant equations, and since all the data outside the hole — including the full distribution of matter — are identical in the two cases, the field equations plus all physically specifiable initial data fail to determine the metric inside the hole. The theory is therefore indeterministic: even a Laplacean demon given complete information up to the hole's boundary could not predict the geometry within.

Einstein drew the conclusion that this indeterminism is physically intolerable, and hence that a satisfactory gravitational theory cannot have generally covariant field equations. This is why, during his Zurich period, he temporarily abandoned the requirement of general covariance and published the non-generally-covariant Entwurf theory. In setting out the argument, Einstein also introduced the point-coincidence consideration — the observation that the observable content of a physical theory reduces to spatiotemporal coincidences, such as the intersections of worldlines — as a premise showing that the metric inside the hole is not physically meaningful.

The standard contemporary diagnosis is that the apparent indeterminism depends on treating the original and dragged metrics as describing genuinely different physical situations. That treatment presupposes manifold substantivalism: the view that the points of the bare manifold have their identities independently of the metric and matter fields defined on them. If spacetime points are individuals in this sense, then assigning different metric values to the same point yields a different physical state of affairs, and the hole argument goes through. The Leibniz equivalence response denies this: diffeomorphism-related models are one and the same physical situation described twice, just as a situation described in Cartesian and in polar coordinates is one situation described twice. The dragged metric is merely the original solution re-expressed in different coordinates, and Einstein's worry accordingly rested on conflating a change of description with a change of physical facts.

Does this dissolve the problem? I think it does. Once we see that the two models differ only coordinatively, determinism is restored at the level of invariant structure — the hole argument shows at most that the metric at a bare manifold point is not an observable, and no determinism worth wanting requires determinism about non-observables. The response is therefore a genuine solution rather than a relabeled preference, because it identifies a false metaphysical premise (manifold substantivalism) whose rejection removes the appearance of underdetermination.
