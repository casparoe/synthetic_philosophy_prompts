After a forty-year career in atmospheric chemistry, I have been spending my retirement in a philosophy reading group, and we recently worked through the non-identity problem in connection with climate policy. I tried to reconstruct the reasoning as a formal argument, in the style I would have used for a proof in my old field, and I now find myself in the uncomfortable position of having produced what looks like a valid derivation of an absurdity. Since the rest of the group was equally stumped, I would appreciate a professional diagnosis. Here is the argument:

(1) An action or policy is wrong only if there is some particular person who has a complaint against it — that is, only if it leaves someone worse off than they would have been under the available alternative. (2) Which particular people will exist in the future depends heavily on large-scale policy: a different energy policy would so alter migration, partnership, and the timing of conception that, within a few generations, the population under the high-emissions path and the population under the low-emissions path would consist of almost entirely different individuals. (3) Even under the high-emissions path, future people will have lives that are diminished but plainly worth living. (4) A person whose life is worth living, and who would never have existed under the alternative policy, is not made worse off by the policy actually chosen, since the alternative for that person was not a better life but nonexistence. (5) Hence no particular future person has a complaint against the high-emissions policy. (6) Hence that policy is not wrong — and, since the reasoning generalizes, no long-horizon policy can be wrong on account of the people it affects, provided it leaves them lives worth living. Choosing a depleted and impoverished world over a flourishing one is, morally speaking, a matter of indifference. (7) But this is absurd; if anything is a moral obligation, the duty not to wreck the climate for future generations is.

The argument appears to prove too much in a further respect: by the same reasoning, a prospective parent who conceives now, knowing the child will inherit an avoidable and serious condition, rather than waiting three months and conceiving a healthy child, does nothing wrong — the child conceived now would not have existed otherwise, and her life is worth living. My reading group found that result even harder to swallow than the climate one, which makes me suspect a hidden assumption somewhere in the middle of the chain. My own instincts: I would like to retain (1), since it captures something I took to be deep, namely that morality is ultimately about what happens to people; and as a scientist I am quite confident of (2), which is a straightforward empirical claim about the sensitivity of identity to initial conditions. My suspicion falls on the step from (3) to (4), which seems to require comparing existence with nonexistence as if nonexistence were a state a person could be in, but I cannot articulate the objection precisely. Which step should I reject, why did it appear innocuous, and what exactly does each available rejection cost? If the prices differ between the climate case and the prospective-parent case, I would like to understand why.
