I've drafted a short essay for an online philosophy-and-cognitive-science newsletter I help edit, and I'd like your comments before I send it to our two volunteer reviewers, since I trust your judgment and you can be franker than they tend to be. The essay tries to introduce a distinction I came up with after re-reading the famous discussion of the sphex wasp's nest-provisioning routine — the wasp that drags its cricket prey to the burrow entrance, leaves it to check inside, and will repeat this sequence indefinitely if the prey is moved a few inches away each time. That example is usually deployed to unsettle us about human freedom, but I felt something was missing in the standard treatment. The draft is below, followed by what I want from you.

---

Two Kinds of Sphexishness

The sphex wasp has become philosophy's favorite insect. Her hunting ritual is efficient, even elegant: sting, drag, inspect, retrieve. But interrupt it in the right place and she reveals herself. Move the cricket a few inches while she inspects the burrow, and she will drag it back, drop it at the threshold, and inspect again — forty times, if you have the patience. She never adapts the plan. The lesson drawn from her is usually this: what looks like intelligent, goal-directed behavior can be a fixed loop, and perhaps much of what we call our own deliberation is the same trick played on a grander stage.

I think this conclusion is too quick, but not for the usual comforting reasons. I want to distinguish two varieties of rigidity that the sphex discussion tends to blur together.

Horizontal sphexishness is what the wasp displays: rigidity across time within a single behavioral episode. The same steps repeat because the organism has no access to a representation of what the steps are for — or so the standard story goes. Each iteration is fresh; there is no memory of the previous failure to learn from, because there is nothing doing the learning.

Vertical sphexishness is different and, I think, more disturbing. A vertically sphexish agent is flexible at the surface and rigid underneath. She adapts her means endlessly — she learns, deliberates, invents, changes strategies when thwarted — but the goal those means serve is itself fixed in a way she cannot inspect or revise. Her flexibility is real; it is just flexibility in the service of something she never chose and could not choose otherwise. If the sphex wasp is a broken record, the vertically sphexish agent is a jazz improviser whose repertoire consists of a single note, varied forever.

Why does this distinction matter? Because the usual philosophical maneuver is to argue from the wasp to us by way of scale: we are more complicated loops, but loops all the same. I think the more honest worry is vertical, not horizontal. We are plainly not horizontally sphexish; we notice repeated failures and switch tactics. The harder question is whether our tactical brilliance ever touches the level at which ends are set — whether the things we want most (status, attachment, the approval of the dead and unborn, whatever it may be) are goals we hold or grooves we run in. A person can reflect on a desire and even act against it, but the reflecting is itself driven by other desires, and it is not clear that the regress ever bottoms out in a chooser rather than in another loop.

One might object that the distinction collapses: if vertical rigidity exists, it must be implemented in horizontal mechanisms, so there is only one phenomenon at two resolutions. I am not sure. A chess engine's opening book is rigid in a way its midgame search is not, and these are genuinely different components, not the same thing described twice. Whether minds are like engines in this respect is an empirical question, but it is at least a question, and treating the wasp as a simple parable of unfreedom closes it prematurely.

The right moral, I want to say, is not that we are the wasp. It is that flexibility is a worse credential for freedom than we assume, because flexibility can be the rigid thing's way of working. The wasp fails to surprise us; we should worry more about what does the surprising.
