I'm in Year 12 and our history society has been arguing all term about whether we may judge historical figures by present-day moral standards -- the so-called "man of his time" defence keeps being invoked. I've realised that my own reactions are inconsistent, or at least I can't find the rule behind them, so I'd like help working out what principle, if any, captures my verdicts. Please note that I'd like the answer in British English.

Here are my verdicts. Aristotle defended slavery as natural for some people: I don't think this makes him wicked or that we should stop reading him, since essentially everyone around him accepted it. Jefferson, who kept and traded slaves while writing about universal liberty, I do blame: the wrongness of slavery was plainly available to him as an idea, and his own words show he felt the tension. A medieval peasant with the standard sexist assumptions of his era: no blame at all, not even a little. A Victorian MP who actively campaigned against women's suffrage when the question was being publicly debated: some blame, because he had to argue for his position against live opposition rather than simply inherit it. Columbus: full blame for his brutality, not just because of what he did but because even some of his contemporaries were appalled by it. And yet -- here's the pair I can't reconcile -- when I think about whether future generations will condemn us for factory farming, I instinctively want the "person of my time" defence for myself and my family, even though the arguments against factory farming are just as available to me as abolitionist arguments were to Jefferson. I'm also genuinely undecided about Churchill and the Bengal famine: does it matter whether he understood the consequences of his decisions, and whether anyone around him said so at the time?

So my request is threefold. First, state the principle (or two or three rival candidates) that best systematises these verdicts -- something about the availability of the correct view, the effort required to reach it, or the contrast with one's peers, presumably, but I'd like it stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Second, apply the resulting principle to two cases I haven't listed: a Roman citizen who found gladiatorial games distasteful but attended them, and a 1960s scientist who accepted industry funding without asking questions we would now consider obvious. Third, tell me honestly which of my verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate -- I suspect the factory-farming one will have to go, but I'd rather be shown than told. If no coherent principle gets more than most of them, say that too, and explain whether that means moral blame for the dead is simply incoherent or whether my intuitions are the problem.
