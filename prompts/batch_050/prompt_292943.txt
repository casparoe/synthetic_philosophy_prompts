So I've been going back and forth with a friend about statues and renamings -- a university building here just got renamed, and the usual arguments got trotted out: he was a man of his time, everyone thought that back then, you can't judge the past by today's standards. And I realized I don't actually have a worked-out view of what a good practice of judging historical figures would even look like, as opposed to a grab-bag of intuitions I apply when the target is someone I already dislike. So: write the desiderata. What does any adequate account of retrospective moral judgment have to deliver? I mean the cases it has to get right (Jefferson writing about equality while enslaving people, Churchill's record on India, a mid-level colonial administrator nobody's heard of, an abolitionist who was vicious to his family), the distinctions it cannot collapse -- I'd guess at minimum: what the person believed vs. what was knowable and contested at the time vs. what was actually mainstream; being a beneficiary of an injustice vs. an architect of it vs. an active perpetrator; judging the person vs. judging whether to honor them now; and whether moral ignorance excuses when the ignorance was motivated or convenient. Give the case or consideration behind each requirement, not just the label.

A couple of things I specifically want the list to grapple with. The 'man of his time' defense can't be a blanket exemption, because there were always contemporaries who saw clearly (abolitionists existed while Jefferson lived), but it also can't be nothing, or we're committed to condemning essentially everyone before some arbitrary date, which makes the whole practice pointless. And there's a trilemma-shaped worry I keep bumping into: the account has to (a) avoid excusing everyone by appealing to context, (b) avoid condemning everyone by appealing to our current standards, and (c) still yield verdicts that track what the person actually did rather than how good our evidence about them happens to be. I suspect you can't fully satisfy all three at once -- if that's right, say so and say which one you'd relax and why; if it's wrong, show the list is jointly satisfiable.

Mark each desideratum as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp (e.g., the 'moral progress is real so of course we judge' people vs. the 'moral judgment across cultures is parochial' people), and identify which single requirement does the most work in excluding the lazy versions of both sides. Brief scorecard at the end is fine -- the 'full presentism' view, the 'pure contextualism' view, and some middle position -- but keep it short, since I mainly want the spec itself to argue with people from. Oh and it should apply to living people too, or explain why it doesn't -- I don't want a theory that only works once everyone's safely dead.
