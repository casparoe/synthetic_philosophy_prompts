I'm co-organizing a workshop between two scholars who have debated immigration policy for years and have agreed, as an experiment, to attempt an adversarial collaboration — a jointly signed document rather than another exchange of polemics. The first participant is a cosmopolitan economist in the open-borders tradition. She argues that today's restrictions trap most of the world's poor in places where their labor is worth a fraction of what it would be elsewhere (the so-called "place premium"), that the resulting global losses swamp any plausible domestic costs, and that the standard restrictionist worries — fiscal burden, downward pressure on native wages, strain on housing and public services — either shrink to manageable size under scrutiny or can be handled with what she calls "keyhole solutions": targeted instruments like immigrant-specific tax surcharges, staged waiting periods for benefit eligibility, or savings bonds, which address the alleged harm without restricting movement itself. The second participant is a civic-nationalist communitarian. He concedes that immigration is a large net economic positive but maintains that the welfare state is a bounded solidarity project whose fiscal and political sustainability depends on a shared identity that open borders would erode; that the open-borders camp systematically discounts long-run "deep roots" risks — the possibility that migrants from low-trust, poorly governed societies carry norms into the host country that degrade its institutions across generations, a cost that won't show up in ten-year fiscal impact studies; and that the keyhole solutions actually worth having (permanent exclusion from citizenship, permanent welfare ineligibility) are precisely the ones his opponent's own egalitarian commitments would eventually compel her to repeal, making them unstable as compromises.

Draft the joint statement the two of them could both sign. Structure it as follows. First, a short preamble defining the scope of the collaboration — what question they are actually disagreeing about (policy for a mid-sized wealthy democracy over the next fifty years, say), and what they take "open borders" and "the welfare state" to mean for purposes of the document. Second, a numbered list of propositions both parties endorse without reservation; these should be substantive rather than anodyne, and should include, wherever the evidence genuinely warrants it, findings each side previously resisted (for instance, on the fiscal effects of low-skilled immigration, on labor-market competition, or on the track record of immigrant assimilation). Third, a section of residual disagreements: each one stated in a single sentence both parties accept as neutral, followed by a short paragraph from each side describing its own position in its own words — and wherever a disagreement turns out, on honest inspection, to be about terminology rather than substance, the statement should say so explicitly instead of dignifying it as a real dispute. Fourth, a crux register: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party, and for each crux, the empirical finding, natural experiment, or case both agree would bear on it, with each side's advance prediction written down (e.g., what the economist expects from a comparison of welfare generosity across countries with very different immigration levels, and what the communitarian expects from it). Close with a concessions ledger: two or three points each side grants the other, plus one red line each will not cross.

The hard part, and the thing I most care about, is the drafting: formulations that are neither so watered-down that anyone could sign them nor so slanted that one party would be endorsing the other's framing. Keep the whole statement to what a careful reader of both literatures would recognize as the actual state of the debate — no strawmen, no invented studies, and no citations, since this is a document of positions and predictions, not a literature review.
