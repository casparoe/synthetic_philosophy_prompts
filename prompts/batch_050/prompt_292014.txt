Last week I spent most of an evening arguing with a friend about whether anything in Locke's account of personal identity survives Reid's gallant officer, and I took a beating on one point I now want to defend properly rather than improvise. Next month I'm presenting a paper to our department's graduate colloquium, and the paper is my attempt at the defense.

The thesis, briefly: numerical personal identity over time consists in psychological connectedness — overlapping chains of direct autobiographical memory links — rather than in the persistence of a substance or in direct memory connections between every pair of life-stages. On Reid's case, I concede that the elderly general has no direct memory of the boyhood flogging, but argue that he is nonetheless identical with the flogged boy because the general directly remembers being the young officer, who directly remembered the flogging; identity rides the chain. I add two repairs to Locke: a causal condition requiring that apparent memories be appropriately caused by the original experience (so that mere apparent memory doesn't count), and a non-branching clause for fission-style cases. Where a life stretches so long that early and late stages share no psychological content at all, I hold that identity nonetheless persists so long as each adjacent pair of stages is connected.

The audience: two historians of early modern philosophy, one of whom works on Reid's Essays on the Intellectual Powers and treats the officer case as a refutation rather than a puzzle; the other works on Locke's reception and will certainly raise Locke's own concessions about the sober man who does not remember what he did drunk, and about the forensic character of the word person. Also present: an endurantist metaphysician of persistence who I suspect will press the circularity worry associated with Butler, and a philosopher of mind who works on memory reconsolidation and confabulation and takes the empirical literature to show that the memory criterion individuates far too liberally.

Prepare my Q&A sheet. Give me the objections I will actually get from this audience, not from a generic philosophy-of-mind crowd, ordered roughly by how much damage each would do to the paper. Phrase each objection the way its likely author would phrase it — the Reid scholar in the vocabulary of the officer case and the transitivity of identity, the Locke scholar in the vocabulary of the Essay's forensic turn, and so on. For each, give me: what I should concede up front, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a candid note on where that reply is soft. In particular, my friend ended our argument by pressing a Methuselah-style worry — that a chain of connections can hold between two stages that share no psychological content whatsoever, and that calling such stages identical strains the notion past recognition — and I was not satisfied with my answer. I want the objection I am hoping nobody asks, stated in its strongest form, together with the best fallback position available to me if my main reply fails: whether I can retreat to claiming the criterion governs only what matters in survival rather than strict identity without thereby abandoning the paper's thesis.
