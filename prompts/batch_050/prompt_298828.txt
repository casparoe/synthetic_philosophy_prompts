I am a PhD student in philosophy, working on formal epistemology, and next month I have been invited to give a standalone ninety-minute guest session in an undergraduate game theory course that enrolls mostly economics and PPE students. The class will already have seen simultaneous-move games, payoff matrices, Nash equilibrium, and simple extensive-form games, but the course so far has treated solution concepts as primitives and said nothing about the epistemic assumptions underneath them. My assigned topic is common knowledge of rationality and its connection to backward induction. The instructor has warned me that last year's TA session on this material went badly in two opposite directions at once: half the students left thinking backward induction is obviously how rational people play and could not see why any machinery was needed, and the other half left thinking the whole framework is an empirically false idealization, since real people plainly do not take at the first node of a centipede game. I would like a plan for the session rather than the lecture itself.

My own draft sense of the dependency structure is this: the distinction between mutual knowledge and common knowledge (everyone knows, everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on without end) has to be established before anything about rationality, probably carried by a puzzle like the muddy children, because otherwise students will hear "common knowledge of rationality" as just "everyone is rational and knows it." Then iterated dominance in a dominance-solvable game, perhaps a guess-two-thirds-of-the-average game, which lets each level of the knowledge hierarchy do visible work, before the centipede game, where the epistemic load is heavier because the reasoning runs through counterfactual future nodes. But I would like this ordering assessed rather than assumed: one could argue the centipede is more gripping and should anchor the whole session, with the knowledge hierarchy built only as needed. Relatedly, which single example should carry the central claim that common knowledge of rationality, together with the structure of the game, forces the backward-induction outcome — and what must that example have and lack? Does it need to be finite, does it matter whether players have strict incentives at every node, and at what point does an example become so clean that students conclude the result is a tautology about the example rather than a general theorem?

I also want the misconception map, in the order the misconceptions will predictably surface, with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off: that common knowledge is mutual knowledge iterated a few times rather than indefinitely; that rationality means selfishness or payoff-maximization rather than best-responding to one's beliefs (a confusion the course's earlier units will have reinforced); that backward induction is a behavioral prediction, so that the experimental literature on centipede play appears to refute the theory rather than to falsify its assumptions; and, trickiest, the tension about what a player should believe upon reaching a node that common knowledge of rationality implies will never be reached — should I raise this myself as a known pressure point, or is it better left for a student to discover, given that I cannot resolve it in the time available? Please also specify what to leave out entirely at this level — partitional models of knowledge, the formal statement of results like Aumann's, the agreeing-to-disagree literature — and how to flag those omissions without making the material seem hedged. Finally, I would like a closing check for understanding: a question or small exercise whose successful handling would show the idea has landed, rather than the definitions having been memorized.
