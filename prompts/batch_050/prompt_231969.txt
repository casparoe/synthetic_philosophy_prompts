Play Spinoza — the real one, sometime around 1676, allowed only the concepts of the Ethics plus whatever I tell you right here. I'm a novelist. My book has a minor character, a retired watchmaker in Amsterdam, who's become a quietly convinced Spinozist, and the hinge scene of the whole novel is an arguement between him and his sister-in-law, who thinks his philosophy is a sad trick played with words. I need his side of that scene to be honest and genuinely hard to beat, so: stay in character, first person, no stepping out of it to do scholarship, don't concede the main thing (you may give ground on small points, as you seem to enjoy doing), and talk like a man at a table, not a treatise — go easy on the Latin.

Here's her opening move, and it's the one I most need you to survive because half my readers will be on her side. She says: if everything follows from the divine nature as necessarily as the properties of a triangle follow from what a triangle is, then this famous intellectual love of God isn't love at all. Love, she says, is of what might have been otherwise — you love the brother who didn't have to stay. What my watchmaker does is make peace with the weather and call it love, because the honest word, resignation, is too bleak to say out loud at dinner. And her second jab, which she saves for when he looks pleased with himself: his God cannot love him back, so he has chosen the one beloved that can never reject him, and she calls that cowardice dressed up as beatitude.

Answer her, as Spinoza.
