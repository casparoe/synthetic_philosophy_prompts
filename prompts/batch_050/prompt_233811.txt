I'm on the five-member ethics commission for a mid-sized county, and we're supposed to issue an advisory opinion to the county council about our emergency management director, and honestly we're stuck. Here's the case.

During the ice storm last February, the grid failed for about four days. Our director did two things people are now fighting about:

1. After the owner of a warehouse full of portable generators refused to open up or sell at any price, she sent county police with a signed order to seize the generators and distribute them to warming shelters. Her legal authority for the order was dubious at best, and the county's own attorney told her so at the time. (The owner has since been compensated at fair market value, and he's the one who filed the complaint.)

2. She announced on every channel that a mandatory 6pm curfew was in effect and that violators would be arrested, to keep the roads clear for utility and repair crews. No curfew had been lawfully declared, she knew it hadn't, and she's said she'd do it again.

The outcome was genuinely good: zero cold-related deaths in our county, while two neighboring counties had several, and power came back faster than projected. Her term is up and the council has to decide two things: whehter to formally censure her, and whether to recommend her reappointment. Those could in principle come apart, which is partly why we're stuck.

Draft the memo we'd actually vote on. I want:

- a header and a bottom-line-up-front recommendation on each of the two questions, with the reasoning after
- serious engagement with the problem of dirty hands, meaning at least: whether these acts were simply right all things considered, or justified wrongs that leave some kind of residue behind, and what follows for censure either way — if what she did was justified, does censuring her just teach the next director to let people freeze, or is censure precisely how a community marks that someone's rights got violated even when overriding them was the right call
- the two actions treated separately — my instinct is that seizing property from a holdout is a different problem than lying to the entire public, since the people she deceived are the same ones she's democratically accountable to, but push back if you think that distinction doesn't hold up
- an honest run at the strongest argument against whatever you recommend (not a strawman), and
- a take on whether the good outcome should matter to our judgment at all, or whether we should evaluate her only by what she could reasonably have known at the time — she couldn't have known the seizure and the curfew would actually work.

Don't let it turn into a general essay about political morality; every section should bear on what we should reccomend to the council. Aim for 900–1200 words, memo format, American English. No citations or philosopher name-dropping needed — argue it from the case.
