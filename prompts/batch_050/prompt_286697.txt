I run a monthly reading group for fellow software engineers (about 10 people, no philosophy background, though all comfortable with recursion, interpreters, and meta-programming). We meet for 90 minutes. Next session's theme is tangled hierarchies: situations where a system's levels of description loop back on themselves, the way Escher's Drawing Hands shows each hand drawing the other, and the way self-modifying code can blur the line between program and data. I want to design an in-person activity or game, not a discussion, that makes participants feel two things firsthand: first, the disorientation of a level-crossing loop, where acting on a system turns out to be acting on yourself; second, the pull of the "inviolate level" idea -- the intuition that something must sit outside the loop, untouched, for the whole thing to run at all, and the discomfort when that something is hard to locate.

The catch: participants must discover this through the rules or incentives of the activity itself, not through me explaining it. They should hit the loop, get confused about which level they're operating on, and only afterward hear the vocabulary. Please give me concrete rules and materials (nothing requiring a computer -- cards, paper, tokens are fine), a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the two ideas above. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which feature of tangled hierarchies the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply in words.
