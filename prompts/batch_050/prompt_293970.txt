I am a professor of philosophy, and for the past semester I have moderated a graduate reading group on the foundations of interpretability in machine learning. Two of my most capable participants have argued past each other since October, and I have told them that the seminar's final meeting will be devoted to a joint written statement rather than another debate. I would like you to draft that statement for them.

The first participant is a realist about learned representations. Her view is that a trained neural network genuinely contains features: structures in the network's activations and weights that are individuated by the network's own functional organization rather than by the investigator's choices, and that interpretability research succeeds insofar as it discovers these features and fails insofar as it merely projects them. On her view, asking what a particular network represents has a correct answer, the way asking what a particular enzyme does has a correct answer, even if our methods for finding out are fallible and theory-laden.

The second participant is a skeptic. His view is that the question of what a network represents is not well posed independently of a method of interrogation. For any trained network, he argues, there exist many mathematically legitimate decompositions of its activations, each carving the computation differently, each highlighting different structure, with no principled, observer-independent criterion for privileging one carving as the network's own. Interpretability findings, on his view, are facts about the interaction between a network and a chosen lens: genuinely useful, perhaps, but discoveries about our tools as much as about the network.

Draft the document they could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. It should contain:

First, a numbered list of agreed claims. These must be substantive rather than vacuous. Candidates I expect to appear include concessions the realist should be willing to make (for instance, that no interpretability method is theory-neutral, that some published findings have not survived scrutiny, and that intervention on activations is stronger evidence than passive observation) and concessions the skeptic should be willing to make (for instance, that some interpretability results have yielded genuine predictive and causal leverage over model behavior, and that the mere existence of multiple mathematical descriptions of a system does not by itself show that none of them tracks the system's real structure).

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should state one residual disagreement twice: once in wording the realist would accept as a fair statement of her position, and once in wording the skeptic would accept as a fair statement of his. Where you find, in drafting, that an apparent disagreement is actually terminological, do not put it in the table: retire it in a short note explaining what each side meant and why nothing remains in dispute.

Third, a cruxes section. For each crux, specify a concrete result or observation that both parties agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. I will suggest three candidate cruxes, but you should revise, replace, or extend them if you can do better: (i) cross-seed convergence — whether, and how strongly, it would matter if networks trained independently on similar data turned out to develop highly similar internal structure, or instead wildly dissimilar structure supporting similar behavior; (ii) causal sufficiency under intervention — what it would show if manipulating the structures a given method identifies reliably and specifically produced the behavioral changes that method's story predicts, or failed to; (iii) superposition and the failure of clean individuation — whether the fact that networks seem to pack more structure into fewer dimensions than any neat one-feature-per-unit scheme allows tells against feature realism, or only against a naive version of it.

The document should run to roughly 1,200–1,800 words, in a formal register suitable for circulation to the department. Neither party should be made to concede anything in disguise.

Finally, a request about your own posture as drafter. Close the document with a brief drafter's note doing three things: identifying the formulation in the agreed-claims list or the table where neutrality was hardest to achieve and where you are least confident you avoided slant; stating which of the crux tests you expect would most change your own assessment of the dispute if you were forced to have one, and in which direction; and naming the assumption you relied on most heavily in framing the disagreement, so that my students can examine whether the document itself rests on a contested premise.
