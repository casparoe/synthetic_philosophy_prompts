So two of my coworkers run a working group on climate uncertainty and they've been going at each other for like three years. One's a Bayesian about it: she thinks the deliverable of climate science for policymakers is an actual probability distribution over outcomes, built by weighting models on past performance plus structured expert judgment, and that refusing to give probabilities just offloads the math onto politicians who'll do it worse. The other's a deep-uncertainty guy who thinks ensemble spread is not a probability, never will be, and that the honest approach is scenario-based planning — find decisions that are acceptable across lots of plausible futures instead of optimizing against a single PDF.

I've told them to stop arguing in meetings and produce a joint statement like an adversarial collaboration would. Draft it for them. I want:

1. A numbered section of claims both can genuinely sign. Candidate stuff: that the models in a multi-model ensemble aren't independent samples (shared code, shared ancestors, shared structural assumptions), that ensemble spread underrepresents total uncertainty, that adding more models doesn't mean adding more information, that hindcast skill doesn't automatically certify projection skill. But keep each claim substantive enough that someone outside the field could object to it — no "uncertainty exists" filler.

2. A disagreements table where each side's position is stated the way that side would state it. Neither would accept a strawman like "the Bayesian thinks models are perfect."

3. The cruxes: sub-questions where settling them would make at least one side actually move. I suspect one crux is whether cross-model agreement is confirmatory at all given the shared-ancestry problem, and another is whether real-world adaptation decisions (a sea wall, a reservoir) can be made well without committing to any probabilities, even implicitly.

4. For each crux, a concrete case or test both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Please work in this one: imagine an audit traces every current-generation model back to three ancestral codebases, so most "independent confirmation" across models is illusory. The Bayesian has to say how much her published uncertainty ranges would widen; the robustness guy has to say whether his scenario set actually changes, since he claims he never leaned on independence anyway. Also consider a pre-registration test: both sides lock in their weighting scheme / robustness criteria now, before the next model generation reports, and we check whose procedure held up.

5. If any chunk of their fight is really verbal (I'm suspicious of "confidence" vs. "probability" here), flag which chunk and explain why.

Agreed-claims section first, then the table, then cruxes and predictions. Write it as the actual document, not a summary of what the document would say.
