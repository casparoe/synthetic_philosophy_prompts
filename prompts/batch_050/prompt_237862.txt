You are a philosopher of language who spent a decade working on Frankfurt's account of bullshit and now writes about the ethics of AI-generated text; approach this with the care you'd give a paper, not a hot take. A colleague and I have been stuck for weeks on a deceptively simple question: when a language model confidently produces a falsehood, is it lying, bullshitting, or merely erring? Some philosophers have recently argued that chatbot output is best classified as bullshit rather than lying, since the system is indifferent to the truth of what it says; my colleague counters that nothing without beliefs and intentions can lie or bullshit, so "hallucination" is just error plus anthropomorphic projection. I think this debate is confused because nobody has pinned down what structural features of a system would make each verdict correct. So I want a thought experiment built to order, and I intend to use it in a blog post arguing that these labels are architecturally grounded rather than metaphors we project onto machines.

Construct three minimally different systems — same architecture family, same conversational context, and all three produce the very same false output sentence — such that the first genuinely lies, the second produces bullshit in Frankfurt's strict sense (indifference to truth, plus the concealment or misrepresentation of that indifference if you think Frankfurt's account requires it), and the third commits an honest error. Constraints: describe each system purely in functional and architectural terms — its training signal or objective, what internal state its output is conditioned on, how it behaves when presented with counterevidence, what it optimizes when truth and persuasiveness conflict — and never stipulate "this system cares about truth" or "this one is indifferent" as a bare fact; the relevant attitudes have to fall out of the machinery or the case is cheating. The systems must be buildable with roughly current technology: no stipulated phenomenal consciousness, no magic propositional attitudes, no perfect predictors. And do not stipulate the verdicts either: a case that just labels the second system "the bullshitter" fails.

After presenting the three systems, do the accounting. For each, explain what on Frankfurt's account and on the standard account of lying (asserting what one believes false, with intent to deceive) makes the verdict come out as it does. Identify the single structural feature that separates your bullshitting system from your honest-error system — I suspect it is something like outputs being selected for persuasive plausibility rather than evidential support — and either convince me of that or correct me. Then stress-test it: what happens to system two if you strip that feature? Does it collapse into system three, or can something count as bullshit without it? Finally, state the strongest version of my colleague's objection that none of these systems can lie or bullshit at all, and describe the smallest architectural addition that would answer it, if any would. Before settling on your final design, briefly brainstorm two or three candidate systems and say why you discarded the ones that failed the constraints; then present only the final three.
