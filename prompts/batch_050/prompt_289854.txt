At the lending company where I consult, two senior reviewers have been deadlocked for over a year about the fairness assessment of our credit-scoring model, and I have finally persuaded them to attempt an adversarial collaboration rather than another round of memos. I need you to draft the joint statement such a collaboration should produce.

The two positions, as fairly as I can state them. The first reviewer — call her the auditor — holds that fairness is a checkable property of a deployed model's outputs. There is a small family of well-understood statistical criteria: comparable approval rates across groups, comparable error rates among those scored, and scores that carry the same meaning about default risk regardless of group. It is now established that such criteria cannot all be satisfied simultaneously when default rates genuinely differ between groups and prediction is imperfect, but she takes the mature response to be choosing deliberately which criterion the context calls for, documenting the choice and its costs, and monitoring it over time. The second reviewer — call him the proceduralist — holds that the impossibility is not a scheduling problem among metrics but a reductio of the whole approach: no collection of outcome statistics can settle whether a decision procedure treats people fairly, because fairness attaches to how individuals are considered, informed, and answered, and the metrics at best register symptoms of deeper problems and at worst convert a political judgment into an apparently technical one.

Draft the document with three sections, each under a heading:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of claims both reviewers could sign without reservation. These must be substantive — not platitudes such as "fairness matters" or "models can be biased" — and should include the uncontested mathematics stated in plain language, together with whatever follows from it that neither side disputes.

2. Disagreements. A table with three columns: a single disagreement stated in one neutral sentence that each reviewer would accept as an accurate description of the dispute; the auditor's position in terms she would endorse; the proceduralist's in terms he would endorse. Where an apparent disagreement dissolves on inspection — where the two were using a word like fairness or measure differently and turn out to agree once it is disambiguated — do not put it in the table; list it separately as a dissolved dispute, with a brief note identifying the equivocation.

3. Cruxes. The two or three sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one reviewer. For each crux, supply a concrete case, experiment, or piece of evidence that both would agree bears on it — for instance, something about how rejected applicants respond to a decision they can neither understand nor contest, or about what happens when a carefully documented tradeoff is re-examined by a court or regulator several years later — and record each side's prediction about that case in advance, so that the document could in principle be revisited against the outcome.

Two constraints govern the whole. First, neither reviewer should be able to accuse the document of smuggling in the other's premises, and the agreed claims must survive a hostile reading by both. Second, wherever the statement depends on a distinction — say, between treating a metric as a diagnostic test and treating it as a definition of fairness — make that distinction explicit rather than letting it do its work through tone.
