I am a novelist at work on a book about a contemporary populist governor, and I have been rereading Robert Penn Warren's All the King's Men as the master text for my material. I want a critical reading of Warren's novel against a passage from Rousseau's The Social Contract (Book IV, Chapter 1, "That the General Will Is Indestructible"), which I have copied below from a public-domain English translation:

"Finally, when the State, on the eve of ruin, maintains only a vain, illusory and formal existence, when in every heart the social bond is broken, and the meanest interest brazenly lays hold of the sacred name of "public good," the general will becomes mute: all men, guided by secret motives, no more give their views as citizens than if the State had never been; and iniquitous decrees directed solely to private interest get passed under the name of laws. Does it follow from this that the general will is exterminated or corrupted? Not at all: it is always constant, unalterable and pure; but it is subordinated to other wills which encroach upon its sphere."

Please address the following, treating the reading as a test of both the novel and the passage:

First, Willie Stark's career seems to narrate Rousseau's sequence almost in order: the upright simplicity of the young treasurer who fights the corrupt schoolhouse contract gives way to a politics of crowds, patronage, and coercion in which private interest repeatedly drapes itself in the language of the public good. But Warren complicates the schema, because Stark's roads, bridges, and free hospital are genuine public goods, pursued by corrupt means. Does that complication break Rousseau's frame, or does it show that the frame is subtler than it looks -- that the general will can be mute even while its objects are, so to speak, correctly guessed at by a usurper?

Second, consider the crowds. Stark's rallies are exercises in acclamation rather than deliberation; he addresses his audiences as fellow hicks and manufactures the will he claims to voice. Is Warren's crowd the Rousseauian people gone mute, or something Rousseau never imagined -- a public whose "will" is an artifact of the speaker?

Third, the harder problem. Jack Burden's Great Twitch -- his theory that all human action is mere twitching, absolving everyone of responsibility -- denies that there is any will at all, general or otherwise. Rousseau can keep the general will "constant, unalterable and pure" beneath corruption; the Twitch permits no such floor. Does the novel's ending, in which Jack abandons the Twitch and accepts the awful responsibility of living, amount to a Rousseauian restoration -- the will survives its muting -- or does Warren concede something darker, that there was never a general will to begin with, only particular wills and the men who impersonate their unity?

Please be faithful in both directions: I do not want the novel flattened into an illustration of Rousseau, and I do not want Rousseau bent to fit Warren. Where the reading fails -- and Warren's essentially religious vocabulary of sin and guilt is one place I suspect it fails -- say so plainly. The practical stakes for me are real: whether a novelist should write a populist as the muter of an existing common will or as the author of a fictitious one changes how I must write every crowd scene in my book.

Please keep the response to roughly a thousand words, and end with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that states, in one sentence, whether on your reading Warren's Louisiana still contains a general will -- silenced but indestructible -- or only a will of all, amplified.
