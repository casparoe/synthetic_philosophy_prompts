Last night a friend and I got into a long, unresolved argument about advance directives, and I want to pin down where our disagreement actually lives. The case we kept circling: a woman in her fifties, sharp and fully competent, watches her mother die slowly of dementia and signs a directive stating that if she ever reaches a state of moderate dementia in which she can no longer recognize her children, she is not to receive life-extending treatment for otherwise curable conditions (pneumonia, say, or a treatable infection). Twenty years later she is in exactly that state. By the staff's account she is content: she enjoys her meals, listens to music, sits in the garden. She has no memory of signing the directive and, when things are explained to her simply, she says she wants to stay. She develops pneumonia. My friend insists the directive is decisive: the person who wrote it is the person whose life this is, and honoring her settled, reflectively endorsed values is what respecting her amounts to. I found myself arguing that the contented patient in the garden is the one whose interests are now at stake, and that the directive's authority depends on a claim about the earlier self's jurisdiction over a later self she can no longer even comprehend.

So here is what I want from you. Give me your actual credence in the following proposition: in this case, all else equal, the hospital should withhold the antibiotics, and the fact that the patient now seems content and wants to continue living does not defeat the directive. I don't want a survey of positions or an it-depends. I want a number, and I want the decomposition: roughly, (a) the probability that the demented patient is the same person as the signer in the morally relevant sense, or if you think personal identity is a red herring here, the probability of whatever continuity claim you take to be doing the real work; (b) the probability that critical, life-narrative interests survive the loss of the capacity to grasp them, as opposed to only present experiential interests mattering once competence is gone; (c) the probability that the patient's current contentment and expressed wish to live are genuine preference rather than artifact of the disease; and (d) whatever epistemic term you think belongs, e.g., how confident we should be that the signer, if she could have truly occupied her later perspective, would have maintained the directive. Assign a rough probability to each, say in one line why, and show me how they combine into your final number, including which terms you are multiplying versus which are doing independent work.

Then do the part I most care about: identify which term my friend and I most likely disagree about, given that her credence on the proposition is probably around 0.85 and mine is around 0.3. If the decomposition suggests our disagreement is actually somewhere else than where I described it, say so. Finally, tell me which term in your own decomposition you are least confident in, and what argument, case, or piece of evidence would move it most.
