I am preparing a philosophy seminar session that pairs Kubrick's film A Clockwork Orange (alongside Burgess's novel) with the contemporary dispute between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso over free will, desert, and the justification of punishment. Write a careful critical reading of the work through the lens of that debate, addressing the following.

First, the work's central objection to the Ludovico technique — voiced in the story by the prison chaplain — is that goodness imposed without the possibility of choosing otherwise is not goodness at all: Alex becomes a creature wound up to perform virtue. State precisely what view of moral agency this objection presupposes, and then test it against the two philosophers. Dennett is a compatibilist who denies that moral responsibility requires contra-causal freedom, and Caruso is a skeptic who denies that anyone deserves punishment in the basic sense. Does the work's objection actually engage either position, or does it attack a libertarian picture both men have already abandoned? If the objection can be rebuilt in Dennett-friendly materials — for instance, as the claim that Ludovico destroys Alex's competence to recognize and respond to reasons rather than his metaphysical freedom — reconstruct that version and explain how it differs from what the work itself seems to say.

Second, is the Ludovico technique best classified as punishment or as treatment? Caruso's proposed replacement for retribution is a public-health model: incapacitate the dangerous roughly as we quarantine the sick, by the least restrictive means, and without withdrawing concern for the person incapacitated. Ludovico looks closer to this model than to retribution, yet the work presents it as monstrous. Does the case show that the quarantine model needs further constraints, and if so, which constraints does the story itself suggest?

Third, use the two endings. The film ends with the conditioning reversed and Alex restored to his former appetites; the novel's final chapter, absent from the edition on which the film draws, has Alex simply mature out of violence on his own. How does each ending alter the philosophical verdict — and does the novel's ending lend unexpected support to Caruso's insistence that society address the causes of crime rather than the criminal?

Conclude by marking where the work resists the reading: is there anything in its picture of evil, choice, and personhood that neither Dennett's compatibilism nor Caruso's skepticism can fully absorb? The reading should be faithful in both directions — neither reducing the work to an illustration of a thesis nor bending the philosophers' positions to fit it. Do not quote the text; paraphrase scenes as needed.
