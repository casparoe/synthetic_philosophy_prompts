I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I keep circling back to a standoff in the philosophy of medicine that has outlasted most of the careers spent on it. On one side, the naturalists hold that health and disease are objective, value-free notions: a disease is a failure of some part or system of the organism to perform its statistically normal function, where normal function is fixed by the organism's biological design -- ultimately, by what its parts were selected to do. On the other side, the normativists reply that no purely biological fact settles what counts as disease, since calling a condition pathological always involves a judgment that the condition is bad for the person who has it -- a judgment that reflects human values, not nature. Hybrid views try to have it both ways, requiring both a biological dysfunction and a harmful effect.

What strikes me, as an outsider reading this literature, is that each side has long since produced its best counterexamples to the other and absorbed the blows without converting. The naturalist is handed homosexuality, drapetomania, and other discredited diagnoses as proof that values smuggle themselves in; the naturalist answers that these were misapplications of the naturalist standard, not refutations of it. The normativist is handed cases where we confidently call something a disease while knowing nothing about anyone's values -- a tumor in an unnoticed organ, a genetic disorder in an animal -- and answers that the confidence itself is the value judgment. Fifty years in, the argument patterns seem to repeat with new examples but no new structure.

My working hypothesis, which I'd like tested rather than accepted, is that the debate stalled because the two sides are answering different questions under one word. The naturalist is asking a classificatory question for the purposes of biology and epidemiology: which organismic states are malfunctions? The normativist is asking a practical question for the purposes of medicine and social policy: which states entitle a person to care, sympathy, insurance coverage, and excuse from ordinary obligations? Because 'disease' does duty for both, each side treats the other's question as a confusion and its own as the real one. If that is right, the impasse is not evidence that one theory is wrong but that the concept is overloaded -- and the interesting further question is whether medicine actually needs a single unified concept of disease at all, or whether it could do its work with two: a biological notion of dysfunction and a normative notion of what merits treatment.

Tell me whether this diagnosis is right. If the disagreement is genuinely substantive -- if both sides really are answering one and the same question -- then identify what that question is and where, exactly, the disagreement bottoms out: in rival accounts of biological function, in different intuitions about contested cases, in different views about whether normative facts can be reduced to natural ones, or somewhere else. Whichever way you come down, I want to know what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a sharper account of function, a distinction the field has been missing, a decisive case, an empirical finding about how clinicians and insurers actually deploy the concept, or an argument that we should retire the word from foundational work and let the two successor notions go their separate ways. Reasons over verdicts; and if the honest answer is that no resolution should be expected, explain what feature of the concept makes that so.
