I teach an intro phil course and every year we spend two weeks on whether philosophical intuitions are the same across cultures, and every year the same weird thing happens in discussion. Quick version: some studies have run the classic thought experiments (the fake-barn cases, the Chinese room, trolley stuff) on students in different countries and gotten different answer patterns than what Western analytic philosophers treat as obviously correct. So I present both sides. And here's what I noticed: my armchair-philosopher colleague says, about the non-Western students, well, they must not have understood the vignette, or their translation of the concept is off, they haven't been trained to think about these cases properly. Fine, maybe. But then a grad student who loves experimental philosophy does the exact mirror-image move: when philosophy professors give the traditional answers, she says the professors are the weird ones, their intuitions got warped by years of training in one particular tradition, so of course they all agree with each other. And it hit me that both people are doing the same operation. Each of them decides who counts as a competent, reliable user of the concept by checking whether the person's judgments match their own side. Agreement counts as competence; disagreement counts as misunderstanding or contamination. Which means the empirical question "do intuitions actually vary?" can never come out as yes, because whenever it looks like variation, you just reclassify the disagreeing group as incompetent and the variation evaporates. And it also can never come out as a clean no, since the other side plays the same trick in reverse. The disagreement gets relabeled as error on both sides at once, so nothing can settle it.

Is there a name for this specific move? I don't think it's just No True Scotsman, because nobody's redefining a category on the fly to protect a universal claim; they're fixing who counts as a competent judge in advance, using agreement as the test, and doing it with a straight face on both sides. It's also not exactly ordinary question-begging, at least not the textbook version. It seems adjacent to things like the "experts know best" defense people make against survey results in philosophy, and to motivated reasoning generally, but the symmetry feels like the important part -- two camps each laundering their conclusion into their method for measuring competence.

So: does the literature on experimental philosophy or anywhere else have an established concept for this? If it does, tell me the name but also how the thing I described differs from the official version, because I suspect it's not a perfect fit. And if there isn't an established concept, coin one, define it precisely, and give me a couple of boundary cases -- e.g. is a physicist dismissing a flat-earther's "intuitions" doing the same move, and if not, what exactly separates the two? A bare name on its own won't help me, I need to know where the edges are.
