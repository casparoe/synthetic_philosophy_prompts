I'm a PhD student (moral psychology, but I sit in on the ancient phil seminar) and at a workshop dinner last week two people got into a genuine argument about the Stoic dichotomy of control that I'd love to see settled. Ana said the dichotomy is just false as stated: almost nothing that matters is either fully up to us or not at all up to us -- health, a career, whether a relationship survives are all *partly* up to us, which is why modern revivers end up quietly patching it into a trichotomy of full/partial/no control, and the patch is really an admission of defeat. Marcus said the trichotomy misses the point entirely: the dichotomy was never a claim about causal influence, it's a claim about where to stake your judgments and your wellbeing, and adding a "partial control" category just reintroduces exactly the outcome-anxiety the doctrine is designed to remove. Ana came back with the Stoics' own archer analogy -- taking aim is yours, hitting the target isn't -- and said look, even the ancients couldn't state the view without smuggling in degrees, so the clean two-way split does no real work. Marcus replied that the archer case actually proves his reading: the dichotomy kicks in *after* you redescribe your goal internally (shoot well, not hit), and the appearance of partial control is just an artifact of describing your goal by its outcome. Ana's final move was that this is a protective redescription trick: any disappointment can be internalized away after the fact, which makes the advice unfalsifiable, and constantly renaming your goals in advance is basically pre-emptive sour grapes. Marcus said demanding falsifiability of a practice is a category error and that "discipline" was always the claim, not theoremhood. Neither budged. Can you adjudicate move by move -- who's right about which specific point, who's overreaching where, and is there anything both of them seem to be missing?
