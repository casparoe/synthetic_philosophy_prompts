I've been teaching Bertrand's paradox to undergraduates and put together the following argument to motivate it; the trouble is that on reflection it seems to prove far too much, and I cannot locate the misstep. Consider:

(1) If your evidence gives you no reason to favor any member of a set of exclusive and exhaustive possibilities over the others, rationality requires assigning them equal credence.

(2) A factory produces cubes with side lengths between 1 and 3 cm by a process about which nothing further is known. Applying (1) to side length, the probability that a given cube's side is at most 2 cm is 1/2. Applying (1) to volume, which ranges from 1 to 27 cm³, that same event has probability 7/26, since side ≤ 2 just in case volume ≤ 8.

(3) A principle that yields contradictory credences from the same evidence cannot be a requirement of rationality; so (1) is usable only where some parametrization of the possibilities is privileged.

(4) Whether a parametrization is privileged cannot be settled a priori, and absent physical knowledge of the process, side, surface area, and volume stand on a par.

(5) Nor does restricting (1) to finitely many outcomes help: any partition can be coarsened or refined. Indifference over {1, ..., 6} gives a die roll of 1 the credence 1/6; indifference over {1, not-1} gives it 1/2. Privileging one partition without empirical information about the mechanism would be arbitrary.

(6) Therefore (1) is rationally applicable only where we already possess frequencies or physical knowledge of the chance setup; pure indifference never justifies any credence on its own.

(7) So it is irrational to assign 1/2 to the first toss of a freshly minted coin, or any definite credence to a one-off event such as next year's election, on the basis of ignorance alone.

I cannot accept (7): it would gut decision theory and leave insurance, prediction markets, and ordinary planning without any rational footing. If forced to choose, I would rather keep (1) in full generality than accept (7). Which step of the argument should I reject, why did that step look compelling, and what does rejecting it cost me?
