So I'm taking an upper-level seminar on population ethics this term and we just spent two weeks on spectrum arguments — the setup where you line up a long chain of pairwise comparisons (a life of constant ecstasy vs. one that's a tiny bit shorter or has one mild headache at the end, and so on down to something mediocre) where at each step the next option seems clearly better or at least not worse, but the first option is obviously better than the last. Our prof walked us through how this is supposed to put pressure on the transitivity of "better than," and honestly I came away convinced. I now think betterness really isn't transitive, that the all-things-considered ordering just doesn't chain all the way down the spectrum, and that the usual objections just show that rational choice has to be understood in some non-binary way rather than that the arguments fail.

But my roommate (econ major, annoyingly confident) keeps insisting the whole thing is an artifact of how the cases get presented, and I've got a term paper due in six weeks where I basically want to defend intransitivity, so I need to stress-test this properly before I commit. What would count against the view? And I don't just mean "transitivity is an axiom of rational choice theory, QED" — I've heard that one and it feels like it assumes what's at issue. I want the defeaters that would actually give a committed intransitivity person pause. For instance: is there a version of the money pump that targets all-things-considered betterness rather than just preferences, and does it work if I'm allowed to revise which comparison standard I'm using mid-sequence? If it turned out the pairwise judgments in the spectrum are all predicted by some well-documented psychological effect about how we compare very similar vs. very different options, would that refute the view or merely explain why I was seduced by something false — and how would we even tell the difference? What happens to the view if "better than" splits into betterness along separate dimensions and the apparent intransitivity is really equivocation between respects? And what would the philosophers who actually defend spectrum arguments themselves concede as counting against them, as opposed to what they'd just bite the bullet on?

Rank the defeaters from most serious to merely awkward, and be honest with me about one more thing: if every apparent counterexample to intransitivity can just be redescribed as vagueness, or as a case where the pairwise judgments were "about different things," is that a strength of the view or a red flag that it's been qualified into something unfalsifiable? If the view turns out to be basically insulated, I'd like to know whether that's actually a problem for a position in axiology or just par for the course.
