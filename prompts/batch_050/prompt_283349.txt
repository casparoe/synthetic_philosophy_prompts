I host a small philosophy meetup and last week two regulars, Marcus and Dana, got into a long fight about punishment and desert that neither of us could settle. Somebody had mentioned the Dennett–Caruso debate on free will and just deserts, and it took off from there. Here's the exchange as best I remember it. (1) Marcus: take a fraudster who knowingly spent years lying to retirees to drain their savings -- nobody coerced him, he understood the rules, he weighed the risks and did it anyway. That's exactly the kind of agent our blame practices are built for, so he deserves prison, not as a deterrent calculation but as a response to what he did. (2) Dana: the "he chose it" move is doing all the work. If determinism is true he couldn't ultimately have done otherwise, and if indeterminism is true it's just luck which way the indeterminacy broke. So nobody ever basically deserves punishment; at most we're justified in incapacitating him the way we quarantine a disease carrier -- and we never say the carrier deserves quarantine. (3) Marcus: then you have to give up the whole economy of responsibility, not just blame -- nobody deserves their paycheck, their medal, the gratitude they get. And the quarantine analogy fails because quarantine doesn't address the carrier as a reason-responsive agent at all; punishment at its best is exactly an appeal to reasons, which is why we don't punish sleepwalkers. (4) Dana: praise and gratitude survive fine as expressions and reinforcement without any metaphysical desert behind them, and the "reasons-responsive" point just redescribes the practice -- the question was whether the practice is fair to someone who is, at bottom, a product of constitutive luck. Marcus then said Dana was one premise away from abolishing moral responsibility entirely, Dana said "maybe I am, and so what," and we ran out of time.

Please act as a professional philosopher who works on free will and criminal justice, think this through carefully step by step (a quick brainstorm of the strongest steelman for each side before you judge would help), and then adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four numbered moves, say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or defensible-but-contested, and why. I'm not asking which team you join overall -- I want the point-by-point refereeing, including whether either of them is changing the subject or leaning on a premise the other already rejected. Don't hedge everything into "philosophers disagree"; give verdicts and take the risk of being wrong. Then, at the end, tell me what would change your mind on the move you're least confident about -- what argument or consideration would flip your verdict there, or where your reasoning is most likely to fall apart.
