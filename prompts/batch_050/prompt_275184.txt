I spent my career in experimental physics, where a result that fails to replicate across laboratories is considered refuted. I have now read that much of moral psychology rests on samples drawn from WEIRD populations, and that folk moral judgments -- about harm, fairness, intentionality, and the like -- vary considerably across cultures. I take this to mean that moral psychology's findings do not generalize, and I am now puzzled that moral philosophers continue to appeal to intuitions about cases (trolley dilemmas, promises, distributive questions) as evidence for their theories. Given that these intuitions are demonstrably culturally local rather than universal, what justifies treating them as data at all? Does cross-cultural variation in folk morality not simply show that intuitions are measurements taken with an uncalibrated instrument, and shouldn't philosophy abandon them the way physics abandoned the ether once the measurements turned out to be artifacts of the apparatus?
