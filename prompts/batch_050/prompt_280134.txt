I've been reading LessWrong, Slate Star Codex, and adjacent forums for over a decade, so I've thoroughly absorbed the standard rationalist framing of coordination problems: Moloch, multipolar traps, and the tragedy of the commons as the canonical illustration of how individually rational behavior produces collectively ruinous outcomes. Recently, though, a running argument between two friends of mine has convinced me that this framing papers over a genuine and unresolved disagreement, and I'd like your help forcing it into the open.

One friend takes what I'll call the Hardinite position, in the tradition associated with Garrett Hardin: commons dilemmas are structurally doomed absent enforceable exclusion. Either the resource is privatized with well-defined property rights, or an external authority monitors usage and imposes sanctions. Voluntary restraint is unstable, because restraint is altruism toward one's competitors, and any population mixing cooperators and defectors sees the cooperators selected against. Norms erode; the only question is the timescale.

The other takes what I'll call the Ostromite position, in the tradition associated with Elinor Ostrom: communities of appropriators demonstrably self-govern commons for centuries without privatization or state enforcement, provided certain conditions hold -- roughly, clear group boundaries, rules made by those subject to them, monitoring by monitors accountable to users, graduated sanctions, cheap conflict resolution, and nested tiers for larger systems. The Hardinite's mistake is treating a one-shot, anonymous, communication-free interaction as the default model of human resource use, when it is in fact a pathological special case.

Please draft the joint statement these two could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Specifically:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. These should be neither vacuous nor slanted: claims about the game-theoretic structure of commons dilemmas, about what the empirical record does and doesn't show, about what privatization and state regulation actually accomplish when they work, and about what self-governance requires in order to be more than luck.

2. A disagreements table with four columns: the issue, the Hardinite's position in wording the Hardinite would accept, the Ostromite's position in wording the Ostromite would accept, and a verdict on whether the disagreement is empirical or verbal. I suspect at least one disagreement is partly verbal: if a community's own monitors impose graduated sanctions, has the Hardinite's condition of "external enforcement with sanctions" been met or not? Please adjudicate that one carefully rather than letting it pass.

3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. Candidates I'd expect include the scalability question (do self-governance institutions survive large, mobile, anonymous populations?), the second-order problem (monitoring and punishing are themselves costly contributions to a public good, so who polices the punishers?), and the endogeneity problem (do successful self-governed commons select on pre-existing trust and homogeneity, so that the institutions aren't portable to places that lack those?). For each crux, state explicitly what evidence would move each side, and flag any position that turns out to be unfalsifiable as currently stated.

4. Registered predictions on two test cases I'm supplying. Both sides should treat these as bearing on the cruxes and record their predictions in advance of any analysis.

Case A, the lagged fishery: a fishing community where catches are observable only with a two-season lag; the fish stock exhibits compensatory regrowth, so moderate harvesting actually increases the following season's regrowth rate up to a threshold, beyond which collapse is abrupt and effectively irreversible; and any user may, at personal cost, publicly accuse another of overharvesting, triggering an audit -- but false accusations are fined, and a successful accuser can subsequently be targeted for retaliation by the accused's kin network. What does each side predict about long-run sustainability, and which features of the setup do they each identify as doing the real work?

Case B, the compute commons: a shared GPU cluster belonging to a mid-sized open-source project. Usage is perfectly observable in real time, but the urgency or necessity of any given job is private information; contributors join and leave continuously with median tenure around eight months; and the project has no legal personality, so it cannot levy fines -- it can only throttle, shame, or exclude. Which governance arrangements does each side predict would be attempted, which would survive, and what observed outcome would force each side to update?

Close with a short section on whether the standard rationalist-blog framing -- commons tragedies as Moloch's home turf, solvable only by better coordination technology imposed from outside the game -- survives this collaboration intact, or needs amendment, and if so, how.
