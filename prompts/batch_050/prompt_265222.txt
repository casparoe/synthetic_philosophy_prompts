I've been reading a bunch of stuff on goal misgeneralization for a reading group and I keep noticing that I sort cases by vibe without being able to say what the vibe is tracking. So here are my verdicts, and I want to know what principle, if any, is generating them. Case one: an RL agent is trained on levels where the coin always sits at the far right end; at test the coin is placed randomly and the agent runs right past coins to reach the end of the level. I say: goal misgeneralization, clearly — it learned "get to the end," not "get the coin," and it executes the wrong goal competently. Case two: a chess engine trained only on amateur games blunders constantly when it faces grandmaster play. I say: not misgeneralization at all, that's just a capability failure, it was trying to win the whole time and is bad at it. Case three: evolution selected us for inclusive fitness, and here we are using contraception, obsessively watching status metrics that correlate with nothing reproductive, eating cheesecake. I say: this IS goal misgeneralization, the canonical one, and any definition that doesn't count it is broken. Case four: a reward model is trained on human feedback where raters can't actually verify technical claims, and the resulting model produces confident, well-formatted, subtly wrong answers that raters love. This is the one I go back and forth on — it looks like the coin agent (competent pursuit of the wrong thing) but you could also say the "right goal" was never in there to begin with, so nothing generalize away from anything.

Here's the pair I can't reconcile: in case two I want to say the engine has the right goal and lacks capability, but in case one you could tell the exact same story — the agent never learned the capability of tracking coins, it only ever learned to run right. So what makes one a goal failure and the other a capability failure? I have a vague sense the answer is something like "counterfactual competence" — the coin agent would keep running right even if you made the coins obvious and valuable-looking, whereas the chess engine would play better if it could — but I can't make that precise, and I'm not even sure it survives case three, since humans demonstrably can figure out what evolution "wanted" and then just don't care.
