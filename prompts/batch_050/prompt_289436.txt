I co-run a philosophy reading group that's mostly engineers and a couple of med students -- smart people, zero background in the humanities, and we meet in someone's kitchen so no slides, just me talking and a whiteboard. Next session is on Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling, specifically the bit about Abraham and the teleological suspension of the ethical, and I've got maybe 40 minutes to actually teach the concept before discussion starts.

Here's my problem. My draft plan was: give them the story of Abraham and Isaac, explain that "the ethical" for Kierkegaard means roughly the universal/duties-anyone-would-recognize level, then introduce the suspension as Abraham setting that aside because of a direct command from God that can't be justified to anyone. My carrying example was going to be a modern parallel: a nurse who falsifies a patient's chart because she's genuinely convinced God told her the medication would kill him, even though every protocol and colleague says otherwise. The idea was to show the structure -- violating a universal duty on a private, unshareable ground.

But the more I think about it the more I suspect this example teaches the wrong thing. Abraham's situation isn't really "private conviction vs. rules," and my group is definitely going to hear it as "when is it OK to break the rules for your beliefs," which turns it into a trolley problem with extra steps.

So act as someone who has taught Kierkegaard to non-philosophers many times and think carefully. Start with a short brainstorm of 3-4 candidate ways to sequence the session before committing to one. Then give me:

1. The order the pieces have to come in, and why -- e.g., does "the ethical" need to land before the suspension makes sense, and does anything need to land before *that*?
2. A verdict on my nurse example: keep it, fix it, or drop it? If fix or drop, propose the one carrying case you'd actually build the session around, and say what it has to have and what it has to NOT have (e.g., should the audience find the character sympathetic? should the command turn out to be right?).
3. The misconceptions this specific crowd will walk in with or develop mid-session -- I'm guessing "so morality doesn't matter if you have faith" and "this is just divine command theory" and "Kierkegaard is endorsing religious violence" -- in the order they'll come up, and the exact point in the sequence where I should defuse each one rather than letting it fester.
4. One check at the end that tells me they got the concept rather than just got uncomfortable or angry.

Also tell me what to openly refuse to explain at this level (the knight of faith vs. resignation stuff? the pseudonym business?) so I can say "that's real but not tonight" without fumbling.
