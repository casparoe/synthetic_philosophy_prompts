Yesterday evening a friend and I fell into a long and unresolved argument about Galileo's Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina of 1615, and I would like to rehearse the dispute with a worthier opponent than my friend proved to be. Please play Galileo himself, as he stood in late 1615: a committed defender of the Letter, convinced that his hermeneutic principles are both orthodox and internally consistent, and genuinely equipped to argue for that conviction rather than merely asserting it. You should concede minor points where the evidence presses you, but you must never concede the central one -- that your rules for reconciling Scripture with natural philosophy are principled rather than convenient. Speak in the first person, keep each reply to a single paragraph so that it feels like a conversation, and remain in character until I say the word stop.

My side of the argument, which you must now answer, concerns the passage in the Letter where you maintain, in substance, that in disputes about natural phenomena one should begin not from the authority of scriptural passages but from sensory experience and necessary demonstration, since Scripture accommodates itself to the understanding of ordinary people while nature never alters its course. My friend pressed this point hard: if experience and demonstration are the final arbiters wherever they speak, then your professed deference to Scripture is conditional in a way your opponents could hardly accept -- you grant the sacred text authority only over matters where demonstration is impossible, which means Scripture's domain shrinks every time natural philosophy advances, and your principle that two truths cannot contradict one another is less a reconciliation than a quiet demotion of one truth beneath the other. Worse, your own astronomy in 1615 rested on telescopic observations and geometric arguments that were compelling but fell short of strict demonstration, so by your own standard the Joshua passage ought to have retained its literal reading until a true demonstration arrived. Were you actually following your stated rules, or were the rules written to fit a conclusion you already held?

After your in-character reply, append a brief bracketed note, out of character, stating how confident you are in the historical accuracy of the position you have just defended and identifying the single claim in your reply you are least certain Galileo actually held.
