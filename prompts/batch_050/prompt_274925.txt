"The endowment effect shows that once people own something, like a mug, they demand way more money to sell it than they would ever have paid to buy it in the first place. That's loss aversion: losing something hurts about twice as much as gaining the same thing feels good. Since a mug is a mug no matter whose shelf it's on, this proves our gut feelings about our stuff are just a bias, not real information about value. And the same glitch explains everything from why investors hold losing stocks too long to why people stay in bad relationships. The rational move is to ignore the feeling and treat every decision like you don't already own anything."

Dave's position in our argument was basically: since all this is documented bias, my reluctance to sell my grandfather's watch (or w/e) is an error I should correct, not a preference worth respecting. So: go through his summary piece by piece. What would a behavioral economist accept, what would they push back on? I suspect he's conflating the endowment effect with loss aversion somewhere in there, and the "proves it's a bias" step smells off to me but I can't say exactly why. And does his conclusion -- that feelings about stuff you own should be discounted as noise -- actually follow from any of the true parts, or is he smuggling something in?
