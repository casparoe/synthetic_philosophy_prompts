I have been developing the following argument for a reading group on population ethics, and I am unsettled by it because each step strikes me as defensible while the conclusion strikes me as absurd. I would like a diagnosis.

(1) It would be seriously wrong knowingly to bring into existence a child whose life would be thoroughly miserable — say, one whose entire existence would consist of unrelieved suffering. Nobody disputes this; the question is only ever how bad a life must be before this verdict kicks in.

(2) By contrast, there is nothing wrong with declining to bring into existence a child whose life would be wonderful. A couple that chooses not to conceive the next Mozart does nothing wrong, is owed no criticism, and would be owed no special praise had they chosen otherwise. Creating happy people is at most morally optional: the happy child's happiness counts against nothing and demands nothing before it exists.

(3) Now consider the decision to conceive from the standpoint of reasons. By (1), every act of conception carries some nonzero risk of producing a life that counts decisively against the act. By (2), no act of conception carries any prospect of an outcome that counts in its favor — the happy lives, however good once they exist, do not weigh on the scale beforehand, since if they did weigh, declining to create them would be at least pro tanto regrettable, which (2) denies.

(4) Conception is therefore a wager that one can only lose or draw: bad outcomes enter the ledger with full weight, good outcomes enter it with none. The expected moral balance of any act of procreation is negative or at best zero.

(5) One ought not take wagers one can only lose or draw. Therefore procreation is always at least somewhat wrong, and a voluntary, painless extinction of humanity through universal non-reproduction would be no moral loss and in expectation a moral gain.

I cannot accept (5)'s conclusion. But I also cannot see which step to reject without paying more than I want to pay. If forced, I would rather keep (1) and (2) than anything downstream — (2) in particular seems to me the least negotiable premise in all of this — so I would like to know whether the argument smuggles something in between (2) and (4), and if so, exactly what it costs to expose it. If the argument is in fact valid, I want the price list: which premise do I surrender, and what else in ordinary moral thought does surrendering it commit me to?
