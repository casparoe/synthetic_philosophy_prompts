A colleague and I have been stuck on the following argument, which appears to show that Bayesianism imposes no substantive constraints on belief at all. Neither of us can accept the conclusion, but neither of us can locate the mistake.

(1) When evidence is certain — when I simply learn that E — rationality requires updating by conditionalization: my new credence in any hypothesis H should become my old conditional credence P(H | E).

(2) Much of our evidence, however, is uncertain. A glimpse in bad light may leave me, say, 0.7 confident that the cloth is blue, rather than certain. Jeffrey's rule covers exactly this case: P_new(H) = r · P(H | E) + (1 − r) · P(H | ¬E), where r is the new confidence in E that the experience produces.

(3) Nothing in the Bayesian machinery determines r. My prior credences and my conditional probabilities P(H | E) and P(H | ¬E) fix the form of the update given r, but they are silent about what r itself should be.

(4) What the machinery does not fix, rationality leaves open. So any value of r counts as a rationally permissible response to a given experience.

(5) By choosing a suitable partition {E, ¬E} and a suitable value of r, I can reach essentially any posterior credence in any hypothesis from any prior. For any credences I happen to end up with, there is some description of my evidence under which they result from a Jeffrey update.

(6) Therefore Bayesianism rules nothing out: every possible pattern of belief revision can be represented as rational updating on evidence, certain or uncertain.

(7) But a theory of rational belief that rules nothing out is not a theory of rational belief. So either conditionalization is not, after all, the rational response to certain evidence, or the Bayesian framework is empty.

I would very much like to keep (1), and I would rather deny that uncertain evidence admits of any formal rule at all than accept (6). But every step looks plausible to me, and my colleague insists the argument is valid. Which step should be rejected? Why does that step look compelling even though it fails? And what exactly does rejecting it cost — in particular, if (4) is the culprit, I would like an account of what does fix r, if neither the prior nor the agent's free choice does; and if (5) is the culprit, I would like to know which updates the rule forbids that my construction overlooked.
