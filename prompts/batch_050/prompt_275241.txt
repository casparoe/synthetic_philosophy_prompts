I'm TAing a philosophy/econ crossover course and grading short-answer responses. The exam question was:

"State Harsanyi's aggregation theorem (the social aggregation theorem from his 1955 paper) as precisely as you can, and explain what it does and does not establish on its own about the truth of utilitarianism."

Here's the student's answer, out of 10 points:

"Harsanyi's theorem says: if each individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, and society's preferences over the same lotteries also satisfy those axioms, and society's preferences respect the Pareto condition (if everyone's indifferent society is indifferent, and if at least one person strictly prefers something and no one opposes it, society prefers it), then social utility must be a weighted sum of the individual utilities. So the social welfare function is linear in individual utilities.

This establishes that utilitarianism is the correct moral theory. Since the social utility is a sum of individual utilities, and there's no principled way to justify weighting one person's utility more than another's, the weights must be equal, which is just classical utilitarianism. The vNM framework also gives us cardinal utilities, which solves the problem of interpersonal utility comparisons, since each person's utility is measured on a cardinal scale rather than just an ordinal one. So the theorem closes off the main objections to utilitarianism about aggregation and measurability."
