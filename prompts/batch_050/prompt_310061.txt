I'm teaching a unit on advance directives in a bioethics seminar, and my students keep splitting into two camps that talk past each other, so I'd like a model dialogue I can use as a teaching text. The scenario is this: a woman in her fifties, sharp and fiercely independent, watches her mother decline into dementia and, deeply unsettled by what she sees, signs an advance directive stating that if she ever reaches a comparable state, she refuses all life-prolonging treatment, including antibiotics for infections. Twenty years later she has advanced dementia. She no longer remembers signing the directive and would not understand it if shown. But by all appearances she is content: she enjoys her meals, her music, her grandchildren's visits, and when a nurse asks whether she is happy, she says yes. She now develops pneumonia, which could be treated with a routine course of antibiotics -- or left to run its course, in which case she will likely die comfortably within days.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers disagreeing about what should be done. One defends the authority of the earlier self: the directive expresses the patient's most settled, reflective values, the values around which she built her whole life, and the present demented woman is not so much a person with a new preference as a person whose capacity for preference has been diminished -- so letting her die honors who she actually is. The other defends the authority of the present self: the woman who signed the directive no longer exists in any morally relevant sense, the person in front of us plainly values her current life, whatever we say about its quality from the outside, and a document written by a stranger two decades ago cannot license letting her die of a treatable infection. Both characters should be philosophically competent and neither should be a strawman. In particular, the defender of the directive should press hard on the problem of how we can call it "her" good if the person enjoying the life bears so little psychological continuity with the person who will benefit from the decision, and the defender of the present self should press hard on the counterfactual: what the signer would have wanted had she been able to foresee that her demented life would be happy rather than the horror she imagined.

The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth -- each character responding to the other's actual arguments, conceding points where concession is due, and exploiting concessions rather than restarting from talking points. Please let at least one genuinely difficult move land without a satisfying reply (for instance, the question of whose interest the directive was protecting in the first place, or whether a happy demented patient can even have the interest in dying that the directive attributes to her). It should run roughly 600 to 900 words, end without a tidy resolution, and be readable enough that an undergraduate could follow it while still being substantive enough that I couldn't have written it by stitching together a textbook summary.
