I have a set of verdicts about which outcomes are better than which, and I want to know whether any single principle captures them — and if not, which verdict has to go.

1. A week with a barely noticeable itch is better than a week of agony.
2. Consider a spectrum of experiences running from that itch to that agony, each differing from its neighbour by the smallest difference anyone could detect. No single step, I hold, can make things worse: a difference too small to notice cannot turn a better outcome into a worse one.
3. Betterness must be transitive. Anyone who denies it can be led round a circle of deals, each accepted as an improvement, that returns them to where they started minus a fee; intransitivity is a defect of judgement, not a feature of value.
4. Adding people with lives well worth living to a population does not make the world worse, even if it lowers the average quality of life.
5. Yet a world of ten billion people with lives barely worth living is worse than a world of one billion with wonderful lives, even if the first contains more total happiness.
6. Some pairs have no ranking at all: a short life of extraordinary achievement and turmoil versus a long life of quiet contentment strike me as neither better, worse, nor exactly equal — and this unrankability relation need not be transitive either.

Verdicts 1–3 cannot all stand, and 4–5 reproduce the same shape of problem through steps each individually acceptable. Give me a principled account that saves as much of this pattern as possible. Test at least the following candidates against every verdict: deny that imperceptible differences are always irrelevant, and explain how the resulting vagueness is to be handled; reject transitivity of 'better than' while keeping choice transitive; distinguish 'better than' from 'rationally preferable', so that the money-pump argument constrains only choice; and treat some comparisons as genuinely indeterminate. For each candidate, state precisely which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and run it against a case I have not considered: a three-way choice in which each pairwise comparison turns on a different dimension, so that the ranking flips depending on which two options I happen to compare first. Conclude with the candidate you would recommend and the honest cost of adopting it. Please answer in British English.
