Ok so quick background because it matters for why I care. I'm a grad student and for the past year I've had this pattern where I know exactly what I should be doing — write in the mornings, end a relationship that needs ending, call my grandmother more — and I just... don't. A friend who's into Chinese philosophy said I needed to read Wang Yangming because his whole thing is the unity of knowledge and action, and honestly it hit me like a truck. He has this example somewhere (I think in his commentary on the Great Learning but don't quote me): when you smell something awful, you don't first smell it and THEN, as a separate second step, dislike it. The disliking is already there in the smelling. Genuine knowing is like that. And he says somewhere that knowing without acting isn't real knowing at all — like claiming to know what something tastes like without ever tasting it.

But then I tried to reconstruct the doctrine as an actual argument and it seems to prove something insane. Here it is:

(1) Really knowing that something is good — the way you know a bad smell is bad, not the way you know a fact from a textbook — just is being disposed to act on it. There's no extra step between the knowing and the doing.

(2) So someone who consistently fails to act on what they claim to know doesn't really know it. What they have is secondhand report, hearsay, whatever you want to call it.

(3) Since knowing and acting were never two separate things, the only route INTO real knowledge is action itself. Words and explanations can produce at most the hearsay from (2).

(4) So no amount of teaching, reading, or lecturing can give real moral knowledge to anyone who isn't already practicing.

(5) But Wang Yangming spent most of his adult life doing exactly that — lecturing, writing long letters to students, running what were basically academies — all apparently aimed at getting people to really know the good.

(6) So the doctrine implies its own author's life's work was an embarassment, pointless by its own lights.

That can't be right, so something has to give, but every step looked fine when I wrote it down. I'd really like to keep (1) and (2) because they're the part that explained my whole year to me, and I'd like (5) to not be a disaster. Which step do I reject, why did it look convincing, and what does rejecting it actually cost? If more than one repair is available, price them against each other. And separately: does the bad smell example even support (1) the way I'm leaning on it, or am I squeezing it too hard?
