A colleague of mine has settled views about moral responsibility and luck, and I want to extrapolate them to a case we have been arguing about. Her verdicts so far:

- Two drivers are equally careless in failing to check their brakes. One's brakes fail on an empty road; the other's fail as a child steps into the street, and the child is killed. She holds that the second driver deserves a substantially harsher punishment, though she admits the first is no better as a person.
- Two poisoners act from identical motives and with identical plans. One's victim drinks the tea and dies; the other's victim happens to pour it out. She holds that the two are equally blameworthy and deserve the same punishment — the lucky failure aggravates nothing.
- Identical twins are separated at birth; one is raised gently, the other brutally, and becomes cruel. When the cruel twin commits a crime, she refuses any discount for his upbringing: he is fully blameworthy.
- A man becomes violent because of a brain tumor pressing on the relevant regions; absent the tumor he would have been peaceable. She holds that he is not blameworthy at all.
- Of two people with the same ruthless disposition, only one is ever presented with the opportunity to betray a friend, and takes it. She holds that the untested one has been merely lucky, but that her blame of the tested one is unaffected by this — the untested one is not thereby to be condemned, nor the tested one excused.

The disputed case: a would-be murderer takes a careful shot at his enemy from a rooftop. The shot misses purely by chance — a gust of wind. At that very moment, and entirely independently, the victim suffers a fatal aneurysm and dies on the spot. The intended outcome occurred, but the agent did not cause it.

Please do the following. First, predict her verdict on the disputed case: blameworthiness and deserved punishment alike, relative to both the successful murderer and the failed poisoner. Second, identify the rule she is most plausibly running — for instance, one that tracks harm caused, one that tracks harm that merely occurs, one that tracks the completeness of the agent's will, or some hybrid — noting that her earlier verdicts are consistent with more than one of these. Third, say which candidate rules diverge on this case and what each predicts. Fourth, describe the further case, not among those above, that would best discriminate between the rules that survive.
