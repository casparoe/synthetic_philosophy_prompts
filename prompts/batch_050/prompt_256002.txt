I'm in a reading group on decision theory and we just finished the unit on imprecise probabilities -- the idea that rational credence might be a whole set of probability functions rather than a single precise one, like in Ellsberg-style cases where people hate betting on ambiguity. At some point someone claimed that agents with imprecise credences can rationally turn down free information, which seems wild, because for a precise Bayesian there's the classic result (Good's theorem) that free evidence can never hurt you: you always weakly prefer to look at the info before you decide. Our group got stuck trying to actually construct a clean case of this, so I'm outsourcing it to you.

Here's what I want: build a decision problem where an agent with imprecise credences rationally refuses free information (or even pays a small amount to not receive it), while no precise Bayesian placed in the same situation would refuse it. Constraints: the setting must be ordinary and realistic -- hiring, a medical test, buying a used car, whatever -- no urns full of colored balls and no casino setups, since those make the imprecision feel artificial. The information has to be genuinely free and genuinely decision-relevant; no cheating by making it costly to read or upsetting to know. And the refusal has to actually fall out of the imprecise agent's decision rule, not be stipulated.

After the scenario, do the work: show the reasoning for both agents step by step, and be explicit about which decision rule you're using for the imprecise agent (maximin, Gamma-maximin, whatever you pick) and why the agent's credal set makes looking at the info worse than not looking. Then say which feature of the case is doing all the work and what happens if you remove it -- does it collapse back into Good's theorem going through? Finally, steelman the precise Bayesian's counterattack: presumably they'll say the imprecise agent is just being dynamically inconsistant, or that the decision rule is smuggling in a weird kind of pessimism that isn't really about information at all. Tell me whether you think that attack lands.
