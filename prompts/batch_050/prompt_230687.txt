So I do forecasting for a small grantmaking org — we put probabilities on stuff like "will this org hit its milestones by Q3," "will this policy pass the state legislature this session," "will this research program produce a replicable result within two years" — maybe 30-40 forecasts a quarter, an hour or two each, and the program officers actually make decisions off them, which is mildly terrifying.

I've read all the standard stuff about the Principal Principle and deference to objective chance, at least at the level of a smart layperson who's been on LW since 2015. And the thing that bugs me is that the principle, as I understand it, only tells you what to do once you have the objective chances: set your credence equal to the chance, conditional on admissible info. But basically every decision I face happens way upstream of that, in figuring out what even counts as a chance worth deferring to. Sportsbook odds, a base rate from a reference class I picked, the output of some statistical model, a Metaculus community prediction, my boss's gut feeling after twenty years in the field — all of these get called "the expert probability" by somebody, and they can't all be chances in the relevant sense, since some of them are just other people's credences in a trenchcoat.

Write me an actual procedure I can run per-forecast, in order, for deciding how much to defer to whatever "objective-ish" number is on the table versus my own judgment. Concretely:

1. A test for whether the candidate number is the kind of thing the deference norm even applies to — a genuine chance or a good-enough stand-in — versus someone's dressed-up opinion, and what to do with the trenchcoat cases (weight them as evidence instead? defer partially?).
2. An admissibility check: how to tell whether I hold information that makes deferring to the chance wrong even when the chance is legit. Especially the self-referential case, which is like half my workload — when I forecast whether an org will hit its milestones, my own forecast can change what the program officers do, which changes the outcome. Does the procedure just break there, or is there a principled patch?
3. What to do when two candidate "chances" disagree — market says 40%, model says 70% — given that you can't defer to both and averaging feels like it violates the spirit of the thing.
4. An explicit branch for "no admissible chance exists," since I suspect that's most of my cases, and I want the procedure to say so out loud instead of letting me quietly substitute a vibes-based base rate.

Then run it on three of my actual recurring cases: (a) the milestone forecast above where the forecast itself is an intervention, (b) a ballot initiative where polls, betting markets, and a fundamentals model all disagree, and (c) a "will this AI capability demo work by end of year" question where there's genuinely no reference class. I want at least one case where the procedure tells me to do something I'll hate, like ignoring my shiny inside view.

Also mark the steps where the philosophy genuinely runs out and it's judgment all the way down — I'd rather have a short honest procedure than a long one that pretends Lewis settles things he obviously doesn't. If parts of my framing are confused (e.g. if "defer partially" isn't even coherent) say that instead of accomodating it.
