After forty years in biochemistry, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy, and I keep returning to the question of whether a person could survive the gradual replacement of their brain's components by functionally identical synthetic parts. I have watched two colleagues of mine -- one a committed functionalist who holds that what matters is the preservation of pattern and causal organization, the other a biological-continuity theorist who holds that identity is tied to the living tissue itself -- argue about this for months without producing anything either would co-sign. I would like you to draft the document they should have produced instead: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between them.

The statement should contain the following, in this order:

First, a numbered list of claims both parties can accept. I expect these to include mundane but relevant facts (the continuous turnover of matter and cells in an ordinary human body; the successful clinical use of neural prostheses; the absence of any observed discontinuity in personality when individual neurons die and are not replaced), as well as agreements about what the thought experiments do and do not establish.

Second, a precise statement of each remaining disagreement, each formulated so that the party whose view it describes would accept the wording as fair to their own position. Please take care to distinguish genuine disagreements from ones that are merely terminological, and say explicitly which you take to fall in each category and why.

Third, a list of cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would compel at least one side to revise its position.

Fourth, for each crux, a case or line of evidence both parties agree bears on it, with each side's verdict recorded in advance. Please include at least the following cases: (a) replacement of one neuron per day by a functional prosthesis over several decades, with no interruption of consciousness; (b) the same procedure, except the removed neurons are kept alive and, at the end, reassembled into a second biologically original brain; (c) instantaneous destructive scanning followed by reconstruction from new matter; (d) ordinary metabolism, in which the constituent atoms of the brain are exchanged over time with no prosthesis involved. If the two camps give the same verdict on one of these cases but for different reasons, note that, since it may conceal a disagreement that a further case would expose.

Close the statement with a short section, signed by both parties, specifying what -- if anything -- each concedes would count as a reason to abandon their view.
