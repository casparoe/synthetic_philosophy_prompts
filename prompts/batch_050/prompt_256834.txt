A friend in my department wrote up the following argument and has been defending it all semester. I've gone over it repeatedly and cannot find the step to reject, but I also cannot accept the conclusion, because if it's sound then nothing I do as an individual consumer or citizen has any moral weight whatsoever, and that can't be right. Here it is, as he presents it:

(1) Whether a chicken is raised and slaughtered is determined by the ordering decisions of grocery retailers and restaurant suppliers, not by the contents of any individual shopping cart. A supermarket orders poultry in large batches based on aggregate sales patterns.

(2) For any particular purchase I might make, that purchase falls below the threshold that would alter any retailer's order. If I buy the chicken, the same number of chickens are ordered next month; if I decline, the same number are ordered. The causal chain from my cart to the slaughterhouse runs through an aggregation mechanism that is insensitive to any single unit.

(3) Therefore no particular purchase of mine causes any additional chicken to be raised or killed.

(4) An action that causes no additional harm is, with respect to that harm, morally permissible. (Blame and obligation track what one actually brings about; morality cannot require me to refrain from actions that change nothing.)

(5) Therefore buying the chicken is morally permissible, and ethical vegetarianism motivated by concern for the animals is, at best, symbolic self-expression rather than a response to any real difference I make.

(6) The same reasoning applies to other collective harms: a single transatlantic flight's emissions are climatically undetectable and the plane flies regardless; a single vote in a national election never decides the outcome. So conscientious abstention in all such cases is equally symbolic.

(7) Since (6) extends the conclusion to every large-scale collective harm, and since the conclusion in the voting case is just obviously true (no one thinks my staying home from the polls changes who governs), the reasoning must be sound across the board, and the discomfort we feel at (5) is mere squeamishness about admitting it.

I want to keep (1) and (2) if possible — they seem to be straightforward empirical facts about how modern food supply chains work — and I'd also like to keep the intuition behind (4), that moral fault has something to do with what you actually bring about. What I cannot accept is (5) generalized via (6): the view that all individual participation in collective harms is morally inert. That conclusion seems to prove too much.
