I run a peer-learning circle in my town — a dozen of us, none with formal training, who have been working through material on AI safety and ethics together for the past year. We recently adopted a practice from formal schooling: one member drafts a written exam, another takes it, and a third grades it, so that we all get practice from each role. It is my turn to grade, and I want to do it properly rather than by gut feeling. Below is the exam question (written by a fellow member) and the answer submitted by the student. Could you assign a grade on the usual A-through-F scale, and give me point-by-point comments that justify it? I would like each comment to identify a specific claim or step in the student's reasoning and say whether it is correct, incorrect, or incomplete, and why — I will then adapt these into my own feedback, so please flag anything subtle rather than just the obvious points.

Exam question: Writers on advanced AI often distinguish "intent alignment" (getting an AI system to do what its operator intends it to do) from "value alignment" (getting an AI system to pursue what is actually good or right for humanity). Explain the distinction, explain why achieving intent alignment alone could still be dangerous even if every operator were sincere and competent, and say which of the two you think is the more urgent research problem and why.

Student's answer: Intent alignment means the system does what its operator asks, in the sense the operator meant it; value alignment means the system acts in accordance with human values. Intent alignment is mainly a technical problem of getting the system to understand and follow instructions, while value alignment is mainly a problem of getting enough input from users so the system reflects what most people want. Intent alignment alone is insufficient because a well-intentioned operator can still issue commands with unforeseen side effects — an intent-aligned system follows the intention as the operator actually has it, so if that intention was formed carelessly, the damage is done by faithful execution. In practice, though, value alignment reduces to intent alignment scaled up: once a system is intent aligned to many operators simultaneously, balancing their competing intentions, the result just is alignment to our collective values, since our values are nothing over and above what we collectively intend and prefer. So the more urgent problem is intent alignment — get that right and make operators representative, and value alignment follows automatically.
