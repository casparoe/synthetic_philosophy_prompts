Hi, I'm taking a second-year poltical philosophy course and we've just finished Mill's On Liberty and moved on to a unit on paternalism and nudging. For seminar prep we have to write down where we stand, and I'm worried my position doesn't hang together. This bit of chapter IV is doing a lot of work for me:

"But neither one person, nor any number of persons, is warranted in saying to another human creature of ripe years, that he shall not do with his life for his own benefit what he chooses to do with it. He is the person most interested in his own well-being, the interest which any other person, except in cases of strong personal attachment, can have in it, is trifling, compared with that which he himself has; the interest which society has in him individually (except as to his conduct to others) is fractional, and altogether indirect: while, with respect to his own feelings and circumstances, the most ordinary man or woman has means of knowledge immeasurably surpassing those that can be possessed by any one else. The interference of society to overrule his judgment and purposes in what only regards himself, must be grounded on general presumptions; which may be altogether wrong, and even if right, are as likely as not to be misapplied to individual cases, by persons no better acquainted with the circumstances of such cases than those are who look at them merely from without."

So here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Mill's argument in this passage is basically right, and it's why I oppose hard paternalism, e.g. banning cigarettes for the smoker's own good.
2. Soft-paternalist nudges are fine and often good policy — opt-out pension enrolment is my paradigm case.
3. The behavioural science is sound: inertia, present bias and so on mean people systematically fail to pursue even their own good in self-regarding choices, which is exactly why the pension default does so much work.
4. Sin taxes aimed at the buyer's own good (sugar tax, cigarette duties) are illegitimate in a way defaults aren't, because you can't practically avoid a tax whereas you can always just opt out.
5. The line I want to draw is steering someone's choice versus overruling their judgement about their own life, and I take myself to be getting that line straight out of the Mill passage.

Do these five actually cohere? Please audit them: which pairs genuinely conflict, which only look like they do, ranked by severity, with what I'd have to give up for each and the cheapest single revision. Two things I'm especially unsure about: does Mill's "means of knowledge" claim survive the bias research in (3), or is he making a comparative claim (you versus any outsider) that the research doesn't actually touch — and if it's comparative, does the pension case break it? And can (4) really stand once (3) says inertia is the whole reason defaults work, since that makes the opt-out pretty nominal? Also tell me honestly whether my reading in (5) is right, given what Mill goes on to say about persuasion and advice being permissible. Please answer in British English.
