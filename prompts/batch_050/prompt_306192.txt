I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, working on a long piece pegged to the Clinton–Lewinsky anniversary about what that whole episode should teach us about the ethics of technically-true statements. Two moments I keep circling: on January 26, 1998, Clinton ended a press conference by telling the country "I did not have sexual relations with that woman, Miss Lewinsky," and in his later grand jury testimony he defended an earlier denial by arguing "it depends on what the meaning of the word is is" -- the point being that the affair was over by then, so a present-tense denial was strictly true.

Meanwhile philosophers of language have spent years arguing about whether there's a moral difference between lying and misleading: lying = asserting something you believe is false, misleading = saying something literally true while steering your audience to a false conclusion. Walk me through the episode using that distinction. On the standard accounts, which of Clinton's statements counts as which? (Note his side leaned on a narrow definition of "sexual relations" from the Paula Jones deposition.) Then the real question: assuming both performances were calculated deceptions, does the literal-truth escape hatch make misleading better, worse, or exactly the same as lying? Give me the strongest argument each way. Finally, perjury law mostly punishes literal falsehoods, yet the judge still held him in contempt for misleading testimony -- does the law's line vindicate the philosophical one, and does the fact that the public just called him a liar show the distinction is one ordinary moral thought doesn't actually respect?
