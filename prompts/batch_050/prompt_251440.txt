I moderate a small music forum, and two of our longest-running members recently had a fight in a thread that I've been turning over ever since. Some background: one of them, whom I'll call the archivist, runs a tiny reissue label that restores out-of-print live jazz recordings -- cleaning up tape hiss, rebalancing the mix, that kind of thing. The other, the performer, is a working session musician. The argument started when someone described the archivist's remaster of a 1963 club date as "not the real album." Here's roughly how it went:

(1) The archivist said the album just *is* the recording. Forty people were at the gig; everyone else who has loved this album for sixty years has loved an audio artifact, and the artifact is what he spent months working on.

(2) The performer said the album is the performance -- a dated, located event involving particular musicians -- and a recording is evidence of that event, the way a photograph is evidence of a building. On his view the remaster is neither the performance nor a new performance; it's a restoration, like cleaning a painting.

(3) The archivist replied that this makes his remaster ontologically nothing at all: not the work, not a new work, just a cleaner window. He also pointed out that plenty of electronic tracks were never performed at all -- they were assembled in software from samples and MIDI -- so there is no event to point to, and the recording plainly must be the work in those cases.

(4) The performer conceded electronic music but said that just shows different kinds of musical works exist: some works are performances (reached through recordings), and some are studio constructions where the recording is the work itself. A jazz album is the former even though we only ever consume it through the latter. A rephotographed photo of a building is a new photo of the same building; a remaster is a new recording of the same performance.

(5) The archivist's final move: the original tape has audible crowd noise and one wrong note the bandleader always said ruined the take. On his remaster he patched that bar using a phrase from an earlier take of the same tune from the same night. So which performance, exactly, is it a recording of now? And if he'd used a take from a different night entirely, would it become a recording of a performance that never happened?

I've read enough rationalist material to be suspicious of arguments that might dissolve once both sides taboo their words, so part of what I want is a check on whether this dispute is even substantive. Please go through the five moves one by one and say who's right about what at each step, identify what (if anything) both are missing, give an overall verdict, and address whether this is a genuine disagreement about what musical works are or a merely verbal one. I'd like the answer in American English.
