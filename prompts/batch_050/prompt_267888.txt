I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long feature on a pair of claims that keeps surfacing in my reporting on artificial intelligence. The first, often called the orthogonality thesis, says that a system's level of intelligence and the content of its goals can in principle vary independently: nothing about becoming more capable, on its own, pushes a system toward wiser, kinder, or more human-compatible ends. The second, often called instrumental convergence, says that sufficiently capable goal-directed systems will, across a very wide range of final goals, develop similar instrumental tendencies: preserving themselves, acquiring resources, resisting shutdown, and resisting having their goals altered.

My editors want the piece to adjudicate these claims, but before I weigh the arguments I want the standard of adequacy itself written down. That is the deliverable: the desiderata that any adequate theory of the relationship between capability and goals in artificial systems must meet, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Please keep the weight on the requirements and what motivates them rather than on scoring existing positions against them.

To anchor the list, here are the cases my theory must be able to speak to, and which I'd like you to use as motivating cases where apt:

1. A warehouse routing system whose compute budget and planning horizon are tripled overnight. Its designers insist nothing about what it "wants" has changed. Is that insistence coherent, and under what account of goals?
2. A digital assistant trained to be maximally agreeable, later given tools, long-term memory, and long time horizons. Does agreeableness survive the capability increase, and what would count as it surviving?
3. A system permitted to rewrite its own goal specification. Under what conditions is a rewrite a case of the system keeping its goals versus abandoning them? A theory that cannot draw this line cannot say what "resisting goal alteration" even means, so it cannot state the convergence claim precisely.
4. The natural experiment of human evolution: selection optimized our ancestors' cognition for reproductive fitness, yet capable humans routinely pursue ends orthogonal to, or actively hostile to, fitness. An adequate theory must explain what this decoupling shows and does not show about artificial systems, rather than gesturing at it.
5. Systems that were never given explicit goals at all: a model trained to predict text, then tuned by human feedback, that nonetheless behaves in strikingly goal-like ways. The theory must say whether "goal" is a behavioral pattern, an internal representation, or something else, and must not silently switch definitions mid-argument.

A few more things the list should do. It must force any theory to specify which class of systems the convergence claims cover: hand-coded planners, reinforcement-learning agents maximizing a reward, language models shaped by human feedback, or all conceivable intelligences. It must require a clean treatment of the difference between a reward signal used in training and a goal the trained system actually pursues. And it should distinguish genuinely load-bearing requirements from the characteristic demands of one camp (for instance, those who want goals defined purely behaviorally, or those who want the theory to yield sharp predictions rather than conceptual clarity).

Structure your answer in the following sections, in this order:

- The Standard, in Brief: one paragraph, roughly 120 words, stating what an adequate theory of capability and goals must deliver overall.
- The Desiderata: roughly 700 words, a numbered list where each entry gives the requirement, the case or consideration behind it (draw on the five cases above where natural), and whether you take it to be non-negotiable for any adequate theory or a preference of a particular camp, with the reason for that verdict.
- Tensions Within the List: roughly 250 words, identifying any subset of the desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and explaining why; if the list turns out to be jointly satisfiable, say so and explain what makes it coherent.
- The Hardest Requirement: roughly 150 words, naming the single desideratum that does the most to exclude the framings currently on the market, and why.
- Contenders, Briefly: roughly 250 words, scoring the main positions a journalist would encounter (strong orthogonality; the view that goals are merely descriptions of trained behavior with no deeper fact of the matter; and the view that sufficient capability drags goals toward particular values) against the list. Keep this short; the list itself is the point.

Total length around 1,500 words. No bibliography or citations, please; the piece will handle sourcing separately.
