Over the summer I fell in with a reading group working through Szasz's critique of psychiatry, and one member -- a medical student who is sympathetic to the critique but increasingly alarmed by where it seems to lead -- presented us with an argument she has been refining. I will lay it out as she gave it, because none of us could find the exit, and I want a proper diagnosis. (1) A condition counts as an illness only if there is some bodily abnormality -- a lesion, a dysfunction, a pathogen, a chemical derangement -- that can be identified as the cause of the condition's manifestations. (2) The abnormality must be identifiable independently of those manifestations: if the only evidence that the brain is disordered is the behavior the diagnosis is supposed to explain, then the diagnosis is circular, a label masquerading as an explanation. (3) For the central conditions psychiatry classifies as mental illnesses -- depression, schizophrenia, ADHD, the anxiety disorders -- no such independently identifiable abnormality exists; diagnosis proceeds by behavioral checklists and self-report, and the brain differences occasionally reported in studies are statistical, after the fact, and play no role in anyone's actual diagnosis. (4) Therefore these conditions are not illnesses but problems in living, and the entire edifice built on calling them illnesses -- involuntary commitment, the insanity defense, sick-role exemptions, coerced treatment -- rests on a category error. Each premise struck us as defensible, and she wants the conclusion.

Here is where it starts to prove too much. She noticed, to her discomfort, that (5) the very same reasoning applies to migraine, tinnitus, fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome, and most chronic pain: these are diagnosed from the patient's report of subjective experience, no lesion or dysfunction is independently identifiable in the typical case, and the report is the evidence. (6) So by (1)-(4), these are not illnesses either, and a neurologist treating a migraineur is committing the same category error as a psychiatrist treating a depressive. And then someone in the group drove it further: (7) the independence requirement in (2) could not have been met for almost any illness before the nineteenth century, since consumption, epilepsy, dropsy, and scurvy were identified by their manifest presentations long before anyone could identify a bacillus, a seizure focus, or a vitamin deficiency -- so on this argument almost nothing was an illness until quite recently, and every physician before Virchow was merely labeling problems in living. That cannot be right; the history of medicine is not the history of a single centuries-old category error suddenly corrected by microscopy.

So the argument must go wrong somewhere between (1) and (4), or else one of those premises must be given up. Please tell me which step you reject, explain why that step looked fine -- it really did look fine to all of us -- and then price the alternatives: if I weaken (1) or (2), what exactly is left of the critique of psychiatry that my friend wants to preserve, and is there a version of her argument that still bites against, say, involuntary commitment or the medical framing of ordinary sadness, without also ruling migraine and pre-modern tuberculosis out of the category of illness? She would most like to keep (4) in some form and would rather sacrifice (1) than (2), but she wants to know what each choice actually costs.
