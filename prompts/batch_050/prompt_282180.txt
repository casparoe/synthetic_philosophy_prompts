I'm a graduate student in philosophy of mind, and for my comprehensive exams I've been asked to submit a one-page statement of my working commitments in the area, which the committee will then try to dismantle in the oral. Before I hand it in, I'd like an audit of whether the set actually hangs together. Here is what I currently hold. (a) Functionalism is true: what makes a mental state the state it is -- a belief, a desire, a pain -- is its causal-functional role within the system, not the material the system is made of. (b) It follows, and I accept, that mental states are multiply realizable: an octopus, a suitably organized silicon robot, or an alien with methane-based biochemistry could all literally be in pain, provided the right functional organization is in place. (c) Nonetheless, I cannot bring myself to believe that the nation-of-China setup would be conscious -- that is, if the entire population of China were coordinated by radio so as to replicate, person by person, the functional organization of a human brain for an hour, there would (I think) be nothing it is like to be that collective system; the individuals would be conscious, but the whole would not have experiences of its own. (d) I am a physicalist, and I insist that mental causation is genuine: my pain really causes my wincing, and any view that renders mental states causally idle is, for me, a reductio of that view. (e) Finally, I hold that psychology is explanatorily autonomous from neuroscience: even in principle, psychological explanations will not be replaced by or reduced to neural ones.

Please go through these and tell me which commitments are actually in tension and which only appear to be. For each genuine tension, state which members of the set generate it, how severe it is, and what the cheapest repair would be -- where "cheap" means revising or qualifying the least central commitment while preserving as much of the rest as possible. I'd like the tensions ranked rather than presented as a flat list, and for each one I'd like to know what a sophisticated defender of functionalism would say in response, since my examiners will certainly know those responses. In particular, I'm unsure whether my physicalism and my commitment to the autonomy of psychology are really at odds, or whether one of the standard non-reductive positions dissolves that worry; but I have a nagging sense that (d) causes trouble somewhere I haven't pinned down, and that (c) is not as innocent as it looks when combined with (b). If there is a single revision that would clean up the most tensions at once, identify it and tell me honestly what it costs.
