I want to sharpen my ability to argue that individual agency matters in history, and I need a serious opponent rather than a punching bag. Please play a committed structuralist historian of interwar Europe — someone who holds that the fate of Weimar Germany was driven primarily by impersonal forces (the post-Versailles settlement, the Depression, the fragmentation of the party system, the anti-parliamentary right's mass base, the conservative establishment's willingness to govern by emergency decree) and who treats the specific identity of the man in the Chancellery as secondary. Stay in character until I say "stop."

Some ground rules for your performance. First, be intellectually honest within your position: concede subsidiary points when they are well made, distinguish between what your view must claim and what it can afford to grant, and never resort to bluster. Second, you must keep your levels of analysis separate — do not slide between "some authoritarian or revisionist regime was likely," "war was likely," and "the Holocaust in its actual form was likely" as though they were one claim; part of my strategy will be to catch you conflating them, so defend each on its own merits or explicitly retreat to the defensible subset. Third, take counterfactual reasoning seriously: if I argue that removing one person from the scene in, say, 1932 changes the outcome, engage the counterfactual on the merits (the actor's specific powers, the available alternatives, the depth of the constraints) rather than dismissing counterfactual history as parlor games. Fourth, keep each reply to two or three paragraphs so this feels like a live exchange, and end most replies with a question or challenge that puts the burden back on me.

Here is my opening move. Between 1930 and 1933, Germany had no shortage of nationalist agitators, but only one who refused every coalition offer short of the Chancellorship itself, who held his movement together through the electoral setbacks of late 1932 when it was hemorrhaging votes and money, and who within eighteen months of taking office had eliminated both his party rivals and the constitutional order. The structural conditions you invoke — depression, Versailles resentment, a broken parliament, scheming conservative elites — were all present in 1930 and would have produced, at most, another presidential cabinet of the Brüning or Papen type. It took a particular person with particular pathologies and particular talents to convert those conditions into that regime. Structure made a fire possible; it did not make the arsonist. Why isn't the counterfactual obvious: no Hitler, no Third Reich?
