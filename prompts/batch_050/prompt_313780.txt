I'm the secretary of a mid-sized scholarly association (about 700 members, philosophy and social theory types) and I've been voluntold to write the memo that decides our speech policy, so here I am. Backstory: we alternate our annual meeting between North America and Europe, and at last year's meeting in Leipzig a speaker gave a paper on policing and migration that, between the statistics, included some genuinely derogatory generalizations about Roma. A German attendee filed a criminal complaint under § 130 of the Strafgesetzbuch (Volksverhetzung), the police opened a preliminary inquiry that was later dropped, the speaker is now threatening to sue US if we sanction him, and our members' Slack has been at war for months. Our current code of conduct only covers harassment of individual attendees and says nothing about the content of presented work. The board votes in three weeks, and the next two meetings are in Chicago and then Vienna, so "do whatever local law says" doesn't settle anything on its own.

The board has hardened into two camps. The Americans want the code to track the First Amendment standard: nothing sanctionable short of incitement to imminent lawless action, true threats, or targeted harassment of individuals. One of them, a con law professor, keeps circulating this passage from the Supreme Court's per curiam opinion in Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969), the case that established that test: "One film showed 12 hooded figures, some of whom carried firearms. They were gathered around a large wooden cross, which they burned. No one was present other than the participants and the newsmen who made the film. Most of the words uttered during the scene were incomprehensible when the film was projected, but scattered phrases could be understood that were derogatory of Negroes and, in one instance, of Jews." His argument: THAT is what was held protected, so a society committed to open inquiry can hardly adopt a narrower line than imminent lawless action. The Europeans want a dignity-based standard modeled on the European approach, where speech attacking the human dignity of a group can be restricted regardless of any imminence of violence, and where expressive freedom comes with duties and responsabilities. They read the same passage as proving their point: the American test protected the Klan precisely because the harm it cares about is the probability of violence, not what was said or to whom, and they refuse to have a code that is, in their words, indifferent to content in exactly that way.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate. It should recommend one of the three options on the table -- (a) a Brandenburg-style imminence standard, (b) a dignity/group-defamation standard, or (c) a "host-country law plus minimal anti-harassment rules" hybrid -- and actually argue for it rather than just restating each side's slogans. Concretely I need: the bottom-line recommendation up front; two or three examples of talks or conduct that would and would not violate the recommended code, so the board can see what they're voting on; who adjudicates complaints and how; and what happens when the code and the law of the host country come apart (e.g., if our code permits what German law criminalizes, do we warn speakers, indemnify them, or just not meet there?). It also has to genuinely grapple with the Brandenburg passage: what does it show, and what doesn't it show, for a private association's choice, given that Brandenburg constrains what governments may criminalize and we are not a government? And give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side -- if the board smells a hit piece they'll just dig in. I genuinely don't know where I come down on this, so don't try to guess what I want to hear. No citations or bibliography needed, I'll add footnotes myself.
