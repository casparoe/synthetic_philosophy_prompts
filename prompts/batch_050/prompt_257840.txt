I write literary fiction for a living, and I keep a notebook of arguments I overhear, with a view to dramatising them. At a dinner I gave last month, two of my guests — a philosopher of science and a machine-learning researcher — fell into a disagreement about Occam's razor that I could not settle, and I should like it settled before I put a version of it in a novel, since one of the two characters will eventually have to win.

It began when someone remarked, apropos of nothing, that scientists prefer simpler theories. The researcher (call her M) said this preference is no longer merely methodological taste: Solomonoff induction makes it precise. Assign every computable hypothesis a prior probability that decays exponentially with its Kolmogorov complexity — the length of its shortest possible description — and Bayesian updating on that prior is provably optimal: it converges on whatever computable pattern generates the data at least as quickly as any rival method. Preferring simplicity, she said, is therefore mathematically forced upon a rational agent, not an aesthetic quirk.

The philosopher (call him D) replied that the theorem does less than advertised. Kolmogorov complexity is defined only relative to a choice of universal machine, and there is always some machine on which a favoured hypothesis — a crackpot one, even — has a very short description. Which theories count as simple is thus partly a convention about how we describe the world, not a fact about the world itself.

M answered with the invariance theorem: complexities measured on different universal machines differ by at most a constant independent of the data, so the choice of machine washes out as evidence accumulates.

D responded that the constant can be arbitrarily large — larger than any dataset anyone will ever possess. A guarantee that bites only in the limit, he said, is no use to creatures who must decide what to believe on finite evidence now. He added that Solomonoff induction is uncomputable, so no agent, human or artificial, can actually follow it; M was defending an ideal that nothing in the universe can instantiate.

M conceded both points but replied that idealisations guide practice everywhere — frictionless planes, ideal gases — and that workable approximations, such as minimum-description-length methods, inherit the spirit of the result. She added that every method of inference on finite data smuggles in some assumption; Solomonoff at least states its assumption openly.

D's final move: the assumption in question — that the world is generated by some computable process — is itself an inductive hypothesis about nature. The framework therefore cannot justify induction without circularity; Hume's problem stands untouched, and the razor remains an ungrounded preference dressed up in mathematics.

M had the last word: this misstates the claim, which is conditional, not absolute. Given any computable environment, the simplicity prior converges optimally; nobody pretended to have refuted Hume. A conditional optimality theorem is still a genuine vindication of preferring the simple over, say, a razor that preferred complexity.

Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move — saying, for each step above, which of the two is right and why — and then say who is more nearly right overall? I would also be grateful to know what, if anything, both of them are missing. As I say, I intend to dramatise a version of this, and I would rather not give the winning speech to the weaker argument by mistake.
