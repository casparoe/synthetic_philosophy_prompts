The European Union combines several practices that seem to rest on different pictures of what a polity is. EU law claims primacy over conflicting national law, including, on the Court of Justice's view, national constitutional provisions. Yet most binding legislation is passed by qualified-majority voting in the Council, where states, not citizens, are the formal units, and a blocking minority of large states can stop measures a majority of individual Europeans might favour. Meanwhile, EU citizenship gives a French citizen living in Warsaw the vote in Polish local and European elections, but not in Polish national elections, and the treaties invoke ever-closer union among Europe's peoples rather than a European people. What conception of sovereignty, and of the demos, does this ensemble presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does qualified-majority voting already presuppose that legitimacy can flow through a European-wide constituency, while the electoral rules presuppose that only national demoi can authorise a government? Are these commitments compatible, or is the Union philosophically underdetermined between a federal reading and a compact-of-states reading? Say which features of the practice strictly require which view, and what would concretely have to change -- in voting rules, citizenship rights, or the primacy doctrine -- if the Union were rebuilt on the rival view.
