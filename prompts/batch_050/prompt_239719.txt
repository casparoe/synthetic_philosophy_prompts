So I'm a novelist, five books in, and lately I've been stuck on this analogy someone used on a writing podcast. They said a career in publishing is basically Machiavelli's river from The Prince: fortune is this flood that sweeps through, most people get carried off, and the only thing that saves you is having built your dikes in advance -- for a writer, that's craft, a backlist, a name, maybe a day job. The podcast guy used it to argue that writers who complain about luck in publishing are just lazy dike-builders.

I've read enough Machiavelli to half-remember the picture but I want to know how far it actually holds as an account of how writing careers go. Give me the mapping -- what plays the role of the river, the dikes, the flood season, the person swept away -- and then tell me where it breaks. Like, in Machiavelli I think virtù is partly about boldness and timing, not just preparation, and I don't know what the publishing version of that even is. Also: does the analogy smuggle in the idea that fortune is basically fair, rewarding the prepared, when Machiavelli himself seemed to think fortune could ruin even the virtuous? Because that matters for whether the podcast conclusion (stop complaining, build better dikes) survives or whether it was doing all the work.

One more thing: is there a better analogy for what luck does to a writing career, if the river one doesn't cut it?
