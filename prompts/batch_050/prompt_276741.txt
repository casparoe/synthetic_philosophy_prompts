After a career in experimental physics, I have been reading moral and political philosophy with the habit, perhaps unfortunate, of treating normative claims like instruments whose calibration must survive known error rates. A former student sent me the argument below, and although each step has the clean look of a variance calculation, the conclusion seems to prove either far too much or embarrassingly little. I would like a diagnosis rather than a bibliography.

The argument runs as follows. (1) A coercive state may impose a harm only when the expected moral cost of imposing it in error, discounted by probability and weighted by irreversibility, is lower than the expected moral cost of withholding it in error under the same weighting. (2) Execution is uniquely weighty because death admits no post-exoneration compensation, whereas a mistaken decision not to execute remains revisable by later evidence, incapacitation, or release. (3) Empirical guilt is never certain, so the false-positive term for execution can never be driven to zero. (4) Therefore capital punishment is unjustifiable in principle, not merely as policy. (5) But the same formal structure governs every sanction with residues that cannot be restored after a mistake: decades in prison, a permanent record, involuntary commitment, deportation, castration of a career by accusation alone, and also the irreversible victims produced when a dangerous person is mistakenly left at liberty. (6) Since every serious choice has some irreversible branch, consistency requires either that the state abstain from all high-stakes coercion or that it mark execution alone by some discontinuity not derived from error-weighting. (7) If that discontinuity is retributive, then a sufficiently depraved murderer can reopen the case for execution; if it is deterrent, then sufficiently strong evidence of marginal deterrence can reopen it too; if it is procedural, then a perfectly reliable procedure would reopen it as well. (8) Hence the innocent-risk argument abolishes either nothing distinctive or nearly everything.

Where does this bend, if it does? I would most like to keep two claims: that killing an innocent prisoner is a catastrophe not freely exchangeable against speculative deterrent gains, and that a decent state may still punish, imprison, quarantine, and sometimes risk tragic error. Tell me which numbered step is the cheapest to reject, what rejecting it costs for retribution, deterrence, and the protection of the innocent, and whether the pressure comes from a hidden slide between nonzero risk and any risk, from treating irreversibility as binary when it is graded, from confusing unrecoverable life with unrecoverable time, or from a lexical veto disguised as an expected-value premise.
