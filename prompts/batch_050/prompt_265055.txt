Last weekend I got into a long and genuinely unresolved argument with a friend about what lawyers call naked statistical evidence. The setting was the classic gatecrasher problem: a thousand people attend a rodeo, only five hundred have paid, the organizer sues one attendee picked at random for the price of admission, and on the bare numbers there is a 50.1% chance this defendant crashed the gate. My friend insisted that since the civil standard is merely the preponderance of the evidence — more likely than not — a rational legal system should find liability here, and that our reluctance to do so is a squeamishness we would do better to outgrow. I held the other line: that something is missing when the entire case against a person is a base rate, even when that base rate puts the probability of liability above the threshold. Neither of us moved.

I now want to stage this disagreement for my students rather than lecture it. I teach an upper-undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of law and evidence, about 18 students, and I have a single 90-minute session. Please design a structured classroom activity — part mock civil trial, part betting game — that makes students feel the force of both sides of this argument before either side is named or theorized. I want the reluctance to convict on statistics alone, and the equal and opposite embarrassment of being unable to justify that reluctance, to arise from what the students themselves do and vote on, not from anything I say up front.

Here is the specific design constraint that matters to me, and I'd like it built into the activity's architecture: the students must confront, back to back, two cases in which the defendant's probability of liability is stipulated to be exactly the same number, but which differ in how that probability is generated. One case should be purely statistical in structure (like the gatecrasher or the blue bus problem: the defendant belongs to a reference class most of whose members are liable, and that is the whole of the evidence). The other should rest on individualized evidence of explicitly stipulated equal reliability (say, an eyewitness whose accuracy is fixed at the same percentage). Verdicts on the two cases should be collected separately and anonymously, and the discrepancy in the class's aggregate votes — which I fully expect — should be the data the debrief works from. Within each case, also build in a betting step: before voting on liability, ask each student privately whether they would accept a small-stakes bet (course points, not money) that pays out if the defendant really is liable. The divergence between willingness to bet and willingness to find liability is, I suspect, the single most revealing data point the activity can produce.

The prompt I want you to write should give me: the two cases written out as concrete vignettes (invent fresh ones; do not just reuse the rodeo and the bus company), roles if any are needed, the exact sequence and timing across the 90 minutes, the wording of the questions put to students at each stage so that nothing tips the lesson in advance, and a procedure for collecting and displaying the votes and bets so the class sees its own pattern only after both cases are decided.

Then the debrief plan. It should map what happened onto the candidate explanations for why statistical proof feels insufficient — the thought that statistical evidence, unlike the eyewitness, cannot be sensitive to the truth in the actual case; the thought that a verdict resting on a base rate is true only by luck when it is true; the knowledge-based thought that finding someone liable requires something like knowing they are liable; and my friend's deflationary position that the discrepancy is an error and the vote pattern is just bias. For each explanation, tell me which moment in the activity most directly illustrates it and what follow-up question to ask the students about their own behavior. Also tell me honestly what the activity cannot reproduce — I assume the stipulation of exact probabilities is doing real work that real trials never allow — and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (students who refuse to take the stipulated probabilities seriously, students who game the bet to look consistent, the friend-position devolving into a straw man) and how to handle each.
