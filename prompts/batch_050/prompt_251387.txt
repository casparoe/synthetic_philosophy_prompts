I am a final-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of mind seminar on the temporal structure of experience, and we have just spent a fortnight on the colour phi phenomenon and Dennett's treatment of it. In case the terminology varies: two spots of different colours are flashed in rapid succession at slightly different locations, and subjects report a single spot that moves and changes colour partway along its trajectory — apparently experiencing the colour change before the second spot has even been presented. Dennett distinguishes two ways of explaining this. On the Stalinesque picture, the brain fabricates the motion-and-change sequence in a pre-conscious editing stage, so the experience never contained a stationary first spot at all. On the Orwellian picture, the subject genuinely experiences the first spot standing still, but memory is rewritten within fractions of a second, so the subject sincerely misremembers having seen motion. I have come away persuaded by Dennett that, at these sub-second timescales, there is simply no fact of the matter about which picture is correct: the distinction presupposes a determinate moment at which content becomes conscious, and that is precisely what his multiple drafts account denies exists.

For my assessed essay I want to stress-test this position rather than defend it, so I am asking: what would count against it? Please take the position to be the strong one — not merely that the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction is often hard to apply, but that at short timescales there is nothing for the two pictures to disagree about. I would like to know: which conceivable experimental results, if any, would force the distinction back into being (for instance, what would a sufficiently fine-grained neural correlate of the onset of consciousness have to look like to settle the matter, and is that the right kind of evidence at all, or does the dispute concern what would even count as settling it)? Which intuitions or first-personal facts is the position committed to dismissing, and how costly is that dismissal? Where does the position stand or fall with wider commitments — about the metaphysics of temporal experience, about the evidential status of retrospective report, about whether consciousness has a determinate content at an instant — such that a defeat elsewhere would drag it down? And is there a defensible halfway house, on which the distinction lapses at sub-second timescales but reasserts itself over longer intervals, and if so, what principled boundary could the view give without smuggling the problematic 'moment of becoming conscious' back in?

I would also like an honest verdict on the position's exposure. Which of these would Dennett's defenders themselves concede as counting against the view, and which would they wave away as question-begging? Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Finally, I want an assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — whether 'no fact of the matter' is a substantive claim that sticks its neck out, or a verdict that no evidence could dislodge — and, if the latter, whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind or exactly what one should expect.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. Kindly write in British English.
