I spent four decades as an experimental condensed-matter physicist before retiring, and over the past year I have been working my way through the philosophy of mind. The argument that has gripped me most is the causal exclusion argument, which I take to run roughly as follows: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause; mental properties are not identical with physical properties; so if a mental property ever causes a physical event, either that event is systematically overdetermined or the mental property's causal contribution excludes the physical one, contrary to closure. Since systematic overdetermination strikes me as absurd and closure strikes me as close to non-negotiable, I have provisionally accepted the conclusion: mental properties, considered as distinct from their physical realizers, do no causal work. As a scientist I find this oddly easy to live with, which itself makes me suspicious -- the positions one slides into are exactly the ones worth stress-testing. So I am asking you to stress-test it for me.

Do not give me a survey of the literature's options and leave me to choose. I want you to take a definite stand at each step: which premise of the argument is genuinely the weakest, which alleged defeaters you judge fatal and which merely awkward, and how exposed the position actually is once the honest accounting is done. I am aware of some candidate pressure points -- the worry that the argument, if sound, generalizes to exclude the causal efficacy of all higher-level properties (biological, geological, economic), which even I balk at; the suggestion that causation is a matter of proportionality or difference-making in a way that lets mental causes in without violating closure; the flat-footed embrace of overdetermination; and the denial of closure itself. Tell me which of these you think actually lands, and whether the generalization worry in particular is a reductio of the argument or a bullet its defender should bite. I would also like you to distinguish what a committed defender of the exclusion argument would themselves concede as counting against the view from what only its critics regard as damaging, since those are rarely the same list. And if the position turns out to be well insulated -- protected by qualifications such that no possible result could touch it -- say so plainly and say whether you take that to be a virtue or a symptom.

Please structure the response under the following headings, in this order, at roughly the indicated lengths: The Argument at Its Strongest (about 250 words -- reconstruct the best version, not the strawman); Map of Exposure (about 400 words -- the load-bearing premises, ranked by genuine vulnerability, with what the world would have to look like for each to fail); Decisive versus Merely Awkward (about 400 words -- your verdicts on the candidate defeaters, including the generalization problem, with reasons); What a Defender Should Concede (about 250 words); and Final Verdict (about 200 words -- how exposed the position is, and whether its degree of insulation is reassuring or damning).
