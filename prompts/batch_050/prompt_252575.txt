I'm trying to pin down what principle about identity and indiscernibility my judgments on cases actually commit me to. Here are the verdicts I'd give:

- Two coins struck from the same die and atom-for-atom alike are two coins; their spatiotemporal separation settles it.
- Black's spheres — two qualitatively identical iron spheres alone in an otherwise empty universe, a fixed distance apart — are two; I take the bare fact that each stands at a distance from something it is not identical with to be enough, even though that symmetric relation gives neither sphere a property the other lacks.
- Two bosons prepared in exactly the same quantum state in a single trap, sharing one wavefunction with no spatial relation to tell them apart, are nevertheless two; the quantum statistics itself counts the state as doubly occupied.
- The complex numbers i and −i are two, even though every property stateable in the language of fields without naming either one belongs to both, since complex conjugation swaps them.
- In an exactly repeating cosmos, the cycles are numerically distinct; my counterpart in the next cycle is a perfect duplicate of me, not me living my life over again.
- Yet a homogeneous, featureless expanse of matter of perfectly uniform density is not secretly many objects; any way of counting its parts would be conventional, with no fact of the matter.

Is there a single principle of individuation — some precisification of the identity of indiscernibles, together with an account of what grounds numerical distinctness — that delivers all six? If not, which of my verdicts have to go?
