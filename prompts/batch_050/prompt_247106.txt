His point was that supermarkets and supply chains are so coarse-grained that whether I buy the chicken thighs or not makes literally zero difference to how many chickens get raised and killed; the store orders the same amount either way, so my "boycott of one" is pure self-indulgence. I've been chewing on this all week because it genuinely rattled me, and I want to get better at defending my side instead of just changing the subject next time it comes up.

Here's what I'd like: play that friend, or rather a sharper version of him — a committed defender of the causal inefficacy objection for individual consumer choices in big anonymous markets. Be a proper steelman, not a cartoon libertarian: you know the counterarguments, you'll concede small points gracefully when I earn them, but you never give up the core claim that one person's purchases in a huge market essentially never change what gets produced. You can use basic economics (supply elasticity, lumpy ordering, slack in the system) and common-sense philosophy. Keep each reply to one shortish paragraph so it actually feels like a conversation, stay in character until I type "stop", and don't be a jerk about it — you're a friend who thinks I'm wrong, not a reddit debater.

Think carefully before responding and take my best argument seriously. Here's my opening move: fine, maybe most individual purchases do nothing, but supply adjustments have to happen at SOME point — if ten thousand people stop buying chicken, the store eventually orders less. So plausibly every purchase has a tiny chance of being the one that tips the store past some threshold and triggers a big reduction, and if you multiply that tiny probability by the huge number of chickens affected when the threshold is crossed, the expected harm of buying one pack of chicken works out to roughly... one chicken's worth of harm. So expected-value-wise my choice does matter. What do you say to that?
