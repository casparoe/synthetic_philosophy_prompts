I teach a second-year undergraduate course in the philosophy of history, and I want to open the unit on explanation before the students have met any of the standard positions. There are 18 of them, we have a single 90-minute seminar, and they have no prior exposure to the covering-law model or to narrativism. I want them to arrive at the dispute themselves, and I want the session organised around a passage they will have read in advance.

The set reading is chapter 9 of Aristotle's Poetics (1451b; I use Bywater's 1920 translation, which is out of copyright). There, Aristotle distinguishes the historian from the poet on the ground that the historian relates what has actually happened while the poet relates the kind of thing that might happen, and he ranks poetry above history as the more philosophical and more serious pursuit precisely because its statements concern universals whereas history's concern particulars. Do not quote this passage back at me; design around it. What I want is an activity whose outcome lets the class put that ranking on trial: does an explanation of a particular event get better, or worse, as its statements become more universal?

Design me a runnable in-class activity on the following lines, though you may improve on any element. Students work in teams on a single invented incident (invented, so that nobody has background knowledge to lean on -- say, the collapse of a market-town footbridge on the morning of the annual fair). Give each team an identical hand of fact cards describing particulars (the inspector's state that morning, a budget batch of rivets, a mill owner who lobbied against repairs, the doubled crowd load, and so on) and a separate hand of law cards stating general regularities at various scopes. The session should run in stages under changing constraints: one stage where any explanation goes, one where an official-inquiry rule admits only explanations whose every step is backed by a law card, and one where the reverse constraint forbids all general statements and permits only the ordered narration of particulars. Include a lightweight payoff scheme (tokens, not money) so that teams must stake something on which rival explanations genuinely answer the question why it collapsed on that day rather than merely describe it, and so that the constraints bite rather than being advisory.

For your answer I need: the full rules and materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, the payoff scheme, and the card design (including how many cards, and the trick of offering laws at different degrees of generality). Crucially, a debrief plan that maps what happened onto the philosophical terrain only after the fact: which moments correspond to Hempel's covering-law intuition and its well-known embarrassments (explanations that are really just redescriptions once the law's scope is narrowed far enough), which moments correspond to the narrativist claim that emplotment of particulars is itself explanatory, and how to run the closing vote in which the class adjudicates Aristotle's claim that the universal-leaning account is the more philosophical one. No lecture up front, and the students should not hear the names Hempel, Dray, or narrativism until the debrief.

Finally, two honest appendices. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- I anticipate at least one team smuggling particulars into a law by narrowing its scope until it is just the narrative in disguise, students inventing facts not on the cards, and a fluent storyteller winning tokens regardless of explanatory merit -- and how to handle each, treating the first of these as a teaching point rather than a violation. Second, which features of the real philosophical dispute the activity cannot reproduce (for instance, the evidential predicament of actual historical research, and whatever card-ordering fails to capture about narrative's temporal structure), and what I must supply by direct instruction afterwards.

Please write your answer in British English. The passage does not count toward any length you choose to give the activity materials.
