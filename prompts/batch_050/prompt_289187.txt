Lately I've been reading some popular-level books about Wang Yangming, the Ming dynasty philosopher-general, and I keep turning over his doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action. As I understand it, his claim was not just that we ought to act on what we know, but something stronger: that nobody genuinely knows something in the moral sense without acting on it, so that a person who "knows" they should honor their parents but behaves unfilially simply does not yet know it — the kind of knowing that flows into action is the only real knowing, and what fails to produce action is at best hearsay or memorization. What struck me is how sharply this seems to cut against modern life, which is full of people who absorb enormous amounts of information and never do anything with it, and I'd like to test the doctrine against some cases he obviously never encountered.

So here is my question: if Wang Yangming were alive today, how do you think he would diagnose the following three phenomena? First, a friend of mine who has read a whole shelf of books on diet and exercise, can explain insulin and macronutrients better than most personal trainers, and yet has not changed a single habit in five years — would Wang simply say my friend does not know these things, and if so, what exactly would he say is missing, and how would one acquire it? Second, the now-common pattern of people who feel they have discharged a moral duty by sharing a post about some injustice, a kind of virtue-by-click; I suspect Wang would have something scathing to say here, but I'd like to hear whether the case is actually as clear-cut as it looks, since the person sharing the post might insist this is their action. Third, and strangest, the AI chatbots we now have that can discourse fluently about courage, temperance, and compassion but are incapable of doing anything at all — would Wang regard them as knowing nothing whatsoever, and would he say the same about a human scholar who only writes commentaries, or does the fact that the chatbot cannot act in principle rather than merely failing to act make a difference to his framework?

I'm not looking for a generic summary of his philosophy, which I already have from my reading. I want a careful, best-guess reconstruction of how his framework would engage these specific cases, with honest flagging of where the extrapolation becomes speculative or where interpreters disagree about what the doctrine even commits him to. And if my understanding of the unity of knowledge and action is off in some important way, or if you think the whole exercise of projecting a sixteenth-century view onto diet books and chatbots is misguided or rests on a confusion, please tell me that directly rather than politely playing along.
