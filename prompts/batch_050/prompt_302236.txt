My half-formed idea is this. Laplace treats probability as a uniform remedy for a uniform problem: we are ignorant, and the calculus of probabilities is how we reason well anyway. But chaotic systems seem to show that ignorance is not uniform in the way his picture assumes. For a well-behaved system, a small measurement error in the initial conditions stays small; for a chaotic one, it grows at a rate that is itself a deterministic, measurable feature of the system. So the growth of my ignorance looks like a law-governed fact about the world, not just a fact about me — and that seems to blur the clean division Laplace assumes between deterministic laws on one side and merely epistemic probability on the other. My question is whether this tension is real and what should be said about it: does chaos vindicate Laplace (by making the decay of our knowledge itself something deterministic science can compute), does it break his framework (by showing that probability in a deterministic world cannot be purely a measure of ignorance), or is there a coherent middle position?
