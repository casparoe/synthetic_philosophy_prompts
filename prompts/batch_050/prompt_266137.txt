I'm writing an essay on The Picture of Dorian Gray and want to read it through Quinn's self-torturer puzzle — the guy who can dial up his own pain one imperceptible notch at a time, gets paid for each notch, and walks himself into agony because every single step seems worth taking. Dorian's corruption works the same way: no individual indulgence feels like the one that ruins him, and the portrait absorbs the cumulative damage so he never has to look at the total. Is the novel basicaly dramatizing Quinn's point that rational choice step-by-step can be irrational over the whole sequence, or does Wilde complicate it? I'm especially interested in the portrait as a device: it makes the imperceptible increments visible again in aggregate, which is exactly the information the self-torturer lacks. Does that mean the novel thinks the real problem is hidden ledgers rather than intransitive preferences? And what do I do with the ending — when Dorian finally confronts the total and stabs the portrait, is Wilde saying the stepwise reasoning was fine until the aggregate became undeniable, or that it was rotten all along? I'd like a reading that takes the puzzle seriously on its own terms (imperceptible differences vs. actually inconsistent preferences) but also says where the novel resists being turned into an illustration of it.
