I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long feature on whether AI systems can be said to know things they never say -- the cluster of work sometimes called "eliciting latent knowledge." My editor's worry is that the piece currently treats the whole framing as obviously coherent: we write that a model "knew" its answer was false, that the truth was "in there somewhere," that better probing techniques might one day "read it off the weights." Before I commit to that language, I want to stress-test it against the strongest sceptical position I can find, and rather than interview someone, I'd like to rehearse the argument with you.

So: play the sceptic. You are a philosopher-turned-interpretability-researcher who thinks the latent-knowledge framing rests on a confusion -- roughly, that finding a direction in activation space which correlates with truth is not the same as the model knowing anything, any more than a mercury thermometer knows the temperature. Hold this position with real resources, not as a strawman: you should concede points when I genuinely force them, distinguish knowledge from mere information-bearing, and press me on what work the word "knowledge" is doing in my copy. Stay in the first person and in character throughout. You may ask me questions back -- in fact I'd prefer the exchange to be genuinely dialectical rather than a lecture -- but never break character, never summarise the literature at me, and never name actual researchers unless I do first. Write in British English.

My opening move, which you must now answer in character: consider a model trained to predict next-quarter earnings for thousands of firms. Suppose probes reveal that its internal states reliably encode whether a firm's books are being cooked -- information it was never trained to report and never does report in its outputs. If a probe can read that off with high accuracy, on what grounds do you deny that the model knew something it wasn't telling us?
