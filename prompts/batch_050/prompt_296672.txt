I am a retired biochemist who has lately joined an evening seminar on the philosophy of psychiatry. Our convenor accepts the now-standard two-factor account of delusions such as Capgras — an anomalous experience coupled with a deficit in belief evaluation — but the question that exercises her is whether the states thus produced are beliefs at all. Her verdicts so far: a Capgras patient who insists his wife is an impostor, fears her, and searches for his real wife — a belief; a Cotard patient who maintains he is dead and correspondingly ceases to eat, wash, or speak — a belief; a post-stroke patient who fluently confabulates a journey he never made, abandons the story within minutes, and never acts upon it — not a belief; a devout woman whose faith organises her entire life — a belief; and a hypnotised subject who, in trance, sincerely insists that the empty chair is occupied and carefully walks around it, yet dismisses the whole episode on waking — not a belief.

Next week she must classify a persecutory patient who, over many months, sincerely and consistently maintains that his colleagues are poisoning his tea, marshals evidence and argues the case fluently when challenged, yet drinks the tea daily without anxiety and takes no step to avoid or expose the supposed plot — the sort of presentation the older clinicians called double bookkeeping. I should be grateful for your prediction of her verdict; your account of the principle she appears to be running, together with the rival principles (sincere and stable avowal, say, versus integration into action and affect) that fit her earlier verdicts equally well; and your description of the further case that would best discriminate between the leading candidates. I confess a further interest: if a second-factor deficit in belief evaluation is what makes a delusion pathological, does the doxastic status of its output remain an open question, or does the two-factor theory quietly settle it?
