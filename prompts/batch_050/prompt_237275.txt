I run the investigations office at a regional hospital network. Roughly twice a week, a complaint lands on my desk in which the entire evidentiary record is testimony: no documents, no recordings, just one person's account of what was said or done to them, often contested by another person's account. Historically we have resolved these by having an investigator interview the parties and write a credibility assessment, and I have become convinced that this step is where our process does its worst work -- that we systematically discount some reporters and overweight others in ways that track status and demeanor rather than reliability. I have been reading about what philosophers call epistemic injustice: the idea that hearers can wrong speakers by deflating (or inflating) the credibility they assign them on the basis of prejudice, and that people can be unable to make their experience intelligible to others, or even to themselves, because the shared vocabulary for naming it does not exist or has not reached them.

I would like you to turn that body of theory into an intake procedure my investigators -- none of whom have philosophical training -- can actually run during a ninety-minute credibility review. Specifically:

1. A sequence of questions the investigator asks about the case, in order, with a statement of what would count as a sufficient answer to each and what the investigator does next depending on the answer. The procedure should at minimum distinguish: a credibility deficit that appears to track the speaker's identity or role rather than their history of accuracy; a credibility excess doing parallel work in the other direction (the senior physician whose account is accepted against three juniors); a report that is vague or conceptually unstable in a way that suggests the speaker lacks adequate concepts for their own experience, as opposed to one that is simply incoherent; and a case where the two accounts genuinely conflict on the facts and no credibility distortion is doing the work.

2. For each stage, an explicit statement of whether it can be settled by the procedure or must be handed to a human judgment call, and what record the investigator should leave when it is the latter. I want the handoffs to be honest: if a step is really judgment dressed up as procedure, say so.

3. A set of failure modes the procedure itself introduces, with instructions for mitigating them. The two I am most worried about are that the procedure turns into an identity audit that stereotypes in the opposite direction, and that correcting a credibility deficit gets confused with finding the complaint substantiated -- an epistemically wronged speaker can still be wrong.

4. A worked run of the procedure on each of the following: (a) a nursing aide who describes, haltingly and without settled vocabulary, a pattern of being excluded from shift handover information in ways she cannot quite name, in a workplace where no term for it circulates; (b) a dispute between a renowned surgeon and two residents in which the surgeon's account has been treated as authoritative at every prior stage; and (c) a case where your procedure should conclude that the complainant suffered exactly the credibility deficit the procedure is built to catch, and that the complaint should nonetheless not be credited. That third case matters most to me: if the procedure cannot deliver an answer its operator will dislike, it is not a procedure.

Close with a short account of which distinctions from the theory survived translation into procedure, which you had to drop or simplify, and what the procedure structurally cannot do -- in particular, whether it can detect distortions that are invisible to the institution running it.
