I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been turning over a puzzle about the law for a while now, and I would like help figuring out what assumptions lie underneath it.

As I understand it, in most of the English-speaking world there is no general legal duty to rescue a stranger. If I stroll past a shallow pond, see a child drowning, and keep walking because I do not want to ruin my shoes, I may be a terrible person, but in most places I have committed no crime. Yet the exceptions are striking. A parent who watches her own child drown in that same pond will be prosecuted. So, it seems, will the lifeguard on duty, or the person who accidentally knocked the child in, or -- at least in some jurisdictions -- someone who began a rescue and then abandoned it halfway. Meanwhile, the same legal system that refuses to punish the bystander has no difficulty saying that a parent caused her child's death by failing to feed her. So the law appears to hold that an omission can be the cause of a death in one case and no cause at all in another, even when the physical facts -- water, lungs, a person nearby doing nothing -- are much the same.

What picture of causation, or of responsibility, does this pattern actually presuppose? Is the law committed to the idea that doing nothing is never a cause of anything -- that the pond, and only the pond, brought about the death -- with the exceptions carved out on some entirely different basis, such as promises made or roles taken on? Or does it quietly accept that omissions can cause outcomes but hold that we only answer for some of what we cause? And whichever it is: is the line between the stranger and the parent a principled one, or does it rest on a distinction between "my effects" and "not my effects" that collapses once you ask where exactly it is drawn and why?

I would like three things. First, a reconstruction of the implicit theory: what the law seems to assume about what it is to cause something, and what it would have to say causation by omission is, if it allows that there is such a thing at all. Second, an honest assessment of whether the practice strictly requires that theory or merely sits comfortably with it. Could someone who thinks omissions are perfectly ordinary causes still defend the no-duty-to-rescue rule -- on grounds of personal liberty, of the need for a workable line, or of the impossibility of enforcing a duty to help everyone -- and if so, is the rule then philosophically underdetermined, compatible with several rival views about causation? Third, tell me what would have to change if the law fully committed to the view that standing by is a way of causing a death. Would every jurisdiction need a general duty to rescue, and where could the limits of such a duty come from, without collapsing into the conclusion that each of us is causally on the hook for every preventable death anywhere in the world -- which is, I take it, precisely the conclusion the law as it stands is built to avoid?
