Next month I am giving the closing talk at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop on reasoning and representation, hosted by a cognitive science department that has kept one foot in classical AI. The thesis I intend to defend has three parts. First, the frame problem as it arose within the logicist program was never actually solved by the formal machinery offered for it: non-monotonic logics, successor-state axioms, and their descendants relocated rather than eliminated the difficulty of specifying what persists when an action occurs, and the difficulty reappeared whenever the domain grew even slightly realistic. Second, the broad success of statistical learning shows that the problem was, in effect, dissolved rather than solved: systems that acquire world-models from data never have to axiomatize persistence in the first place. Third, and this is the claim I expect to carry the fight, the dissolution is temporary and superficial, because the frame problem has returned in contemporary large language models under a new guise — not the problem of tracking what changes when the world is acted upon, but the problem of selecting what is relevant across long chains of reasoning and planning, where, I argue, these systems possess no principled mechanism and merely simulate one statistically.

The audience will comprise three constituencies: two senior philosophers who came of age during the classical period and regard the situation-calculus tradition as having handled the problem adequately; several younger machine learning researchers who suspect that "the frame problem" is simply the name philosophers attach to whatever neural networks cannot yet do; and two or three cognitive scientists convinced that the genuine frame problem was always about biological cognitive architecture rather than formal systems.

Please draft my question-period preparation sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually receive from this audience — not the ones a specialist conference on non-monotonic reasoning would produce — ordered by the damage each could inflict, phrased in the vocabulary of the constituency most likely to raise it, each with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Indicate what I should concede at the outset to avoid contesting bad ground, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks, together with an honest assessment of whether my distinction between the change-tracking and relevance-selection versions of the problem is sturdy enough to bear the weight the talk places on it.
