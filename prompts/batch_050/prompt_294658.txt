Last spring my city's historical commission released findings that, between 1949 and 1963, the municipal government used a combination of racially targeted rezoning and eminent domain to dismantle a predominantly Black commercial district; the cleared parcels became a highway interchange and a civic auditorium. In the aftermath, the city council chartered a reparations study commission, and I was appointed to its community advisory panel. One of my responsibilities is to run a monthly public deliberation evening: mixed groups of about twenty-four adult residents of every background, no philosophical training assumed, roughly one hundred minutes per session.

The first session went badly in an instructive way. When I tried to explain the conceptual difficulties up front — the non-identity problem, the question of the counterfactual baseline, the possibility that intervening events can erode a claim over time — the room either glazed over or heard the difficulties as bureaucratic stalling. So I want to stop explaining and start staging. Design me a structured, multi-round simulation that lets participants run into these problems themselves, in their own deliberation, before any of them are named.

Some constraints and requirements:

1. The simulation should span several "generations" of a fictional city. In the first round, a state-sanctioned dispossession occurs (participants hold paper assets — deeds, businesses, savings — in simple form, and some are stripped of them). Subsequent rounds compress generational time: players inherit, new participants join as descendants, and the facilitator introduces exogenous events (an economic boom, a wartime draft, a later well-intentioned urban renewal program).

2. By the final round, when the group must act as the commission and answer "who is owed what, by whom, and how would we even compute it," the setup should have quietly produced the hard cases: at least one descendant who can only trace their existence to the injustice (their ancestors met in the relocation housing); at least one destroyed asset that enriched no one, so there is loss with no beneficiary to bill; at least one current property holder who bought in good faith at market price decades later; and at least one injustice from round one that has been papered over by so many subsequent events that participants themselves begin to argue it no longer grounds a claim.

3. Give me concrete materials (envelopes, deeds, a public ledger, whatever works), the rules for each round, timing, and a way to create mild real stakes without money — points, privileges in the final deliberation, or something better you devise.

4. The debrief matters most. Specify how the facilitator maps what actually happened in the room onto the non-identity problem, the counterfactual-baseline question, the difference between compensating victims and taxing beneficiaries, and the supersession worry — and do it in an order that lets participants name the problems in their own words first, with the terminology arriving only at the end.

5. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first two times I run this — I suspect participants will game the ledger like a board game, and I worry about a resident whose family actually lived through the dispossession finding the exercise trivializing — and give me concrete facilitator moves for each failure mode.

6. Be honest about what a paper-and-envelope simulation cannot reproduce: the phenomenology of real loss, the standing of actual descendants, the weight of genuine historical memory. Tell me which of these gaps the facilitator must explicitly bridge, and how, rather than paper over.
