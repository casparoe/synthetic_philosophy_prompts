Next month I'm presenting at a constitutional theory colloquium a paper defending Frederick Douglass's mature position that the U.S. Constitution is an anti-slavery document. My argument rests on the interpretive rule Douglass states in his 1860 Glasgow speech, "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?":

"It would be the wildest of absurdities, and lead to endless confusion and mischiefs, if, instead of looking to the written paper itself, for its meaning, it were attempted to make us search it out, in the secret motives, and dishonest intentions, of some of the men who took part in writing it. It was what they said that was adopted by the people, not what they were ashamed or afraid to say, and really omitted to say."

My thesis is that this is not a debater's convenience but a serious theory of legal meaning, and that applying it honestly — including to the fugitive slave clause and the three-fifths clause — vindicates the anti-slavery reading better than Garrison's intention-based reading does. The audience: two originalist law professors who locate constitutional meaning precisely in the framers' intentions Douglass brackets, one historian of the founding era who has written on slavery at the Convention, and several political theorists sympathetic to Garrisonian disunionism. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, phrased as each camp would phrase them and ordered by how much damage they would do; for each, what I should concede, what I should hold, and a reply deliverable in two minutes, with a note on where the reply is soft. In particular, pressure-test whether Douglass's textual rule can carry the weight he puts on it where the text is most awkward for him, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
