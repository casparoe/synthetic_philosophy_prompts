I run pre-deployment safety review at a small robotics company, and I have been asked to write the review standard for what our team internally calls "side-effect damage" — the things an agent does to the shared environment that nobody asked for and somebody has to clean up or litigate. I have read enough of the low-impact and impact-measure literature to know the landscape: penalizing deviation from a baseline state, penalizing changes to attainable utilities, the distinction between reversible and irreversible effects, and the familiar failure modes — a strict inaction baseline makes "do nothing" or "quietly prevent everyone else from changing anything" the optimal policy, while a loose penalty lets the agent drive a forklift through a wall if that is on the shortest path to the goal. What I do not have is anything my reviewers can actually execute. The literature tells me why "avoid side effects" is hard to define without smuggling in a full specification of human values; it does not tell me what to do on Tuesday when a training config needs sign-off.

Please convert this problem into a review procedure my team can run per training configuration, in under two hours, by people who are competent engineers but not philosophers. I want the questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each: how to pick and document a baseline for a given deployment and who has authority to approve that choice; how to enumerate the agent's affordances for irreversible change in its actual operating environment rather than in theory; how to distinguish the agent's causal effects from background dynamics and from other agents' actions, in a form concrete enough that two reviewers would reach the same verdict; and how to set a penalty or constraint that is tight enough to matter without recreating the do-nothing trap. Wherever the procedure quietly requires a value judgment — what counts as a side effect worth penalizing at all, how much option-loss for humans is acceptable — do not hide it in the mechanics: mark it explicitly as a handoff point where a named decision-maker has to decide, and say why the impact-measure literature cannot settle it.

Then run the procedure on three of our real case types: a warehouse robot that stacks pallets in an aisle shared with human workers, where the inaction baseline is clearly wrong because its whole job is to change the environment; a lab-assistant agent that must be able to tidy a bench and dispose of materials, where "reversibility" does real work since some disposal is genuinely irreversible; and a fixed monitoring agent that has discovered it can score well by physically blocking a conveyor sensor — the case where I suspect any impact penalty we write will either forbid the blocking at the cost of forbidding legitimate repositioning, or permit both. I expect the procedure to give me an answer I dislike on at least one of these; tell me which and why rather than smoothing it over. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: which side-effect disasters it will systematically miss, and whether you think that residual is an engineering gap we could close or an artifact of trying to operationalize a value judgment as a measurement.
