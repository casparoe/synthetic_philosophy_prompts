I got into it last week with a colleague who sent me a longform piece from a science-and-tech magazine about transhumanism, and there's a paragraph summarizing the "abolitionist project" associated with David Pearce that I want a second opinion on, since I'm teaching a grad seminar on the ethics of well-being next semester and I'm considering assigning a critique of it. The article's summary runs like this: "The philosopher David Pearce argues that suffering is a purely technical problem, like smallpox, and that we have a moral obligation to eradicate it from the biosphere using biotechnology -- gene editing, designer drugs, and eventually rewiring the brains of humans and wild animals alike. Since pain and pleasure are just states of the brain's reward circuitry, Pearce claims that once we understand the relevant neuroscience, we can simply reprogram that circuitry so that every sentient being is maximally happy at all times: a world of constant, uniform bliss. He admits this would mean giving up things like grief at a loved one's death, righteous anger at injustice, and most of great art, but he bites that bullet, since on his view nothing has value except pleasant experience. Critics point out, correctly, that this amounts to advocating compulsory wireheading for the whole planet, and that a being with no capacity for suffering could have no motivation to do anything, so the project is self-defeating even on its own terms."

My sense is that this is mostly in the ballpark but that at least a couple of things are off -- for instance, I seem to remember that Pearce is actually careful to distinguish the kind of hedonic recalibration he favors from both wireheading and from just being stoned forever, and that he thinks gradients of well-being can still sustain motivation and even information-seeking behavior. But I read the manifesto years ago and might be misremembering, or confusing him with later sympathetic writers. So: how fair is this summary, taken as a whole? Please go through it claim by claim and flag what's accurate, what's a defensible simplification for a popular audience, and what misrepresents the view -- especially whether the "maximally happy at all times" gloss and the "admits art and grief must go" bit are things the position actually commits to, and whether the critics' objections quoted at the end are fair characterizations or just the standard objections dressed up as refutations.

One more thing, since I'll be using your answer to decide how to frame the seminar reading: please be explicit about what's more or less settled here versus what's genuinely contested. That is, distinguish (a) what Pearce's view actually is, where I'd want a high-confidence answer, (b) which of the standard objections are widely regarded as serious versus which ones defenders have a decent reply to, and (c) where philosophers who actually work on this stuff disagree among themselves. Don't present a contested assessment as the consensus view, and if you're unsure about some detail of the position, say so rather than guessing.
