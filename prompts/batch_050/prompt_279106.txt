I am setting the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate course on formal systems and minds, in which students have studied Hofstadter's MU-puzzle and his notion of 'jumping out of the system'. Since they all know the MU-puzzle's solution, the question must be built around an unfamiliar system, so that memorised arguments cannot simply be replayed. Please use the following system, devised for the purpose.

The KITE system. Every string consists of an initial K followed by any number of I's, T's and E's. Axiom: KI. Rules: (1) if a string ends in I, you may append T; (2) from any string Kx you may derive Kxx; (3) any occurrence of IIII may be replaced by a single E; (4) any occurrence of EE may be deleted.

Draft one question, answerable in 60 minutes, with three parts: (a) derive KE, showing each rule applied (easy, purely within-system work); (b) prove that KIIIT is not derivable; (c) a discussion of (b)'s method — the proof required reasoning about the system rather than within it, so does it follow that such reasoning is non-mechanical, and if a theorem-proving program could produce the same proof, would that vindicate or undermine the contrast, often drawn in discussions of the MU-puzzle, between mechanical derivation and genuine insight? For your information: KE is derivable via KI → KII → KIIII → KE, whereas KIIIT is not, because the number of I's in any derivable string is never congruent to 3 modulo 4 (the property holds of the axiom and is preserved by all four rules). Note also that this test is one-way only: an I-count of five passes it yet is unreachable, since the count can only double or drop by four.

Give a model answer and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, stating the marks each is worth (totalling 20) and, for each, a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. At least two points should concern part (b), where the usual failures are asserting underivability on the strength of a failed search, and stating an invariant without verifying it against the axiom and every rule. The five-count observation may serve as a discriminator for full marks.
