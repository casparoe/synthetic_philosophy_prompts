Write an essay on whether forgiveness can be obligatory, and whether it can be unconditional. Take both questions seperately but also think about how they interact -- e.g., is obligatory forgiveness *necessarily* conditional, or vice versa?

To keep you from just rehashing the usual takes, grapple with this slightly odd setup: imagine a culture where it's normal to trade what they call "forgiveness futures." Before marrying or going into business together, people publicly commit to forgiving their partner for certain classes of future wrongs, no questions asked, no repentance required. Breaching a forgiveness future is treated as a serious betrayal in its own right, sometimes worse than the original wrong would have been. Assume these commitments are sincere, not coerced, and that the practice genuinely reduces the amount of simmering resentment in the culture.

Some things your essay should figure out along the way: Is a forgiveness future actually forgiveness at all, or just a promise not to hold grudges -- and does that distinction even matter morally? Does the fact that the forgiveness precedes the wrongdoing (and hence precedes any apology, restitution, or even the possibility of assessing the wrong) make it incoherent, or does it reveal that standard accounts overrate the role of repentance? And if forgiveness in advance can be obligatory by promise, what would ground an obligation to forgive someone you *didn't* promise anything to -- can there be such a thing, or does making forgiveness obligatory always destroy the gift-like quality that supposedly makes it valuable in the first place?

Weigh the best arguments on both sides, but don't sit on the fence: reach a conclusion on each question and defend it. It's fine if your verdict is complicated (e.g., "obligatory yes, unconditional no"), but it should be a verdict.
