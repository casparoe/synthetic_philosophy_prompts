I'm in an undergraduate seminar on free will, and last week we read about the digger wasp made famous in the free will literature: the wasp that drags a paralyzed cricket to the entrance of its burrow, goes inside to check that all is well, and then comes back out to retrieve the cricket -- and if an experimenter nudges the cricket a few inches away while the wasp is inside, the wasp drags it back to the threshold and repeats the whole inspection routine from the beginning, apparently without limit, never adapting to the fact that the situation keeps resetting. Dennett coined the term "sphexishness" for this kind of superficially clever but mechanically rigid behavior, and our professor's provocation was that the entire compatibilism-versus-libertarianism stalemate might just be a disagreement about how sphexish human beings actually are. After three sessions of discussion, the class has split into two camps that keep talking past each other, and I want a diagnosis rather than another verdict.

Here's the structure of the impasse as I see it. One side argues that what matters for freedom is flexibility of response: humans, unlike the wasp, notice when their routines are being frustrated, revise their plans, reconsider their reasons, and can in principle reconsider their way of reconsidering -- and this recursive openness just is what "could have done otherwise" was ever trying to capture, so determinism is irrelevant. The other side replies that every layer of flexibility must bottom out somewhere: your way of revising plans is itself a fixed disposition you didn't choose, your standards for reconsidering reasons are given, and so the regress of flexibility terminates in a human equivalent of the wasp's reset loop -- just one that's deeper and better hidden. When pressed, the first side accuses the second of demanding a kind of freedom that is conceptually incoherent (an agent with no fixed character at all would be random, not free), while the second side accuses the first of changing the subject from freedom to mere behavioral complexity. My hypothesis is that the disagreement is not empirical -- nobody expects neuroscience to settle it -- but is instead a disagreement about what kind of explanation would even count as locating freedom: inside the causal order, at some privileged level of description, or nowhere. But I can't tell whether that's right, or whether there's a genuine factual question buried here, for instance about whether human deliberation really does exhibit a regress of modifiability that insect behavior lacks.

Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it verbal -- two camps using "free" to mark different explanatory stopping points? Is it a clash of starting intuitions about whether hidden rigidity undermines freedom, of a sort that argument cannot touch? Or is there, as I half-suspect, an unexamined empirical or formal question doing the work -- say, whether the flexibility of a system can be measured independently of the observer's choice of timescale or level of description? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says when pressed, and then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction, a result from the study of control systems or adaptive behavior, an experiment, or an argument that the question should be dissolved or replaced. If your honest assessment is that no progress is possible and the debate is a permanent fixture, argue for that conclusion explicitly rather than gesturing at it.
