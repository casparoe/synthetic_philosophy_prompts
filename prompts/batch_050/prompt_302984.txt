So last week I was at the pub with a couple of friends and we got onto whether you can really doubt everything, like evil-demon level doubting. One friend shut the whole thing down by going "I think, therefore I am" and then, when pushed, said Descartes basically proved the self is an immaterial thinking thing, so dualism is fine actually. I'm pretty sure he's smuggling something in but I couldn't pin down exactly where the move goes wrong, and it's been bugging me for days.

Here's what I'd like: take "I think, therefore I am" and lay out the different precise claims it could be making, as a menu. Like -- is it an inference, where there's a hidden premise such as whatever thinks must exist, and if so is that premise something the doubter is allowed to use? Or is it not an inference at all but more like the doubting itself trips over its own feet, so that trying to doubt your own existence is self-defeating in performance rather than in logic? Or is it just a direct report of something obvious, where the "therefore" is kind of a mistake? For each reading, say whether it's trivial, whether it's actually defensible, and what exactly the "I" is allowed to be on that reading -- a substantial self, or just the bare fact that thinking is going on, which seems way thinner.

Then the payoff question: which of these readings, if any, support the uses my friend put the slogan to? He wanted (a) total victory over the skeptic, i.e. that this defeats doubting the external world too, and (b) that it establishes the self as a purely thinking thing, which he then ran into dualism. I have a hunch the strongest reading of the slogan gets you almost none of that, but maybe I'm wrong. Also, someone at the table claimed Descartes himself, in his more careful writings, doesn't even frame it as an inference with a "therefore" -- is that right, and does it matter for how we should read the slogan? Reach actual verdicts per reading, don't just give me a tour.
