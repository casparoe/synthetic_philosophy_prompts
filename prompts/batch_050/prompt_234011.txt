In Book IV, Chapter I of The Methods of Ethics (1874), Henry Sidgwick argues that a consistent utilitarian cannot aim at the greatest average happiness per person. Since the utilitarian end is happiness on the whole rather than any particular individual's happiness, a population increase is desirable whenever the happiness gained by the additional people outweighs what the rest lose; the optimum is reached not where average happiness peaks, but where the product of the number of persons living and their average happiness is at its maximum.

Now consider how modern governments actually evaluate policies, because they seem unable to decide whether Sidgwick was right. In public health and regulatory cost-benefit analysis, agencies assign substantial value to extending the lives of existing people -- a statistical life saved, a year of healthy life gained -- but assign no value whatsoever to policies that would bring additional happy people into existence. A family planning program that averts births is scored purely on its effects on those already alive; nobody adds a credit for the lives that will now not be lived, and nobody debits one either. Existence-adding, in this corner of policy, counts for nothing. Yet in climate policy and in official discussion of existential risk, the opposite assumption does all the work: the catastrophic cost of a permanently collapsed civilization, or of a severely degraded planet, is calculated largely in terms of the welfare of future generations -- the trillions of lives that would not be lived, or would be lived at diminished quality. Here, the non-existence of happy people appears to count as an enormous loss, exactly as Sidgwick's principle implies. Meanwhile, finance ministries treat declining birth rates as a fiscal emergency requiring pronatalist subsidies, while the same governments' ethics advisory bodies insist that no one has a duty to have children and that a child not conceived is not a wronged party.

What theory of the value of population does this patchwork of practices presuppose, if any? Reconstruct the implicit axiology of each practice: which ones fit Sidgwick's totalist principle, which fit the average view he explicitly rejected, and which fit a person-affecting view on which only the welfare of people who will exist anyway can ground reasons? Are the practices jointly consistent -- can one coherently hold that extinction is catastrophic because of forgone future lives while holding that contraception costs nothing because of forgone future lives -- or does consistency require revising one of them? Where are the practices philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival axiologies would license the same scoring conventions? Finally, take seriously the standard objection to Sidgwick's principle, that it ranks a vast population of lives barely worth living above a smaller flourishing one whenever the total is larger: would a policymaker who adopted his principle without modification have to set population and climate policy differently than even the most future-focused governments now do, and does that consequence show the principle to be wrong, or merely show that current practice is a compromise no theory fully endorses?
