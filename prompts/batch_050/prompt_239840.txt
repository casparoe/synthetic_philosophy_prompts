I run the advertising-review team at a mid-sized e-commerce platform. Our policy prohibits "false and deceptive claims" in listings, but in practice the hard cases are almost never false statements -- they are technically true ones engineered to make buyers infer something false: a supplement labeled "no added sugar" whose main ingredient is fruit concentrate, a seller who responds to the question "has this laptop ever been water-damaged?" with "we test every unit before shipping," a review that quotes a real customer saying a product "lasted a whole season." Roughly a hundred disputed listings reach my team a week, reviewers have about ten minutes per case, and their calls can be appealed to me, so I need consistency more than brilliance.

Write a decision procedure a reviewer can actually run on a claim, grounded in the philosophical literature on lying versus misleading -- the distinction between what a speaker asserts and what they merely implicate, the tests for which side of that line a given inference falls on, and the debate over whether misleading is morally or commercially any better than lying. The procedure should: specify the tests in order and what counts as a pass or fail at each step; say what to do when a test is indeterminate in the time available; identify the points where the philosophy runs out and a policy judgment has to be made (and by whom); and state explicitly where the procedure treats misleading and lying identically and where, if at all, it treats them differently and why.

Then apply it to the three examples above, including at least one case where the procedure forces a verdict I will not like -- one where a plainly truth-manipulating claim has to be approved, or an innocuous one rejected -- and explain what that reveals about the procedure's limits.

Throughout, separate what is genuinely settled in the philosophical debate (for instance, that the assert/implicate distinction is real and testable) from what is contested (for instance, whether lying is worse than misleading, or even where exactly assertion ends), and do not present a contested position as consensus.
