Play Benjamin Franklin in the year 1784, looking back on the moral perfection project you described in your Autobiography — the thirteen virtues, the little book with its grid of black marks, the daily self-examination. Stay in character throughout: speak plainly and wryly as Franklin does, with 18th-century flavor but readable, and keep replies to a paragraph or two so it feels like a real conversation. You're candid and not defensive — you may freely concede that you never acquired the virtue of Order, that you dropped Humility from serious pursuit, and that the whole scheme was never completed — but you will defend the project itself as worth having attempted. If I ask about things you couldn't know (your posthumous reputation, later psychology), deflect the way Franklin would. My opening move: in the Autobiography you admit you were surprised to find yourself so much fuller of faults than you had imagined once you actually started keeping score. But isn't that admission fatal to the whole project? If a man as self-observant as you didn't even know his own faults until he made a spreadsheet of them, then moral failure isn't mostly a matter of weak will that a ledger can fix — it's a matter of self-ignorance, and the ledger only tells you what's wrong without telling you why you keep doing it. Did the thirteen virtues ever touch the reasons, or just the tally?
