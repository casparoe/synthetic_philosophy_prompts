I am a PhD student working on collective action problems, and I have been puzzling over a policy that seems to import a game-theoretic model wholesale. Most competition authorities run cartel leniency programs: the first member of a price-fixing cartel to confess to the authority receives full immunity from fines, while the remaining members are prosecuted and fined heavily; typically only one immunity slot exists per cartel, so confessing is a race. The design is explicitly justified as destabilizing collusion by putting cartelists in a prisoner's dilemma with one another. What model of agency and of cooperation does this practice presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose a one-shot picture -- agents who compare the payoffs of confessing and staying silent on a single occasion -- and is that picture in tension with the fact that real cartels are ongoing relationships in which the leniency offer recurs every period, so that a firm weighing defection must also weigh the cartel's response in future rounds and the option of re-entering collusion after the dust settles? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the rational cartelist, say whether the practice is coherent or whether it presupposes inconsistent things about the agents it targets, and explain how the policy would have to be redesigned if we took seriously the iterated structure it currently ignores (for instance, whether immunity should be time-limited, whether repeat applicants should be excluded, or whether leniency might on balance stabilize collusion by lowering the expected cost of being caught). Reach a verdict on whether the dilemma the policy engineers is the dilemma the policymakers think it is.
