Had a right old row with my mate Tom down the pub last night about whether ordinary soldiers fighting an unjust war are doing anything wrong, and after three hours neither of us budged. I want to know if my position is actually coherent or just a pile of intuitions that happen to feel right. As best I can reconstruct it this morning, I believe:

(1) Soldiers on both sides are moral equals. A conscript on the unjust side who fights bravely and keeps to the rules of war is not a murderer. The crime of the war belongs to the politicians who launched it, not the lad in the trench.

(2) The reason (1) holds is that ordinary soldiers are in no position to judge the justice of their cause: they've been lied to by their government, they lack the information, and refusing means prison or worse. Making that call is the leaders' job, which is why the leaders carry the blame.

(3) All the same, soldiers who commit war crimes -- shooting prisoners, massacring civilians -- are fully culpable. "I was ordered to" doesn't excuse that.

(4) A mercenary or true believer who volunteers for the unjust side with his eyes open is culpable in a way the reluctant conscript isn't.

(5) And it's right that after the war we put the leaders on trial and leave the rank and file alone.

Tom kept insisting I'm smuggling in a contradiction but could never pin down where, which makes me think he just doesn't like the conclusion. So: does this lot stand up? If there are tensions, rank them by seriousness and for each one say what I'd have to give up to fix it and what that costs. And don't just flag everything -- if two of these only look like they're in tension but actually aren't, tell me that too. Keep it under 500 words.
