After forty years of running a university physics laboratory, I have lately taken up epistemology, and I have been struck by a problem whose shape I recognize from instrument calibration. A gauge reads full; from the reading I infer that the tank is full; since reading and inferred level agree, I infer that the gauge is accurate; and after enough such repetitions I conclude, by enumeration, that the gauge is reliable. Every step seems licensed by the one before, yet plainly no confidence in the gauge has been earned. Epistemologists discuss reasoning of this sort under the heading of bootstrapping, and a nearby case as easy knowledge: the wall looks red, so it is red, so the lighting must be normal. What I should like is a specification — the desiderata a theory of epistemic justification must meet to count as an adequate solution for a practicing scientist. In particular, which of the following are genuine requirements and which are the prejudices of one school: that the theory explain why the gauge argument yields no justification; that it not thereby condemn every rule-circular argument, since a blanket ban on circularity appears to make deduction and induction themselves unprovable by their own lights; that it say what distinguishes legitimate calibration (two independent clocks correcting one another, which laboratories do constantly) from illegitimate bootstrapping, given that the two can be made to look formally identical; that it treat easy knowledge with the same machinery or else explain why a separate treatment is warranted; and that it leave ordinary scientific and perceptual knowledge standing. Please state the case or consideration motivating each desideratum, mark which you regard as negotiable, and tell me whether the full list is jointly satisfiable or contains an ineliminable tension — that is the part I most want to see. Skip the survey of named positions; the criteria are what I am after.
