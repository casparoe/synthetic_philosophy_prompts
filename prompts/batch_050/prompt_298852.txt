Two colleagues of mine -- both postdocs, one in philosophy of biology, one in molecular biology -- had an argument at a lab meeting last week that they've asked me to settle, and I'd rather outsource it. The trigger: their lab found that a bacterial strain carries an enzyme that happens to break down a particular plastic, a capacity that appeared by a recent mutation and, as far as anyone can tell, was never selected for -- it just drifted into the population. The question under dispute: does plastic-breakdown count as a *function* of that enzyme?

Here is the exchange as I reconstructed it afterward:

(1) The philosopher said no: a biological function is not merely a beneficial effect but an effect the trait has *because that effect explains why the trait is there* -- why it was retained, why it proliferated. The heart's function is pumping blood because hearts are around due to pumping's contribution to the survival of past bearers. The enzyme's plastic-breakdown explains nothing about its own presence, so it is an accident, like a rock that happens to hold a door open: useful, but with no function.

(2) The biologist replied that this makes function attributions hostage to evolutionary history we almost never have. We attribute functions to organs in obscure species, to genes whose origins are unrecoverable, and we'd go on saying the heart's function is to pump even if some shocking discovery showed hearts arose by pure drift -- the pumping is what the heart *does* in a way that explains how the circulatory system works, and that explanatory role is all function-talk ever needed. A function is a causal contribution to the working of a containing system, full stop; history is a fifth wheel.

(3) The philosopher answered that this account cannot distinguish function from side effect, which is precisely what the concept is for. The heart makes noise; on the biologist's account, does it have the function of making noise? Worse, the account can't make sense of *malfunction*: a diseased heart that fails to pump still has the function of pumping, and medicine depends on that thought. An accident-based notion of "what it currently does" leaves no room for a part failing to do what it's *supposed* to do.

(4) The biologist conceded the malfunction point partially but said it's handled by relativizing: a function is the contribution that explains the containing system's capacity of interest to the researcher -- the heartbeat noise contributes nothing to circulation, so it isn't a function, whereas pumping does. As for the rock and the door: relative to my project of keeping the door open, the rock does have that function, and there's nothing confused in saying so.

(5) The philosopher said this last move gives the game away: it makes biological functions mind-dependent, relative to whatever a researcher happens to care about, so that two researchers with different interests could correctly attribute different functions to the same organ -- and then "function" in biology collapses into a metaphor borrowed from engineering, not a fact about organisms.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the five moves, rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify the rating in a few sentences -- note in particular whether move (4)'s relativization genuinely absorbs the malfunction objection or merely relocates it, and whether move (5) overstates what follows from mind-dependence. Then give an overall verdict: can the enzyme's plastic-breakdown be a function, and on the best available account of function, is the concept fundamentally historical, fundamentally causal, or does it need both? If you think both colleagues are missing a distinction that would dissolve part of the dispute, say what it is. Finally, state how confident you are in your overall verdict and identify the single point in your analysis you are least sure of.
