And she argued that multiplying a huge number by "the probability that a metaphysical theory we have no way to test is true" is just not the same operation as ordinary expected value reasoning — it's more like moral fanaticism dressed up as math. I pushed back that refusing to act under deep uncertainty is itself a decision, and that her view basically lets you ignore arbitrarily large stakes whenever the probabilities get uncomfortable. We both still think we're right.

Neither of us should be a strawman — she should have genuinely good responses to the "you're just dodging expected value" charge (maybe drawing on ideas like risk-weighted expected utility, ambiguity aversion, or threshold views where sufficiently small probabilities round to zero), and I should have genuinely good responses to the fanaticism worry (maybe pressing her on where the cutoff is, or on why theory-uncertainty about consciousness should be treated differently from ordinary empirical uncertainty). Have us actually engage with each other's specific points, concede things where the other lands a real blow, and let the argument get into the weeds — e.g., whether there's a principled way to assign credences across theories of consciousness at all, whether "low probability, huge stakes" cases can be handled consistently without generating absurd results elsewhere, and whether the insect case is special or just one instance of a general problem.
