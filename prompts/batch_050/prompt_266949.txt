I'm taking a political psychology elective this semester and last week I got into a long argument with my roommate about something that seems simple but we couldn't resolve at all: is the left/right distinction actually real? Like, is there one genuine axis that politics runs along, or is it just a historical accident we keep mistaking for a natural kind? The thing that got me hooked on this is how weird the bundling is. Why should someone's opinion on gun ownership predict their opinion on carbon taxes predict their opinion on immigration? There's no obvious logical thread connecting those, and yet the correlations are incredibly strong, at least in the US. My prof mentioned that the contents of the bundles also shift across time and place -- things like environmentalism or religiosity haven't always sat where they sit now, and in some countries the package deals look pretty different. So the bundles aren't necessary truths. But they're not random either, they're stubbornly stable within a country at a time, and people really do feel that the other side is far away from them on everything at once.

Roughly, the two sides in our seminar (and in the readings, as far as I can tell) are these. One camp says the axis is psychologically deep: there's some underlying dimension -- openness to change vs. preference for order, egalitarianism vs. hierarchy, maybe something about temperament -- and the issue positions fall out of that, which is why they correlate. The other camp says the axis is just coalition glue: positions get bundled by parties and social sorting, people absorb their team's package wholesale, and the "dimension" is an artifact of that process, not its cause. My roommate's hypothesis, which I sort of share, is that this whole debate has stalled because it's actually two or three different disagreements wearing one costume: there's a factual question about how dimensional people's attitude spaces really are (which apparently comes out very differently depending on whether you study politicians and activists vs. ordinary voters), a conceptual question about what would even count as the axis being "real" vs. merely useful, and maybe a disagreement about what holds each side together that both camps keep answering with different evidence.

So what I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who wins. Is this a substantive disagreement about a psychological fact, a verbal dispute about the word "real," or two groups answering different questions (one about where attitudes come from, one about why they cluster) under a single heading? Engage with what each side actually appeals to -- the within-country stability, the cross-country and cross-decade shuffling of the bundles, the elite/mass difference in coherence -- and tell me which considerations are doing the real work and which are decoration. And then: given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here? A cleaner distinction between the questions, a specific kind of dataset (cross-cultural? longitudinal within-person?), some formal result about when a latent dimension is the right posit? Or is this one of those debates where we should expect no convergence at all because "real" was never a well-posed predicate for social categories, and if so, explain why that's not just giving up. Please don't give me a survey of the literature, I want the structure of the impasse itself.
