Write a villanelle about the debate over internal and external reasons for action, associated above all with Bernard Williams. Let the two refrains be the internalist thesis — that an agent has a reason to act only if the action connects, through sound deliberation, to motivations she actually has — and the externalist rejoinder — that some reasons bind an agent whether or not she could ever be brought to care about them. Across the five tercets, complicate the simple opposition: show how deliberation on the internalist picture can extend, revise, or prune an agent's motivational set rather than merely consulting a fixed inventory of desires; let one middle stanza voice the worry that internalism makes reasons hostage to whatever a person happens to want, and another the externalist's difficulty explaining how a consideration can be someone's reason if no deliberative route from her current outlook could lead her to act on it. In the closing quatrain, the two refrains should meet in a way that suggests the real disagreement concerns what counts as a sound deliberative route, not whether reasons matter at all. Keep the strict villanelle form and its rhyme scheme, but let the returning lines accumulate new shades of meaning each time they reappear. A philosopher familiar with the debate should recognize both positions rendered fairly, with neither caricatured, and a reader who has never heard of Williams should still find the poem worth reading as a poem.
