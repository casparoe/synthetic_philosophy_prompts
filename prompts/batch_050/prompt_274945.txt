I'm TAing a first-year ethics seminar that's doing two weeks on chapter VI of Moore's Principia Ethica ("The Ideal"). The lectures covered the famous bit — personal affection and aesthetic enjoyment as the greatest goods — and my supervisor wants me to run one 90-minute session on a corner of the chapter she skipped. Here's the passage (§118), where Moore is asking whether the *truth* of your belief about the thing you admire adds any value:

"This reason for superiority is undoubtedly of great importance in the case of human affections, where the object of our admiration is the mental qualities of an admirable person; for that two such admirable persons should exist is greatly better than that there should be only one: and it would also discriminate the admiration of inanimate nature from that of its representations in art, in so far as we may allow a small intrinsic value to the existence of a beautiful object, apart from any contemplation of it."

As I read him: my admiring my friend's generosity is a better thing if my friend really is generous — not because true belief is useful, and not merely because generous friends are good to have around, but because the whole state of affairs (admiration + actually generous friend) beats admiration pointed at a fiction. And likewise for loving a real landscape over vividly imagining the same one.

Plan the session. Concretely I need:

- The order of ideas: what has to be in place before this passage reads as a claim rather than a platitude or a muddle. I'm guessing intrinsic vs. instrumental value, then organic unities, then an anatomy of admiration itself (cognition of the qualities, the emotion, the belief that they're really instantiated) — but tell me if thats the wrong order and what the actual dependencies are.
- The one example to carry the session. I want a single case, ideally friendship-flavored, that makes the passage's distinction do work — say, admiring a real friend's courage vs. being taken in by a fictional or fabricated one — and what the example must include and must *lack* so it doesnt drag us into the wrong debate (deception, hurt feelings, etc.).
- The misconceptions in the order they'll show up: e.g. that Moore is just saying true belief is handy, or that he's saying it's good that admirable people exist (true, but not the point?), or that organic unity means "the whole is more than the sum of its parts" in the poster sense. Where in the sequence do I head each one off?
- An end-of-session check: one question whose obvious answer is the wrong answer, so I can tell who actually followed.
- What to leave out entirely at this level (Sidgwick? the two-worlds bit from ch. III?) and what to say out loud about skipping it.

One more thing, honestly: I'm not sure the passage even works. Why would my friend's existence add value *to my admiration*, as opposed to just adding separate value next to it? If the plan can end the session on exactly that as the live question, thats ideal.
