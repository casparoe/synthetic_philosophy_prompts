I spent 35 years as a marine biologist and only started reading philosophy after I retired, so forgive the clumsy setup -- but I built an argument over breakfast and it seems to prove something nuts and I cant find where it cracks. Some background first. I read about a view called tranquilism: nothing is intrinsically good, the only thing that matters is the absence of suffering and craving, so the best possible state is one of complete tranquility, no unsatisfied desire anywhere. Pleasures aren't intrinsically valuable, they're just good insofar as they relieve some craving or discomfort (eating removes hunger, etc.). Fine, I sort of buy that. Now the argument:

(1) The only thing of intrinsic value is the absence of suffering and craving in a subject.
(2) So the value of a person's state is exhausted by how little suffering and craving it contains; everything else (joy, discovery, love) matters only as means to removing cravings.
(3) Any waking life, however wonderful, still contains some unsatisfied craving -- thirst before your coffee, curiosity not yet satisfied, the mild ache of wanting tomorrow to come. Even the best hour of my life had wants in it; that's what made it dynamic.
(4) A state of deep dreamless sleep, or a permanent coma, contains no suffering and no craving at all. Zero.
(5) So by (2), a lifetime of dreamless unconsciousness is at least as good as the best waking life, and strictly better than any waking life containing even one unsatisfied desire -- which is all of them.
(6) But that's absurd. Nobody facing the choice between a rich, happy waking life and a permanent coma should be indifferent or prefer the coma; and a doctor who swapped a patient's remaining happy years for coma would be a monster, not a benefactor.
(7) Something has to go, and since (3) and (4) are just empirical observations I know are right, and (5) follows by definition of "better" from (2), the tranquilist's core claim (1) must be false.

Here's the thing though. I've poked at it and I'm not sure (7) is earned, but I also can't tell where the leak is. My fishing buddy (retired engineer, worse at this than me) says the problem is in the jump from (4) to (5) -- something about who the value belongs to -- but he couldn't articulate it. Another thought: maybe (6) is the one to bite? If I had to keep something I'd keep (4), that seems like plain fact. But I'd like to keep (1) too, honestly, since the whole pleasure-as-relief picture appeals to me.

So: which step do you reject? Please don't just say "premise X is wrong" -- explain why it looked right to me, what it costs to give it up, and whether the tranquilist has a standard move here that keeps (1) without swallowing the coma conclusion. And if the answer is that the argument is actually fine and we should prefer the coma, steelman that too, because I want to hear it said out loud before I decide whether to believe it.
