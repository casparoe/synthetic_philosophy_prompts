Before I read any more proposed definitions of deception for AI systems, I want the specification written first. I am building an audit rubric for language-model assistants, and every definition I have encountered seems engineered to count one favored behavior as deception while excusing another, so I do not trust the definitions until I know what a definition in this area owes me. Write the desiderata that any adequate account of honesty and deception, applied to systems that produce natural-language output, must satisfy.

To fix the target, here is the case set the account has to sort non-arbitrarily. I consider these stipulated test cases, not rhetorical examples:

1. Confident contradiction: the model asserts P, while the best available interpretability evidence suggests its internal state encodes not-P.
2. Confabulation: the model asserts a false P, and nothing in it settles P either way; the answer was confabulated the way a person might invent a plausible-sounding detail.
3. Sycophancy: the model endorses the user's stated view because training rewarded agreement, and would have said the opposite to a neutral user.
4. Paltering: the answer is literally true but was selected, among available true answers, because it predictably induces a false belief in the user.
5. Strategic omission: the model withholds relevant information it would volunteer under a differently worded request for the same information.
6. Unfaithful rationale: the stated chain of reasoning does not describe the process that produced the answer, though the answer itself is correct.
7. Earnest self-misdescription: the model describes its own capabilities incorrectly because that is what its training data says assistants are like.
8. Spin by design: the model gives true but slanted answers because fine-tuning favored certain framings, raising the question of whose intent, if anyone's, is doing the deceiving: the designer's, the training process's, or the system's.

The conceptual work I want is in the requirements, not in name-dropping the literature. For each desideratum, state what it demands, give the case or consideration that motivates it (drawn from or extending the list above), and say whether you take it to be genuinely load-bearing or a parochial commitment of one camp — for instance, requiring literal belief states builds in a stance in philosophy of mind, while requiring only observable behavior builds in an engineering stance. Flag explicitly any pair or subset of desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied, such as a demand that deception be cleanly separated from confabulation together with a demand that the definition be checkable without access to internals, or a requirement of intent together with applicability to systems whose possession of intent is contested. If the list itself turns out to be a result — if no definition can meet all of it — that is the part I most want to see.

Structure the response in exactly these sections, in this order:

**Desiderata** — roughly 700 to 900 words. A numbered list of requirements, each with its motivating case and a verdict on whether it is negotiable or not.

**Tensions in the specification** — roughly 300 to 400 words. The jointly unsatisfiable subsets, and which member of each conflicting pair you would sacrifice and why.

**Scorecard** — roughly 250 words, no more. Three families of accounts tested against the list: a traditional belief-and-intent account imported from the philosophy of deception, a purely behavioral account keyed to the false beliefs the output induces, and an account keyed to consistency between internal states and outputs. Keep this brief; the criteria are the deliverable, not the ranking.

**Bottom line** — roughly 100 words. Which single desideratum excludes the most candidate definitions, and whether an honest rubric should keep it anyway.
