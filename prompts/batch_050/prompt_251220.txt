I am preparing the final examination for my upper-level undergraduate seminar, "The Philosophy of Catastrophe," and I need one essay question on the moral and political status of famines. The seminar has spent four weeks on Amartya Sen's entitlement approach and its application to the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward famine of 1958–1961. My students have absorbed the standard material well enough — food availability decline versus entitlement failure, the "boom famine" in Bengal, food exports continuing out of Ireland during the blight, the role of procurement quotas and information suppression under Mao — and that is precisely the problem: any of them could recite it. In lecture I introduced a distinction of my own that they found genuinely difficult, and I want the exam to turn on it. I distinguished two different questions one can ask about a famine: the explanatory question (why did these particular people starve, given that food existed somewhere?) and the culpability question (is anyone blameworthy for it, and in the way one is blameworthy for a crime rather than merely for an error?). My worry, which I pressed on them, is that Sen's framework is built to answer the first question and that its very success there may obscure the second: a famine fully explained by market forces and shifting exchange entitlements, with no villain in sight, seems to leave no room for the thought that the Great Leap famine was a political atrocity rather than a policy mistake — and yet the intuition that there is a moral difference between the Great Leap and, say, a drought that overwhelmed a competent and responsive state is very hard to give up.

Please write one essay question (answerable in a ninety-minute exam, worth 40 of 100 marks) that forces students to confront this tension directly rather than summarize Sen. The question should present them with a constructed case of my devising — I will leave its invention to you, but it should be a famine in which every element of Sen's entitlement analysis applies cleanly, no official intends anyone's death, no law is broken, and yet something has gone morally wrong that the entitlement framework seems unable to register — and ask them to say whether that residue is real or illusory. Then write a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme. I want the model answer to take a definite position on the question rather than survey the options and leave the verdict to the reader; I have my own view, but I care more that the model answer commits, with reasons, than that it agrees with me. The marking scheme should separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific argumentative moves each must contain (for instance, what an A answer does with the counterfactual that a democratic government with a free press would not have permitted the famine), and should identify the two or three most common failure modes — answers that merely recite the three historical cases, or that conflate causal responsibility with moral culpability without noticing they have done so — and state how many marks each should cost.
