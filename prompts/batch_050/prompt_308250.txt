You are a philosopher with extensive experience teaching distributive ethics to advanced undergraduates, and you think carefully about pedagogy: what an audience must already grasp before a given idea can land, where they predictably go wrong, and how to sequence an explanation so that each misconception is headed off before it hardens. Before drafting your answer, brainstorm internally: list the conceptual components of the material below, sort them by dependency, and generate a candidate misconception map. Then present only the finished plan.

Here is the situation. I teach a third-year course in moral and political philosophy. Next week I have a single seventy-five-minute seminar on prioritarianism and the leveling-down objection. The students have already done a unit on utilitarianism, so they come in fluent with aggregative reasoning, and they have a rough, folk-egalitarian intuition that inequality is bad — but they have never encountered the distinction between comparative and non-comparative ways of valuing distributions, and I suspect that is where all my trouble will come from.

Plan how I should teach this. Do not give me the lecture itself; give me the architecture of the explanation. Specifically:

1. The order of ideas. What must be in place before the leveling-down objection can even be stated without being misunderstood, and why. My hypothesis is that the students need to see equality as a claim about relations between people's holdings before they can see the objection as targeting that relational form rather than the concern for the worse-off — but tell me if I have the dependency structure wrong, and give me the correct sequence with a reason for each step's position.

2. The carrying example. I need one example, ideally a single two-person or two-group comparison of three outcomes, that makes the objection vivid: one outcome is strictly worse for some and better for none, yet better with respect to equality. Specify what the numbers or descriptions must show, what the example must deliberately omit so that students cannot dodge the objection (for instance, it must be stripped of any instrumental story about resentment, incentives, or social cohesion, or they will think the objection is about consequences rather than values), and why a natural disaster or lottery framing is better or worse than a policy-choice framing for this purpose.

3. The misconception map. Name the misunderstandings these students will predictably form, in the order they will form them, and identify the exact point in the sequence where each should be defused. I anticipate at least these, but correct and extend my list: that the leveling-down objection is just the familiar complaint that equality is inefficient or a form of envy; that it refutes all concern for distribution rather than one specific shape of it; and, most dangerously, that prioritarianism escapes the objection merely by being non-relational — I want students to see *why* weighting the worse-off more heavily does not value anyone's being worse off, and to be able to say it in their own words rather than as a slogan.

4. The transition to prioritarianism. Where and how prioritarianism should enter — as the view that survives the objection, or introduced earlier and then tested against it? There is a real choice here about whether the objection is presented as a refutation of egalitarianism or as a diagnostic tool that sorts distributive views, and I want the choice made deliberately with a justification.

5. The check for understanding. One question or task, doable in the last ten minutes, whose answer would show that a student has grasped the structure — for instance, something that would catch a student who can recite that prioritarianism avoids leveling down but cannot explain what feature of the view does the work.

6. What to leave out. Name the material I should explicitly defer at this level — formal weighting functions, the person-affecting objection, deontic versus telic readings of egalitarianism, or whatever you judge dispensable — and say for each why including it would blur the central distinction rather than enrich it.
