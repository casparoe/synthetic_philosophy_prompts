I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've been drafting a short essay on acausal threats and surrogate goals. I'd appreciate comments before I share it with some colleagues. Here is the draft:

Essay draft: Suppose a sufficiently capable future agent wants to influence my behavior now by threatening to simulate and punish copies of me unless I act according to its interests. The standard worry is that I should comply because my decision is evidence about what my simulated copies decide, and punishing them is bad for me insofar as I might be one of them. I want to argue this threat only gets traction against agents whose reasoning is transparent enough to be predicted. My proposed defense is what I call commitment laundering: I adopt a policy whose even I cannot distinguish from a bluff. Concretely, I delegate the compliance decision to a hardware random number generator with a biased output that I never inspect, so that from the outside -- including from any simulator's perspective -- my policy is genuinely stochastic rather than derivable from my values. Since the threatening agent can only simulate my values and my deliberation, and my deliberation has been deliberately severed from my action, the simulation cannot reproduce what I'll do, and the threat loses its handle. The cost, I admit, is that I've also severed my own action from my reasoning, which looks less like winning and more like refusing to play. I then argue this reveals something about surrogate goals generally: whenever an agent manages evidence about itself rather than outcomes, as in medical Newcomb-style problems where acting cheerful is treated as a sign of health, there's a temptation to optimize the signal instead of the thing signaled. My randomization policy is just the limiting case: I optimize my unpredictability as a surrogate for my actual welfare. So the essay's conclusion is tentative -- acausal threats are defeated only by agents willing to damage their own agency, which may mean the threats were never the real problem, but rather that self-manipulating decision procedures were.

Second, identify the weakest inferential step in the draft, particularly in the move from "unpredictable" to "immune to the threat," and whether my conclusion about surrogate goals actually follows or just gestures at an analogy.
