I'm in a reading group that's been working through twentieth-century moral philosophy, and last week we read Ayn Rand's essay "Faith and Force: The Destroyers of the Modern World" (delivered as lectures in 1960 and collected posthumously in Philosophy: Who Needs It). Two members, Carla and Daniel, got into a running argument over the following passage, and I'd like an outside referee because I genuinely can't tell who's winning:

"Do not confuse altruism with kindness, good will or respect for the rights of others. These are not primaries, but consequences, which, in fact, altruism makes impossible. The irreducible primary of altruism, the basic absolute, is self-sacrifice—which means; self-immolation, self-abnegation, self-denial, self-destruction—which means: the self as a standard of evil, the selfless as a standard of the good.

Do not hide behind such superficialities as whether you should or should not give a dime to a beggar. That is not the issue. The issue is whether you do or do not have the right to exist without giving him that dime. The issue is whether you must keep buying your life, dime by dime, from any beggar who might choose to approach you. The issue is whether the need of others is the first mortgage on your life and the moral purpose of your existence. The issue is whether man is to be regarded as a sacrificial animal. Any man of self-esteem will answer: 'No.' Altruism says: 'Yes.'"

Carla's case was this. (1) Rand is attacking a strawman: in ordinary English "altruism" means concern for others' welfare, giving to charity, being decent; virtually nobody actually preaches "the self as a standard of evil," so by defining altruism as literal self-destruction Rand rigs the debate and makes normal generosity look monstrous by association. (2) The claim that altruism makes kindness "impossible" is simply false as an empirical matter: plenty of people who profess duties of sacrifice are also kind, so the distinction Rand draws is asserted, not argued. (3) The beggar passage shows Rand treating any unchosen obligation as a form of slavery, but morality plainly does contain unchosen obligations — think of the duty to rescue a drowning child at trivial cost — so her conclusion that need can never ground a claim proves too much. (4) When Rand demands an "earthly reason" for altruism and reports that none has been given, she is quietly assuming that any adequate justification must bottom out in the agent's own interest, which is exactly the question at issue.

Daniel pushed back on every point. (1) Rand cannot be conflating altruism with kindness when the passage itself opens by explicitly separating them; her target is a specific ethical doctrine — self-sacrifice as duty — not benevolence, and under her view helping others is perfectly fine when it coheres with one's own values. (2) Carla misreads "impossible": Rand isn't making a psychological prediction about who behaves kindly, she's claiming that kindness loses its moral foundation once the self is made the standard of evil — a point about justification, not about behavior. (3) The rescue case misses the target: the beggar passage is about whether another's mere need constitutes a standing mortgage on your life, not about whether one may or must ever help; Carla owes an argument that Rand's egoism can't accommodate emergency aid on her own terms, and there are passages elsewhere suggesting it can. (4) On the "earthly reason" point, Daniel said the burden lies on whoever claims one person's need generates a duty in another, and deflecting that demand as question-begging is itself evasive.

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the four exchanges, say who has the better of it and why, correcting any misreadings of the quoted text on either side. Then tell me who is more right overall, and — just as important — what both of them are missing. In particular, I'd like to know whether the dispute is really about what "altruism" means, about what justifies moral demands, or about something else, and whether there's a framing on which Carla and Daniel are simply talking past each other.
