I've spent years reading LessWrong and adjacent blogs, so I'm familiar with the standard impossibility landscape in algorithmic fairness at the level of slogans: calibration, equalized odds, and demographic parity can't all hold at once when base rates differ across groups, except in degenerate cases. What I've never seen written out is the specification itself. Before I read another paper proposing yet another fairness metric, I want the desiderata a theory of group fairness must meet to count as adequate, stated as requirements rather than as axioms someone already likes.

The setting to keep in mind: a lender deploying a risk score across two legally protected subpopulations with genuinely different base rates of default, where the regulator can audit outcomes but the lender cannot condition decisions on group membership. The deliverable is a requirements list, roughly 800 words maximum. For each requirement, give the concrete case or failure mode that motivates it -- not just the name of the desideratum, but what goes wrong in the lending scenario if it's dropped. Distinguish at least: the properties any candidate criterion must have to be a criterion of fairness at all rather than a performance metric wearing fairness clothing; the phenomena it must get right (e.g., what it should say when perfect prediction is available versus when it isn't); and the results from the impossibility theorems it must be consistent with rather than quietly contradict.

Two further constraints on the list. First, mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp (consequentialist auditors, proceduralists, individual-fairness advocates), and where a requirement is camp-specific, say which camp and why they'd insist on it. Second, and this is the part I care about most: identify the minimal jointly unsatisfiable subsets. If some combination of desiderata on my list cannot be simultaneously satisfied given unequal base rates, show the collision explicitly rather than gesturing at it, and say which single requirement does the most excluding -- which one, if dropped, buys back the most of the rest.

Finally, I'm proposing a desideratum I haven't encountered in the literature, and I want it stress-tested rather than welcomed: call it partition invariance. A fairness verdict about a decision system should not change if the regulator merges two protected categories into one or splits one into finer subgroups -- the system itself, its scores, and its decisions are untouched; only the bookkeeping changes. Is this a genuine requirement or a prejudice? Does it conflict with base-rate-sensitive criteria in a way that isn't already covered by the standard impossibility results, or is it merely a restatement of something known? If it turns out to be satisfiable, say which existing criteria survive adding it; if not, say what it costs. End with a short paragraph scoring calibration, equalized odds, demographic parity, and predictive parity against the final list, but keep the scoring brief -- the criteria and their collisions are the deliverable, not the league table.
