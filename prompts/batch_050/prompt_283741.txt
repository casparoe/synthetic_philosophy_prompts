A handout from a lecture course I attended glosses Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals like this: 'Lewis proved that the probability of an indicative conditional can never equal the conditional probability of its consequent on its antecedent. Since matching conditional probability is simply what it is for a conditional to be assertible, this shows that indicative conditionals have no truth values: they are not propositions but expressions of a speaker's degree of confidence. Lewis's own conclusion, and the consensus since, is that conditionals therefore fall outside the scope of ordinary logic — embedded conditionals are strictly meaningless, and an argument couched in conditionals cannot even be assessed for validity.' I have the sense that the first sentence states a real theorem but that the rest inflates it. Which parts would a specialist accept as a fair statement of what Lewis actually established, which present assumptions or presuppositions of the result as if they were its findings, and which go beyond anything the result or its standard interpretation supports? In particular, does the no-truth-value view follow from the theorem, and would the claims about embedding and validity follow even if it did? Please answer in British English.
