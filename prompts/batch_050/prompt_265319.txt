I write literary fiction for a living, mostly novels and the odd story, and I keep hitting the same annoying problem in revision: readers know my people are made up, and yet I need them to mourn, fear, root, feel betrayed, etc. Sometimes a draft becomes moving only when everyone kinda forgets the invented-ness for a few pages; sometimes it gets stronger when the book admits, briefly, that this is a constructed thing. I definately don't want a lecture on the paradox of fiction, I want a shop procedure I can run during line edits after coffee, maybe 20 minutes per scene, before my agent or editor sees it. I make these calls constantly, the stakes are a scene landing or going dead, and if a real person's tragedy is in the mix my spouse/first reader gets veto power.

Turn whatever is philosophically solid in the old why-do-we-cry-for-fictional-people puzzle into a repeatable checklist for me: questions in order, what evidence in the manuscript counts as an answer, where the procedure says stop and cut, where it says leave alone even if it feels cheap, and where it has to hand off to taste because no rule can decide. Keep the parts that matter, like the difference between emotion aimed at a character, emotion borrowed from a real event, and emotion produced by formal tricks; drop the jargon unless it earns rent.

Build in hard cases: unreliable narrator, metafiction, child readers, a character modeled on my dead friend, historical atrocity used as backdrop, comedy that suddenly wants pathos, and a tearjerker ending. Include little tests a novelist can actually do, like the remove-the-names test, the would-this-work-as-a-true-anecdote test, the is-the-sadness-doing-plot-work test, and a too-much-grief-meter that flags when I'm just mugging the reader.

Then run the procedure on three mini-cases I invent: a refugee-brother death scene that is technically gorgeous but emotionally unearned; a cancer subplot lifted from my family that hurts in a way I suspect is exploitative; and a playful narrator who keeps reminding you nobody here exists right before the climax. Make one verdict be something I won't like, preferably telling me to cut my favorite sentence. End by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do, and where it will misfire if I follow it like a robot.
