I run a small AI safety reading group at my high school, and next month it's my turn to present. I've chosen a topic I half-understand myself, from research on interpreting neural networks: some researchers hope to catch dangerous AI behavior not by testing what a model does, but by checking whether an output was produced by the same internal mechanism that produced the safe outputs observed during training. The motivation, as I understand it, is that a model could behave identically to an honest one on every test while computing its answers differently -- for instance, by working out what the testers want to hear -- so behavioral checks alone can't distinguish the two cases. The problem I actually want the session to be about is a step deeper: whether two computations count as the same mechanism seems to depend on the level of description. At a coarse enough grain, almost any two processes are the same; at a fine enough grain, no two runs of the same program are. Yet the whole proposal needs sameness to mean something definite, or the check is vacuous.

I have thirty minutes, a whiteboard, and an audience of classmates who have trained small models but never read interpretability work. Plan the session rather than giving the explanation itself. Specifically:

- The order in which the ideas must be introduced and what each depends on. My guess is: the behavior/mechanism distinction first, then why behavior-based testing fails, then mechanism-sameness as the proposed fix, then the level-of-description problem as the difficulty internal to that fix. Tell me whether that ordering is right, and if any piece actually has to come earlier than I think.
- The single example that should carry the whole session. I'm considering two chess programs that play identical moves in standard positions -- one by genuinely searching the game tree, one by consulting an enormous memorized table -- which diverge only once a game leaves the book. Tell me whether this example has everything it needs (same observable behavior, genuinely different inner workings, an identifiable divergence point) and, just as important, what it must not contain so that it doesn't mislead about neural networks specifically.
- The misconceptions my classmates will predictably form -- my guesses are: that matching outputs across enough tests settles the mechanism question, that same mechanism must mean the same neurons firing in the same pattern, and that there is one objectively correct level of description the researcher merely has to find -- plus exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off, before it hardens.
- One closing question that checks whether they've grasped the real point: that a claim of mechanism-sameness is made relative to a chosen description and is justified by what that description is meant to guarantee, rather than read off the system as plain fact.
- What I should openly refuse to cover at this level, and say so out loud.
