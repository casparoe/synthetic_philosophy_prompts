So last night my friend Dan and I wasted about four hours arguing about Parfit's teletransporter (the one from Reasons and Persons where you're scanned on Earth, destroyed, and rebuilt atom-for-atom on Mars) and I'm still annoyed about how it ended, which was: nowhere. Not "nowhere" as in we agreed to disagree — nowhere as in by the end we were both just repeating our opening moves louder. What I want is a diagnosis of what actually went wrong, not another take on the case itself.

Our positions, concretely. Dan: you die in the booth, full stop. The person who steps out on Mars remembers your life, loves your partner, has your terrible taste in films, and is nonetheless someone else — a brand-new being with copied memories. His killer move was the branch-line version: if the scanner doesnt destroy the Earth-you, and the Mars copy phones you up, obviously the copy isn't you (you're standing right here), so why would vaporizing the original change who the copy is? Death plus replacement either way, he says; the normal version just hides it better. Me: I said everything I could possibly care about survives on Mars — memory, character, intentions, the whole psychology — and the "but is it really YOU" question is an empty extra bolted on top. I used the fission case against him: if I'm split into two successors I can't be identical to both, but surely being split beats being shot, so identity can't be what's doing the work.

We went around: him saying I'd rather be shot than replaced, me saying he can't point to a single thing that's missing on Mars, him saying what's missing is ME, and so on. At one point he literally said "surviving just means being the same person later" and I said "and being the same person just means the psychology continuing" and we both noticed we were just defining our conclusions into existence. Thats when it stalled for good.

Afterwards I went back to Locke, since this whole debate is downstream of him, and found this right at the start of the chapter on identity and diversity (Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, ch. XXVII) — not one of the bits people usually quote:

"one thing cannot have two beginnings of existence, nor two things one beginning ; it being impossible for two things of the same kind to be or exist in the same instant, in the very same place ; or one and the same thing in different places. That, therefore, that had one beginning, is the same thing ; and that which had a different beginning in time and place from that, is not the same, but diverse. That which has made the difficulty about this relation has been the little care and attention used in having precise notions of the things to which it is attributed."

So, two questions.

First, the passage itself. Dan would love the first half: the Mars-replica plainly has "a different beginning in time and place," so by Locke's own principle it's not the same thing, end of story. But the last sentence seems to undercut deploying the principle that confidently — Locke says the difficulty with identity comes from not having precise notions of WHAT we're attributing identity to. So does the "two beginnings" principle actually settle anything about the teletransporter, or does it just relocate the fight to "same what?" (same organism? same person? same stream of consciousness?) — which is awkward for Dan, since settling that is exactly the thing Locke says nobody bothers to do carefully. Tell me how to read the tension between the two halves of this passage.

Second, diagnose my argument with Dan. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: we stalled for exactly the reason in Locke's last sentence — we never fixed what "I" refers to, so we were answering different questions under one word ("survive"), and it was a precision failure verging on verbal, not a deep metaphysical disagreement. But I can think of rival diagnoses: (a) it's substantive, about whether identity is what matters or merely tags along with it; (b) it's a hidden formal disagreement about whether survival must be one-one, with the fission case quietly doing all the work; (c) it's bedrock gut intuitions about a scenario neither of us can argue below; (d) the question "would it be ME?" is just ill-posed and the stall is the symptom. Which diagnosis fits best, based on the actual moves we made rather than the textbook versions of our views? And given your diagnosis: what would count as progress — a distinction we failed to draw, a better thought experiment, an argument one of us hasn't heard — or should I accept that nothing moves Dan here, and why?
