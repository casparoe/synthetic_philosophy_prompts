I am a novelist at work on a book in which a seemingly innocent puzzle, posed at a dinner party, quietly corrodes a friendship. To write the scene authentically I tried the puzzle on two mathematically literate friends of mine, and their real argument turned out to be far more interesting than anything I had scripted. Before I fictionalize it, I want to be sure I understand who was actually right about what. The puzzle: draw a chord of a circle "at random." What is the probability that the chord is longer than the side of an equilateral triangle inscribed in that same circle?

Marcus answered 1/3. Fix one endpoint of the chord anywhere on the circumference; by symmetry it does not matter where. The other endpoint is equally likely to land anywhere around the circle, and the chord exceeds the triangle's side exactly when that second endpoint falls within the third of the circumference opposite the first — hence 1/3. Priya answered 1/2. Every chord is determined by its midpoint's perpendicular distance from the center, and the chord exceeds the triangle's side exactly when that distance is less than half the radius; if the distance is uniformly distributed between 0 and the radius, the answer is 1/2. Marcus objected that her method was no less arbitrary than his, and proposed settling it empirically: scatter pencils across a large paper circle and count. Priya replied that the experiment settles nothing, because the physical scattering procedure itself builds in a choice of distribution — roll pencils toward the circle's center and you approximate her setup, spin a pointer from a point on the rim and you approximate his. Marcus then changed tack: he had somewhere encountered the claim that only one of the candidate methods gives an answer that stays the same if the circle is drawn larger, or shifted on the page, and that this singles out 1/2 as the correct answer after all. Priya was unmoved: insisting that a probability be indifferent to the size of the page struck her as an aesthetic preference masquerading as a mathematical requirement, and her conclusion stood — the phrase "at random" is simply underspecified, and the puzzle has no answer. Marcus's parting shot was that this refusal is a dodge: ordinary speakers mean something when they say "random chord," and if one interpretation is the natural one, that is the answer.

Please adjudicate the dispute point by point: whether each of the two calculations is internally valid; whether Marcus's proposed experiment would settle anything; whether Priya's "no fact of the matter" verdict follows, or whether Marcus's appeal to the natural reading has force; and whether the invariance consideration Marcus raised is a legitimate constraint on what "random" must mean, and if so, whether it actually delivers 1/2. Where both of them are missing something, say so. After your analysis, give me a table with exactly these columns: Point of Dispute; Marcus's Position; Priya's Position; Verdict (Marcus, Priya, Both, or Neither); and Basis, a single sentence stating the grounds for the verdict. I intend to use the table as scaffolding for the chapter, so precision matters more to me than diplomacy toward either of them.
