For a handout in a second-year undergraduate metaphysics module on persistence through time, write a "which theory of persistence do you actually hold?" quiz. It should have eight to ten questions, each built around a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto five positions: classical endurantism, perdurantism in its worm-theory version, stage theory (exdurantism), mereological essentialism, and a deflationary option on which persistence facts are partly a matter of how we choose to talk.

Some constraints on the questions. First, no question may mention any of the five labels or ask anything a student could answer by recognizing a slogan ("objects are wholly present at each time," and so on); every question must be a case on which the positions genuinely come apart, such as a gradually repaired artifact, a reassembly of discarded parts, a coincident statue and lump of material, or an object that undergoes fission. Second, please avoid the two most worn-out illustrations in this literature -- the ship of Theseus and Gibbard's clay statue -- and invent fresher cases with the same logical shape, so that the quiz tests judgment rather than recall. You may use at most one of the classic cases if you genuinely cannot do without it. Third, include at least one question specifically designed to separate worm theory from stage theory, since both are four-dimensionalist; if you find that no single case cleanly separates them, say so and explain what additional question (about counting, reference, or parthood over time) would be needed.

For the scoring key: show which combinations of answers indicate each position, including the two hybrid patterns a thoughtful student might produce (an endurantist with essentialist leanings; a four-dimensionalist who will not commit between worm and stage). Identify at least one answer pattern that corresponds to no position anyone holds, and diagnose whether that region of the logical space is empty because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Finally, flag explicitly any pair of positions the quiz as a whole cannot reliably separate, and say in one or two sentences why the instrument fails there rather than disguising the failure in the key.
