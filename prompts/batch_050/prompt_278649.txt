I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar that just spent two weeks on Malcolm's attack on the idea that dreams are experiences undergone during sleep, and on Dennett's suggestion that a "dream" might be nothing more than a memory-like recording loaded into consciousness at the moment of waking. A classmate, defending a rough synthesis of the two, produced the following argument. I find the conclusion it reaches in its final step intolerable, but when I press on individual steps each one looks locally defensible, and our instructor declined to arbitrate. Here is the argument as he stated it:

(1) All the evidence anyone ever has for the occurrence and content of a dream consists in what the dreamer is inclined to say, remember, and report after waking.
(2) If two accounts are compatible with exactly the same total evidence -- not merely the evidence we happen to have gathered, but any evidence we could possibly obtain -- then there can be no rational grounds for accepting one account over the other.
(3) Consider account A: during sleep I underwent experiences, which my waking memory now imperfectly records. And account B: no experiences occurred during sleep at all; the apparent memory of them was produced at or shortly before waking. A and B are compatible with exactly the same total evidence, since every check I can run on a dream I run while awake, upon my present inclinations.
(4) Therefore there are no rational grounds for believing that anything was experienced during sleep; the sleeping experience is an idle posit, and the sober view is that a dream is constituted by the waking report rather than reported by it.
(5) Nothing in steps (1) through (4) is specific to sleep. Substitute yesterday's headache for the dream: all my evidence consists of present memories and present traces, and the accounts "the headache occurred and memory records it" and "the world, memories and traces included, came into being recently" are compatible with exactly the same total present evidence.
(6) So by the same principle, there are no rational grounds for believing that I had any experiences whatever before the present moment.
(7) That conclusion is absurd, so something above must go -- yet each premise looks like plain empiricist hygiene.

For what it is worth, I would rather keep (1) and (2) than any other part, and I would like the diagnosis to explain why dreams and headaches come apart, if in fact they do -- Malcolm and Dennett plainly thought the dream case was special, and I want to know whether that conviction can survive contact with (5). Which step do you reject, why did it look acceptable, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? Please lead with your verdict in the first sentence -- naming the step you reject -- and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the argument.
