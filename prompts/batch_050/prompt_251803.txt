I recently took over as secretary of a long-running community organization, and the role has left me preoccupied with a question I keep running into in both directions at once: when is it legitimate to say that something exists for a reason, or embodies one, even though no one ever intended or even understood that reason? Some of our bylaws turned out to be quietly wise in ways nobody alive could explain; others turned out to be pure dead weight. Around the same time I found myself reading about what Dennett calls free-floating rationales in evolution, and I realized I don't actually know what my criterion is. So here are my verdicts on a range of cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

The honeybee's waggle dance has a genuine rationale -- it communicates the direction and distance of food -- even though no bee understands it. Verdict: a real free-floating rationale. The heart's function is to pump blood, and saying so is more than shorthand; a heart that fails to do it is malfunctioning. Verdict: legitimate. But when a rock in a stream happens to aerate the water and thereby supports fish, I don't think the rock has the function of aerating anything. Verdict: no rationale there.

Among our bylaws: we require that membership applications be read aloud at two consecutive meetings before a vote. No one knew why, but I eventually worked out that the delay reliably screens out applicants who join in a burst of grievance and lose interest within a month. Verdict: there is a real rationale for the rule, and it was there before I found it; I discovered it, I didn't invent it. By contrast, we also require paper sign-in sheets in triplicate, which is simply a leftover from a pre-digital era that persists because changing it takes effort. Verdict: no rationale, just inertia -- even though, like the first rule, it has survived for decades.

Two more cases, and these are the ones I suspect don't sit well together. When a competitive market's prices aggregate information that no single participant possesses, I want to say the price system embodies a rationale that no one holds. But when a speculative bubble inflates, driven by the same decentralized mechanisms of imitation and feedback, I don't want to say the inflated price embodies any rationale at all -- and yet in both cases the outcome is produced by selection-like processes operating without anyone's comprehension. Finally: a trained neural network classifies images using a heuristic no programmer designed or understands. I lean toward saying the heuristic is a genuine free-floating rationale, but I notice I'm less confident here than with the bees, and I can't articulate why.

My tentative principle was something like: a rationale exists without a reasoner whenever a pattern was shaped and is maintained by a selection process that rewards the effects the rationale would explain. But this seems to count the bubble as rationalized, and possibly the triplicate forms too, since institutional inertia is arguably a kind of selection. Is there a better single principle that captures most of my verdicts -- stated precisely, with the clause that each case motivates -- or a small set of candidates? For each, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to surrender, and what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed: a nervous tic that happens to calm the person who has it, and the grammatical structure of a natural language. If you think some of my verdicts are simply wrong rather than in need of a cleverer principle, say so plainly and explain which.
