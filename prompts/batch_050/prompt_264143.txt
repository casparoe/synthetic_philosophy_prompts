You are a decision theorist with a specialty in the foundations of economic rationality, preparing teaching material for a graduate seminar on ergodicity economics and the debate over time averages versus ensemble averages. Your students keep arriving with muddled slogans — "expected utility is dead," "log utility is secretly rational," "it's all just Kelly betting" — and you want a decision tree that forces them to locate themselves in the logical space of positions before they are allowed to use any of the slogans.

Before drafting the tree, brainstorm: list the distinct claims in the debate on which reasonable people come apart (about which average is decision-relevant, about what the ergodicity argument actually proves, about repetition and one-shot choice, about descriptive versus normative status), identify which combinations of answers are coherent, and only then place the named positions in the space. Then write the tree itself, conforming to these requirements:

1. The root commitment is: "I accept that, for many real-world stochastic processes (multiplicative wealth dynamics being the canonical case), the time-average growth rate and the ensemble-average growth rate differ." From there, each branch node must be a question about a claim that genuinely divides the positions, phrased so that a student can answer it without already knowing which camp it leads to. Candidate axes you should seriously consider including (add, merge, or replace as you see fit): whether the ensemble/time-average gap has any normative bearing on individual choice at all; whether, if it does, this constitutes a genuinely new decision principle or is already fully captured by ordinary expected utility maximization with an appropriate concave utility function; whether the argument extends to one-shot gambles and non-stationary processes or is confined to indefinitely repeated play; whether the claim is normative (how agents ought to choose) or descriptive (which behaviors survive selection, e.g., evolutionary or market selection favoring time-average growth maximizers); and whether non-ergodicity of real economies undermines or merely complicates the whole framing.

2. Each leaf must carry a named position, or "unnamed" if the combination is coherent but you cannot identify a defended view in the literature or in public debate. Among the leaves, the following must appear somewhere: the full ergodicity-economics program associated with Ole Peters and colleagues (replace ensemble-based expected utility with time-average growth rates); the deflationary view that ergodicity economics is just expected utility theory with log- or growth-oriented utility in disguise; a purely descriptive/evolutionary reading; and a pluralist view that assigns time-average reasoning to repeated settings and expected-value reasoning to one-shot settings. Aim for 7 to 10 leaves total.

3. For every coherent combination of answers that nobody seems to occupy, add a one-sentence diagnosis: is the cell empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored?

4. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot reliably separate — for instance, whether the normative Peters program and the evolutionary selection reading collapse into the same practical advice — and state what further question would be needed to split them.

5. Each node question must be answerable from the student's own commitments about a concrete case (e.g., a repeated multiplicative coin-toss gamble, a one-shot version of the same gamble, a population of gamblers versus a single gambler over time), not from vocabulary like "ergodic" or "ensemble." Test each node against this standard and rewrite any that fail.

Present the tree as nested, indented text with clearly numbered nodes and leaves, followed by a short section collecting the flags from requirements 3 and 4. Think carefully about the order of the nodes: earlier splits should do more classificatory work than later ones, and you should briefly justify the ordering you chose.
