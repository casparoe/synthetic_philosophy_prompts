I'm putting together teaching materials for an undergraduate seminar on individual responsibility for collective harms, and I want a decision tree that sorts students by what they actually believe rather than by which label they've heard of. The starting point on the tree should be the familiar inefficacy worry in its strongest everyday form: my one flight, my one burger, my one vote makes no perceptible difference to climate change, factory farming, or the election outcome, so why should I bother? Please act as a professional philosopher who works on collective action problems, and think carefully about the logical space of responses before settling on the tree's structure -- I'd suggest you begin by sketching, in a few lines, the branching questions you considered and rejected before presenting the final instrument.

Build the tree from diagnostic questions first and positions second. Each branch node should be a concrete yes/no or either/or question that a thoughtful student could answer without knowing where it leads -- for instance, questions about whether they accept expected-value reasoning when probabilities are tiny, whether a single purchase can ever trigger a supplier's threshold (e.g., the supermarket ordering one more case of chicken), whether wrongness requires making an outcome worse or can consist in participating in a jointly harmful practice, and whether they'd still act differently if it were *guaranteed* that the outcome was already fixed. Leaves should then be located in the space these questions generate. The leaves must include at least: expected-value consequentialism (the inefficacy premise is empirically false -- small probabilities times huge stakes), the threshold/trigger view (individual acts do make a difference in expectation because of lumpy supply responses), participation or complicity accounts, Kantian or universalizability approaches, rule-consequentialist or cooperative-scheme views, expressive or virtue-based accounts, and the skeptical terminus where the student accepts the inefficacy argument and concludes there is no individual obligation at all. Feel free to add leaves if the questions generate them naturally.

A few further requirements. First, the tree should run on a single running case -- please use one person deciding whether to buy a particular meat product -- so that the questions stay concrete, though you may note where a climate or voting analogue would change the verdict. Second, explicitly flag any two leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, and say what further question would be needed to pry them apart. Third, after the tree, list any combinations of answers that are logically available but unoccupied by a named position, and briefly diagnose each: is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored in the literature? Fourth, keep the tree to between eight and twelve branch nodes, and phrase every node as a question, not as a statement of a view. Finally, end with a short note (a paragraph at most) on where you think the tree's most controversial branching lies -- the node where a critic of your taxonomy would say you've already smuggled in a substantive assumption just by asking the question in that form.
