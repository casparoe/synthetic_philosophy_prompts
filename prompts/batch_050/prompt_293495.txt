I'm a backend engineer at a company that does content moderation tooling, and lately I've fallen down a rabbit hole about deepfakes and what they're doing to our ability to trust recordings. A friend of mine who's in law school told me about this idea philosophers have, the "epistemic backstop" -- roughly, the idea that photos and video function as a kind of floor under how much people can lie, because in principle there's always a recording you could be checked against. And the worry is that deepfakes (or even just the *possibility* of deepfakes) knock that floor out. I've read a couple of pop pieces on this and they all gesture at "trust" without being precise, and I keep having the feeling that the arguments are talking past each other because nobody's said what a good theory here would even have to accomplish.

So before I read any more, I want the specification first. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of what synthetic media does to the epistemic backstop must meet: the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it can't collapse. To seed you a bit, the cases I keep tripping over are: (1) the empirical fact that people largely *haven't* stopped believing video footage -- doorbell cam clips still settle arguments, so a theory that just predicts total collapse of trust seems wrong; (2) the flip side, people dismissing real footage as AI-generated when it's inconvenient (I think people call this the liar's dividend?), which is a *different* failure mode than being fooled by fakes; (3) provenance-based fixes like cryptographically signed capture metadata, which seem to relocate the backstop rather than restore it -- you're trusting the camera manufacturer or the signing authority now; (4) the difference between "this video is fake" and "this real video is decontextualized," which cheap editing achieves without any AI at all; and (5) the gap between individual-level epistemics (can I tell this clip is real?) and society-level epistemics (can a court or a news ecosystem function?), which might have opposite answers. For each desideratum give the case or consideration behind it, mark which ones you think are genuine requirements vs. prejudices of one camp, and say explicitly if any subset looks jointly unsatisfiable -- that's the part I most want to see, since it would mean the interesting result is in the list itself.

Then, briefly, score the main loose camps I keep seeing against the list: the "backstop is dead, welcome to the epistemic apocalypse" people, the "nothing changed, photos were always fakeable, see Stalin-era airbrushing" skeptics, and the provenance-tech optimists. Keep the scoring short though -- the criteria and their motivating cases are where I want the depth, since I can do the scoring myself once the list is good. I'm a software person with zero philosophy training, so avoid jargon or define it as you go. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- e.g., the single most demanding desideratum on the list, or whether the desiderata are jointly satisfiable at all -- then give the reasoning, no preamble and don't restate my question back at me.
