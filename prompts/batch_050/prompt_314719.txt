I am a PhD student in political theory writing a dissertation on the justification of paternalistic regulation, and the standard justificatory move in my corner of the field is to appeal not to what citizens actually want but to what they would want under better conditions: fully informed, calm, reasoning carefully, unmoved by advertising, vividly aware of the consequences. I keep running into a structural difficulty with this move, and I need to know whether it already has a name before I inflict a neologism on my committee.

The difficulty, in three forms. First, sequence. Take a real person — say a smoker in her forties who also finds her work meaningless and vaguely wishes she had become a musician. If you improve her by first confronting her with the medical facts, the reflection that follows tends to consolidate her around health and family; if you instead first lead her through sustained reflection on what makes a life worthwhile, she may come out caring less about longevity than about the years having a certain shape. Both steps are exactly the sort of "better conditions" my literature invokes, and neither is rigged, yet which you apply first appears to fix where she ends up. Second, route. Two procedures for idealizing the same person — one that amplifies whichever concern is currently strongest in her, one that seeks equilibrium among all her current concerns — are each individually defensible, and they deliver different idealized verdicts about the same policy. Third, the starting snapshot. Extrapolating "more of what she already is" yields one endpoint if you begin from her before some formative experience (a bereavement, a religious conversion, a year abroad) and a different endpoint if you begin after, and I can find no non-question-begging grounds for privileging either snapshot.

The general shape: whenever we say what someone would want under idealized conditions, the answer seems to vary with the path, the procedure, or the starting point of the idealization, and the choice among these cannot itself be settled by appeal to idealized wanting without circularity. My questions:

I have a vague recollection of an objection — I believe called the conditional fallacy, associated with a philosopher named Shope, though I may be misremembering both the label and the attribution — aimed at analyses where the test condition itself changes the thing being tested; I also know there are well-known objections to full-information accounts of well-being (I associate the target view with Railton, again subject to correction), and a body of psychology suggesting preferences are constructed in elicitation rather than merely revealed. For each of these, please say whether my phenomenon is the same thing, a special case of it, a generalization of it, or merely adjacent to it — and be precise about what each covers that mine does not, and vice versa. In particular, is the conditional-fallacy objection about the metaphysics of dispositional analyses, about epistemology, or about something else, and does it actually extend to my sequence and starting-snapshot cases?
