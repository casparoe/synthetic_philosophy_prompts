So last week at my local LessWrong meetup we ended up in a three-hour argument about metaethics (as you do) and I got properly stuck on something, so I want to stress-test it here. I've been fairly sympathetic to expressivism for ages — the idea that "stealing is wrong" isn't a description of the world that could be true or false, but more like booing stealing, or disapproving of it, or ruling it out in your plan for how to live. But someone brought up the old Frege-Geach problem and I couldn't defend my position. Roughly: consider someone who accepts "stealing is wrong" and also accepts "if stealing is wrong, then stealing to feed your kids is wrong". Intuitively they're committed to "stealing to feed your kids is wrong", and it would be some kind of rational failure to accept the first two and shrug at the third. But if moral sentences aren't truth-apt, then the conditional in the middle isn't a normal conditional connecting propositions, so it's not clear modus ponens even applies — and the so-called commitment starts looking mysterious.

Here's the bit I want argued out. The standard expressivist reply I've seen (I think it traces to Blackburn and Gibbard, though I might be mangling the history) is that the commitment isn't logical inconsistency but a clash of attitudes: accepting those two things while refusing the conclusion amounts to being in a fractured, self-defeating state of mind, roughly like intending to do something while intending not to. My meetup friend insisted this reply just relocates the problem rather than solving it. Please argue his side: make the strongest case you can that the "clash of attitudes" response fails, on the grounds that the allegedly fractured mental state is only genuinely irrational if the conditional has descriptive, truth-evaluable content — that is, a mere mismatch of sentiments isn't a rational defect at all unless there's a fact of the matter the attitudes are answerable to, which is exactly what the expressivist denies. Feel free to steelman the expressivist along the way if it makes the case tighter; I'd rather have the argument survive the obvious comebacks than win cheap.

Write in British English please, and keep it readable — I've read the standard stuff but I'm not a philosopher by training. At the very end, give me a last line of the form "Verdict: <one sentence stating whether the expressivist escape succeeds or fails, and why in a nutshell>".
