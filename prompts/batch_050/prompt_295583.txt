I am grading a take-home exam for an upper-level course on moral uncertainty and collective choice. Below is the exam question, followed by a student's answer. Please assign a grade out of 10 and justify it with point-by-point comments identifying what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong, and which mistakes are substantive rather than merely verbal. Do not give a general impression; I want the specific observations that would go in the margin.

Exam question: An agent is torn between two value systems she takes seriously: W, a total welfarism that evaluates outcomes solely by the sum of welfare, and P, a prioritarian view that evaluates outcomes by a weighted sum giving extra weight to the worse-off. Define a safe Pareto improvement over the status quo as an option that both value systems rank at least as good as the status quo by their own lights, with at least one ranking it strictly better. Consider three alternatives to the status quo S: option A greatly improves the position of the worst-off but slightly lowers total welfare; option B greatly raises total welfare but slightly worsens the position of the worst-off; option C modestly improves both total welfare and the position of the worst-off. (a) Which options, if any, are safe Pareto improvements over S? Explain. (b) Give one consideration in favor of restricting choice to safe Pareto improvements in contexts of moral uncertainty, and one objection to doing so.

Student's answer: (a) A Pareto improvement, in the standard sense, makes at least one party better off without making anyone worse off, and the safe version extends this to value systems: an option is a safe Pareto improvement when every value system the agent takes seriously endorses it over the status quo. Option C is clearly a safe Pareto improvement, since both W and P prefer it to S. Option A is also a safe Pareto improvement: P ranks A far above S, and although W ranks A slightly below S, P's strong preference outweighs W's mild opposition when the two are compared on a common scale, so A passes the unanimity test on balance. Option B fails by symmetry. (b) One reason to restrict choice to safe Pareto improvements is that doing so lets the agent act without ever having to compare the two theories' units of value, since the criterion only requires each theory to be scored cardinally on its own scale before the scales are merged into a common metric. One objection is that the restriction is biased toward the status quo: there may be options that would be best by the lights of the theory the agent is most confident in, yet are ruled out because some less credible theory vetoes them.
