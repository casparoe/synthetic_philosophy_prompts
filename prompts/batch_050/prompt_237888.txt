I work at a mid-size economics journal as an associate editor, and a chunk of my job is triaging papers that lean on models with obviously false assumptions -- infinitely lived agents, perfect foresight, representative consumers, that sort of thing. Referee reports are all over the place on this. Some referees dismiss a paper the moment an assumption is unrealistic, others wave everything through with a vague nod to Friedmans 1953 essay (the whole "assumptions don't matter, only predictions do" thing), and honestly both moves feel lazy. I want a screening procedure I can run myself in maybe 20 minutes per paper, and eventually hand to referees, for deciding whether a paper's unrealistic assumptions are a problem for what the paper is actually trying to do.

Ground it in what philosophy and methodology of economics actually has to say about this, but translated so someone who hasn't read Musgrave or Cartwright can use it. Things I'd want it to capture: whether the assumption is doing idealization work (stripping away stuff that plausibly doesn't matter for the question) versus distorting work (building the answer into the setup), whether the false assumption is one the paper's conclusion is *robust* to or one where relaxing it flips the result, the difference between a paper making a predictive claim and one making a mechanism/explanation claim (since the F-twist defense arguably only covers the former), and whether the authors even tell you which assumptions they know are false. The procedure should say what to do when I can't tell -- which will be often, since I'm not re-deriving anyone's model.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical submissions so I can see it work: (1) a macro model with a representative agent and rational expectations used to forecast the effect of a tax change, (2) a game-theoretic model assuming common knowledge of rationality that's used to *explain* why firms collude, and (3) a model where the clearly-false assumption (say, zero transaction costs) is doing all the heavy lifting for a policy recommendation. At least one of these should come out with an answer I won't like -- i.e., the procedure defends a paper that smells fishy to me, or kills one I'd normally accept.

Also flag the points in the procedure where its really just judgment dressed up as steps, and where it will misfire. I'd rather know the limits than pretend this is mechanical.
