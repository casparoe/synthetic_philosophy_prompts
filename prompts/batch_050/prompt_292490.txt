I serve on the board of a mid-sized AI research organization whose charter commits it to ensuring that advanced AI systems remain under meaningful human control. At next month's meeting the board will vote on a proposed operating policy, and I have been asked to prepare the memo around which the discussion will turn.

The context: two of our senior researchers have sketched a long-run strategy they call the deliberate-trailer policy. Rather than compete to be first to each new capability threshold, the organization would deliberately stay roughly one product cycle behind the frontier developer, while investing heavily in the capacity to understand, audit, and adapt whatever the leader builds. Their proposal rests on an assumption I want the memo to examine rather than take for granted — call it the strategy-stealing assumption: that any workable plan a leading project could execute to gain durable strategic advantage could, in principle, be adopted and executed instead by a slightly-behind project, since a good plan works for whoever executes it. If the assumption holds, trailing is cheap insurance: we lose little by not leading, because anything worth doing first can be done second by a better-motivated actor. If it fails, trailing may mean conceding the only window in which the trajectory could have been shaped.

Please draft a memo of roughly 800–1,000 words that I can circulate before the meeting. It should do the following:

- Recommend a clear position on the deliberate-trailer policy: adopt, reject, or adopt in a specified qualified form, with concrete conditions or triggers attached — including what observable developments should cause the board to abandon the policy if adopted.
- Analyze the strategy-stealing assumption itself: under what conditions concerning the technology and the competitive environment it is plausible, and under what conditions it fails. I particularly want the memo to consider strategies whose first irreversible step forecloses later imitation (for instance, locking in standards or infrastructure that others must then build on); strategies available only to an actor willing to cut safety corners, so that a more scrupulous trailer cannot in fact execute them; and dynamics in which a capability lead is self-reinforcing, so that an actor who is slightly behind does not remain slightly behind.
- Engage seriously with the strongest argument on whichever side the memo recommends against: if it recommends trailing, the case that only a frontier developer is positioned to set the norms and safety practices everyone else will inherit; if it recommends competing for the lead, the case that racing dynamics are precisely what our charter exists to resist.
- Close with an actionable bottom line the board can vote on, including what we should monitor over the coming year to learn whether the assumption is in fact holding.

The memo should be written for intelligent non-specialists: define terms as needed, avoid gesturing at any literature, and argue from the structure of the situation rather than from authority.
