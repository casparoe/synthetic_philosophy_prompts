I teach a second-year undergraduate module in normative ethics, and next term I want to devote one ninety-minute seminar to the heterogeneity problem for hedonism about well-being: the objection that the things we call pleasures — the taste of coffee, finishing a proof, warm water on sore muscles, someone else's misfortune — seem to have nothing in common as feelings, so that "pleasure" may not name one thing at all, let alone the one thing welfare consists in. The students will already have met crude sensory hedonism and Nozick-style experience machine worries, but nothing more technical. Do not write the seminar for me; plan how I should run the explanation. I want the following, with definite choices rather than a menu of options.

First, the order of ideas. State which distinctions have to be in place before the heterogeneity worry can be posed as a genuine problem rather than a definitional quibble — in particular, whether students first need hedonism-as-a-theory-of-welfare separated from hedonism about value generally and from psychological hedonism, and whether the attitudinal response (pleasure as an attitude of taking pleasure in things, rather than a shared feeling) should be introduced early as the view under construction or held back as a rescue attempt that students watch get into its own trouble. Justify the ordering by what depends on what.

Second, the carrying example. Choose one example or tightly linked pair of examples that bears the whole session, and say what it must have and what it must lack. My guess is that it must juxtapose pleasures so phenomenologically different that the shared-feeling view looks hopeless, while remaining ordinary enough that nobody treats the problem as an artifact of a weird case — and that it must not be the experience machine, which pulls attention toward authenticity instead. Confirm or correct that guess, and explain why your chosen example forces the right question.

Third, the misconception map. Predict the confusions the students will bring or form, in the order they will surface: for instance, that heterogeneity straightforwardly refutes hedonism; that "pleasure" just means bodily sensation and the objection is trivially true of a straw man; that moving to an attitudinal account dissolves the problem rather than relocating it (since one can then ask what the attitude has in common across its objects, or whether the attitude itself is one thing); and conflating the claim that pleasures differ in kind with the claim that they differ in value. For each, say where in your sequence it should be headed off and what move does the heading-off.

Fourth, the check for understanding. Give me one question I can pose in the last ten minutes whose answer would show that a student has grasped that heterogeneity is a problem about the unity of a purported phenomenon — about whether there is one thing for the theory to be a theory of — and not merely about how good pleasure is or whether it is the only good.

Fifth, what to leave out. Name at least one adjacent issue (for example, whether neuroscience's talk of a common reward currency settles the question, or whether the same problem re-arises for desire-satisfaction and objective list theories) that I should explicitly announce I am bracketing, and why bracketing it openly is better than letting students raise it derail the session.

Throughout, commit: pick the example, fix the sequence, and where philosophers disagree about how damaging the objection is, take a side and give your reasons rather than presenting the landscape and leaving me to choose.
