I am a PhD student working on the epistemic regress problem, and I have been trying to bring Aristotle's account of demonstration to bear on the contemporary dispute among foundationalists, coherentists, and infinitists. In Book I, Part 2 of the Posterior Analytics, Aristotle writes: "The premisses must be primary and indemonstrable; otherwise they will require demonstration in order to be known, since to have knowledge, if it be not accidental knowledge, of things which are demonstrable, means precisely to have a demonstration of them."

My current reading is that this remark, taken together with Aristotle's rejection of circular demonstration, settles the structure of justification in advance. If every premise capable of being supported by argument must actually be so supported before it can be known, then an infinite chain of reasons never arrives at anything known, and a network of mutually supporting beliefs is just circular demonstration drawn on a larger scale. On that basis, coherentism and infinitism seem not to be rival solutions to the regress so much as ways of declining to solve it: the infinitist offers a demonstration whose first premises are forever deferred, and the coherentist offers a circle whose circumference is simply wide enough to hide the return to the starting point.

Am I therefore right that, by Aristotle's criterion, foundationalism is the only position that even counts as a theory of epistemic justification, with the only remaining question being what makes the foundations epistemically privileged? If I am wrong, I would like to know precisely which inferential step fails: the move from a thesis about the premises of demonstration to a thesis about the justification of belief generally; the assumption that what is indemonstrable must be self-evident, certain, or unrevisable; or the identification of coherentist mutual support with the circular proof Aristotle excludes?
