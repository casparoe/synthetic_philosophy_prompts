I am a data scientist at a mid-sized company that ranks job applicants with a machine-learned model, and I have been asked to write the internal memo recommending which formal fairness criterion we should commit to. I have worked through the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — the theorems showing that, when groups differ in their base rates, a risk score cannot simultaneously be calibrated within each group and also equalize error rates (false positive and false negative rates) across groups, except in degenerate cases. Please describe the most important implications of these results for my situation.

Do not restate the theorems; I know their content. What I want is what follows from them. Specifically: which of the desiderata my stakeholders keep listing conflict mathematically and which merely conflict in practice as a contingent matter of our data; what design space genuinely remains open once the incompatibility is accepted — for instance, abandoning a single shared score, setting group-specific decision thresholds, investing in better features or a better outcome variable, or simply accepting one criterion and documenting the sacrifice — and whether each of these actually escapes the impossibility or merely relocates it; who bears the cost of each option, distinguishing costs to rejected applicants, to hired applicants, to the company, and to the groups as collectives; and what the results imply for how I should frame the memo. In particular, I keep hearing colleagues conclude from these theorems that fairness is a value judgment rather than a technical matter, and I want you to assess whether the results actually license that conclusion or whether it is an overreach that smuggles in extra premises — and if the latter, state the extra premises.

Please flag how confident you are in each major claim, and at the end single out the one implication you are least sure of and explain what would change your mind.

After the prose discussion, provide a structured summary as a table with exactly these columns: option (the fairness criterion or design choice under consideration); what_it_demands (the requirement it imposes on the model or process); what_it_sacrifices (which competing desideratum it gives up, and whether that sacrifice is forced by the theorems or merely likely in practice); who_bears_the_cost (the parties primarily harmed and how); defensibility_for_hiring (high, medium, or low, with a one-sentence justification specific to applicant screening); confidence (your confidence in that row's overall assessment, high, medium, or low).
