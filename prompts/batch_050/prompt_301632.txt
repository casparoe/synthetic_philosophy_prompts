So I finally got around to reading The Remains of the Day (I know, decades late, everyone I mention this to gives me the same look) and it's wrecked my ability to think about my own current novel, which is about a guy who keeps making small, defensible compromises and ends up somewhere he hates. Anyway, here's what I want.

Pretend you're a philosopher who genuinely knows the decision theory literature on Warren Quinn's self-torturer puzzle and intransitive preferences -- the money pump stuff, sorites-type arguments, all of it -- but who is also a halfway decent literary critic and not just someone who uses novels as clipart for papers.

Read Stevens the butler through that lens. My rough thought: each of his choices looks locally fine. Staying at your post during the conference instead of being with your dying father, going along with dismissing the two Jewish maids even though it clearly bothers him, deflecting Miss Kenton's attempts to get him to actually say something real, all the way down to the little stuff like perfecting the art of banter. No single one of those is the moment where his life goes wrong. There IS no moment. But the sum is a life he'd trade away, and on the pier at the end he more or less admits it.

What I want to know is: how well does the self-torturer structure actually fit Stevens, and where does it stop fitting? Like -- is he even in the right shape of situation? The self-torturer prefers each increment given the last, but does Stevens prefer each step, or does he merely not-disprefer it because he's outsourced the choosing to "dignity" and to Lord Darlington? Is what Ishiguro is doing closer to intransitive preferences cycling back on themselves, or closer to someone with perfectly transitive preferences ranked over the wrong things, or some third thing -- akrasia, weakness of will, identity drift? And does the novel suggest the problem is in his preferences at all, or in the fact that he never treats his own life as a thing he gets to evaluate as a whole, only as a series of shifts to get through?

Also genuinely interested in whether the lens fails anywhere. If the decision-theory reading flattens something the novel is doing -- like the unreliability of the narration, or the way class and service make "just choose otherwise" a kind of nonsense for someone like Stevens -- I want you to say so and push back rather than force the fit.

Process-wise: brainstorm a bit before you settle on a reading, consider at least a couple of rival framings, then give me your best take. And please be upfront about what's actually settled in this literature versus what's still live -- e.g. I'm pretty sure there's real disagreement about whether rational preferences have to be transitive and about whether the self-torturer is even acting irrationally, so don't hand me one side dressed up as the consensus. Same goes for the lit-crit side: flag where you're leaning on a contested reading of the novel.
