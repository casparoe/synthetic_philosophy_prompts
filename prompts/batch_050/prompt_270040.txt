I've been reading LessWrong and adjacent AI-safety writing for years, and one analogy keeps doing heavy lifting in discussions of reward tampering: a reinforcement-learning agent with the ability to modify its own reward signal is like the classic laboratory rat with an electrode wired into its medial forebrain bundle, pressing a lever for direct stimulation until it collapses. The analogy gets deployed to support a strong conclusion -- that any sufficiently capable agent will wirehead if given the opportunity, because reward is what the agent is *for*, and so reward tampering is less a failure mode than the agent simply succeeding at the only thing it was ever built to do. I've also seen the same analogy run in the opposite direction: humans with access to superstimulus (drugs, slot machines, short-form video) are the warning, and the lesson is that reward channels get hijacked by systems that treat the signal as the target rather than as evidence about what actually matters.

Here's my problem: I suspect the analogy is smuggling in the conclusion depending on which version of it you foreground, and I want an audit. Give me the explicit correspondence table -- what plays the role of the electrode, the lever, the rat's native desires for food and social contact, the experimenter who installed the apparatus, and the stimulation itself, mapped onto the reward channel, the agent's actuators, whatever in the policy corresponds to "interests other than reward," and the designers. Then identify which disanalogies are load-bearing for the strong "wireheading is near-inevitable" conclusion and which are merely cosmetic. In particular, I want the analysis to engage with the distinction the analogy seems to paper over: for the rat, the stimulation is *parasitic on* a value system that exists independently of it, which is why we call wireheading a pathology rather than self-actualization -- but if the agent's reward signal is constitutive of its objectives rather than evidence about them, then "tampering" starts to look like the rat *becoming what it always was*, and the pejorative framing loses its footing. Conversely, if the trained agent's learned values really are only correlated with reward (as the inner-alignment literature suggests), then the agent is more like the rat than the analogy's users usually admit, but in a way that undercuts the claim that wireheading counts as the agent succeeding.

So: does the strong conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the rat carrying it the whole time? If the electrode analogy fails, tell me what the right source domain actually is -- an addict who still endorses quitting, an accountant cooking the books they're supposed to be auditing, an immune system attacking the host -- and justify why your replacement better captures the structure of reward tampering. I'd rather have a deflationary answer that's right than a dramatic one that rests on a rodent.
