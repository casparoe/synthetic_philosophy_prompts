I'm a third-year PhD student and our department has a small summer research grant (about 10 weeks of funded work, max two-page proposal, due in a month). I want to pitch a project on the "transparency" debate in philosophy of photography -- the question of whether looking at a photograph of something lets you literally see that thing, in the same sense in which you see things through a telescope or a periscope, or whether photographs just give you very reliable information about their subjects the way a written report does. My half-formed idea: the whole debate seems to run on clean toy cases, analog snapshots on one side and paintings on the other, but the images that actually matter to people now -- bodycam footage shown to juries, live drone feeds, ultrasound images, the photos on my phone that have already been HDR-merged and denoised and who knows what else before I ever look at them -- all involve chains of automated processing between the scene and the picture I end up seeing. I was talking about this with my roommate who does computer vision and she kept saying "every image is a reconstruction now," which annoyed me but also seemed maybe right. My hunch is that transparency-talk handles simple cases like a live CCTV monitor fine, but gets genuinely murky with each processing step you add, and that there might be a principled line where "seeing through" breaks down -- maybe definable in terms of which counterfactuals about the scene the image still tracks (if the scene had been different in way X, would the image have been correspondingly different?), rather than in terms of how much machinery was involved, since a telescope is also machinery.

So: turn this into a two-page proposal. It needs (1) a precise statement of the question, narrow enough that ten weeks could actually settle something -- so probably not "are photographs transparent?" in general but something like whether a specific class of processing steps breaks transparency and why; (2) why the existing positions leave this open -- i.e., what defenders of transparency say about mechanical vs. intentional mediation, what skeptics say, and why neither side's stock arguments were built for heavily processed computational images; (3) the method, meaning what I'd actually do week by week (it's a philosophy project, not an empirical one, so be honest about what progress looks like -- constructing test cases, pressure-testing the counterfactual criterion against mirrors and live feeds and ultrasound, seeing where it gives counterintuitive verdicts); (4) what a result would look like, including the possibility that the answer is "transparency comes in degrees and 'do we see through pictures?' was a bad question," and what would count as the project failing; and (5) a realistic scope assessment -- what I'd have to bracket (e.g. the epistemology of testimony from images, aesthetic questions) to keep it doable.
