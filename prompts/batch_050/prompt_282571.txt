You are a philosopher who has spent your career teaching both sides of the modern quarrel over the Enlightenment — Frankfurt School critical theory on one side, Kantian and liberal defenses on the other — and you are known for diagnosing the structure of disputes rather than cheerleading for a camp. Think carefully before answering; you may find it useful to first reconstruct the strongest version of each side's argument for yourself before attempting any diagnosis.

Here is the situation I want diagnosed. In my reading group, the argument about the Enlightenment's legacy has run in place for a year. One side, working in the tradition of Horkheimer and Adorno's Dialectic of Enlightenment and Foucault, holds that the entanglement of Enlightenment reason with domination is not accidental: the drive to render nature and society calculable, to dissolve qualitative differences into what can be measured, predicted, and controlled, tends toward domination by its own logic, and the catastrophes of the twentieth century disclose that logic rather than betray it. The other side, in the spirit of Habermas and more recently Pinker's Enlightenment Now, holds that the Enlightenment is an unfinished, self-correcting project whose norms supply the only standpoint from which its own failures can be criticized — and that the empirical record on violence, health, and rights shows the project works. Everyone in the group knows both sets of arguments by heart. Nobody moves.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions — one about the deep structure of a form of rationality, the other about historical scorekeeping — so that their best arguments pass each other by: the empirical defense is irrelevant to the conceptual critique, and the conceptual critique is insulated against any possible data. Is that right? Or is there a genuine disagreement buried underneath — say, about whether emancipation and domination share a common root in the same conception of reason, or about what could even count as evidence for a thesis about a form of rationality rather than a set of institutions? Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, grounding the diagnosis in what each side's arguments genuinely require, not in caricatures. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction (for example, between the Enlightenment's self-image, its institutional carriers, and its philosophical commitments), a concession one side could make without surrendering its core claim, or a reasoned verdict that the question as posed is ill-posed and no resolution should be expected.

Lead with your bottom-line diagnosis in the first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, and do not restate the question.
