I spent thirty-five years in a chemistry department (retired now) and have been reading philosophy of biology for fun the last couple of years, and I keep tripping over something my old colleagues say without noticing they're saying it: that DNA is a code, a program, an instruction manual, that the genome "encodes" the organism. Textbooks say it, the people selling consumer ancestry tests say it, grant applications say it. And when I first met the genetic code back in the 70s it seemed perfectly innocent -- there's a lookup table from codons to amino acids, what else would you call it? But the philosphers I read (the developmental-systems folks mostly, plus some who defend the information talk) seem to think a lot hinges on whether this is a literal description or a metaphor that got out of hand, and honestly I've lost the plot on who's right.

So: audit the analogy for me. Treat "the genome is a code/program that specifies the organism" as an argument someone is using -- my old department used it to justify pouring money into sequencing, journalists use it to explain why a single gene "causes" aggression or whatever -- and tell me how far it actually holds. I want the correspondence table first: if DNA is like code, what plays the role of the compiler or interpreter (the ribosome? the whole cell?), what's the hardware, what are the inputs, who or what wrote the program and is "evolution as author" doing real work or just papering over the fact that code normally implies a coder? Where does the analogy quietly import things DNA doesn't have -- semantics, intentionality, a user manual that exists apart from the hardware, error-correction being a designed feature rather than an evolved one? And where is it actually literal? I've come to suspect it's literal for the codon-to-amino-acid mapping (that really is an arbitrary symbolic correspondence, the way philosophers use the term) but metaphorical for everything above that level -- regulation, development, the organism. Is that the right place to draw the line, or is even the codon table more like chemistry than like code?

Then the verdict: my old field's working assumption was that more sequence data = more understanding of organisms. Does that conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the code metaphor carrying it? If it fails, say what the better metaphor is, or whether we should just talk chemistry with no metaphor at all.

One more thing. Don't just give me your conclusion -- tell me what would change your mind. Which consideration, if I produced it, would flip your verdict, and where do you think your own reasoning is most likely to be going wrong? I spent decades talking this way so I'd rather you err on the side of doubting yourself.
