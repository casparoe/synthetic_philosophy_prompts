I am preparing a position statement for a graduate seminar on Buddhist ethics, and before I circulate it I want an audit of whether my commitments are jointly tenable. Here is the set:

(1) Buddhist ethics is, at bottom, a form of welfarist consequentialism: the ultimate justification for the precepts, the perfections, and the path as a whole is the reduction of dukkha for all sentient beings, and elements of the tradition that look deontological are best understood as skillful means (upāya) in service of that end.

(2) The karmic weight of an action is fixed by the agent's volition (cetanā), and this tracking of intention is intrinsic rather than instrumental. Two agents who bring about identical outcomes differ in karmic standing if their volitions differed; an attempted murder that fails is karmically graver than an accidental killing, and not merely because murderous intentions are predictive of future harm.

(3) The doctrine of non-self is true: persons are conventionally real but ultimately nothing over and above causally continuous psychophysical aggregates. Following roughly Parfit's line, I take this to diminish the rational significance of the boundaries between lives and thereby to support impartial concern for all beings.

(4) In the ship-captain story from the Skill in Means Sūtra, the captain who kills a passenger plotting to murder five hundred merchants acts rightly, yet nonetheless incurs real karmic demerit. I read the residual demerit as genuine moral remainder, not as a pedagogical fiction for those who cannot yet grasp the consequentialist point.

(5) Karmic results can ripen in later lives of the same causal continuum; that is, rebirth is real and is what gives karma its long-run structure.

Please proceed as follows. First, go through the commitments pairwise and classify each apparent conflict as either genuine or merely apparent; I suspect at least one of the tensions a careless reader would flag — I have my eye on the combination of (3) and (5) — dissolves once the distinction between conventional and ultimate truth is applied properly, and I would like that confirmed or refuted rather than assumed. Second, rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damaging, and for each one state precisely what would have to be given up on each side to resolve it. Third, identify the single revision that would restore the most coherence at the lowest cost to the overall position, and say what that revision costs in terms of fidelity to the tradition. Where a tension turns on a distinction I have failed to draw rather than on an inconsistency, say which distinction does the work.
