Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been working my way through political philosophy, and I keep running up against the concept of democratic backsliding. A friend of mine — a retired comparativist — uses the term freely, and I have been trying to reverse-engineer her classificatory practice the way I would any scientist's: from her verdicts on cases. Her rulings so far: Hungary since 2010, backsliding; Poland between 2015 and 2023, backsliding; Turkey under Erdoğan, backsliding; Weimar Germany, not backsliding — she calls that breakdown and insists the distinction matters; the United States in recent years, not backsliding, despite what she concedes is measurable deterioration on the standard democracy indices; Italy under Berlusconi, not backsliding, despite the media concentration and conflicts of interest.

Here is the new case I want to put to her. Imagine a long-stable European democracy that suffers a terrorist attack and then a pandemic within five years. Emergency powers granted in each crisis are never fully repealed and gradually become ordinary instruments of governance. Successive governing coalitions — no single dominant party or leader, no evidence of any coordinated plan — politicize the civil service and the public broadcaster through routine appointments. Elections remain free and are sometimes lost; the courts remain formally independent but are increasingly slow and deferential. Over fifteen years, the country's scores on the standard indices decline by roughly as much as Poland's did.

Please do the following. First, predict my friend's verdict on this case, and say how confident you are. Second, reconstruct at least two rules that are each consistent with all six of her prior verdicts but that deliver opposite verdicts on the new case — I suspect one will turn on deliberate incumbent capture of constraining institutions, and another on measurable decline in horizontal accountability regardless of its cause, but formulate them properly yourself. Third, identify which of her actual cases is the closest analog to my hypothetical, and defend the choice. Fourth, describe the further case — real or invented — that would best discriminate between your candidate rules, and explain why it is diagnostic. Finally, step back with me: my physicist's instinct is that a classification without a decision procedure is just an epithet, and that backsliding is whatever the classifier's political opponents are doing. Say what, given your reconstruction of her practice, my friend could honestly reply — who, on her view, gets to decide what counts, and whether the concept survives the charge of being a partisan label with a methodology section attached.
