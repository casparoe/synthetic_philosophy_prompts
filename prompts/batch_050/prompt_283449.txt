I'm a novelist -- literary fiction, the kind where not much happens but everybody feels terrible about it -- and I run a monthly craft workshop for about ten other writers, mostly people with MFAs or day jobs, zero philosophy background. Next session's theme is how characters make moral decisions on the page, and I want to build the whole thing around Bernard Williams's "one thought too many" objection, because I think it's the single most useful idea I've ever stolen from philosophy for writing morally loaded scenes. The problem: every time I've tried to explain it off the cuff, the room lands on "so thinking is bad" or "so just be biased toward your family," which is not it.

So don't explain the objection to me -- plan how I should explain it, in about fifty minutes. What has to be in place before the objection even makes sense to them (I'm guessing something about the gap between a theory justifying an action from the outside and a person actually deliberating from the inside, but you tell me what the real load-bearing pieces are and what order they come in). Pick the one example that should carry the session and tell me what it must have and must not have -- specifically, should I use Williams's own drowning-wife setup or build a fresher one, and why? Then give me the misconception map: the wrong versions these writers will form and exactly where in the sequence I should head each off, before it hardens. I want a check for understanding at the end that fits the room -- something like having them write a two-page scene where a character has one thought too many, and we can all spot where the thought is -- tell me if that's a good check or if there's a better one. And tell me what to openly refuse to cover at this level (the wider integrity objection, the attack on "the morality system," whatever) so I don't get pulled into it.

Two more things. Cap the plan at 500 words, in American English, casual is fine. And since this is basically a bet about how a particular audience misreads an idea: end with a short note on where your sequencing is most likely to fail with writers specifically -- like, which step you'd reorder first if the room pushes back, and what pushback would convince you the plan itself (not the students) got Williams wrong.
