Since retiring from my old lab (physical chemistry, thirty-odd years of it) I've been sitting in on a continuing-ed seminar on epistemology at the local university, mostly for something to argue about on tuesdays. This week we got to Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge, and the part that genuinely scandalized me is that he apparently just bites the bullet on denying closure: you can know p, know full well that p entails q, and still not know q. Trouble is, every example of this anyone in the room could produce was the usual brain-in-a-vat rigmarole, which I find insufferable -- nobody is ever going to be a brain in a vat, and if closure only fails in the philosophy-basement cases I'm inclined to shrug and keep it. The professor insisted the failure is a live possibility in the actual world wherever knowledge is method-dependent, but she couldn't produce a convincing specimen on the spot.

So build me one. Construct a thought experiment in which an ordinary person, in an ordinary setting where knowledge actually matters (a hospital, a courtroom, my old lab bench, take your pick), knows some p by Nozick's lights, knows that p entails q, and yet does not know q by Nozick's lights. Constraints: no skeptical hypotheses, no evil demons, no lotteries, and no fake barns either; the scenario itself should fit in one paragraph; and the failure must not come from the person being sloppy about the logic -- she is fully entitled to the entailment and even believes q on its basis. After the scenario, do the check properly: state p and q, state the method by which she forms each belief, lay out the relevant nearby worlds, and show why the sensitivity and adherence conditions come out as they do for each belief, so that the closure failure is generated by the theory rather than just declared.

Then two honest addenda. First, which feature of your case is doing the work -- if I remove it, does the example collapse back into a disguised brain-in-a-vat case, or is the closure failure robust to that? Second, Nozick sometimes wriggles out of closure objections by individuating belief-forming methods finely, so that knowing p and knowing q count as done by different methods; anticipate that move, show whether your case survives it, and say what the most stubborn defender of tracking would try next if it does.
