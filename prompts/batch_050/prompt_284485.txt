I teach a graduate seminar in practical rationality to a deliberately mixed cohort: roughly half philosophy students, half economics students who are fluent in game theory and dynamic choice but have had almost no exposure to the philosophy of action. One ninety-minute session is devoted to Kavka's toxin puzzle: an eccentric billionaire offers an enormous sum of money on the condition that, at midnight tonight, the subject genuinely intends to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin produces a day of intense misery but no lasting harm; the payout is determined entirely by the sincerity of the midnight intention; and whether the subject actually drinks the toxin is irrelevant to whether the money changes hands.

Design the session as a teaching plan rather than a lecture script. Specifically, I need the following:

1. Order of ideas. Specify the sequence in which the components must be introduced — the distinction between intending to act and merely predicting that one will act, between reasons to intend and reasons to act, the causal irrelevance of the drinking to the payout, and the apparent impossibility of holding the intention sincerely once one sees there is no reason to drink — and justify each dependency. In particular, explain what goes wrong pedagogically if the reasons-to-intend / reasons-to-act distinction is drawn before the students have felt the pull of the case in their own person, and why the comparison with Newcomb's problem should be deferred rather than offered up front.

2. The carrying example. Choose the single rendering of the case around which the session will be built, and state what it must contain and what it must conspicuously lack. Must the intention be verifiable by some mechanism, or is the subject's own honesty about the offer enough? How large should the prize be, and how severe the illness? I assume the example must exclude any opportunity to lie about the intention, any lasting harm, and any effects on third parties — confirm or correct me. Then indicate which parameters I should vary later as probing exercises: a version in which the money is deposited in advance and clawed back if the midnight intention fails to materialize, a version in which drinking the toxin would also fund a worthy cause so that genuine reasons to act re-enter the picture, and a version in which the subject can hire a hypnotist to manufacture the intention — and say which of these variants sharpens the puzzle and which merely dissolves it, and why the difference matters.

3. Misconception map. Predict the misreadings each half of my cohort will bring or form, and identify the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I anticipate at least the following: the economists' reflex that this is simply a time-inconsistency problem soluble by a commitment device in the style of Ulysses and the sirens; the assimilation of the case to Newcomb's problem or to evidential decision theory; the thought that the puzzle merely shows that intentions are forecasts of one's own behavior; the objection that one can form a sham or self-deceptive intention and collect the money; and the moralizing misreading that the case is really about whether one may rationally accept suffering for payment. For each, say whether it is a distortion of the puzzle or a legitimate route into it, and how to tell the difference in the room.

4. Check for understanding. Propose one exercise whose successful completion would demonstrate that the reasons-to-intend / reasons-to-act distinction has actually landed. It should require students to construct a case rather than classify one — for instance, asking them to build a scenario in which a person has a decisive reason to intend something and no reason whatsoever to do it, without recycling the toxin — and it should be structured so that the two halves of the cohort are likely to fail in instructively different ways.

5. Deliberate omissions. State what I should openly set aside at this level — the literature on rational irrationality, resolute choice, the regress about intending to intend, the question whether the billionaire's offer is even intelligible — and how to mark those omissions so they read as scope decisions rather than evasions.
