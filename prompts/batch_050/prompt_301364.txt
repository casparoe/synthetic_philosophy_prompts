An article I read on the politics of atrocity terminology summarised Raphael Lemkin's concept of genocide, as set out in his 1944 book Axis Rule in Occupied Europe, in roughly these terms. Lemkin, it says, coined the word genocide not to denote mass killing as such but to name a broader process unfolding in two phases — the destruction of the oppressed group's own national pattern, followed by the imposition of the oppressor's pattern upon the surviving group or upon its territory. From this the article infers that, on Lemkin's view, genocide need not involve killing at all, so that measures such as banning a minority's language or closing its schools amount to genocide in the strict sense whenever they erode the group's culture. It goes on to say that the 1948 Genocide Convention codified Lemkin's concept into international law, which is why the treaty's definition turns on the intent to destroy a group as such, and it concludes that because the Convention obliges states to prevent and punish genocide, a government that officially names an ongoing atrocity genocide thereby assumes a legal duty to intervene — and that this, rather than any dispute about facts, is the real reason governments are so reluctant to utter the word.

I am inclined to trust the piece, since it appears well informed, but the move from Lemkin's two phases to the modern law feels suspiciously tidy, and I cannot tell where. Which parts of this summary would a careful reader of Lemkin and of the Convention accept; which parts misrepresent or overstate either his view or the treaty's content; and does the concluding explanation of governmental reluctance to say genocide actually follow from anything accurate in what precedes it?
