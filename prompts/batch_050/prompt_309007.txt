In our phil of science reading group there's a guy who, whenever anyone brings up the pessimistic induction -- you know, phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether, all these once-successful theories we now think are false -- fires back with some version of "it would be a miracle if theories this successful weren't true." Last week he used it to say the whole history-of-discarded-theories stuff is irrelevant, because no matter what happened in the past, OUR theories predict too well to be totally wrong. And honestly I feel like he's equivocating but I can't pin down where.

So what I want is: what are the distinct precise claims that "miracle" line could be making? Because it seems like it could be anything from a trivial point (a totally false theory probably wouldn't get novel predictions right, sure) to a huge metaphysical claim (our best current theories are approximately true in their ontology, full stop), and the trivial one definitely doesn't do the work he needs it to do. Lay the readings out as a menu I can bring to the next meeting.

For each reading please get specific about:

- What "success" has to mean for the argument to even start -- novel predictions? empirical adequacy? technological spinoffs like building transistors? Because a theory can nail all the observable stuff and still be wrong about what's underneath, and that gap seems like exactly where the pessimistic induction bites.
- What "miracle" is doing -- is this a probability argument (the success would be too improbable under falsity), an inference-to-the-best-explanation move, or just rhetorical? If it's probabilistic, does it smuggle in priors about how likely true-ish theories were in the first place?
- What the conclusion is actually about -- the theory as a whole, its mathematical structure, its "working parts" vs idle wheels, or just that something in the vicinity is right? I've heard people retreat to structural realism or to realism only about the parts of the theory that were "essential" to the predictions, and I want to know if those retreats still get him his conclusion that the pessimistic induction is irrelevant, or whether they concede the thing the induction was saying all along.

The key thing: for each reading, say whether it's actually defensible AND whether it supports the specific use he put it to -- namely dismissing the historical track record. A reading that's plausible but only supports "our theories are empirically adequate" doesn't beat the induction, right, since the dead theories were empirically adequate too?

Also, one thing I keep tripping over: the meta-induction is about theories that were successful AND turned out false, so does the miracle argument even apply to them retroactively? Like, would he have made the same speech about caloric theory in 1820? If yes, the slogan seems self-undermining; if no, he owes us some criterion that distinguishes our success from theirs, and I'd like the menu to include whatever readings try to supply that criterion (maturity? novel prediction specifically? degree of success?) with verdicts on whether they hold up.
