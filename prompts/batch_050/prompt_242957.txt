I am a novelist at work on a book whose pivotal figure is a retired moral theologian, Father Anselm, who in his later years kept a kind of informal confessional correspondence: acquaintances and strangers wrote to him describing acts of betrayal they had committed or contemplated, and he pronounced, in each case, whether the act was forgivable or not. I have established his verdicts in five earlier chapters, and I now need him to rule on a sixth case in a way that is true to the character I have built. I would like your help determining what his verdict must be.

His rulings so far:

1. An intelligence analyst who had sworn an oath of secrecy leaked classified documents exposing her own agency's program of warrantless surveillance of citizens. Anselm judged the betrayal of her oath forgivable.

2. A soldier abandoned his post without leave in order to warn villagers that his own unit planned an attack on them. Anselm judged this desertion forgivable.

3. An executive sold his firm's trade secrets to a competitor purely for personal enrichment. Anselm judged this unforgivable.

4. A woman revealed her closest friend's long-concealed, decades-old affair to a mutual acquaintance, out of nothing more than spite after a quarrel. Anselm judged this unforgivable.

5. A seminarian broke the seal of what a dying man had told him in confidence in order to alert the police to an unsolved murder the man had confessed to committing. Anselm judged this forgivable, though he called it the hardest case he had taken.

The new case, which I intend as the climax: a translator employed by a humanitarian organization discovers that the organization's director has been embezzling relief funds. Before she acts, the embezzlement is uncovered by an internal audit, the money is quietly repaid from the director's own pocket, and the matter is closed. The translator nevertheless leaks the documents to the press -- by her own admission, not to stop any ongoing harm, but because the director had years earlier sabotaged her career, and she has waited for her chance. The leak destroys the director and badly damages the organization's ability to operate.

Here is what I need. First, Anselm's verdict on this sixth case, rendered as he would render it. Second, an articulation of the rule or rules under which all five of his earlier verdicts fall together -- I am aware there is more than one such rule, and I want at least two candidate principles laid out, with an account of how each one decides the new case and why. Third, your judgment as to which principle best captures the man as I have written him, and therefore which verdict keeps him consistent with himself. Fourth, the closest analog to the new case among the five earlier ones, with reasons. And finally, if his record genuinely underdetermines the verdict, tell me what seventh case -- one I could insert in an earlier chapter -- would settle which principle he is actually running.
