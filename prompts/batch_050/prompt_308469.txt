I'm writing the final exam for my upper-level philosophy of perception seminar and want one essay question on the argument from illusion and disjunctivism. It should be answerable in about 75 minutes and worth 25 marks.

The problem: students can all recite the setup -- veridical experience and hallucination can be subjectively indistinguishable, so (the argument says) they must share a common mental factor, so what we're directly aware of even in the good case can't be the external object. I want a question that forces them past the recitation. Specifically, it should make them identify where the argument actually needs a premise beyond mere indistinguishability (i.e., why does "you can't tell them apart from the inside" have to mean the two experiences are mentally the same, rather than just that one of our epistemic capacities is limited?), and it should make them assess whether the disjunctivist is denying a psychological claim, an epistemic claim, or a metaphysical one -- and wich of those denials is actually defensible.

So please write: (1) the exam question itself, phrased the way I'd put it on the paper; (2) a model answer of around 600 words; (3) a marking scheme with five must-hit points, the marks for each (totaling 25), and for each point a one-line note on how weaker students typically flub it. The scheme should make clear what separates an answer that gets the crux from one that just correctly summarizes both positions -- I'm happy for a well-argued defense of the common-factor view to get full marks, as long as it engages the crux rather than dodging it.
