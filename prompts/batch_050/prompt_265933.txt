So I'm in this upper-year course on ethics and narrative (basically: what do stories owe to reality?) and we have a term paper coming up where we pick a case and run it through a theory. I want to do Amadeus -- the 1984 Forman one, from Shaffer's play -- and I want you to basically pressure-test my paper idea before I commit to it.

Quick recap so we're on the same page, in case your memory of the film is fuzzy: it's framed as the elderly Salieri, in an asylum, confessing to a priest that he killed Mozart out of jealousy -- not literally with poison in the film so much as by scheming, draining him, and driving him into the grave while posing as his friend. The real Salieri was a successful, respected court composer, taught Beethoven and Schubert among others, and while the poisoning rumor did float around in his lifetime, basically no historian buys it and the film's whole murder-confession arc is invented. Meanwhile Mozart comes off as a giggling obscene manchild whose genius is almost an insult to everyone trying hard. One weird twist: the film singlehandedly dragged Salieri out of obscurity -- his music gets performed and recorded now largely *because* this movie made him infamous.

Here's the lens I want to use. My prof floated this idea (or I half-invented it from something she said, honestly not sure) that dramatists owe the real dead an *accuracy debt proportional to how much of the person's legacy they borrow*: the more your story coasts on the audience recognizing the real name, the real fame, the real works, the less license you have to make stuff up about them. So a fully fictional composer -- say anything. A thinly disguised one -- almost anything. But borrow Mozart's actual name, actual symphonies, actual death, and the meter starts running.

Analyze the film through this. Specifically:

1. Do the mapping carefully. Which portrayals in the film actually incur the debt on this view -- the murder confession, obviously, but also Mozart's vulgarity, Salieri's piety-and-mediocrity arc, his suicide attempt and insanity? Is there a difference, debt-wise, between inventing a crime and inventing a personality? Does it matter that the whole thing is framed as the ravings of an old madman, i.e. the film technically never asserts the murder happened?

2. Run the obvious objection: the dead can't be harmed, there's no subject left to wrong. Does the accuracy-debt principle presuppose posthumous harm is possible, or can it be rebuilt on something else (interests surviving death, wrongs to the living who care, something about the practice of truthtelling itself)? Pick whichever version you think is strongest and use it, but say what you picked.

3. The Salieri-revival twist. If the film defamed him but also resurrected him -- he's the rare case where a hit piece is the only reason anyone remembers the guy -- does the principle judge by outcomes (net effect on the legacy) or by the disrespect shown regardless of outcome? Which reading is better, and does Amadeus come out excused on the outcome reading?

4. Then turn it around: attack the principle itself with the case. Does proportionality-to-borrowed-legacy perversely imply you get *more* license to lie about obscure dead people than famous ones, and is that absurd or actually right? Does the principle even explain why the murder-invention feels like the core wrong, or was it never about accuracy at all?

End with a verdict: does Amadeus pay its debt, default on it, or does the case show the whole framing was confused? Don't give me a film review and don't recap the plot beyond what the analysis needs -- I know the plot, I want the ethics.
