I keep running into this defence of string theory and I can't work out if it's clever or dodgy. The idea is that string theory today is like atomic theory in the 1800s: Dalton and his lot couldn't see atoms either, plenty of respectable scientists grumbled that unobservable tiny things were metaphysics not science, and then a century later we were basically photographing them. The conclusion people draw is always the same -- stop demanding empirical tests right now, give it time, maybe our instruments just haven't caught up yet. It sounds persuasive but also suspiciously convenient. Can you audit the analogy for me? Structure it like this: The mapping (roughly a paragraph or a small table -- what plays the role of the atom, the chemist, the sceptics, the microscope); Where it holds (one or two paragraphs on what the comparison genuinely gets right about how science deals with unobservables); Where it breaks (the meat of it, two or three paragraphs -- did atomic theory make testable predictions back then even without seeing atoms, does string theory do anything comparable, does the sheer time scale and the energy scales involved matter, and does it matter that atoms had rival theories to compete against); and The verdict (one paragraph -- whether the analogy can still carry the "be patient" conclusion once the disanalogies are on the table, and if not, whether there's a better historical comparison it should be swapped for).
