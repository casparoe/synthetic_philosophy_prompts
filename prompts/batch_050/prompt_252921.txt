I am a high school senior, and two of my classmates have spent the entire semester of our civics course arguing about the ethics of voting. One of them holds that voting is a civic duty, and that in any election where the outcome matters, a voter is obligated to support the lesser evil even when that candidate is genuinely bad, because abstaining or casting a protest vote effectively helps the worse candidate win. The other holds that voting is fundamentally expressive, that a vote is a personal endorsement no one can be morally required to give, and that there is no duty to lend one's endorsement to a candidate one considers unworthy — at most there is a duty to stay informed and to participate in political life in other ways. Our teacher has asked them to stop re-running the same debate and instead produce a single joint statement, the kind an adversarial collaboration between opposing researchers would publish, and I have been asked to help them draft it. Please write that document for us.

The statement should contain the following, in this order. First, the claims both sides can honestly sign: for example, that a single vote almost never decides a large election, that voting has both a consequences-oriented and an expressive dimension, and that neither of them believes anyone should be legally compelled to vote. Second, the exact points of disagreement, each formulated neutrally enough that the person who holds the view would accept the wording as a fair statement of their own position, without smuggling in either side's preferred vocabulary. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling even one of them would move at least one of them. I suspect these include whether refusing to vote for the lesser evil makes you partly responsible if the greater evil wins, and whether the expressive meaning of a vote is something the voter controls or something fixed by how others will read it — but please identify the cruxes yourself rather than taking my guesses on faith, and feel free to separate disagreements that turn out to be merely verbal from ones that are genuine. Fourth, for each crux, a concrete case or observation both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction about that case recorded in advance. As one such case, consider whether their arguments survive contact with a small election, like our student council vote last spring that was decided by three votes, since the duty-based argument seems to lean heavily on the fact that one vote almost never matters in a national election.

One request about presentation: although the document has four parts, please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would write a formal letter, with no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold text, and no tables, so that my two classmates can read it aloud to the class as a single flowing statement.
