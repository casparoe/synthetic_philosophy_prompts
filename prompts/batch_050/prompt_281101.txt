Two friends of mine run a philosophy podcast and they've been bickering about the doomsday argument for like six episodes now, and I want them to just sit down and produce one document they can both sign. One of them is a convinced doomsdayer (Carter/Leslie/Gott style: we're probably not unusually early in the human timeline, so total future population is probably bounded). The other thinks the argument is a neat parlor trick that dissolves once you're careful about self-locating assumptions and reference classes.

Draft the joint statement they'd come out with after a real good-faith adversarial collaboration. Specifically:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. These should cover at least: what the argument formally establishes *if* its assumptions are granted (e.g., that it's a claim about our position in a sequence, not a causal prediction), that plain base-rate estimates of extinction risk aren't touched by it either way, and that some version of the urn/ball analogy is fair to both sides.

2. A disagreements section where each residual disagreement is stated precisely, phrased so each side would accept the wording of its own position. I expect these to include at least: (a) the choice of reference class -- does it matter whether we count future digital minds or uploaded persons as "humans" for the birth-order counting, and does the argument change if you do? (b) self-sampling vs self-indication and whether the "presumptuous philosopher" style objection (SIA making you wildly confident in huge universes) is a genuine reductio or a bullet the doomsday critic just has to bite; (c) whether Gott's delta-t version and Leslie's Bayesian version stand or fall together.

3. For each disagreement, a crux: the sub-question such that settling it would actually move at least one of them, plus a case or observation both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Include this hypothetical I came up with as one of the tests: suppose SETI succeeds and a galactic census shows that intelligent species almost always go extinct within 10,000 years of inventing radio. Does the doomsday argument become redundant, stronger, or irrelevant? Both should record what they'd predict the other side will say and why.

End the statement with a short section on whether any part of their dispute turned out to be verbal -- I have a hunch some of the "typicality" talk is, but make them prove it or retract it.
