In the philosophy of language seminar I'm TAing, we just finished the unit on Frege's puzzle about identity statements. A recurring student objection — which also gets thrown around at our department's informal reading group — is the one-liner that a true identity statement "only says of something that it is identical with itself." The person deploying it last week used it to cut off discussion of the Hesperus/Phosphorus case: if Hesperus is Phosphorus, then there is just one planet involved, so any statement of their identity merely records that the planet is itself, and the astronomical discovery could not have been a discovery of anything linguistic or cognitive — end of story. Some students found this devastating; others felt something had gone wrong but couldn't say what.

I'd like a resource I can hand out before our next meeting. Take the slogan as the object of analysis and disambiguate it into its distinct possible precisifications: the readings on which it is a platitude (e.g., about the object-level fact that a thing is itself), the readings on which it is a substantive thesis about the semantics or epistemology of identity claims, and any readings on which it simply equivocates (for instance, sliding between what a statement is about, what it says, and what someone can learn from it). For each reading, give a verdict — trivial, defensible but substantive, or confused — and say briefly why. Then state which of these readings the dismissive use above actually requires in order to undercut Frege's puzzle, and whether the slogan, on that reading, remains plausible once the required distinctions are enforced. Present the readings as a numbered list with verdicts, followed by a short closing assessment, in no more than 650 words total.
