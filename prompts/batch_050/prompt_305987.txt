I read an article in a general-audience philosophy magazine about the debate over biological functions, and I'd like to know how much of it to trust. Here's the relevant passage:

'Philosophers have two main theories of what "function" means in biology. On the causal-role theory, a part's function is just whatever it actually does that contributes to the organism — the heart's function is pumping blood because that's the effect it produces. The problem is that this makes function-talk subjective, since which effects count as contributions depends on what we happen to be interested in, and it also can't make sense of malfunction: if a diseased heart stops pumping, then pumping simply isn't its function anymore, so nothing counts as going wrong. The rival etiological theory says a trait's function is whatever past natural selection explains its presence — hearts are here because they pumped, so pumping is their function. But this theory has problems of its own: it means you can't say what a trait's function is without first knowing its evolutionary history, which reduces most of physiology to guesswork, and it implies that a molecule-for-molecule copy of a heart that came into existence by sheer accident would have no function at all, which is absurd on its face. Since neither theory survives scrutiny, the honest conclusion is that function-talk is a leftover of thinking of nature as designed, and biologists should drop the word from serious work.'

Something about the last move bothers me — I used the word "function" every day for decades and it never felt like theology — but I can't tell whether my discomfort is with the article's reporting of the two theories or with the theories themselves. Then tell me whether the final verdict — that biologists should abandon function-talk — actually follows from the accurate parts of the summary, or whether the article is leaning on its errors to get there.
