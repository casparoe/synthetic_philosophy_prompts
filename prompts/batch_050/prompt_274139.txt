I'm a software engineer, and a question from my day job has started following me into my reading about biology. In distributed systems work, we constantly have to decide whether a collection of interacting components counts as one system or as several systems that happen to cooperate. The answer matters practically -- for billing, for security boundaries, for blame when something fails -- and I've learned that the obvious criteria (shared hardware, shared codebase, a single deployable unit) all fail in instructive ways.

I recently fell down a rabbit hole about the analogous question in biology, and I want to approach it systematically before reading any particular philosopher's answer. So: write the desiderata for a theory of biological individuality -- a specification of what any adequate account of what makes a living thing *one* individual, rather than several individuals or parts of an individual, must be able to do. For each requirement, give the case or phenomenon that motivates it.

Please make sure the requirements list addresses at least the following kinds of cases, and add any I've missed that are comparably diagnostic: genetically uniform but physically separate stands of clonal plants; physically unified "bodies" that are composed of genetically distinct cooperating entities (colonial marine animals, symbiotic partnerships like lichen); the microbes living in and on a human body and when they count as part of the human; tight collectives like ant colonies where the colony seems to have organism-like properties; and cancers or transplanted organs, where something's allegiance to a host individual seems to shift. For each, state what a theory has to say about it and why getting it wrong would be a defect rather than a mere surprise.

Also distinguish, among the desiderata, between (a) things any theory must deliver on pain of contradicting established biology, (b) distinctions the theory must preserve even if it redraws them (e.g., being one thing metabolically versus being one thing for purposes of evolution and natural selection), and (c) demands that are really just one camp's prejudices -- my guess is that genetic uniformity and spatial continuity are candidates, but tell me if I'm wrong.

If some subset of the plausible desiderata turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, flag that explicitly; in my experience the impossibility results are the most valuable output of an exercise like this. Finally, identify the single requirement that eliminates the most naive criteria of individuality at a stroke. A closing paragraph on how the main families of criteria (physiological, evolutionary, immunological) fare against the list is welcome but optional.
