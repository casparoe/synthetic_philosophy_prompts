I am a novelist at work on a historical novel about a fictional condottiere in the Italy of Alexander VI -- a man of real ability who rises on borrowed power and must decide, at the turning point of the book, whether he is the author of his ascent or merely its lucky beneficiary. In trying to get his inner life right, I have been brooding over two passages of Machiavelli's The Prince (in W. K. Marriott's translation) that seem to me to offer two quite different pictures of what fortune is, and I would like your help thinking through whether they can be reconciled. The first, from Chapter VI, concerns Moses, Cyrus, Romulus, and Theseus: "And in examining their actions and lives one cannot see that they owed anything to fortune beyond opportunity, which brought them the material to mould into the form which seemed best to them. Without that opportunity their powers of mind would have been extinguished, and without those powers the opportunity would have come in vain." The second, from Chapter XXV, is the river image: "I compare her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings, bearing away the soil from place to place; everything flies before it, all yield to its violence, without being able in any way to withstand it; and yet, though its nature be such, it does not follow therefore that men, when the weather becomes fair, shall not make provision, both with defences and barriers, in such a manner that, rising again, the waters may pass away by canal, and their force be neither so unrestrained nor so dangerous."

Please compare the two conceptions of fortuna these passages encode. In the first, fortune appears as a benefactor of sorts: she supplies raw material and occasion, and virtù (which Marriott renders as "ability") consists in recognizing the moment and imposing form upon it -- fortune here seems almost passive, waiting to be used. In the second, fortune is an active, destructive force, and virtù looks more like hydraulic engineering: patient, collective, prepared in advance, aimed at containing a power that no one shapes. Are these two pictures merely different emphases of a single theory, or do they rest on genuinely incompatible assumptions about what fortune is and what virtù can do to it? Notice that the second passage has fortune turning her forces "where she knows that barriers and defences have not been raised," which seems to give her intentions and even a kind of cunning, while the first passage's opportunity seems blind. Which picture better fits the rest of what Machiavelli says -- for instance, his claim in the same chapter that the prince who relies entirely on fortune is lost when it changes, and his suggestion that success comes from conforming one's conduct to the spirit of the times?

Finally, a question for the sake of my craft: if my condottiere held the Chapter VI view -- fortune as occasion, himself as sculptor -- how would he narrate his own rise and justify his cruelties, and how would his self-understanding differ if he instead held the Chapter XXV view, seeing himself as a dike-builder in fair weather dreading the flood? Which of the two portraits, in your judgment, produces the more tragic character, and which produces the more Machiavellian one? I would rather you reason from the passages themselves than survey the secondary literature; concrete attention to the metaphors -- sculptor's material versus floodwaters, moulding versus making provision -- is what I am after.
