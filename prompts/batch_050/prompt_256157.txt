At a philosophy-of-physics reading group I attend, two regulars fell into an argument after a session on deterministic chaos, and it was still unresolved when we left. I stayed out of it but have kept turning it over, so I would like a careful adjudication.

Daniel's opening position: a deterministic universe is, in principle, perfectly predictable, and chaos changes nothing about that. Chaos only shows that prediction is practically hard, because small measurement errors amplify exponentially. The distinction between "predictable" and "predictable by us, cheaply, and soon" is doing all the work, and the first is what matters.

Marta replied that "in principle" is itself doing all the work. Predictability in principle, she said, should mean that some physically possible predictor could produce the predictions. But exact knowledge of initial conditions would require infinitely precise measurement, which no physical process delivers; and a predictor located inside the universe that tries to compute the universe's future in full detail runs into self-inclusion problems, since it must model itself modeling. So chaotic determinism is not predictable even in principle — it is "predictable" only for a stipulated fiction.

Daniel answered that Laplace's demon was never offered as a buildable machine. It is a way of stating what determinism is: the laws plus the complete state at one time fix everything that follows. If you grant determinism, the demon's success follows by definition, and objecting that no such demon could exist is like objecting to a proof in Euclidean geometry on the grounds that nobody can draw a perfect circle.

Marta made two points in response. First, if the demon is a fiction, then "predictable in principle" names a property of the fiction rather than of the universe; determinism may still be true, but we should stop saying the future is predictable and say only that it is fixed. Second, she pressed something stronger: since no finite-precision observation can distinguish a deterministic chaotic trajectory from a genuinely stochastic one, the claim that nature is deterministic rather than random may be empirically empty in chaotic regimes — a commitment we bring to the data rather than one we could ever read off it.

Daniel called that verificationism and said the philosophical community settled its fate decades ago; the underdetermination of theory by data is ubiquitous and does not make theories meaningless. Marta said she was not claiming meaninglessness, only that confidence in determinism cannot be justified by observation in chaotic regimes, and that determinism's best defense is the indirect one that our most successful theories happen to be deterministic — a much weaker claim than the one Daniel started with.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the positions and replies above, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible only in a qualified form, and justify each rating briefly. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything both parties are missing — for instance, whether the argument trades on equivocation among several distinct notions of predictability, and whether anything turns on the difference between predicting a system from outside and predicting the whole universe from within.
