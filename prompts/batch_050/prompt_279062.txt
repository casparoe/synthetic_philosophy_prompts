I am a senior in high school and serve as the elected treasurer of our student-run school store, which sells snacks, drinks, and school merchandise during lunch periods. We handle a few hundred transactions a week, keep our own cash box and inventory records, and distribute the profits to student clubs at the end of each semester. Our faculty advisor has asked me to draft a standing decision procedure that the store's student volunteers can use whenever a question arises about who is entitled to what: cash in the register, donated inventory, items traded between clubs, and money owed to or by the store. These questions come up two or three times a month. The amounts are small but the feelings are not, and the volunteers who would run the procedure have no background in philosophy and about ten free minutes during a lunch period.

I would like to ground the procedure in the entitlement theory of justice in holdings from Robert Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974), Chapter 7. This is the passage I want to build from:

> 1. A person who acquires a holding in accordance with the principle of justice in acquisition is entitled to that holding.
> 2. A person who acquires a holding in accordance with the principle of justice in transfer, from someone else entitled to the holding, is entitled to the holding.
> 3. No one is entitled to a holding except by (repeated) applications of 1 and 2.
>
> A distribution is just if it arises from another just distribution by legitimate means. . . . Whatever arises from a just situation by just steps is itself just. Justice in holdings is historical; it depends upon what actually has happened.

Please do the following.

First, translate the passage into an ordered decision procedure a volunteer can actually run: the questions to ask in sequence (corresponding to just acquisition, just transfer, and rectification of past injustice), what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where the procedure stops with a definite "no, the store cannot take or keep this," and where it instead hands the decision to the faculty advisor because the question is one of values rather than facts. Pay particular attention to the recursive structure of the passage: if justice depends on every step back to a just beginning, say how far back a volunteer with ten minutes and incomplete records is supposed to trace, and what the procedure should conclude when the history runs out.

Second, be explicit about what survives the translation and what does not: which distinctions in Nozick's theory must be preserved for the procedure to remain his (for example, the difference between the current pattern of holdings and the process that produced it), and which can safely be dropped at the scale of a school store.

Third, run the finished procedure on three cases: (a) a parent donates a box of new water bottles, and another student claims the parent's employer had discarded them after a cancelled event and that the parent merely took them home first; (b) the chess club offers to trade its leftover concession candy for our spare display case, where the candy was bought with club funds partly raised at a tournament staffed by volunteers who were not club members; and (c) while reconciling old records, I discover that for the past three years the store has kept the proceeds of the courtyard vending machine even though a student-council minute from several years ago records that those proceeds were promised to the class gift fund -- nobody currently involved did anything wrong, and we have been counting on that money to buy a new refrigerator, so this is the case where I expect the procedure to give me an answer I will not like. Show the procedure's verdict on each case, including the reasoning step by step.

Finally, mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and where the procedure will predictably misfire, and say what it cannot do at all. Constraints: one full run of the procedure should take a volunteer no more than ten minutes; we cannot compel anyone to produce documents; any rectification decision requires the faculty advisor's sign-off. Please aim for roughly 900 to 1,200 words, not counting the quoted passage above.
