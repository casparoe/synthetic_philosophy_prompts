I direct a graduate seminar in the philosophy of history, and this term we have spent several weeks on presentism: Benedetto Croce's thesis that all historical inquiry is animated by the questions of the present, Herbert Butterfield's 1931 attack on Whig history and its habit of arraigning the dead before the moral standards of the living, and the contextualist attempt to steer between the two. For the final session I want a case study, and rather than the overworked statue controversies I have settled on the Smithsonian's Enola Gay affair. The chronology, as I understand it: for the fiftieth anniversary of the bombing of Hiroshima, the National Air and Space Museum planned an exhibit, provisionally titled The Crossroads, that would display the bomber's forward fuselage alongside photographs and artifacts from the destruction and Japanese testimony about the attack; draft scripts raised historians' questions about whether the bombing was necessary to end the war and what alternatives were available. One early draft characterized the Pacific war in these terms: "For most Americans this war was fundamentally different than the one waged against Germany and Italy—it was a war of vengeance. For most Japanese, it was a war to defend their unique culture against Western imperialism." (The surrounding passages, for fairness, acknowledged Pearl Harbor and Japanese atrocities.) The Air Force Association, veterans' organizations, and members of Congress attacked the project as revisionist history; the ambitious exhibit was abandoned in favor of a drastically reduced display of the fuselage with minimal interpretive text; the museum's director, Martin Harwit, resigned in May 1995; and since 2003 the fully restored aircraft has sat in the museum's annex essentially uninterpreted.

Analyze this episode through the presentism framework, and do so in both directions. On the mapping: were the curators guilty of moral presentism in Butterfield's sense, or were they merely practicing the epistemic presentism that Croce says no historian can escape, putting 1995's questions to 1945's evidence? And were the critics anti-presentists defending the past against anachronistic judgment, or were they themselves presentists of a commemorative kind, demanding that 1945 be presented according to 1995's civic needs? Consider also the question of standing: the veterans claimed authority as participants, the historians as professionals, and the dead of Hiroshima appeared only through artifacts. Then the harder question: Butterfield's prohibition was formulated for scholarship, and a national museum is arguably not a seminar but a commemorative institution, so say whether the anti-presentist norm even applies to it, and if not, what norm does. Finally, what is the philosophical status of the outcome? A fuselage with almost no text looks like the anti-presentist ideal of letting the artifact speak, but the silence of 2003 is itself a curatorial decision made in 2003, no less of the present than the script it replaced.

Then run the analysis the other way: does the episode confirm the framework (both sides judged the past by present concerns, so presentism is simply inescapable and the only honest question is which present concerns to serve), or does it show that the framework misdescribes what was actually a dispute about national memory, professional authority, and audience rather than about temporal standards at all? Give me a verdict, but tell me also what would change it: which consideration, if given more weight, or which fact of the episode, if I have misstated it, would push you to a different conclusion, and at what point in your mapping you are most likely to be forcing the theory to speak where it is silent.
