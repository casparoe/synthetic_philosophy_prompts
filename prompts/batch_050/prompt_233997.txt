In a seminar on compulsory voting, a classmate ended our discussion by declaring that voting is a duty, not a right, and that this settles the case for legally requiring turnout, the way Australia does. Our readings included John Stuart Mill's Considerations on Representative Government (1861), where, in the chapter on the mode of voting, Mill argues that the suffrage is a trust rather than a personal possession: the voter has no more business consulting his private preferences than a juror does, and is bound to vote according to his conscientious judgment of the public good. What struck me is that Mill's argument seems to be about how one must vote, yet my classmate wielded the slogan to establish that one must vote at all. Please disambiguate the slogan into the distinct precise theses it could express -- for example, a moral duty to turn out, a duty about the quality or aim of one's vote, a claim about what the franchise is for, and a claim about what the state may legitimately enforce -- and for each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or implausible; which reading Mill's trust argument actually supports; and which reading, if any, would genuinely license compulsory turnout, given that the standard arguments for compulsion (ameliorating unequal turnout, taming polarization, civic education) seem to need a duty to show up rather than a duty to vote well. Lay the readings out as a menu with verdicts rather than defending a single one.
