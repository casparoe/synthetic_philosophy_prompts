I don't have any philosophy background but I've been chewing on something since I read about pain asymbolia in a magazine article and then fell down a rabbit hole. Apparently some people, after certain brain injuries or after this surgery called a cingulotomy, still feel pain — they can tell you where it is, how intense, whether it's burning or stabbing — but they just don't mind it. They don't pull their hand away, they don't ask for it to stop, they'll sit there with a painfully hot plate and shrug. Something similar reportedly happens with morphine. This totally broke my assumption that pain is bad because of how it feels, because here the feeling seems to still be there and the badness is what's gone. Or is it? When I described this to my brother he said no, the badness is still there, those people have just lost the ability to be bothered by it — like how a person with depression might stop caring about a bad situation without the situation getting better. So now we have these two camps and we keep going in circles.

I want you to treat us as an adversarial collaboration and draft the joint statement we should be able to produce. Call the camps whatever you want, but roughly: my side says pain is bad because of the raw feeling itself, the unpleasant quality, and asymbolics have lost the badness along with the bother; his side says the badness of pain lives in our reaction to it — the wanting it to stop, the guarding and wincing and preoccupation — and the bare sensation without that is more like a weird neutral sensation, no worse than a bright light. I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both of us can actually sign, including about what asymbolia does and doesn't show, and about the fact that ordinary pain seems to have both a sensation part and a suffering part; (2) the remaining disagreements, each stated so the person who holds that view would accept the wording of their own position — I suspect one real disagreement is about whether an asymbolic who keeps injuring herself without caring is being harmed or just being imprudent; (3) the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions where an answer would actually move one of us. For each crux, give a case or thought experiment we'd both accept as bearing on it and write down in advance what each of us predicts it shows. Please include these two cases somewhere since we argued about them for an hour: the burn of hot chili peppers, which tons of people actively enjoy, and a hypothetical drug that removes the unpleasantness of pain but leaves you fully able to locate and rate the sensation — my brother says that drug would be the best painkiller ever, I say something about it creeps me out and I can't say why.

One more thing: please don't just adjudicate and declare a winner, the whole point is the joint document. But do say how confident you are in the claims you attribute to each side and in your framing of the cruxes, and flag the single point in your draft where you're least sure you've got it right — like whether a real person holding that view would definately sign the sentence you wrote for them.
