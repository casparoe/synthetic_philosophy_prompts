Two colleagues of mine have spent a semester in an adversarial collaboration on fairness in algorithmic risk assessment and now owe their funders a joint statement. One is a statistician who holds that fairness consists essentially in calibration: a risk score must mean the same thing for every protected group, so that among everyone assigned a given score, the same proportion actually go on to reoffend, default, or drop out, whatever their group. The other is a civil-rights scholar who holds that fairness consists essentially in error-rate parity: the rate at which people who would not have reoffended are classified as high-risk, and vice versa, must be equal across groups, because misclassification burdens are what people actually suffer. It is common ground between them that published impossibility results establish the following: except where the groups have identical base rates or prediction is perfect, no informative score can be simultaneously calibrated within every group and equal in its false-positive and false-negative rates across groups. Their collaboration concerns what follows from this, not whether it is true.

Draft the joint statement they should submit. It must contain: first, a numbered list of agreed claims both can sign, which must include the impossibility result itself stated precisely, plus at least four further agreed claims that are neither vacuous nor mere restatements of the mathematics — for instance, claims about what each fairness notion does and does not capture, and about what the theorem does not settle; secondly, a table of residual disagreements, each phrased in words both parties accept as a neutral statement of their own position — I expect these to include whether calibration is a fairness property at all rather than a truthfulness property of the score, whether unequal base rates are data to be accommodated or symptoms of the very injustice the score should not launder, and whether fairness attaches to the score or to the decisions taken on its basis; thirdly, for each disagreement, the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one party; and fourthly, for each crux, a concrete case or proposed policy both agree bears on it, with each party's verdict recorded in advance.

Two cases must appear among these. First, a jurisdiction that equalises base rates across groups by decriminalising the conduct in question: does the calibrationist still insist on calibration, does the parity theorist still demand equal error rates, and do their positions converge or merely stop conflicting? Secondly, a lender that retains a perfectly calibrated score but applies different decision thresholds to different groups in order to equalise error rates: which camp, if either, may call this fair without abandoning its stated view, and does either camp's verdict here reveal that its real commitment was to the decision rule all along?

Mark explicitly at least one disagreement that turned out, in the course of the collaboration, to be verbal rather than substantive, and show how it was dissolved. Conclude with a single sentence each party is willing to sign about what fairness in prediction can and cannot be. Please write in British English.
