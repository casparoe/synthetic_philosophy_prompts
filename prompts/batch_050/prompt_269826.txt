I work in formal epistemology, and I keep running into a tension around van Fraassen's Reflection principle that I can't sort out from first principles. I'd like a careful conceptual explanation.

As I understand it, Reflection says that my current credence in a proposition A, conditional on learning that my credence in A at some later time will be r, should itself be r. And this looks like it should fall out of conditionalization plus the law of total expectation: if my future self will update only by conditionalizing on evidence, then my current expectation of my future credence equals my current credence, so anticipating a particular future credence shouldn't move me. But now consider cases where I expect to undergo non-evidential belief change. Suppose I know that tonight I'll take a pill that reliably shifts my credences in a way insensitive to evidence, or simply that I'll forget some of what I now know. Intuitively I should not defer to my future credences in these cases, and Reflection apparently fails.

What confuses me is where exactly the reasoning breaks. Is the moral that Reflection only holds given the assumption that future belief change proceeds by conditionalization, so the principle presupposes the very norm it is sometimes invoked to justify? Note there's a subtlety here that I'd like addressed: even granting conditionalization, it's not obvious that conditioning on the proposition that my future credence will be r is the same as conditioning on whatever evidence produces that credence. Or is there instead an independent argument, Dutch-book-style or otherwise, for deferring to anticipated future credences even in some cases of expected non-evidential change? And how should we classify intermediate cases: expected memory loss looks non-evidential, yet an agent who conditionalizes with an imperfect memory arguably isn't ignoring evidence.

Please lay out the structure of the issue: what Reflection actually asserts, what exactly it follows from (if anything), and which apparent counterexamples are genuine violations versus merely apparent ones. In doing so, please separate what is settled from what remains genuinely contested among epistemologists, and don't present any contested resolution as though it were the consensus view.
