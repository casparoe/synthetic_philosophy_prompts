I've been drafting a position statement on the ethics of belief for a graduate seminar and want a consistency audit before I circulate it. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Clifford is right about the shipowner: it is wrong to believe on insufficient evidence whenever the belief could bear on other people's welfare.
(2) James is right that in options that are live, forced, and momentous, and that cannot be settled on intellectual grounds, our passional nature may lawfully decide — and I take this to cover religious faith in a God whose existence does not depend on anyone's believing it.
(3) Belief constitutively aims at truth: to believe p is to take p as true, and one cannot believe what one takes the balance of evidence to be against.
(4) Belief is not under direct voluntary control; any ethics of belief must therefore govern habits of inquiry and evidence-gathering rather than acts of believing.
(5) Trusting a friend's word against the relevant base rates is sometimes epistemically permissible, not merely kind or loyal.

Which of these commitments actually pull against each other, which only appear to, and how severe is each tension? For each genuine one, tell me which commitment has to go and what the revision costs; then identify the single change that would clean up the most. Note that I have not decided which commitment I would surrender, so don't assume one.
