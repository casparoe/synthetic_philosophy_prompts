After a career in experimental physics, I have been reading about imprecise probabilities, and I would like your help as an expert decision theorist. Below are my gut verdicts about when a precise probability seems right and when an interval or set of probabilities seems required. Think carefully, and begin by listing two or three candidate principles before settling on the one that best captures my pattern.

My verdicts: (1) A fair coin toss warrants the precise probability 0.5 — nothing else would be rational. (2) For an urn known only to contain 100 red and black balls in unknown proportion, I refuse to assign any single number to "red on the next draw"; a range is the honest answer, and I would pay a premium to avoid betting either way. (3) When two well-calibrated climate models give 0.2 and 0.6 for an event, I feel I should average them to 0.4 rather than carry the whole interval — a single number feels mandatory for planning. (4) For a medical treatment where trials are sparse and the population differs from the patient, an interval like 0.3 to 0.7 seems appropriate, and a doctor who quoted a point estimate would strike me as overconfident. (5) Yet when my mechanic gives me an interval for whether a repair will hold — somewhere between 30% and 70% — I find it evasive rather than honest, and I want the point estimate. (6) I also think it would be irrational for my interval in case (2) to *widen* as I observe more draws from the urn with replacement; more evidence should shrink uncertainty, not grow it.

Is there one coherent principle that delivers all six verdicts? I suspect (3) and (5) sit poorly with (2) and (4), since the structural situation looks similar while my reaction flips. Tell me which verdict a defensible account would force me to surrender, state the resulting principle precisely, and say what it predicts for a case I have not considered: a bookmaker offering odds on the outcome of a one-off election in a country whose politics I know nothing about.
