A friend and I argued late into the night about a mutual colleague who insists his marriage is thriving, yet systematically steers clear of any conversation, gathering, or stray piece of news that might bear on how it is actually going. I called him self-deceived; she replied that, strictly speaking, there is no such thing, and defended this with an argument I have been unable to fault, though its conclusion strikes me as absurd. It runs as follows:

(1) Successful deception requires an informational asymmetry: the deceiver grasps the truth and arranges for the deceived to miss it.
(2) In self-deception, deceiver and deceived are the same person, so no such asymmetry can exist within a single mind at a single time.
(3) Partitioning the mind does not help: if the parts pool their information, there is one knower and no asymmetry; if they do not, then no unified person is deceived — one subsystem merely misleads another, and a subsystem is not a self.
(4) Spreading the process across time does not help either: to set about deceiving myself I must first recognize the truth, and any plan to lose that recognition requires keeping track of what is to be lost, which keeps it in view.
(5) What remains once deception of a person by a person is ruled out is at most wishful thinking, motivated inattention, or insincere avowal.
(6) Therefore genuine self-deception is impossible; every apparent case reduces to one of these lesser phenomena.

I cannot accept (6) — the colleague's case, and countless milder ones, seem to be something more than wishful thinking or pretense — yet each premise looked innocent when my friend introduced it. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If forced to choose, I would rather surrender any particular claim about what deception requires structurally than accept that my colleague is merely pretending or merely inattentive; but I want to know exactly what I am paying for that verdict, and why the rejected step looked so plausible in the first place.
