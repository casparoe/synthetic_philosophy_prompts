I’ve spent way too many years reading LessWrong, SSC, and adjacent forums, and I keep coming back to this sentence from George Orwell’s essay “Politics and the English Language”:

“Underneath this lies the half-conscious belief that language is a natural growth and not an instrument which we shape for our own purposes.”

Please argue that, in the politically important cases, Orwell is mostly wrong: language is not really an instrument that speakers collectively control, but an equilibrium that emerges from lots of local incentives. Build the argument around the following “phrase-drift” setup. A term starts out attached to a fairly concrete test or prototype. Each writer can gain a little by stretching it to cover a nearby case—sounding more aligned, more sophisticated, harder to disagree with, etc.—while nobody has much incentive to police the boundary. Listeners infer the term’s meaning from prestigious usage, the next writer stretches it a little farther, and eventually the term functions mainly as an applause light or tribal password. At that point, anyone who insists on the older, narrower meaning pays a social and communicational cost. Explain why this makes vague political language a stable equilibrium rather than simply a bunch of individual bad habits.

The argument should do three things seperately. First, say exactly which claim in the quoted sentence is being rejected—don’t pretend Orwell thought nobody could ever coin a word or improve a paragraph. Second, explain why his remedy of individual clear writing may sometimes backfire: concrete language can expose ambiguities that a coalition depends on, make a speaker sound naive, or force them to take positions their audience would rather leave unstated. Third, give the strongest Orwellian reply and then answer it, including the point that effects can become causes and that imitation is precisely how bad conventions spread.

End by identifying the conditions under which a community actually can steer its language: for example, a small high-trust group, a term tied to a new technical distinction, an institution with enforcement power, or a sufficiently prestigious exemplar. Please make a real argument rather than just labeling everything “emergent,” and don’t rely on outside sources. Around 1,000–1,300 words.
