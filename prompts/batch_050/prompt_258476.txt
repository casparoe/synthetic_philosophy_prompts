I'm a senior, and my high school's Ethics elective sets aside one student-led block each term. I've been assigned a 75-minute session with a class of 18, and I want to design a structured simulation that makes the group feel why Mill's harm principle is so compelling in the abstract and so slippery in practice: the moment when everyone gladly endorses that only harm to others justifies coercion, and then discovers they cannot agree on what counts as harm. My rough idea, which you should refine or replace, is that the class acts as the council of a small fictional town voting on a series of proposed bylaws, where each proposal targets conduct that initially looks purely self-regarding, and after each vote I open pre-sealed "ripple cards" revealing knock-on effects on third parties, so the line between self-regarding and other-regarding conduct keeps moving under their feet. At least two of the proposals should involve speech (for instance, a speaker whose talks the town finds offensive, and one spreading health misinformation), so the group also hits the free-speech edge of the principle. Please give me concrete rules and roles, the materials I'd need, a mechanism that forces the class to distinguish direct harm, small harms that only matter in aggregate, and mere offense or disgust dressed up in the language of harm, plus a minute-by-minute timeline. Then write the debrief: which moments map onto harm versus offense, paternalism, and Mill's case that even suppressing harmful speech costs us something, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I suspect students will just stretch the word harm to ban whatever they dislike), and an honest note on which part of Mill's view no 75-minute game can reproduce and I'll have to supply by explanation.
