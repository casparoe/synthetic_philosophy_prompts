Next month I am presenting a paper to my department's epistemology seminar defending a thesis I'll call deliberation-relative pragmatic encroachment. The core claim: whether a subject knows that p depends in part on her practical situation, but not merely on the stakes, as standard subject-sensitive invariantism has it. On my view, p must be high enough on the subject's evidential ladder relative to the deliberations p is actually apt to govern -- so encroachment kicks in only when the proposition is doing, or would naturally do, justificatory work in the subject's practical reasoning, not whenever the subject merely stands to lose a lot if p is false. My selling point is that this avoids the classic counterexamples to crude stakes-based views (the high-stakes subject who never considers acting on p, the low-stakes subject whose practical situation is nevertheless epistemically demanding) while preserving the intuitive verdicts in bank-style cases.

The audience: roughly a dozen faculty and advanced graduate students. Two senior members are committed contextualists who think all the bank-case data are better handled by shifting standards for "knows" than by any encroachment on knowledge itself. One is a purist invariantist who thinks knowledge is answerable only to truth-relevant factors and regards all encroachment views as confusing epistemology with the ethics of assertion and action. There is also a philosopher of language who will care about the semantic argument -- the objection that "knows" does not behave like gradeable or context-sensitive terms under embedding, negation, and third-person knowledge attributions -- and several grad students who have absorbed the standard objection that encroachment generates an implausible knowledge-action norm.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet. I want:

1. The eight to ten objections I will actually receive from this specific audience -- not generic anti-encroachment talking points -- ordered by how likely each is to be raised and how much damage it would do to the session if I answer badly. Phrase each the way that particular audience member would phrase it, in their vocabulary, at the length they would actually speak.
2. For each objection: what I should concede outright, the reply I should give in under two minutes, and where that reply is weakest -- the follow-up a sharp questioner would press.
3. An explicit verdict on which objections my deliberation-relativity move genuinely answers versus which ones it merely re-labels. Take a stand here. If the contextualists' gambit is something I should concede ground to rather than fight, say so and tell me exactly how much ground; if the purist's demand to keep practical factors out of epistemology entirely is the deeper threat, say that instead. I do not want a balanced menu of options -- I want a committed assessment of which two objections are the real dangers and a recommendation for how to allocate my preparation time accordingly.
4. The one question I am hoping nobody asks, why it is the most dangerous, and my best available answer to it anyway.

Assume the room is friendly but exacting: nobody is trying to sink me, but the paper's fate depends on whether I leave the contextualists feeling their view was answered rather than talked past.
