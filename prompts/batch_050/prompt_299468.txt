Two weeks ago I had a long, unresolved argument with a close friend about markets in human organs, and I walked away having conceded less than I probably should have. The argument forced me to sharpen a proposal I had been developing informally, and I have now agreed to present it at my teaching hospital's quarterly bioethics colloquium. I want to arrive over-prepared for the question period rather than merely confident.

The position I will defend: a state-run compensated living kidney donation program with a specific institutional design, namely a government monopsony paying a fixed price set well above the black-market level in source countries; compensation split between a modest lump sum and an indexed annuity paid over twenty-five years; lifetime state-funded medical follow-up for the donor, including kidney-specific insurance; guaranteed priority on the transplant waiting list should the donor ever need a kidney themselves; a mandatory six-month cooling-off period with an independent donor advocate; and, controversially, an eligibility rule excluding any candidate for whom the total compensation would exceed half their annual income, on the ground that the state should refuse transactions in which money is doing coercive rather than merely attractive work. My claim is not that organ sales are unproblematic, but that every serious objection to this design is either an empirical bet I am willing to take or a principled objection that proves too much, since it would also condemn practices the objector accepts, such as paid plasma donation, paid surrogacy, or hazardous employment.

The audience is genuinely mixed, which is what worries me. Roughly half will be transplant nephrologists and surgeons, practical people who have watched patients die on dialysis and may be sympathetic in principle but will press hard on outcomes, donor regret, surgical risk, and the international trafficking precedent. A quarter will be clinical ethicists, several of them trained in a broadly Kantian tradition, who care less about outcomes than about what the transaction expresses and what it does to the donor's standing as a person. There will be two or three health economists who may attack from the opposite direction, arguing that my eligibility rule and annuity structure are paternalistic distortions that recreate the shortage by other means. Finally, the colloquium is open, and dialysis patients and their families sometimes attend; I cannot afford an answer that is technically sound but reads as callous to someone who has waited four years for a kidney.

Please prepare me for the questions. I want, first, the objections I will actually receive, ordered by the product of how likely they are and how much damage they would do if I fumble them, with each one attributed to the kind of questioner most likely to raise it and phrased the way that person would genuinely phrase it, not the way a philosophy seminar would. Second, for each objection, a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, plus an explicit note on where that reply is soft and what, if anything, I should simply concede rather than defend. Third, identify the weakest joint in my own proposal, the part I am most tempted to talk past rather than through. Fourth, tell me which single reply has to land cleanly for the proposal to survive the evening, and what the objection is that I am quietly hoping nobody asks, together with the best available answer to it.
