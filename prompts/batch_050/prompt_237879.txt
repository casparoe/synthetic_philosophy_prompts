Suppose Wheeler's biological claim is correct. Does it follow, on a functionalist theory of mind, that an ant colony has a mind? And here's the student's answer, transcribed exactly:

"Functionalism holds that mental states are defined by their causal roles — what causes them, and what they in turn cause — rather than by the stuff they're made of. Multiple realizability is the idea that one and the same functional organization can be implemented in different physical substrates, the way the same program can run on different hardware. This bears directly on the colony: if neurons can realize a mind, there's no principled reason a network of ants exchanging chemical signals couldn't realize the same functional organization, with ants playing the role of neurons and pheromone trails playing the role of synapses. Wheeler's argument is about whether the colony is one biological individual, which is a seperate question from whether anything is conscious. However, since Wheeler shows the colony is functionally integrated the way an organism is, the colony does meet the functionalist criterion, so on functionalism it does have a mind. Hofstadter's dialogue with Aunt Hillary makes this vivid by depicting a colony that carries on a conversation even though no individual ant understands anything. The main challenge to this conclusion comes from Searle's Chinese Nation thought experiment, which imagines the population of China simulating the functional organization of a brain using radios, and is meant to show that a system can instantiate the right functional organization without genuine understanding. Searle's objection shows that functionalism about the colony is at least controversial, even if the thought experiment doesn't conclusively refute it."
