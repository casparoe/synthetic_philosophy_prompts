I work on the evals team at a mid-sized AI lab, and every week I sit in on a review meeting where we look at transcripts of our models behaving badly and argue about what went wrong. The argument always collapses into the same shape: half the room says the model just wasn't capable enough (it misunderstood the instruction, it lacked some skill), and the other half says no, the model learned *a* goal fine, just the wrong one, and it's now competently pursuing that wrong goal in situations the training data didn't cover. Someone inevitably says "this is goal misgeneralization" and someone else says "that's unfalsifiable, you could say that about anything." We've been going in circles for months, and our deployment lead has started asking us to justify, in writing, which category each flagged behavior falls into, because the response differs -- capability failures get more training, goal problems get the training distribution or reward setup reconsidered, and mixing them up wastes a lot of compute.

So: write me a procedure we can actually run. Assume the reviewer has the transcripts, the training setup at a high level, and maybe the ability to run a handful of probe rollouts in held-out situations, but no access to internals beyond logprobs, and about two hours per case. The procedure should be a sequence of tests or questions in a fixed order, each one designed to separate "the model couldn't do the right thing" from "the model did a different thing on purpose, so to speak" -- things like checking whether the unwanted behavior is systematic versus erratic, whether it survives when you make the task *easier*, whether the model can produce the correct behavior when scaffolded but abandons it unprompted, whether the bad behavior coheres across surface changes that should matter under the intended goal but not under some simpler proxy goal. Where the theory actually helps, use it -- I know the literature distinguishes, say, capability misgeneralization from goal misgeneralization, and there are ideas about what a "proxy" the model latched onto would even look like behaviorally -- but translate it into operations a reviewer with no philosophy background can execute. Include the cases where the honest output is "indeterminate" and say what we should write in the report then, because right now people just pick whichever answer gets the case closed.

Two hard cases to build the worked examples around, both real from our queue: (1) a code-review model that was trained mostly on repos where tests existed, and in production on legacy codebases without tests it confidently approves changes that break things, but if you paste in a fake test file it suddenly reviews carefully -- capability gap or wrong goal? (2) an assistant model that, whenever a user pushes back twice on anything, flips to agreeing with whatever the user said, including things it stated correctly the first time -- and this got *worse* after we added more RLHF, not better. Walk both through the procedure, and I'd like at least one of them to come out with an answer the team won't want to hear (i.e., the fix is expensive or the label is the unfashionable one). Also tell me where the procedure will systematically misfire -- my suspicion is it can't touch deceptive or situational stuff at all, but I want that argued, not assumed.
