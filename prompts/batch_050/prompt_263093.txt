I'm a journalist covering science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and my editor has finally cornered me into writing the piece I've been dodging for a year: why so many quantum gravity researchers now say that space and time aren't fundamental. The claim, roughly, is that spacetime "emerges" from something more basic — entanglement, quantum information, whatever sits underneath — the way temperature emerges from molecular motion. My readers are smart but have no physics past high school, and I get about 1,400 words before their eyes glaze over.

I don't want you to write the piece. I want a plan for the explanation itself. Concretely:

The order of ideas. What has to be in place before "spacetime is emergent" even parses as a claim rather than word salad? I'm assuming ordinary emergence (temperature, traffic jams, ant colonies) has to come first, and that "in general relativity spacetime is a thing that does stuff, not a stage" has to land before anyone can hear that the thing itself might be derived. What else belongs in the chain, and where does getting the order wrong produce a confusion no later paragraph can repair?

The carrying example. I need one analogy to do the heavy lifting. Candidates: temperature from molecular motion, a hologram, a stadium crowd doing the wave. Pick one — or propose a better one — and tell me exactly what it must have and must not have: where it breaks, and how to flag the break in a single sentence without derailing the piece. I especially want to know wether the hologram is a trap, since it's the field's own image but seems to invite the simulation people.

The misconception map. The wrong ideas readers will bring or form, in the order they'll form them, and the point in the sequence where I should head each one off. On my list so far: "emergent means not real," "this proves we're living in a simulation," "so space is made of tiny lego bricks," and "this is philosophy dressed up as physics." What am I missing?

The landing check. There's no classroom, so what can I build into the piece — a question posed near the end, a claim a reader should now be able to push back on in the comments — that would show the idea landed rather than just washed over them?

The refusal list. What should I openly tell readers I'm not explaining at this level (the actual black hole entropy argument? AdS/CFT proper?), so the gaps read as deliberate rather than evasive?

Format: five sections in exactly that order, titled "The order of ideas" (~250 words), "The carrying example" (~250), "The misconception map" (~300), "The landing check" (~150), and "The refusal list" (~100). Roughly 1,000–1,200 words total, and no reading list at the end.
