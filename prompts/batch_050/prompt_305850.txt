I am a retired experimental physicist — forty years in condensed-matter measurement, a great deal of it spent on precision thermometry and the round-robin comparisons by which laboratories agree on the value of a constant — and over the past year I have been reading the classical pragmatists on truth, Peirce and James, with no training in philosophy beyond a undergraduate module half a century ago. In Peirce's essay "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (Popular Science Monthly, 1878), I came upon the following remark about Leibniz, which stopped me short:

"That a piece of mechanism could not do work perpetually without being fed with power in some form, was a thing perfectly apparent to him; yet he did not understand that the machinery of the mind can only transform knowledge, but never originate it, unless it be fed with facts of observation."

I want to know how far this machinery metaphor can be pushed, and where it breaks. My request has two parts, and I should like the answer in British English.

First, the translation. Restate Peirce's account of truth and James's rival account in the vocabulary of measurement science that I actually command: estimators and their convergence as sample size grows, calibration against standards, inter-laboratory comparison, the distinction between random and systematic error, and the long-run stabilisation of measured values as technique improves. Take each thinker in turn. For Peirce, treat the quoted passage as the crux: if the mind can only transform and never originate knowledge, then truth presumably behaves like the limiting value of an estimator fed by ever more observation, and my question is whether that reading is faithful to what he says elsewhere in the essay and in his other scattered remarks, or whether it quietly smuggles in assumptions of my own trade — for instance, that there is a fixed true value sitting there to be approached, which I gather is exactly what some commentators deny Peirce is committed to. For James, whose claim that truth is made in the course of verification and bound up with what satisfies our purposes seems to let the machinery originate its output after all, say what in his view corresponds to anything in my vocabulary at all.

Second, and to my mind the more interesting part, a residue report. Which elements of each position translate cleanly into metrological terms, which survive only with distortion, and which find no expression whatsoever? For whatever does not survive, I want a verdict in each case: is the residue a defect of the laboratory vocabulary — something real that my framework simply cannot say — or a sign that the philosophers' claim was less precise than it appeared? Please do not flatter my discipline by translating loosely and calling it a match; the value of the exercise for me lies precisely in the failures. If your conclusion is that the machinery metaphor I quoted misrepresents both men, I would rather hear that than receive a tidy mapping.
