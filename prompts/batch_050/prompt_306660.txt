I'm drafting a post for the Alignment Forum defending debate as a scalable oversight scheme, and before I expose myself to that comment section I want to check my position actually hangs together, because those people will find any contradiction in about five minutes. I've been following this literature on and off since the original debate proposal and the obfuscated-arguments discussion that came after it, and I've definately flip-flopped a few times over the years, which is how I've ended up holding all of the following at once:

(1) The basic case for debate still seems right: the honest debater's job is fundamentally easier, since they just need one true story that stays locally consistent no matter how deep the cross-examination goes, while a liar has to maintain an expanding tree of mutually consistent falsehoods. So in the limit, honesty is the winning strategy.

(2) I also think the obfuscated-argument problem is the real crux and hasn't been solved: a dishonest debater, or a search process generating arguments, can produce one that's flawed but where nobody — including the debater — knows which step fails. In which case "just recurse on the flawed step" isn't executable advice, because you can't zoom in on a flaw you can't locate.

(3) But I think this is fine in practice, because cross-examination doesn't require knowing where the flaw is ahead of time: the honest player can probe, demand examples, and force the liar into ever more specific commitments, and the liar's compute-per-round costs grow faster than the honest player's, so even bounded-depth debate favors honesty.

(4) Human judge biases — favoring fluent, confident answers that agree with what the judge already thinks — strike me as an engineering problem rather than a deep one. Ensembling judges, calibration training, and symmetric presentation of both sides should wash most of it out.

(5) Given (1) and (3), I'm honestly not that worried about what's going on inside the debaters. The training signal rewards winning, winning means honesty, so selection pressure alone pushes toward honest systems and debate mostly sidesteps the whole inner alignment mess.

Go through the set and tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which pairs only look like they do. Rank the real tensions by severity, give the cheapest fix for each and what that fix costs me, and finish by saying which single change would buy the most consistency overall. I genuinely don't know which of these I'd give up if forced, so don't pull punches.
