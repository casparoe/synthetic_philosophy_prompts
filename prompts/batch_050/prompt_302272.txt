I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a long piece about what I'm calling the problem of trivial harms at scale: subscription flows engineered to waste ninety seconds of a hundred million people's time, ad networks that degrade every page load slightly, notification systems calibrated to interrupt you just below the level where you'd complain. No single instance of these rises to the level of a harm anyone would sue over, and yet the people designing them are plainly extracting something enormous in aggregate. In reading around the ethics of this I keep returning to Eliezer Yudkowsky's 2007 post "Torture vs. Dust Specks," which sets up the now-famous choice between torturing one person for fifty years and giving an inconceivably vast number of people a dust speck in the eye. What strikes me is how carefully he specifies the small harm:

"Well, suppose a dust speck floated into your eye and irritated it just a little, for a fraction of a second, barely enough to make you notice before you blink and wipe away the dust speck."

My working position for the piece is the aggregative one: barely-noticed harms genuinely aggregate, and if you multiply even a minuscule disutility by a large enough number of people, it can outweigh a catastrophic harm done to one person. I realize plenty of readers find the opposite conclusion obvious, but I think lexical-threshold views -- no number of dust specks ever outweighs the torture -- are the ones carrying the burden of proof, since we aggregate small harms constantly in ordinary policy (traffic delays, minor pollution, queueing costs) without anyone treating it as monstrous. What I want is a map of my position's exposure: what would count against it? I'd like the defeaters distinguished by type and ranked from fatal to merely awkward. On the empirical side: would it matter if it turned out that irritations engineered to sit at the edge of notice contribute literally nothing to felt ill-being -- that below some threshold of salience there is no negative welfare to add up, so the multiplication never gets started? Would it matter if subjective badness saturates, so that the millionth dust speck in one life is harmless but the millionth across lives is not, or vice versa? On the normative side: the separateness-of-persons objection, claims that disutility isn't additive across lives, sorites-style arguments that any continuum from specks to torture must be broken somewhere, and the suggestion that the passage's stipulation "barely enough to make you notice" is doing hidden work -- that a harm designed to barely register is exactly the case where aggregation breaks down, because you cannot stack experiences that leave no experiential residue.

Two further requests. First, tell me not only what critics would say but what committed aggregators would themselves concede as counting against the view -- is there an observation, an intuition pump, or a formal result (say, about the structure of any transitive, additive welfare function) that a defender of aggregation would accept as a genuine defeater, or has the position been quietly insulated by retreating to "in principle" claims and stipulating linear disutility wherever it's challenged? If it has become effectively unfalsifiable, say so plainly, and tell me whether that's a defect for a view of this kind or just the normal condition of normative commitments. Second, a note on what would move me personally: I would find it most persuasive if someone could show me a principled line that preserves aggregation for the moderate cases my article depends on -- where the individual harm is small but genuinely felt -- while blocking the dust-speck conclusion, without simply planting an ad hoc threshold where convenient. If no such line exists and the choice really is between unrestricted aggregation and arbitrary thresholds, that's an important finding for the piece, and I'd like it argued rather than assumed.
