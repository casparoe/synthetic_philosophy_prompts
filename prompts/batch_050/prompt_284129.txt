Next term I'm teaching an upper-level undergrad epistemology seminar, about 14 students, one 75-minute session, and I want to do the bootstrapping problem / easy knowledge as an activity instead of a lecture. I was complaining to a colleague in the stats department that whenever I present the problem on the board -- the gas-gauge-style case, where you certify a source's reliability by consulting that very source, notice that's circular, and then notice that every check of every faculty seems to bottom out the same way -- students nod politely and treat it as a logic puzzle rather than something that touches their own eyes and ears. So: design me an in-class game or structured exercise where the students run into the problem with their hands, so to speak, before anyone names it. My rough thought is something with several physical "measuring devices" in the room (I can build simple props: boxes with lights, a bag of colored tokens, whatever) where the students' task is to figure out which devices are reliable using only the resources available in the room, so they're driven first to cross-check devices against each other and then to the uncomfortable realization that a whole consortium of devices could pass every such check while being systematically wrong -- and then, ideally, that their own vision is in the same boat. But please improve on that or replace it if you have something better.

Give me the full runnable package: rules and materials, the sequence of phases with rough timings, any small stakes or scoring to keep them invested (no real money), and crucially the moment where they should construct the circular justification themselves rather than have it pointed out. I want the debrief spelled out too: which moments in the game map onto track-record arguments, onto the "easy knowledge" worry that you can apparently bootstrap your way to knowing a source is reliable just by using it, and onto the disanalogy between checking an instrument and checking a sense faculty. The name of the problem should only come up at the very end. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- where students will find a loophole that defuses the problem, or refuse to take the bait, or get stuck on something irrelevant -- and how to handle it.

Two more things. First, be honest about which feature of the actual philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce (my guess: the game lets them appeal to an external standard in principle, whereas with perception there arguably isn't one, and I'm not sure how to stage *that*) and what I'll have to supply in discussion. Second, I don't want false confidence in the design: tell me which specific choice you're least sure about, and what would change your mind -- i.e., what classroom outcome or objection would lead you to redesign the central mechanic rather than just patch it. If some part of the plan only works because students are being cooperative, say so.
