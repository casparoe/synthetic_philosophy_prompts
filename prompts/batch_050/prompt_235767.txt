In my political philosophy seminar, we spent the past month on whether there is a duty to vote for the lesser evil, and the discussion has congealed into two camps that mostly talk past each other. The first camp, who call themselves convergence consequentialists, hold that the moral point of casting a ballot is to bring about the least bad outcome among those actually reachable, so that in any realistic election one ought to vote for the least-bad candidate with a genuine chance of winning, however distasteful that candidate is; on their view, purity at the ballot box is self-indulgence paid for by other people. The second camp, the integrity expressivists, hold that a vote is an act of endorsement for which one is personally answerable, that one may never endorse a candidate who crosses certain moral lines no matter the stakes, and that treating a ballot as a lever on outcomes is a category error given that any individual vote is causally negligible. Our professor, tired of the deadlock, has assigned the two camps an adversarial collaboration, and I have been drafted to produce the first draft of their joint statement. To make things concrete, she stipulated a setting both camps must take seriously: a binding referendum conducted under a system in which 're-run with new options' is itself a listed option and wins if it gets the most votes, so a protest choice is not causally inert in the usual way, and the simple version of the negligible-vote argument is unavailable to the expressivists, while the convergence camp must reckon with the fact that withholding consent can sometimes be an outcome-lever too. Both camps have already informally conceded a few things: the consequentialists grant that some candidates are beyond the pale even at real electoral cost, and the expressivists grant that what one's vote would bring about if others voted likewise is at least relevant, though not decisive.

Draft the joint statement both camps could actually sign. Give exactly five numbered agreed claims, each one or two sentences, formulated precisely enough that neither side would strike them as vacuous or slanted (they may concern what a vote is, what both sides agree a voter owes and to whom, what the stipulated referendum system does and does not change, and which common arguments each side renounces as bad). After the five claims, give a disagreements table with exactly three rows, one per crux: each row should state the residual disagreement in wording each camp would accept for its own position, name the case or argument both camps agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts that case or argument shows. No introduction, no conclusion.
