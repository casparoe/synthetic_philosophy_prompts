I spent forty years as an experimental physicist and have lately been reading formal epistemology. One notion interests me: imprecise probability, on which rational credence in a proposition can be an interval rather than a point, and in particular the phenomenon of dilation, where acquiring new and seemingly relevant evidence widens one's credal interval instead of narrowing it. I would like a thought experiment built to the following specifications. Construct a realistic scenario, drawn from experimental science, medicine, or engineering and not involving urns of unknown composition, in which a rational agent holds an interval-valued credence about some quantity (a calibration constant, a failure rate, a treatment effect), is offered a piece of genuinely relevant evidence at no cost, and finds her interval strictly dilated upon receiving it. Further, arrange matters so that a practical decision hangs on the quantity and the agent would do better to refuse the free evidence than to accept it. The scenario should fit in a paragraph. Then demonstrate that the specifications are met: state the prior interval, the evidence, the dilated posterior interval, and why a precise Bayesian in a structurally identical position would always take the free information. Finally, identify which feature of the case is doing the work (I suspect a dependence between the evidence and whatever generates the imprecision), say what happens if it is removed, and give the strongest reply available to a defender of precise probabilism who denies that rationality could make declining free evidence sensible.
