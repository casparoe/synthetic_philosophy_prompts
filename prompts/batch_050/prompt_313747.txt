Yesterday I spent several hours arguing with a friend about Davidson's account of radical interpretation, and we reached an impasse that I would like you to adjudicate with a careful argument. My friend defends what I take to be the standard reading: the principle of charity is a methodological heuristic the interpreter adopts because she has no other way of getting started, and once interpretation is underway, nothing in principle prevents her from concluding that the speakers she interprets are massively mistaken — that they live, say, in a community of habitual liars, or under a regime so systematically deceptive that most of their beliefs about their own surroundings are false. My position, which I want you to argue for, is that this reading gets Davidson backwards: charity is not a defeasible starting assumption but a constitutive constraint on what it is to attribute beliefs at all, so that the very notion of a belief's content is fixed against a background of largely true beliefs, and consequently the hypothesis of a community in massive, undetectable error is not a coherent interpretive outcome but a misuse of the vocabulary of belief. In developing the argument, please address the strongest version of my friend's rejoinder, which is that a constitutive reading of charity makes interpretation unfalsifiable and thereby empties belief attribution of empirical content — if error is ruled out in advance, he says, then agreement cannot be evidence of anything. Say explicitly, near the end, what would change your mind: which consideration, counterexample, or reading of Davidson's texts would lead you to conclude instead that charity is merely heuristic and massive error genuinely possible, and where you take the argument you have given to be most vulnerable. Please write your answer as continuous prose in the manner of a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
