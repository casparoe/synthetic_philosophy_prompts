I sit on the board of a small community foundation in Oregon, and we've been wrestling with a weird bequest for months. One of our longtime donors died last year and left us about $4 million in trust with an unusual condition: the money is to be invested and paid out as local environmental grants by an AI agent she spent the last years of her life building, and her will says no human is to override its grant decisions — our staff may only handle the paperwork and flag anything unlawful. The lawyer we consulted says the AI obviously can't serve as trustee itself, so a human trustee has to hold legal title, but he floated the idea of wrapping everything in a single-member LLC whose operating agreement delegates all decisions to the system. That suggestion is what set off the fight. Half the board figures corporations are legal persons and are basically just bundles of contracts and code anyway, so this is nothing new and we should stop agonizing; the other half thinks a corporation always has actual humans behind it who can be hauled into court, fired, or at least embarassed, and an AI with nobody behind it is a different animal entirely, so treating it like just another LLC is a category error that will blow up the first time it makes a disastrous or discriminatory grant and a reporter comes calling.

I've been asked to write the memo we'll vote on at next month's meeting. Please draft it for me: recommend a concrete course of action (accept the bequest under the LLC structure as proposed, accept it only with a human override layer bolted on despite the donor's stated wishes, or decline the money outright), and give the reasoning in a way that takes the corporate-person analogy seriously rather than just name-checking it — i.e., get into what legal personhood is actually for, what work it does in the corporate case, and whether that work gets done here. Whichever side you land on, give the strongest version of the losing argument an honest shake, especially on the question of who answers for the system's decisions when something goes wrong. It would also help if the memo said something about whether the donor's intent matters morally here, since the family is split and two of her kids have hinted at contesting the will, though that's drama I'd rather the memo not lean on too heavily.

Please write it as continuous prose, the way a memo or long email actually reads — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Put the recommendation itself right up front so the board sees the bottom line immediately, and then unfold the argument from there. Aim for something I could read aloud in maybe ten minutes.
