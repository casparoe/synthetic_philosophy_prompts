ok so i volunteer on the reader-advocacy committee of a mid-size local newspaper and we're rewriting the ethics policy, and i've noticed the current one has this weird three-tier structure that nobody can quite justify to me:

1. Honest mistakes -- reporter checks her notes, writes something false in good faith -- get a printed correction and that's it. no discipline at all, even if the error did real damage.

2. Fabrication -- reporter invents a quote or a source -- is fireable on the spot. and here's the kicker: it's fireable even if the invented quote turns out to be accurate. there was apparently a case years back where a guy made up an interview, and the subject later said yes i would have said basically exactly that, and they fired him anyway.

3. Meanwhile a reporter who knowingly prints something she believes is false, because her editor pressured her and she figures it's technically defensible, gets treated under a third, mushier provision about "conduct damaging to credibility."

now the thing that prompted all this: the paper wants to start using an LLM to draft routine stuff (meeting recaps, sports roundups), and we need to decide which of the three boxes its hallucinations go in. and i realize i cant answer that until i understand what the existing policy is even committed to. like, is the policy implicitly running something Frankfurt-flavored, where the worst sin isnt falsity or even intent to deceive but indifference to whether what you say is true? if so, a model that has no attitude toward truth at all might be the purest possible case of the fireable offense, which seems like a wild conclusion for a meeting recap about the city council.

so: reconstruct the implicit theory of the three-tier policy. what does it actually presuppose about the difference between error, lying, and bullshit, and which parts of it would several rival theories license just as well (i suspect the corrections bit is less committed than the fabrication bit, but tell me if i'm wrong)? then tell me what follows for the LLM case under each candidate theory, and what in the policy would have to change if we just went outcome-based and only cared about falsity.

also, please flag how confident you are in each claim as you go, and at the end single out the one point in your reconstruction you're least sure of.
