For a first-year undergraduate ethics class (24 students, 75 minutes, no prior exposure to the separateness of persons objection), design a structured activity that makes participants feel the force of that objection from the inside -- but with this twist: the activity must first make utilitarian aggregation seem obviously right, so that the objection, when it lands, lands against something the students themselves just did rather than against a theory they were told about. I'm imagining a staged resource-allocation exercise (a student housing office, a clinic triage queue, a committee distributing a limited budget -- your choice) with explicit rules and payoffs, run in at least two rounds: one where summing across people is the natural way to decide and produces a verdict everyone accepts, and one where the same summing procedure quietly distributes benefits and burdens across different people in a way that makes some participants object, in the moment, that it shouldn't matter to person A's loss that person B gained. The participants should register that complaint before anyone names Rawls or Nozick. Include: the exact rules and numbers for each round (the numbers matter -- design them so the second round's aggregate-maximizing outcome concentrates a real loss on one or two people while spreading small gains widely), a timeline, roles, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the objection's components, says what will predictably go wrong when it's run (e.g., students re-describing the concentrated loss as unfair for independent reasons like desert or rights, which muddies the objection) and how to handle that, and is frank about which feature of the objection the game can't reproduce -- I suspect the fact that in real tradeoffs the losers' complaint isn't a strategic move in a repeated game, but tell me if you think the mismatch lies elsewhere. If you think the two-round structure I've specified can't work as posed, or that the premise that aggregation must be made attractive first is pedagogically confused, say so plainly rather than building around a flaw.
