The exam question was:

"Testimony: philosophers disagree about when beliefs based on what other people tell us are justified. Explain the difference between reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimonial justification, using Hume and Reid as your exemplars of each position. Then consider the following case. On her first morning in a city she has never visited, Adaeze asks a passer-by which platform the Manchester train leaves from. The passer-by, a stranger to her, replies that it leaves from platform 6, and Adaeze believes him. She has no evidence about this particular speaker's track record, and nothing about the situation strikes her as suspicious. For each of the two views, state whether Adaeze's belief is justified and explain why. Finally, say which of the two verdicts you find more defensible, giving one reason."

The student's answer:

"Reductionism about testimony, associated with Hume, holds that testimonial beliefs are not justified in their own right. Their justification has to be derived from more fundamental sources: perception, memory, and inductive inference. Hume's idea is that we trust what people tell us because experience has taught us that there is a regular correspondence between what people say and what is actually the case. Importantly, Hume is not a sceptic about testimony — he thinks testimony is very often justified, just derivatively so.

Anti-reductionism, associated with Reid, denies all this. For Reid, testimony is a basic source of justification, on a par with perception and memory. Just as we do not need to justify perception by appeal to something more fundamental, we do not need to justify testimony either. We are naturally constituted to trust what others tell us, and we are entitled to accept testimony by default. Because this entitlement does not rest on evidence about the speaker, it is also not lost when we gain evidence about the speaker — it is prior to all of that.

Applied to the case: on the reductionist view, Adaeze's belief is unjustified. She has never observed this stranger's reports to be true, so she has no inductive basis for trusting him, and reductionism requires the hearer to have established the reliability of the informant before accepting what they say. On the anti-reductionist view, her belief is justified: she has no particular reason to doubt the stranger, so the default entitlement kicks in.

I find the anti-reductionist verdict more defensible, because the reductionist verdict implies that we are almost never justified in believing strangers, which is absurd given how much of what we know comes from testimony."
