I help run a semester-long reading group — three physicists and two philosophers — that has chosen Maxwell's demon and the physics of information as its theme. Our anchor text is the closing chapter of Maxwell's Theory of Heat, where he introduces the being that Kelvin would later christen a demon:

"if we conceive of a being whose faculties are so sharpened that he can follow every molecule in its course, such a being, whose attributes are as essentially finite as our own, would be able to do what is impossible to us. For we have seen that molecules in a vessel full of air at uniform temperature are moving with velocities by no means uniform, though the mean velocity of any great number of them, arbitrarily selected, is almost exactly uniform. Now let us suppose that such a vessel is divided into two portions, A and B, by a division in which there is a small hole, and that a being, who can see the individual molecules, opens and closes this hole, so as to allow only the swifter molecules to pass from A to B, and only the slower molecules to pass from B to A. He will thus, without expenditure of work, raise the temperature of B and lower that of A, in contradiction to the second law of thermodynamics."

What strikes us is that Maxwell's own framing already contains the ingredients of every later resolution, without endorsing any: the being is explicitly finite, its advantage is purely a matter of sharpened faculties rather than force, and it sorts the molecules "without expenditure of work." The subsequent literature disagrees about where the second law is actually rescued — in the thermodynamic cost of acquiring information about the molecules, in the cost of erasing the records a finite being must keep, or nowhere at all because the law was only ever statistical, as Maxwell's own phrasing suggests. Given one semester for a group with our composition, which two or three open questions in this area should we spend our sessions on, and which debates would you deliberately skip as settled, merely terminological, or overrated? We want a defended agenda, not a survey of positions.
