I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have been reading David Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste" (Dissertation IV of his Four Dissertations, 1757) on my own, and one passage has been nagging at me:

"Homer's general precepts, where he delivers any such, will never be controverted; but it is very obvious, that when he draws particular pictures of manners, and represents heroism in Achilles and prudence in Ulysses, he intermixes a much greater degree of ferocity in the former, and of cunning and fraud in the latter, than Fenelon would admit of."

Later in the essay Hume goes further, claiming in effect that when vicious manners are depicted without the author's disapproval, the work is thereby disfigured — that the moral failing is a genuine artistic defect, not merely a moral one. I want to test that claim. The considerations I have already collected, from my own reading and thinking, are these:

1. The unreliable-narrator point: in works like Lolita the immorality is presented rather than endorsed, so such works are not counterexamples at all.
2. The immoralist point: some works (Riefenstahl's Triumph of the Will, de Sade's novels) seem to owe part of their artistic power precisely to their immoral outlook, so immorality can sometimes be an artistic merit.
3. The content/attitude distinction: we must separate what a work depicts from the stance it takes toward what it depicts; only the latter could count as a defect.
4. The parochialism worry, which Hume's own passage above seems to invite: if what counts as vicious manners shifts between Homer's Athens and Hume's Edinburgh, the test for artistic defect threatens to collapse into the reader's local prejudices.
5. The psychological point: our inability to enjoy such works may be a fact about us — we cannot bring ourselves to adopt the immoral perspective — rather than a flaw in the work.

Please give me one further consideration, for or against Hume's claim, that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or combination of items already on it. Then explain how it bears on the passage quoted — in particular, whether Hume's comparison of Homer and Fénelon supports or undermines the stronger claim he builds on it — and tell me honestly whether you think the new consideration is any good.
