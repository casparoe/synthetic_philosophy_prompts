I'm in a second-year political philosophy course and we just finished a unit on corruption, and after seminar two people from my discussion group, Marta and Dev, got into a proper argument in the hallway that neither would drop. It started from a case in our reading: a local official in a rural patronage network who accepts gifts from villagers -- livestock, food, envelopes at weddings -- and in return makes sure their paperwork moves, their kids get school places, the clinic treats them. Dev's position was basically that calling this corruption imposes a Western standard on a context where it doesn't fit. His points, roughly: (1) these gifts aren't bribes because there's no explicit quid pro quo, they're part of ongoing reciprocal relationships, and refusing them would be an insult that destroys the relationship; (2) in places where the formal state barely functions, the clientelistic network IS the welfare system -- the official is the only person who actually gets things done for these people, and judging him by impartial-bureaucrat standards presupposes institutions that don't exist there; (3) the "office" itself is defined differently in that context -- it's constituted by personal loyalty rather than impersonal rules, so he can't be violating norms of an office that were never his; and (4) Westerners calling this corrupt is rich, given lobbying and campaign donations, which are just gift-giving to officials with better PR.

Marta pushed back hard and I honestly found her hard to answer too. She said: (1) the no-quid-pro-quo point is naive -- the official will obviously favor gift-givers later, so functionally it IS a bribe, just with plausible deniability, and worse, the "gifts" are often paid by poor people to access services that are already theirs by right, which is extraction dressed up as gratitude; (2) the fact that the network fills a gap doesn't justify it, because networks like this actively block impartial institutions from ever forming -- it's a collective action problem where every individual participant is rational and the outcome is bad for everyone, so "it works for them" is only true in the short run; (3) she granted Dev's point about lobbying but said it cuts the other way -- if Western campaign finance is corrupt by the same standard, that shows the standard isn't Western, it's a functional one about public power being steered by private contributions, and it catches practices everywhere including our own. Dev's last move was to say her "functional standard" just smuggles in the ideal of the impersonal modern state, which is itself a culturally specific achievement and not some universal moral default, and Marta's last move was to say that when someone in that village is sick and gets queued past because they couldn't afford a gift, their sense of being wronged isn't culturally specific at all.

Our professor wouldn't adjudicate (annoyingly) and it's bugging me. Can you go through it move by move -- who's right about which specific point, which moves actually answer the previous one versus just changing the subject, and who's more right overall? And also: is there anything BOTH of them are missing? I have a vague sense there's a difference between judging the practice and judging the individual official, and something about whether "universal standard" and "same judgment in every context" are the same claim, but I can't quite articulate it.
