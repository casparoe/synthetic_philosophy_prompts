I work at a mid-size SaaS company on the team that owns our on-call alerting, and I have a problem that I think is basically an epistemology problem, though I have zero philosophy training so forgive me if I butcher the terms.

The deal is: almost none of our "evidence" that something is broken arrives as a clean yes/no. Our anomaly detector outputs a score like 0.73 that the latency spike is real vs. an artifact. A sensor on one region is flaky and right maybe 80% of the time. A customer support ticket saying "the site is down" is strong evidence but sometimes it's one guy on a bad VPN. Meanwhile my prior that there's a genuine incident on any given Tuesday night is honestly pretty low. I keep telling people to just "update on the evidence" but the evidence is never certain, so nobody knows what that actually means operationally, and we end up either paging people at 3am for nothing or ignoring real outages.

A friend told me the formal tool for this is something called Jeffrey conditionalization, where instead of conditioning on evidence E as if you're sure of it, you weight by how probable you think E is. So what I want is: turn that into an actual procedure my on-call engineers can run in like 5 minutes with a sticky note or a spreadsheet cell. Steps in order. What numbers they need to write down before they see the alert (so they're not just back-fitting). What to do when they genuinely can't put a number on something. Where the procedure just says "stop, wake up a human, this is a judgment call."

A few hard cases I specifically need it to handle, because these are where we get burned:

1. Two uncertain signals arriving one after another -- like the detector fires at 0.7, then ten minutes later the flaky sensor agrees at 0.8. Does the order I process them in change the answer? I vaguely heard that with Jeffrey-style updating it actually can, which is horrifying if true, and if it is true I need the procedure to say what order to use and why.
2. Correlated signals -- the detector and the sensor both read the same underlying logs, so treating them as independent feels wrong, but I don't know how wrong.
3. The case where the signal doesn't just move my confidence in "incident happening" but also scrambles my other beliefs -- like a partial outage where my estimate of "if this is real, it's region B" also changes. I read that Jeffrey updating assumes some conditional probabilities stay fixed ("rigidity"?) and I want to know when that assumption breaks in practice and what the procedure should do about it.

Please run the procedure on two worked examples: (a) our deploy-risk classifier flags a friday deploy at 0.65, prior on a bad deploy is maybe 0.05, and (b) a single customer tweet claiming data loss. Ideally one of them should come out with an answer I won't like -- either "don't page" when my gut says page, or vice versa -- because if it always agrees with my gut I've learned nothing.

Also, be honest about the epistemics here: tell me where this procedure is most likely to fall apart in practice, and what finding or argument would change your mind about the whole approach -- like if there's a known failure mode of Jeffrey conditionalization that makes it the wrong tool for sequential alerts, I want to know that rather than get a polished procedure built on sand.
