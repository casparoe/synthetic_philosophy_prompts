I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm assembling a special issue on the psychology of irrationality. Alongside the straight reporting, I want to commission one satirical piece, and I'd like you to draft it.

Write a deadpan self-help column that treats self-deception as a learnable life skill and earnestly instructs the reader in mastering it. The columnist is a relentlessly upbeat lifestyle guru writing in the register of a newspaper advice page, complete with a warm anecdotal opening and a set of numbered tips. The comedy should come entirely from the content: every technique the columnist recommends founders, quietly and without acknowledgment, on the philosophical puzzle at the heart of self-deception. To deceive yourself, you must in some sense know the truth you are hiding; so each instruction should require the very awareness it is designed to suppress. For instance, a tip for forgetting an inconvenient fact might begin by asking you to write the fact down so you know what to avoid; a tip on seeking out only supportive evidence might include a careful procedure for identifying which evidence will be unsupportive in advance. The guru never notices the problem, never winks at the reader, and closes on a note of triumphant encouragement.

The piece should exaggerate a real feature of the target rather than invent one: the self-help genre's confidence that any mental state can be engineered by technique, and, behind it, the casual assumption that deceiving oneself is as straightforward as deceiving someone else. It should be fully enjoyable by a reader who has never encountered the philosophy of self-deception, while a reader who knows the puzzle — how one person can be both deceiver and deceived — should be able to tell that the author knows it too, and ideally should find the satire fair to the genuine difficulty.

Maximum 650 words: one short warm-up paragraph followed by numbered tips, no headings beyond a column title of your choosing.
