I cover physics and philosophy for a general-audience magazine, and I keep returning to a dispute that seems frozen in place. In 1949, Kurt Gödel produced solutions to Einstein's field equations describing rotating universes in which spacetime admits closed timelike curves: paths along which an object could, in principle, travel and arrive at its own past. In such a universe, Gödel argued, there can be no consistent global ordering of events into earlier and later, and hence no objective lapse of time. He then made the further move that has occupied philosophers ever since: if the very laws that govern our world permit worlds without objective temporal passage, then passage cannot be part of what time essentially is -- and so it is absent here too, rotating cosmos or not.

The responses have since hardened into fixed positions. One camp replies that the argument goes through only if Gödel-type solutions count as physically possible worlds, and that physicists have independent grounds for excluding them: no global rotation of matter has been observed in our universe, and various conjectures and energy conditions are taken to rule out closed timelike curves in universes like ours. The opposing camp answers that this misses the point entirely, because the argument is modal: a contingent fact about which solution happens to be actual cannot establish that passage belongs to the essence of time. A third position accepts Gödel's conclusion but denies its sting, holding that what the argument shows is that time is a local, non-fundamental feature of certain spacetimes -- not that time is unreal.

Three quarters of a century on, everyone in this literature knows all of this, and nobody has moved an inch. The physicists I interview tend to shrug: the question is moot, they say, because such universes are excluded or irrelevant to ours. The philosophers I interview reply that the physicists are answering a different question under the same name. My working hypothesis is that the dispute is not really about general relativity at all but conceals two disagreements: what the word "time" refers to (a natural kind whose essence is fixed by the actual world, or a concept that includes passage by stipulation), and what counts as data (only our universe, or the whole space of solutions the laws permit).

Write a diagnosis of this impasse. Is it a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or several debates sharing a single name? Given your diagnosis, state what, if anything, would count as progress -- a theorem, a measurement of cosmic rotation, a conceptual distinction, a change of question -- or argue that no progress should be expected and explain why.
