Last night, over a very long dinner, a friend and I ended up in an extended argument about Nietzsche's genealogy of morality, and I should like an impartial audit of his position before I concede anything to him.

His case, as best I can reconstruct it, ran roughly as follows. Nietzsche demonstrated that what we call morality is a confidence trick: the weak and oppressed, unable to prevail by strength, invented the categories of good and evil out of sheer resentment, recasting their own impotence as meekness and their oppressors' vigour as wickedness. Once one sees this origin, my friend insisted, the thing stands debunked: a belief system born of envy cannot bind anyone, so the rational response is to discard morality altogether and live, as he kept putting it, beyond good and evil. He then added what he took to be a killing blow from within Nietzsche's own framework: the slave revolt in morals was itself a spectacular exercise of will to power, arguably the most successful in history, so Nietzsche, of all people, ought to admire it as a triumph rather than lament it, and his evident distaste for slave morality is therefore a lapse of consistency — a personal squeamishness dressed up as philosophy.

I came away suspecting that this is a caricature which nonetheless contains real components, and I am not confident I can separate them. My questions are these. First, which parts of his summary would a careful reader of Nietzsche accept, and which are misrepresentations? I have in mind, for instance, whether resentment alone is doing the explanatory work he attributes to it, and whether 'invented by the weak' is even the right way to describe who Nietzsche thought engineered the revaluation. Secondly, is his debunking move — the inference from an unflattering origin to the conclusion that morality need not be heeded — actually licensed by what genealogy does, or does it conflate undermining a value's prestige with refuting a proposition, and would Nietzsche himself have regarded that inference as the genetic fallacy it resembles? Thirdly, does his will-to-power rejoinder land? That is, does Nietzsche's own framework commit him to celebrating the slave revolt, or is there a principled way he can acknowledge it as a creative expression of will to power while still judging its product harshly — and if so, on what grounds, given that he cannot simply appeal to the morality he is criticising? Finally, assuming my friend's historical sketch were entirely accurate, would his practical conclusion — that morality should simply be discarded — follow, or does Nietzsche's revaluation of all values amount to something more discriminating than wholesale rejection?
