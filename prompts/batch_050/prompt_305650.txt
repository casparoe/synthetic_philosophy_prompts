People in my AI circles keep leaning on Turing's own analogy from Computing Machinery and Intelligence (1950), so I want to know how much weight it can actually carry. In the learning machines section, Turing proposes that instead of trying to write a program that simulates the adult mind, we should build a child machine and subject it to an education: a teacher dispensing rewards and punishments, with some channels left unemotional so the teacher can fix things directly, and he even floats trying out lots of slightly different machines and keeping the best, which he compares to evolution by natural selection. My friends use this to argue that training a model basically is raising and educating it, so we should expect to shape what it values rather than specify it, and be humble about controlling the outcome.

Audit the analogy. First the correspondence table: what plays the role of the child-machine, the teacher, the lessons, the rewards and punishments, the mutation-and-selection step, and the eighteen years, when the target is modern training of a large model (pretraining, fine-tuning, RLHF, evals)? Then the disanalogies: a child is one continuously learning embodied agent with its own appetites, a model is a frozen snapshot distilled from a huge fixed corpus; Turing's teacher teaches one machine while we train on the whole internet at once; children can deceive, refuse, and want things between lessons. Most important: separate the conclusion Turing actually drew (machine intelligence will come through something like education, not fully explicit programming) from the one my friends draw (so don't worry that we can't specify what it learns). Does Turing's version support theirs, or does it quietly cut agianst it?
