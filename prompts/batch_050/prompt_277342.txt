A colleague and I have been arguing about John Taurek's challenge to aggregation in rescue ethics, and we've stalled because we can't locate the actual source of our disagreement. The precise proposition under dispute is this: in a standard symmetrical rescue case — you can save either one stranger or five other strangers from equally serious, imminent harm, you cannot save all six, no special obligations are in play, the victims are relevantly alike, and the cost to you of either rescue is identical — it is morally *required*, not merely permitted, to save the five. Taurek's position, as we understand it, denies this: the greater number has no decisive claim on you, because harms and benefits cannot be summed across separate persons, so a fair coin flip between the groups is permissible. My credence in the proposition is about 0.85; hers is about 0.4.

There is a complication we keep circling, which I want taken seriously rather than waved at: the proportional lottery. Instead of Taurek's equal-chances coin flip, suppose the rescuer runs a weighted lottery giving each *group* a chance proportional to its size — here, one-sixth to the single person, five-sixths to the five. One side holds that this procedure respects each victim as an individual (everyone gets some positive chance, and no one is used merely as a means) while still letting numbers matter; the other side holds that this is aggregation smuggled back in through the decision procedure, and that if a proportional lottery is morally preferable to a fair coin, the anti-aggregationist has lost the substance of the position. Note that a proportional lottery *expects* the same number of deaths in the long run as always saving the greater number, which seems to blur the line between the two views further.

Third, tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to — the one where a changed probability would move the overall credence most. Fourth, given my 0.85 and her 0.4, diagnose where the disagreement most plausibly lives: which sub-question(s) would have to be assigned very different probabilities to produce that gap? Fifth, state the single argument or consideration that would most change your number, and in which direction.
