Someone in my group chat dropped "you can't reason someone out of a position they didn't reason themselves into" to justify why they stopped arguing with their dad about politics, and then again a week later as a reason why we shouldn't bother writing careful replies to bad arguments online -- mocking them is just as good, apparently. It clearly does a lot of work for a one-liner, and I'm not convinced it can do all of it.

Break the slogan down for me. What are the distinct precise claims it could be making? I can already see a few candidate readings floating around -- something psychological about how beliefs are caused versus how they're defended, something strategic about which persuasion tactics work on whom, something almost normative about who "deserves" a reasoned reply -- but I want the full menu, stated as crisply as you can, with a verdict on each: platitude, plausible-but-contested, or just false.

Some things I specifically want sorted:

1. The premise seems to assume you can tell from outside whether someone "reasoned themselves in." Is that actually knowable, or does the slogan smuggle in a diagnosis of the other side that's just as unfalsifiable as what it's diagnosing? Can the exact same slogan be run against the person wielding it (were they reasoned into believing the slogan?), and does that self-application defuse it, strengthen it, or just make a funny gotcha with no content?

2. Even granting the psychology -- suppose a belief really was adopted on vibes, tribe, upbringing -- does it follow that reasons can't dislodge it? Reasons given later can become the belief's new basis even if they weren't its original cause, right? Distinguish "reasons as the historical cause of the belief" from "reasons as something the person is responsive to now," and tell me which of these the slogan actually needs.

3. My friend uses it to conclude engagement is pointless full stop. But there's a gap between "reasoned argument won't work on this person" and "nothing will" and then another gap to "so contempt is fine." Which readings of the slogan, if any, bridge those gaps, and do any plausible ones survive?

Give it to me as a list I can paste back into the chat, and end with what a more defensible version of the slogan would have to say to still justify his conclusion -- if any version can.
