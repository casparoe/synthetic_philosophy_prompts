I'm a graduate student in a seminar on formal epistemology, and I've been stuck on something for two weeks. We covered Jeffrey conditionalization: the rule for cases where experience shifts my credence in some proposition without making me certain of anything. The standard classroom example is a marble glimpsed under a flickering lamp — my credence that it's black goes from 1/2 to 0.7, and the rule redistributes my credences over all other hypotheses accordingly while holding fixed my conditional credences given black and given not-black. Last week a classmate, Priya, argued that this entire framework — rational updating on uncertain evidence — collapses under inspection, and none of us could locate the slip. Her argument, as I recorded it: (1) Rational updating is a function of priors and total evidence: two agents with the same priors and the same total evidence who reach different posteriors are not both fully rational, and any rule that permits this is arbitrary. (2) Jeffrey conditionalization's entire input is a new probability distribution over a partition — say, P(black) = 0.7 — after which it preserves all conditional probabilities given the partition cells. (3) But a distribution over a partition is never anyone's total evidence: the shift to 0.7 could be produced by a dim glimpse of the marble, by testimony from a partially color-blind friend, or by a hazy memory of the label on the urn. (4) These three experiences plainly justify different beliefs about matters outside the partition — the lighting, the friend's reliability, my memory — and those differences rationally bear on hypotheses like "the urn was tampered with," even holding the black/not-black distribution fixed. (5) Since the rule sees only the distribution, it must return the same posterior over everything in all three cases. (6) So one of two things: either the rule must instead be applied to some finer partition that encodes those differences — but then every case of uncertain evidence has to be re-described as certainty about some proposition, such as what the friend said or how things appeared — or the evidence itself does not determine which partition to use, and the output is arbitrary in exactly the sense (1) forbids. (7) The first horn doesn't terminate: I can be just as uncertain about what appeared to me as about the marble, so pushing the certainty inward never lands on anything I actually possess. (8) Hence there is no non-arbitrary rule for updating on uncertain evidence; we can rationally update only on certainties. (9) But nearly all of our evidence is uncertain, so nearly all ordinary belief revision is irrational.

Priya's own verdict is that (1) is the casualty: rationality simply does not dictate a unique response to evidence, and Jeffrey conditionalization is best understood as a permissivist's tool rather than a constraint. The conclusion at (8)–(9) strikes me as proving far too much — uncertain evidence moves us rationally all the time, and a framework built precisely for the flickering-lamp case shouldn't convict itself of incoherence. I suspect something goes wrong inside (6)'s disjunction, or in the move from (5) to (6), but I can't name it cleanly. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please be concrete about the alternatives: if I keep (1), must I deny the rigidity assumption in (2), or deny that the three experiences in (3)–(4) differ in total evidence? What does it cost to let uncertain evidence constrain conditional probabilities directly rather than only unconditional ones, and is Priya's permissivism actually cheaper than it looks, or does it smuggle in the arbitrariness (1) was designed to exclude? If I'm forced to preserve one claim, I'd preserve (1) — but I want to know the full price before I pay it, and I want to understand why the step I reject looked so innocent.
