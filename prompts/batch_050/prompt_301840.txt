I'm in a course on formal epistemology, and after our lecture on Aumann's agreement theorem, two classmates got into an argument I couldn't settle. For a week they'd been trading forecasts about whether our department's star job candidate would get an offer. Each had private information -- one had talked to the candidate at dinner, the other had overheard faculty gossip -- and after each exchange they announced an updated probability. Their estimates converged from 70/30 to about 55/50 and then stuck there. (1) Marcus argued that, by Aumann's theorem, since they started from a common prior and have now exchanged their information repeatedly, their posteriors must eventually coincide; the fact that they've stalled at 55/50 shows that at least one of them is updating irrationally. (2) Lena replied that the theorem presupposes common knowledge of each other's rationality, which two tired students never actually have, so their persistent disagreement is no evidence of irrationality at all. (3) Marcus countered that this renders the theorem vacuous: if any residual doubt about the other party's rationality licenses permanent disagreement, the result never applies to real people, and that can't be the intended lesson of a theorem people cite against everyday agreeing to disagree. (4) Lena answered that the deeper problem is the common prior assumption -- their different starting forecasts most likely reflected genuinely different priors rather than different information, and where priors differ, agreeing to disagree is rational even between perfect Bayesians.
