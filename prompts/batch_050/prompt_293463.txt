Write a dialogue between two philosophers, both serving as unpaid advisors to a hospital ethics board, on the following case. A patient with a terminal prognosis of roughly three more good, lucid years has requested a new procedure called chronostasis: a painless, irreversible intervention that induces permanent dreamless unconsciousness. Afterwards the body is kept biologically alive, but no experience, memory formation, or awareness of any kind will ever occur again, and no one will visit or be affected by the patient's condition. The board's question to the advisors is twofold: is undergoing chronostasis bad for the patient, and is it any better or worse for her than dying now?

One philosopher defends the Epicurean position that nothing can be bad for a person at a time when that person does not exist, and so the patient's future nonexistence -- whether by death or by chronostasis -- cannot harm her. The other defends a deprivationist position: death is bad for the person who dies insofar as it deprives her of goods she would otherwise have had, and chronostasis is bad for essentially the same reason.

The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth in which each philosopher responds to the other's actual points rather than reciting set pieces, and both must be equally competent. In particular:

- The Epicurean must press the timing problem explicitly -- at what time is the supposed harm of death or chronostasis suffered by the patient? -- and the deprivationist must commit to an answer rather than deflect, whether by locating the harm before death, after it, or by defending the coherence of a harm with no time at which it is suffered.
- The deprivationist must press the symmetry argument's mirror image: if the patient's death is bad for her because it deprives her of three good years, why is it not equally bad for her that she was not born earlier, which deprived her of good years too? The deprivationist must give a principled account of the asymmetry, and the Epicurean must engage that account rather than simply repeating the symmetry intuition.
- Both must take a position on a variant of the case in which the unconsciousness lasts fifty years and is followed by full revival, and must say what the verdict on that variant reveals about whether the badness of death lies in what is lost or in its finality.
- The Epicurean must answer the charge that the view implies a painless killing that nobody ever discovers harms no one.

The dialogue should not end in agreement or in a draw. It should end with the two philosophers jointly identifying, as precisely as they can, the single point on which their disagreement actually turns, and each stating why they cannot accept the other's resolution of that point. Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words.
