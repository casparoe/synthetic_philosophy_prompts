"Philosophers sometimes say that when you're uncertain about which moral theory is correct, you should maximize expected choiceworthiness. (a) In your own words, explain what this means and how it's supposed to work, using a small numerical example. (b) Does moral uncertainty differ from ordinary empirical uncertainty (like being unsure whether it will rain)? If so, how? Here is the student's answer:

"(a) Maximizing expected choiceworthiness means you put credences on the moral theories you take seriously, and each theory rates how choiceworthy each option is. You then take the credence-weighted average for each option and pick the highest. Example: say I give credence 0.6 to a theory T1 that rates donating to charity A at 10 and donating to charity B at 5, and credence 0.4 to T2 that rates A at 2 and B at 9. Then A scores 0.6*10 + 0.4*2 = 6.8, while B scores 0.6*5 + 0.4*9 = 6.6, so I should donate to A even though T2 strongly prefers B. (b) Moral uncertainty is not fundamentally different from empirical uncertainty. It's structurally the same problem: maximizing expected choiceworthiness is just ordinary expected utility maximization applied to a bigger space of possibilities, where some of the possibilities are about which theory is true instead of about how the world is. The same decision theory handles both. (c) The biggest problem is intertheoretic comparison: what does it mean to say A is '10 choiceworthy' according to T1 versus '9 choiceworthy' according to T2 — are those the same units? The standard fix is to normalize each theory's scale, e.g. map each theory's best option to 1 and its worst to 0, and then compare. Once you've done that, the comparison problem is solved and the objection dissolves. (d) One alternative is the parliamentary model: imagine each theory gets seats in a parliament in proportion to your credence, the theories vote on the options, and you do what wins. The advantage is that each theory only needs to rank the options, so you never have to compare cardinal values across theories at all."
