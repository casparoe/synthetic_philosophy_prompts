You are a social choice theorist who works on institutional design, equally comfortable with the formal aggregation results and with the philosophical debates over what those results actually show. Think carefully before writing. I would rather you brainstorm a long list of candidate requirements and then prune it than hand me a tidy but shallow one, and I would rather you be precise about the mathematics where it matters than comprehensive where it does not.

Here is the situation. I sit on the charter committee of a small grant-making foundation. Each cycle, nine panel members individually rank a shortlist of roughly twenty proposals, and the foundation currently converts those rankings into a single funding order by an ad hoc procedure nobody will defend in writing. I have been asked to produce, before we even look at candidate voting rules, a specification: the list of requirements any acceptable aggregation rule must meet for this purpose. Three features of our setup may matter. First, panelists sometimes have conflicts of interest on particular proposals and are recused from those items only, so the effective electorate varies across the ballot. Second, what the foundation actually needs is not a complete social ordering but a top set of funded proposals plus a waitlist, although the board insists that the waitlist be an ordering it can publish and defend. Third, several board members have proposed replacing rankings with numerical scores so that, in their words, strength of conviction can count.

Write the desiderata list. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it -- the kind of concrete failure in our setting that the requirement is meant to prevent -- and classify it along two dimensions: (a) whether you take it to be non-negotiable for this purpose, negotiable at a price, or a preference that one camp in the voting-theory literature treats as sacred without adequate argument; and (b) whether it is a genuinely normative requirement, a technical convenience in disguise, or a constraint inherited from the formal framework rather than from anything anyone actually values. The board's proposal about scoring, for instance, effectively challenges the restriction to ordinal inputs, and I want to know whether that restriction is a real desideratum or an artifact of Arrow's setup that we should feel free to abandon.

Then the part I care about most: state precisely which subsets of your own list are jointly unsatisfiable given the standard impossibility results, treating Arrow's theorem as the centerpiece and bringing in the Gibbard--Satterthwaite theorem or Sen's liberal paradox only if your list contains requirements those results actually bear on (for instance, anything about incentives for honest ranking, or anything giving individual panelists protected say over particular comparisons). Where a theorem does the work, state it as it applies here, and be careful about the distinction between social welfare functions and social choice correspondences, since our top-set-plus-waitlist output sits awkwardly between the two. For each impossibility you identify, name the requirement whose relaxation you judge cheapest in our setting and say what that relaxation costs us in terms a board member could understand. Do not soften this section: if the impossibilities force choices on us, say which choices, and if some requirement on my list turns out to do more work than the others in excluding standard rules, identify it.

Close with at most a page scoring three well-known rules of your choosing against the list -- I want the scoring brief and the criteria long, since the criteria are where the thinking is and I can extend the scorecard myself. One further instruction: the variable electorate from recusals is unusual enough that I want you to say explicitly whether it threatens anything like independence of irrelevant alternatives in spirit, and whether that threat is a genuine problem for us or only looks like one. Throughout, argue from the setup I have described; do not give me a survey of named theorems or a reading list.
