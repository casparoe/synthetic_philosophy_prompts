Take the era of atmospheric nuclear weapons testing — roughly 1945 to 1963, when the US, the USSR and later Britain set off hundreds of bombs above ground and scattered fallout across the entire planet before the Partial Test Ban Treaty pushed testing underground — and analyse it through the ethics of imposing risk on people without their consent. I know the physics and the Cold War politics well enough; what I want is the moral anatomy, done properly.

Start with the mapping, because I don't think the exposed groups are morally interchangeable: the soldiers ordered to stand near ground zero as observers, the Marshall Islanders caught in the Castle Bravo fallout, the downwind communities in Utah and Nevada, and then literally everyone else on Earth, who got a trace dose of strontium-90 in their bones and their kids' milk without anyone ever asking. Who is imposing what on whom in each case, and do the cases need different treatment?

Then the questions I actually want pushed on:

- Does the per-person size of the risk matter? For most of the world's population the added cancer risk was miniscule, but multiplied over billions of people it presumably adds up to a lot of deaths. Is imposing a one-in-a-million risk on a billion people morally equivalent to just killing a thousand people, or is it doing almost nothing to anyone? I genuinely can't tell which view I hold and I'd like the strongest version of each.
- The justification offered was deterrence: the risk was imposed in order to reduce a different risk (nuclear war). Does risk-for-risk reasoning even make sense when the people bearing the imposed risk are not the people whose risk is being reduced — foreigners, neutral countries, future generations?
- Nobody consented, and most people weren't even told. Does the secrecy make it worse, or is it just an aggravating detail on top of an already settled wrong? Counterfactual: if the US goverment had held a referendum on testing and won, would the fallout landing on Canadians and Swedes and Japanese have been fine?
- Where does the theory run out? Scientists at the time publicly and sincerely disagreed about how dangerous low-dose fallout was (think of the Pauling–Teller dispute), so the imposers arguably didn't know what risk they were imposing. What do standard views on risk imposition say about imposing an *unknown* risk — is that better, worse, or a different category of thing?
- Finally, the US eventually set up a compensation scheme for downwinders and test-site workers, decades late. Can ex post compensation retroactively license an ex ante imposition, or does that confuse two different moral questions? And stepping back: is this episode a straightforward confirmation that consent matters in risk imposition, or does it actually show that consent-based theories can't cope with diffuse global risks where "just ask everyone" was never on the table to begin with?

Feel free to tell me where the standard frameworks (contractualist, utilitarian, rights-based, whatever you think applies) go silent rather than forcing them to pronounce.
