I'm preparing a graduate seminar on democratic theory and have run into a wall I want help thinking through. Everyone in the literature agrees that democracy means rule by the people, but the prior question — who counts as the people in the first place, and who gets to decide that — seems to me to get far less traction than debates about what the people should do once constituted. I know the standard answers by their labels: the all-affected-interests principle, the all-subjected-to-coercion principle, various nationality- and residence-based views. What I don't know is what my own intuitions amount to, because when I poll my gut on cases I get a pattern I can't yet state as a principle, and I suspect it may not be statable as one.

Here are my verdicts, using a concrete setup: imagine a stable, mid-sized state of about ten million citizens. (1) Foreign nationals who have lived there for fifteen years, work, pay taxes, raise their children in its schools, and are fully subject to its laws should, I think, have the vote in national elections. (2) Citizens who emigrated twenty years ago, never return, and pay no taxes there should not retain the vote, even though the state's foreign policy still affects them. (3) The state's own children, who are affected by its decisions more than anyone, should not have the vote, and I feel no great anxiety about that. (4) Residents of the neighboring state whose air and water the first state pollutes should not get a vote in its elections, not even a partial say. (5) Tourists and business visitors, who are subject to its criminal law and can be jailed while present, obviously get no vote. (6) If a region of the state seeks to secede, only the region's residents should vote in the referendum, even though the rest of the country is plainly affected by the outcome. And (7), the case I keep flip-flopping on: a resident on a renewable one-year work visa, three years in, no guaranteed path to citizenship, subject to all the same laws as the fifteen-year residents — my gut says no vote yet, but I cannot say why three years differs in kind from fifteen.

My questions: Is there a single principle of inclusion that delivers verdicts (1) through (6), or close to it? If yes, state it precisely enough that I could apply it mechanically, and tell me which clause does the work in each case — in particular, whatever lets me keep (1) while excluding (4) and (5), since long-term residents, polluted foreigners, and tourists are all affected and all subjected in some sense, and the difference must come out of the principle rather than by fiat. If no single principle works, tell me which of my verdicts is the cheapest to abandon and why. Second, does (6) expose a regress that no inclusion principle can solve — since deciding who votes on secession seems to require already having answered the boundary question — and if so, is that a defect in my principle or a defect in the question? Third, apply whatever principle you land on to case (7) and to one further case of your choosing (citizens abroad who remain liable for home-country taxation would be a natural candidate, but pick what you like), and tell me honestly whether the principle's verdict matches my gut or overrides it.

Format: four sections, in this order. "The pattern" — roughly 150 words on what, if anything, my verdicts have in common. "Candidate principles" — two or three precisely stated candidates, roughly 250 words, noting the case that motivates each clause. "The best fit and its costs" — roughly 250 words: your pick, which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and what dropping that verdict costs. "The regress and the hard cases" — roughly 200 words on the secession vote, then your verdicts on case (7) and your additional case.
