After forty years as an experimental solid-state physicist, I retired three years ago and promptly fell into philosophy of mind — initially through Dennett's popular books, which a friend in my hiking group pressed on me, and then, to my surprise, into the primary literature. I have arrived at a position I want to defend in public for the first time: illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, the view associated with Frankish and, in a looser way, with Dennett, holding that phenomenal properties as standardly conceived — intrinsic, ineffable, private qualities of experience — do not exist, and that introspection systematically misrepresents our brain states as possessing them. My own version adds two wrinkles, no doubt reflecting my training. First, I hold that the intuition that phenomenality is directly and indubitably presented to us is itself a representational artifact, analogous to the way a well-engineered instrument presents its readouts as transparent reports of the measured quantity while concealing the layers of processing that produced them. Second, I argue that illusionism should be judged by the ordinary standards of scientific theory choice — parsimony, integration with the rest of our picture of the world — rather than by whether it honors the pre-theoretical datum that there is something it is like to be me, since that seeming is precisely what the theory purports to explain rather than a finding it must accommodate.

The occasion is the visiting-speaker slot in the philosophy colloquium at the university where I spent my career; the chair is an old friend who invited me out of a mixture of loyalty and curiosity. The audience, roughly twenty-five people, will comprise: two faculty members who have published sympathetically on Russellian monism and property dualism; a contingent of graduate students raised on Nagel, Chalmers, and Strawson; one cognitive scientist who defends a global-workspace account and regards the metaphysics as a distraction from the empirical work; and three or four of my former physics colleagues, kindly disposed but philosophically innocent. So far as I know, not one person in the room is an illusionist, and I suspect several regard the view as self-refuting on its face.

Prepare me for the question period. What I want is a prep sheet, not a defense: the eight to ten objections this particular audience will actually raise — not the ones an internet forum would raise — ordered by how much damage each would do if left unanswered. Each objection should be phrased in the vocabulary of the person likely to raise it (the property dualist's challenge will not sound like the cognitive scientist's, and neither will sound like the graduate student who has just read Strawson on the appearance-reality distinction), and each should come with a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, a candid note on where that reply is weakest, and guidance on what I should simply concede up front rather than fight over. I especially want the objection I am hoping nobody asks — my own suspicion is that it concerns pain, since I find it far easier to be an illusionist about the apparent redness of red than about agony, but I would like your independent judgment on where my position is most exposed. End with the single reply that has to land if the talk is to survive, and close with a final line of the form "Bottom line: ..." containing one sentence I can memorize and fall back on if the questioning turns hostile.
