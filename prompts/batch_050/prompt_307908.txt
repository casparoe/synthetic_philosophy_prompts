I'm a software engineer at a mid-sized company, and I've ended up on our internal review panel that evaluates engineering proposals -- mostly decisions about which projects to fund, whether to keep results internal or open-source them, and occasionally whether something is worth the cost of a patent filing. I have no philosophy background, but this came up after an awkward incident last quarter: two of our teams, who hadn't spoken to each other, independently built nearly the same internal migration tool, down to some of the same design choices. A friend pointed out that this happens at the level of whole fields too -- calculus was developed independently by Newton and Leibniz, and natural selection by Darwin and Wallace -- and argued that for most inventions, if the named inventor had never lived, someone else would have produced essentially the same thing within a few years, because the pieces were all lying around and the problem was "in the air." I'm not sure that's always true, but I realized a defensible version of that claim is exactly what our panel needs: a way to estimate, for a given proposal, how likely it is that someone outside the company re-derives the same thing soon regardless of what we do.

Please turn that idea into a reusable procedure our panel could actually run in a thirty-minute review, for someone with no background in history or philosophy of science. I want an ordered list of diagnostic questions -- I'm imagining things like: are all the conceptual and technical prerequisites already publicly available, is the problem one that many capable teams are actively being pushed toward by market or research pressures, and how many actors have both the means and the motive -- with guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer to each, what to do when we genuinely can't assess one of the inputs, and how the answers combine into an overall verdict (even a crude low/medium/high "independent re-derivation risk" is fine). Crucially, the procedure should end in concrete recommendations: at what risk level does secrecy stop being worth its costs, when is racing to publish better than filing, and does high inevitability change how much individual credit a lead engineer deserves for a design, or only how the company should behave strategically? Mark clearly which steps are genuine judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where the panel should stop and escalate because the question is really one of values rather than estimation -- I suspect the credit question goes there, but I'd like that argued rather than assumed.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical proposals: (1) a caching layer that is an obvious-in-hindsight combination of two techniques described in widely read papers from the last two years; (2) a novel consensus mechanism resting on a non-obvious formal insight that one of our researchers had, where the prerequisites exist but nobody seems to have connected them; and (3) an internal developer-productivity tool with no direct market analog but heavy overlap with features appearing across the industry. Include at least one case where the procedure delivers a verdict the proposing team will hate, and say what that team could legitimately push back on. Finally, tell me what this procedure structurally cannot answer -- for instance, whether it can distinguish an invention that was inevitable from one that merely looks inevitable in retrospect, and what we should do about that failure mode.
