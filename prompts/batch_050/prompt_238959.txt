So I'm a retired evolutionary geneticist and about two years ago I joined a philosophy reading group at the local library, mostly retired engineers and a couple of ex-clergy, and we've just spent six weeks on population ethics. The thing that keeps tripping us up is the so-called procreation asymmetry: the intuition that you have a strong reason not to bring into existence a child whose life would be full of suffering, but no comparible reason to bring into existence a child just because her life would be wonderful. Some of us find that obvious, some of us find it deeply suspicious, and our discussions keep going in circles because we argue about labels ("are you an antinatalist??") instead of about the actual commitments.

I'd like a decision tree I can hand out at our next meeting that sorts people into positions on this question by what they actually think, not what they call themselves. Build it from the logical space if you can: start with the basic yes/no on whether the asymmetry holds, then the branch points should each be a concrete question or case on which the positions genuinely come apart. Cases I'd like used: the couple who know any child they conceive now will have a painful degenerative condition but could wait three months and conceive a healthy child instead (the non-identity wrinkle here matters -- the condition doesn't make the child's life not worth living), the question of whether the not-yet-conceived happy child is worse off if never created, and whether "no one is wronged" settles whether there is a reason. Leaves should include at minimum the full-symmetry impersonal view (the happy unborn are a missed good), the person-affecting view that tries to explain the asymmetry, Benatar's axiological asymmetry with its antinatalist conclusion, and a permissive view that denies any duty either way. Each leaf gets a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment, and if some combination of answers lands nowhere occupied, say so and say briefly whether that's because the spot is incoherent or just uninhabited.

Two more asks. First, the tree must handle the person who says "it depends whether the suffering life is worth living or not" -- that answer has to route somewhere rather than break the tree. Second, flag any pair of positions your tree can't separate, rather than fudging a distinguishing question. Roughly six to nine branch questions total, phrased so a smart non-philosopher can answer them without knowing the literature, because half my group refuses to read anything longer than a page.
