I am a diagnostic radiologist, twelve years into practice, and I chair my department's peer-review committee. Four months ago one of my own cases came back through our QA process: I had missed a small lung nodule on a trauma CT, visible in retrospect, in a young patient with no oncologic history. The consensus at conference was that I had, in my colleague's phrase, seen what I expected to see -- that the clinical context had shaped my perception of the image itself, not merely the report I dictated. Our committee is now split over a concrete policy question: whether readers should receive the clinical history before a first pass or be blinded to it. Half the committee speaks as though expectation literally changes what a reader sees; the other half insists that seeing is seeing, and only judgment moves. I gather this is, roughly, a live dispute in the philosophy of perception under the heading of cognitive penetrability, and I would like it translated into the vocabulary I actually think in.

That vocabulary is signal detection theory and Bayesian decision theory. I am fluent: sensitivity and response criterion, ROC curves, priors and likelihoods, precision weighting, perceptual learning understood as recalibration. We use this machinery in our reader studies. Please do not re-explain it to me.

Here is what I want. First, restate the main positions in my terms, as precisely as you can: Pylyshyn's thesis that early vision is cognitively impenetrable; the opposing claim -- with roots in the New Look psychology -- that beliefs, desires, or emotional states can alter perceptual content itself; and the methodological critique associated with Firestone and Scholl, that most apparent effects are shifts in judgment, attention, or task demands rather than in perception. Where a translation requires choosing between readings, say so rather than choosing silently. Second, the residue report: which claims in this debate translate cleanly (I assume criterion shifts are the easy case), which can only be rendered with distortion, and which have no expression in SDT at all. I suspect the untranslatable remainder includes phenomenal character -- the difference between a nodule-looking patch and a patch judged likely to be a nodule -- and the epistemological worry about whether perception can justify the very beliefs that shaped it. Tell me whether I am right about that. Third, a verdict: does the residue mark a genuine limitation of my framework, or does it show that part of the philosophical dispute concerns something no empirical vocabulary could settle?

One constraint: do not smuggle a philosophical conclusion in by selecting a translation that presupposes it. And finally, the practical upshot -- does anything in the untranslatable residue bear on our blinding policy, or is that decision entirely a matter of priors, base rates, and error costs?
