**The Witness in the Bed: Advance Directives as Testimony, Not Command**

When a patient with advanced dementia appears content in conditions that her earlier, competent self judged intolerable — and signed an advance directive to avoid — the standard debate asks whose will should govern. One influential position, associated above all with Ronald Dworkin, holds that the directive should prevail: what matters is the patient's precedent autonomy and her critical interests in the shape of her life as a whole, and the earlier self is the only self ever competent to articulate those interests. Critics, most prominently Rebecca Dresser, reply that the person now in the bed has experiential interests — in comfort, company, small pleasures — that are the interests actually at stake, and that a competent person's authority over her future does not obviously extend to a future occupant of her body who may be barely continuous with her. Both sides speak the language of jurisdiction: either the earlier self retains authority over the future, or that authority has lapsed.

This essay argues that both sides misconstrue what an advance directive is. They treat it as a command — legislation issued by one time-slice of a person, either binding or not on later ones. But a directive is better understood as testimony. The earlier self is a witness, and an unusually well-placed one: she alone observed this patient's values from the inside, over a lifetime, with full competence. Her directive is a deposition about what mattered to the person, delivered in anticipation of a time when the person could no longer testify. Testimony is not legislation. It carries weight in proportion to the witness's competence and opportunity to know, and it can be defeated by counterevidence.

The witness model explains features of our practice that the command model must treat as anomalies. First, vagueness and distance: we instinctively discount a directive written decades earlier, by someone with no experience of the condition it addresses, just as we discount testimony about an event the witness did not observe but only imagined. A directive written after nursing a parent through dementia is probative in a way a checkbox on a hospital form is not — the difference between an eyewitness deposition and a questionnaire. Second, corroboration: we consult the family about what she would have wanted, and the family are not appealing to authority but offering corroborating testimony. Third, defeasibility: even partisans of precedent autonomy flinch at enforcing a stale directive against a manifestly content patient, and the witness model says why — the later self's contentment is not rebellion against authority but counterevidence in the record.

The model also yields a presumption, and here I part ways with the critics. A witness's retraction is itself evidence, but its weight depends on the conditions under which it is given. The content patient in the bed is, by hypothesis, unable to weigh the very considerations — dignity, narrative, the arc of a life — about which the earlier self testified. Her contentment is genuine, and it matters; but it is the testimony of a witness competent about pleasures and incompetent about the disputed question. The presumption therefore favors the deposition: the earlier testimony stands unless the patient's present good is of a kind that the earlier self's own values could have accommodated — unless the contentment is one the earlier self would recognize as good could she survey it. This is not deference to the past for its own sake. It is the ordinary epistemic practice of preferring testimony gathered under conditions of competence to testimony gathered under conditions of impairment.

If this is right, the real authority in the sickroom belongs to neither self. It belongs to the interpreters — family and clinicians — who sit as a jury does, weighing a deposition against the demeanor of a witness who can no longer say what the case is about. Advance directives do not settle the future; they give the future something to answer to. The hard question is not whose will prevails but who is competent to read the record — and on that question both camps have been silent, because both were busy arguing about jurisdiction.

1. **Fairness of the setup.** Does the opening paragraph characterize the two standard positions accurately, or does it caricature either one to make the essay's own proposal look better? In particular, does the essay anywhere conflate distinctions it should respect — for instance, between experiential and critical interests, or between lacking decision-making capacity altogether and lacking it for one specific kind of decision?

2. **The weakest links.** Identify the two or three points in the draft where the reasoning is most exposed: premises asserted rather than defended, or analogies asked to bear more weight than they can carry. For each, state the strongest objection that a defender of each standard camp would press against the witness model. I am especially interested in whether the analogy survives the obvious disanalogy that testimony ordinarily reports facts while a directive expresses values and preferences.

3. **Verdict on the central move.** Does recasting directives as testimony genuinely escape the command model's dilemma, or does the "presumption in favor of the deposition" in the fourth paragraph simply restate precedent autonomy in evidentiary vocabulary? In answering, address two things: whether the witness model covertly requires an account of personal identity over time (testimony is normally testimony about someone else, which seems either to concede the critics' point or to contradict it), and whether the proposed accommodation test — present good that the earlier self's values "could have accommodated" — is workable or merely vacuous.

4. **Craft and interest.** Which paragraph does the most work, and which could be cut without loss? Is the closing image of family and clinicians as a jury earned, or is it a rhetorical flourish that introduces a problem the essay cannot address in its remaining space? Finally, is the thesis interesting enough to be worth developing, and if not, what is the smallest change to the argument that would make it so?
