I'm revising the final exam for my graduate seminar on contractualism and I want to include an argument I've been kicking around that I now think proves too much, but I can't pin down where. Here it is:

(1) On Scanlon's view, an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by some principle that no one could reasonably reject.

(2) Reasonable rejectability is assessed by comparing personal complaints one at a time -- how badly the principle burdens each affected individual, not how many individuals are burdened.

(3) When the strongest complaint on each side is the same in kind and severity, neither side has grounds to reasonably reject a principle that saves the greater number, so numbers get to break exact ties. (Scanlon himself seems to conceed this much.)

(4) But exact ties never actually happen outside textbooks. What happens is near-ties: death on one side, death-minus-one-minute on the other, or death versus a coma from which you never wake. If numbers may break exact ties, then by continuity they may break near-ties, since the difference in individual complaint can be made arbitrarily small.

(5) Iterating (4), numbers count whenever the gap between the strongest complaints on each side is finite -- which is always.

(6) So a great number of small complaints can together outweigh one person's very large complaint, i.e. the view collapses into plain aggregation.

(7) But plain aggregation yields the usual horrors (billions of headaches against one death) -- precisely the results the restriction in (2) was built to block. And if instead we block (4) by refusing tie-breaks altogether, we're stuck saving one person from death over a million from equally bad deaths, which is bonkers.

Which step do I cut? If forced, I'd rather keep (2) than deny (3), but tell me what each option costs -- and why (4) looks so innocent when I suspect it's the culprit.
