I'm TAing a grad seminar in normative ethics and next week is the moral uncertainty session. I want a one-two page handout that's basically a decision tree: it should walk a student from the starting commitment "I genuinely don't know which first-order moral theory is right, and I still have to act today" down to a named position on what, if anything, follows about how to decide.

Act as a philosopher who works on exactly this stuff (the intertheoretic comparability mess, metanormative questions, all of it). Before you write the tree itself, do a few paragraphs of honest brainstorming about the logical space: what are the real choice points, which combinations of answers are occupied, which are empty, and is each empty slot empty because it's incoherent, unstable under reflection, or just historically unexplored? Then give the tree.

Constraints, because my students will otherwise just pattern-match to whatever paper they skimmed:

1. 4 to 6 branch nodes. Every node has to be phrased as a concrete case or a plain question someone can answer without knowing which position it funnels them toward -- so not "do you accept cardinal intertheoretic comparability of choiceworthiness?" but a scenario where their gut verdict implies it. Build nodes around at least three of the following (close variants fine):
   - you're 50/50 between two theories: one says option A is mildly better than B, the other says A is a catastrophe and B is mandatory (this is the comparability/hedging pressure point)
   - you have a small credence, say 2%, in a very demanding theory under which some everyday convenience of yours is monstrous -- does the tiny credence swamp the decision?
   - you could spend the next year (or some chunk of money) investigating which theory is right before acting -- is that ever the *morally* right use of resources, and what has to be true for it to be?
   - two people with different moral credences realize they can each get more of what their favored theory wants by cooperating or trading rather than each pursuing their own top pick
   - the meta move: is "what should I do given that I'm morally uncertain?" even a question over and above "what should I do, all things considered?" -- or does moral uncertainty just collapse back into ordinary first-order normativity?

2. Leaves are named positions: maximize expected choiceworthiness, my-favorite-theory, parliamentary/voting-style approaches, hedging/robustness-first views, plus anything else that earns a leaf -- and if "this isn't a seperate problem, moral uncertainty dissolves" ends up as a leaf, good, I suspect it belongs there. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment (the bullet it has to bite).

3. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves your tree can't pull apart. For instance I've had students complain that hedging and expected-choiceworthiness-maximization coincide in almost every practical case -- if your tree can't seperate them, say so and say what extra question would.

4. One line per unoccupied answer-combination from your brainstorm, diagnosing why nobody sits there.

Keep the tree itself tight (it has to fit the handout) but let the brainstorm ramble a bit. And think carefully about step 1 -- the cases are the whole game; if a student's answer to a node doesn't actually commit them to anything, the tree is decorative.
