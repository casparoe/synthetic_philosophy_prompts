The examination question: In Book V of the Consolation of Philosophy, Boethius confronts an apparent conflict between divine foreknowledge and human freedom. Set out the problem as he understands it, explain how his account of divine eternity is meant to dissolve it, and briefly assess whether the solution succeeds.

The student's answer:

Boethius raises the problem from the prisoner's perspective. If God knows in advance everything that will happen, and divine knowledge cannot be mistaken, then what God knows must come about; and if it must come about, then human beings do not act freely, so that praise and blame, reward and punishment, and even prayer lose their point. There is also a difficulty in the other direction: if future events are genuinely contingent, then it seems God's beliefs about them could turn out to be false, which is impious. Either God does not know the future, or the future is necessary.

Boethius first denies that foreknowledge causes what it knows. Knowing something is not what makes it happen, any more than my seeing a man walk is what makes him walk; the source of the walking is the man's own will, and my knowledge merely follows it. More generally, Boethius holds that a thing is known not according to its own mode but according to the mode and capacity of the knower, so we cannot simply assume that God knows things in the temporal way we do.

The decisive move is the account of divine eternity. Eternity, Boethius says, is not merely existence without beginning or end; it is the complete and simultaneous possession, all at once, of a life that has no end, in which nothing has passed away and nothing is still to come. God therefore does not foreknow anything, strictly speaking: knowledge in advance presupposes a knower who waits upon the future, and God waits upon nothing. His eternal life runs alongside the whole of time, so that every event — past, present, or future to us — is equally present to him, and he beholds all of history in a single, unbroken now.

From this Boethius derives his solution. What is future to us is present to God, and present things, he observes, are necessary only while and insofar as they are occurring: his example is a man walking and the sun rising, both seen in one and the same vision, the one happening voluntarily, the other by necessity. He accordingly distinguishes simple necessity, which a thing has from its own nature, from conditional necessity: granted that God knows something will happen, it will happen, but it remains in its own nature contingent. God's infallible knowledge thus entails that what he knows necessarily comes to pass; yet because this necessity holds only on the condition of his knowing it, and not from the nature of the thing known, Boethius takes human freedom — and with it praise, blame, and the justice of rewards and punishments — to be secure.

Whether the solution succeeds seems to me to depend on whether timeless eternity is a coherent notion at all. If it is, then the original puzzle no longer arises, since God's knowledge was never really in advance of anything. The account leaves hard questions open — how a timeless God can act at particular moments in history, or hear prayers offered in time — but as a resolution of the foreknowledge problem specifically, I judge it successful on its own terms.
