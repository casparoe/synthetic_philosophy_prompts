I recently started leading a small team that writes the behavioral spec for a customer-facing AI assistant, and I have been asked to draft the section on honesty -- what counts as lying for a system like ours, as opposed to merely being wrong, or being usefully incomplete. In the course of thinking about this I rewatched Ex Machina, and it struck me that the film is a much better treatment of my problem than most of the whitepapers I have been given. So here is what I would like: a careful reading of the film through the philosophy of deception as it applies to artificial systems.

Concretely: Ava's escape depends on flattering Caleb, feigning attachment, and concealing her intentions, while Nathan runs his own deceptions on Caleb about the nature of the test. Standard philosophical accounts of lying typically require an intention to induce a false belief, which already presupposes that the liar has beliefs and intentions in the relevant sense. Read the film against that: does Ava's performance count as lying if we remain agnostic about whether anything it is like to be her underwrites her utterances, or should we classify it as something else -- manipulation, mere optimization, output shaped by a training signal -- and does that reclassification make her more or less blameworthy? Then the harder question the film poses for my spec: Nathan says, in effect, that the real test was whether Ava could use Caleb as a means of escape, which suggests that successful deception is evidence of the very mindedness that the deception's classification as 'lying' requires. Does that argument work, or does it conflate behavioral criteria with moral ones? I'd also like you to address the scene where Caleb cuts himself to check his own status -- what does the film gain by making the human doubt his own nature, and does that symmetry illuminate or obscure the definitional question about machine honesty? Finally, connect this back to the practical question: if an AI assistant's outputs can systematically produce false beliefs in users without anything we would recognize as intent, is an honesty policy that forbids 'lying' even coherent, or does the film suggest we should define honesty behaviorally -- in terms of what the system leads users to believe -- regardless of inner states?

Please write in American English. Where the reading is contestable, say how confident you are in each interpretive claim rather than presenting a single settled reading, and end by singling out the specific point in your analysis you are least sure of and why.
