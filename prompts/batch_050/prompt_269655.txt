My roommate and I run a small science-fiction reading group, and we have a standing game: whenever a story involves time travel, she rules on whether the depicted scenario is coherent — whether it could actually happen, assuming time travel is possible at all — or whether it is incoherent. Her verdicts so far: she ruled incoherent a story in which a traveler goes back and kills his own grandfather before the traveler's parent is conceived; she ruled coherent a story in which a traveler journeys back to prevent a famous library fire and accidentally starts it, turning out to have been its cause all along; she ruled coherent a story in which a traveler to 1900 scratches her initials inside a sealed wall and, on returning to the present, finds the scratches have always been there; and she ruled incoherent a story in which a traveler goes back and sabotages the time machine shortly before his own departure, so that the trip that brought him there never occurs.

Last week we read a story that has split us, and she has not yet delivered her ruling. In it, a historian travels back a century and, for petty personal reasons, secretly moves a buried cache of letters from one location to another before its discovery. In the history the traveler remembers, the letters were unearthed at the first location and a particular scholar built her career on them; after the trip, the letters were always at the second location, every record and every living memory reflects this, and only the traveler recalls the original version of events. No one's existence is erased, no journey is undone, and the final timeline is perfectly self-consistent — only the traveler's own memory is out of step with it. I want to predict her verdict before she gives it, and to understand what principle she has actually been applying all along.

Please respond with exactly five numbered items of one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) your prediction of her verdict on the letters case, stated definitely; (2) the rule her four earlier verdicts suggest she is running; (3) at least one rival rule that fits those same four verdicts equally well, together with what it predicts about the letters case instead; (4) your reasons for committing to one of these rules as the better reconstruction of her track record; and (5) a further hypothetical time-travel case, different from all five discussed so far, whose verdict would best distinguish the rule you chose from the rival.
