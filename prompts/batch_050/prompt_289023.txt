I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course in aesthetics, and I am currently grading short-essay responses to the following exam question: "Some philosophers maintain that one cannot come to know that a work of art is beautiful, graceful, or moving purely on the basis of testimony, no matter how reliable the testifier may be; one must experience the work oneself. Explain this view, sometimes called pessimism about aesthetic testimony, and the considerations that motivate it. Is pessimism here simply a local instance of a general distrust of testimony, or does it rest on something distinctive about the aesthetic? Defend an answer."

The grading rubric awards credit for (i) an accurate statement of the pessimist's position, including its characteristic motivation; (ii) a clear treatment of the role first-hand acquaintance is supposed to play; and (iii) a philosophically serious engagement with the final evaluative question, where that means considering the strongest available version of the view rather than only its crudest form. Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

"Pessimism about aesthetic testimony is the view that testimony can never by itself yield aesthetic knowledge: if a reliable friend tells me that a sonata she heard last night is delicate and haunting, I may acquire a true belief, but I do not know that the sonata is delicate and haunting until I listen to it myself. The standard motivation is the acquaintance principle, according to which aesthetic judgements must be based on first-hand experience of the object judged; one cannot properly judge a novel one has not read or a painting one has not seen. Because testimony is second-hand, beliefs formed on its basis violate this principle and are therefore unjustified, however trustworthy the source.

The position is often bolstered by the observation that aesthetic properties are response-dependent: whether something is graceful or garish is a matter of how it strikes suitable observers, not a matter of mind-independent fact. Pessimists infer from this that testimony about aesthetic properties is unreliable in a way that testimony about, say, tomorrow's weather is not, and this is why we are hesitant to accept aesthetic verdicts on trust.

As to whether pessimism is a special case of a general skepticism about testimony, I think it clearly is not. A general skepticism would impugn everything we learn from others — history, geography, science — and that is untenable. The pessimist's claim is narrower: something about aesthetic matters in particular resists transmission by word of mouth. Nevertheless, I believe pessimism fails. We plainly do acquire aesthetic knowledge from testimony. A knowledgeable guide can tell me that a particular painting is a masterpiece, and if the guide is reliable and I have no reason for doubt, then I know that it is a masterpiece. Since testimony does transmit aesthetic knowledge in such cases, pessimism must be rejected."
