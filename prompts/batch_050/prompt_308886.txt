I serve as a community member on the ethics advisory board of a regional hospital, and this month we've been arguing about how to allocate a one-time grant between two programs: one that would fund a handful of intensive spinal-cord rehabilitations, and one that would fund a very large number of minor but chronic pain treatments. The board is deadlocked, and at last week's meeting two of the members, Daniela and Marcus, had an exchange that I've been turning over ever since. Daniela said it's obvious that preventing one case of paraplegia takes priority over preventing even ten thousand cases of mild recurring wrist pain; some harms are simply in a different league, and no number of trivial ones adds up to a serious one. Marcus answered with a chain: surely one paraplegia matters more than two cases of a condition just slightly less severe than paraplegia; and those two matter more than four cases of a condition slightly less severe than that; and so on, each step trading a barely perceptible loss in severity for a doubling in number, until you arrive at an enormous number of trivial wrist pains. Each individual step, he said, is one Daniela must accept -- the severity difference at each step is below anything anyone could notice or care about, and the numbers double -- so by transitivity she's committed to the endpoint she calls absurd. Since she won't accept the endpoint, he concluded, her original intuition has to go: enough small harms can outweigh one catastrophic one, and the board should fund the pain program.

Daniela pushed back on two fronts. First, she said, the argument shows at most that something in the chain gives, and her candidate is transitivity itself: "worse than" might behave like "indistinguishable," where each step is fine but the chain as a whole isn't, because each comparison is made on different features and there's no single scale running end to end. Marcus replied that this is a reductio, not a resolution: a betterness relation that cycles would let a clever agent be pumped for money indefinitely, trading up in circles forever, and any relation that's supposed to guide actual choices by actual institutions has to be transitive on pain of incoherence. Daniela's second move was to concede the transitivity of "worse than" but deny that every step is a genuine improvement -- adjacent cases, she suggested, may be "on a par" rather than one strictly worse than the other, and parity, unlike betterness, is well known to be non-transitive, which dissolves the chain without touching rational choice. Marcus said this misdescribes his steps: he never claimed adjacent cases are on a par; he claimed each is strictly better, and if she wants to deny that she owes us an account of which step fails, because "I can't say which, but one of them must" is not an answer. She said demanding she locate the exact failing step is like demanding she name the grain whose removal turns a heap into a non-heap; he said that's exactly the move that lets people keep contradictory intuitions and call it wisdom.

The board chair has asked me, as the member with some philosophy background, to write up a neutral analysis before our next meeting, and I'd like help doing it properly. Please go through the exchange step by step and say who is right about what: whether Marcus's chain really forces the conclusion he wants, whether Daniela's intransitivity suggestion is coherent or just a way of dodging, whether the money-pump reply actually lands against it, whether her parity move successfully redescribes the steps or merely renames the problem, and whether her heap analogy is a fair defense or an evasion. I'd also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing -- whether there's an option neither articulated -- and on balance which side the board should find more persuasive, and why.
