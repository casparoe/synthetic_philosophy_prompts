I've been reading, on my own and without any training in philosophy, Lewis Carroll's short dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (1895), and I keep getting stuck on its final exchange. For context: the Tortoise has accepted propositions A and B, and then a further statement C ("If A and B are true, Z must be true"), but still declines to accept the conclusion Z until each new hypothetical has been written down in Achilles' note-book as an additional premise. The passage then runs:

"And why must I?"

"Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"

"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"

"You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."

"Very good. I'm quite willing to grant it, as soon as you've written it down. We will call it (D) If A and B and C are true, then Z must be true. Have you entered that in your note-book?"

"I have!" Achilles joyfully exclaimed, as he ran the pencil into its sheath. "And at last we've got to the end of this ideal race-course! Now that you accept A and B and C and D, of course you accept Z."

"Do I?" said the Tortoise innocently. "Let's make that quite clear. I accept A and B and C and D. Suppose I still refused to accept Z?"

"Then Logic would take you by the throat, and force you to do it!" Achilles triumphantly replied.

"Whatever Logic is good enough to tell me is worth writing down," said the Tortoise. "So enter it in your book, please. We will call it (E) If A and B and C and D are true, Z must be true. Until I've granted that, of course I needn't grant Z."

Here is what I cannot settle for myself. On one reading, the Tortoise is simply making a mistake: he treats a rule of inference as if it were just one more premise, and once that confusion is cleared up, the regress stops and nothing about deduction is genuinely threatened. On another reading, the Tortoise has a real point: no statement, however many times it is written down, can by itself compel anyone to draw a conclusion, so the justification of deduction must ultimately rest on something that is not a proposition at all — a practice, a disposition, or simply a way we cannot help but go on. These two readings seem to me to lead to very different conclusions about whether reasoning can ever be fully justified. Please tell me which reading you consider correct and commit to it: identify exactly where the Tortoise goes wrong (or why he does not), and explain what the passage then implies about what, if anything, justifies a single step of deduction. I would rather have one definite, defended answer than a survey of the available options.
