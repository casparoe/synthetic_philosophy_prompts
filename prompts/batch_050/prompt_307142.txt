A colleague of mine draws a sharp line between tool AI and agent AI — systems you operate versus systems whose motivations you have to align — and I have been collecting her verdicts. So far: a chess engine that searches each position afresh is a tool; a self-driving car that plans its own lane changes and routes to reach destinations you set is a tool; a reinforcement-learning system that trades continuously to maximize quarterly returns, inventing strategies nobody programmed, is an agent; a chatbot answering single-turn questions is a tool; the same chatbot given persistent memory and told to keep a team's projects on track over months is an agent; and a drone that spends years deciding for itself when and where to patrol a forest so as to catch fires early is an agent. Now consider two new systems. The first is handed a single objective — run this warehouse efficiently for the next month — plans and acts freely over that entire horizon, generating whatever subgoals it sees fit, and is then wiped completely clean. The second accumulates knowledge and a detailed model of its users over years, but every interaction is a bounded, self-contained request, and it holds no objectives whatsoever between them. Predict her verdict on each system, state the rule that best explains her earlier verdicts, identify the competing rules that fit those verdicts equally well, and describe one further hypothetical system that would most cleanly discriminate among the surviving rules.
