I'm stuck on the middle section of a novel and I think what I actually need is a philosophy exercise, so bear with me. The book follows a junior minister in a mid-sized EU member state who has to decide whether her government complies with or openly defies a specific EU obligation, and the whole third act hinges on this choice feeling genuinely tragic -- meaning smart, decent readers should disagree about what she should do. I keep writing versions where its obviously rigged in one direction (the Commission is corrupt, or the national majority is a xenophobic mob) and my editor keeps throwing them back at me, rightly.

So: construct the crisis for me, as a thought experiment built to spec. Requirements: (1) The decision is binary, comply or defy, set in a concrete and realistic policy domain -- debt-rule enforcement, water standards, fisheries quotas, asylum relocation, your pick, but something a present-day government could plausibly face. (2) Someone who thinks political legitimacy is all about input, authorization by the governed along roughly Scharpfian lines, must clearly judge that defiance is right, while someone who thinks the EU earns legitimacy through output, good results delivered to the governed, must clearly judge compliance is right; and the output benefits have to land on the very citizens who voted against the thing, so the conflict can't be dissolved by spreading costs and gains across different groups. (3) Block the easy escapes. The intergovernmentalist move -- her predecessors signed the treaty, so there is no deficit here -- has to fail, because the obligation flows from a court's teleological reading of the ever-closer-union language in the treaties rather than from anything any national parliament ever voted on. And the national majority has to be right about the domestic costs, not confused or bigoted. (4) That ever-closer-union language should do real work inside the story's legal reasoning, so the case doubles as a test of whether it's an actual legal commitment or just a rhetorical flourish.

After the scenario (one paragraph please, I'll expand it into chapters myself), show me the machine working: the verdict each of the input theorist and the output theorist gives and why; what a no-demos person says about whether there even is a democratic deficit in this case; and whether a regulatory-state theorist in Majone's mold would flip their verdict if I moved the same conflict from a regulatory policy area to a redistributive one -- if so, tell me which side of that line my scenario sits on and whether it could be nudged across. Then be honest about the engineering: which single feature of the case is doing all the work, what happens to the verdicts if I delete that feature (does the whole thing collapse back into boring intergovernmentalism where everyone's a hypocrite?), and how the smartest defender of whichever side comes out looking bad would attack the setup as rigged. Both sides need to survive inspection, because my readers include, depressingly, actual Brussels policy people.
