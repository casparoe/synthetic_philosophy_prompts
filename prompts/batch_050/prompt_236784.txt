I help run a small interdisciplinary reading group at my job (mostly economists, one philosopher, one data scientist who has Opinions) and we just spent three sessions on satisficing versus maximizing and honestly went in circles the whole time. Every meeting collapsed into the same exchange: the maximizers insist that once you count the time, effort, and stress of deciding as costs, the satisficer is just a maximizer with a fancier utility function, and that for any "stop when it's good enough" rule you can cook up some preference ordering that would produce exactly those choices, so the distinction is empty. The satisficers fire back that this proves too much -- if you have to compute the optimal amount of deliberation before you're allowed to stop deliberating, you've smuggled the entire optimization problem back in at one remove, and then you need to deliberate about how much to deliberate, and so on forever, which is exactly the predicament bounded rationality was supposed to get us out of. Then the data scientist brings up the less-is-more results where simple heuristics beat optimizing models at prediction, and the maximizers say that's an interesting fact about statistics, not about rationality, and around we go.

My own hypothesis, which I'll flag as just a hypothesis, is that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: one side is asking what patterns of choice can be *represented* as rational after the fact, and the other is asking what *procedure* a finite agent can actually execute, and "you could always represent it as maximizing" does nothing to touch the second question. But maybe I'm wrong and it's a genuinely substantive dispute about what finite agents should be held to, or a boring verbal one about the word 'rational', or an empirical one about what humans actually do that neither side has the right data for. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? I'd like a diagnosis grounded in what each side actually claims, plus what would count as progress given your diagnosis -- a formal result (like a theorem about whether every stopping rule is representable as optimization), an experiment, a sharper division of labor between descriptive and normative theorizing, or something else. And if your diagnosis is that no progress should be expected because the two projects were never in competition to begin with, I'd like that argued for rather than asserted.
