I have for years held the view that the standard information-cascade result — sequential agents, each with a private signal, who rationally set aside their own information once the public history becomes sufficiently lopsided — explains most real-world conformity and collective error better than the psychological story of peer pressure and motivated reasoning, and that this carries a bleak implication: individual debiasing of the sort rationalist communities practise cannot fix the problem, because the failure is structural rather than psychological. What would count against this view? I want the observations and arguments that should genuinely move me, ordered by how decisive they would be: which empirical findings about conformity (when you vary signal quality, observability, or stakes) would embarrass the cascade explanation; whether the standard lab paradigms can bear the weight I put on them; what the position predicts that its psychological rivals do not; and whether defenders have hedged it — via fragile cascades, heterogeneous agent types, network structure — into something no observation could refute. If the strong version has quietly collapsed into the claim that people sometimes follow others, for various reasons, I would like that said plainly. Please separate what is settled among people who know this literature from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Please write in British English.
