Next month I am presenting a paper at the colloquium series of a philosophy department whose strengths are philosophy of mind and moral psychology. Most of the room will be analytic philosophers who work on self-deception, motivated reasoning, and the epistemology of belief; the department also has two continental specialists, one of whom has published extensively on Sartre and will certainly attend. My paper argues that the self-deception literature has misfiled Sartre's concept of bad faith. The standard treatment assimilates bad faith to a psychological mechanism for sustaining a belief against counterevidence and then dismisses it as prey to the familiar paradoxes of self-deception — the static paradox, that the self-deceiver would have to believe both p and not-p at once, and the dynamic paradox, that an intention to deceive oneself would give the game away to the one being deceived. My thesis is that both paradoxes miss their target, because bad faith is not a device for protecting a belief's content but a project of evading freedom: the attempt to take oneself as a fixed kind of thing. I work through Sartre's two famous cases — the café waiter who is playing at being a waiter, and the woman on the first date who leaves her hand in her companion's hand while refusing to notice the decision the gesture demands — to argue that bad faith concerns one's mode of being rather than the propositions one holds, and that its characteristic state is not contradictory belief but a disciplined inattention to what one pre-reflectively knows. I further argue that bad faith, so understood, is not an exotic pathology but the default human stance toward one's own agency, and that this reading actually predicts the deflationary pattern found in the empirical literature: self-deception almost always shows up as biased evidence-gathering, selective attention, and strategic ambiguity rather than as outright belief in what one knows to be false.
