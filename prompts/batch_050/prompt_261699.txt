I'm in Year 12 and my school's philosophy society has spent the whole term arguing about lifeboat ethics. Two members — call them A and B — have been at it every week. A defends a broadly Hardin-style position: rich countries are like well-provisioned lifeboats, taking on unlimited passengers (through aid or open immigration) swamps the boat, and hard-heartedness now prevents catastrophe later, so the right policy is to hold the line even when it feels cruel. B thinks this is a catastrophic misreading of the world: global wealth isn't a fixed stock of rations, the "boat" metaphor smuggles in the very assumptions it's meant to prove, and the well-off have enforceable duties to the badly-off that don't evaporate because helping might one day go badly. Neither has budged. Our teacher is fed up with the circularity and has set them a joint task, and I've volunteered to draft it for them because I'm on the fence.

Please write the adversarial-collaboration document they should be producing, in the first person plural, as though co-authored by both. Specifically:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of propositions both camps can genuinely sign — for instance, claims about what scarcity does and doesn't imply, about the limits of metaphors in ethics, about incentives mattering to policy even for those with bleeding hearts, and about some real cases where well-intentioned aid made things worse. These must be substantive, not empty peace statements ("we both care about people" doesn't count).

2. A disagreement table. Each genuine remaining disagreement stated in neutral wording, followed by A's position in words A would accept and B's position in words B would accept. I suspect the real fault lines are: whether the duty not to kill by neglect is weaker than the duty not to kill actively; whether the metaphor's fixed carrying capacity describes anything real about modern economies; and whether it's legitimate to count distant-future harms to strangers against present, visible ones. But if you think I've misdiagnosed the fault lines, correct me.

3. Cruxes and tests. For each disagreement, the sub-question such that settling it would actually move at least one side, plus whatever evidence, thought experiment, or historical episode both sides would agree bears on it — with each side's prediction recorded in advance, so neither can move the goalposts afterwards. At least one crux should be empirical and at least one should be purely normative, so the document shows which kind of dispute this really is.

4. Verbal residue. State plainly whether any part of their year-long argument was about words rather than the world (I suspect "saving" and "helping" are doing equivocal work), and if so, which part.

One further request, because I've been burned by confident-sounding summaries before: throughout, separate what is actually settled — among economists, demographers, and moral philosophers who know this literature — from what remains genuinely contested, and don't dress up either camp's favourite claim as the consensus view. Where the honest answer is "experts still fight about this," say so.

Length: around 1,200–1,500 words. Tone: formal enough to hand to the teacher, but it should still read like two sixth-formers wrote it, not a UN report.
