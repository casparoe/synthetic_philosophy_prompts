Bit of an odd question but here goes. I'm a second-year politics student and this term we're doing democratic institutions — sortition, rotation of office, term limits, all the mechanisms for stopping people digging themselves into power. And I keep noticing the same thing happen across basically every case we look at, and I can't find a name for it, so I want to check whether political science got there first before I put it in an essay.

The pattern: a reform is brought in to break the grip of entrenched insiders, and it genuinely works — the old insiders really are cleared out, nobody can build a career in the role any more. But instead of power diffusing to ordinary people, it slides sideways to whichever nearby group has the longest time horizon, because once everyone in the formal role is on a short clock, the only people left with institutional memory and actual know-how are the ones who never rotate.

So for instance: term limits on legislators get sold as a way to break up the career-politician class, and the upshot is that fresh-faced reps cycle through so fast that the people who actually understand the legislation are the permanent staffers and the lobbyists, who never leave. Closer to home, our student union rotates it's officers every single year, and the predictable result is that the permanent union staff and the university admin run rings round them — every new president spends half the year learning the job and the other half being politely managed. And with the sortition cases, Athens and the modern citizens' assemblies, the randomly selected people are famously dependant on whoever briefs them: facilitators, expert witnesses, the secretariat. Kill the insider class with a lottery and a new one sprouts in the advisory layer overnight.

Is there an established name for this? The obvious candidates don't quite fit, which is why I'm asking. It's not Michels' iron law of oligarchy — the formal organisation doesn't become oligarchic, if anything it gets weaker and arguably more representative, while the influence leaks out to adjacent actors. It's not regulatory capture either — nobody's being bought or schmoozed, the whole mechanism is just information asymmetry plus mismatched time horizons. If there is a real name and a literature, I want to know how it carves the phenomenon away from capture and from Michels. If there isn't, can you coin a term and define it properly: necessary conditions, what evidence would show it's NOT happening in a given case, and where the edges are. Does it apply to rotating judges? Term-limited CEOs? Football managers? And does the effect invert if the term is long enough that the rotating people become the experts themselves?

Don't just hand me a label though — mostly I want to know whether what I've spotted is actually one phenomenon or three similar-looking ones I've glued together.
