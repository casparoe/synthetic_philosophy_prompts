Bit of background: I spent 35 years as a molecular biologist, retired a few years back, and since then I've gone down the philosophy rabbit hole, mostly the naturalizing-content literature (Millikan, Papineau, that crowd). Next month I'm giving a little talk at our local philosophy society and before I embarass myself in front of people who actually know this stuff, I want to check whether the view I've landed on is even coherent. Here's roughly where I am:

1. Mental content is fixed by selection history. A brain state means whatever the mechanism producing it was selected for indicating or helping to bring about. That's the core teleosemantics idea and I'm basically sold.

2. If a freak accident tomorrow assembled a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of me on some beach (the swampman scenario), that duplicate would have thoughts with content — about its kids, its old job, all of it. A perfect physical duplicate being a mindless automaton strikes me as absurd on its face.

3. The frog's visual system represents flies, not merely small dark moving things. As I understand it that's the selling point of the whole approach: it's what lets you say the frog is mistaken when it snaps at a lead pellet. If the content were just "small dark moving thing" the frog could never be wrong, and a theory with no room for error is a non-starter.

4. Contents that natural selection never targeted are perfectly possible. I believe things about electrons and double helices; nothing in my lineage was selected for representing electrons. Learning and culture generate genuinely new content within a single lifetime.

5. What a creature's inner states represent shouldn't be hostage to facts about long-dead ancestors. If the environment has changed since the relevant selection took place, content should track what the mechanism is doing now, in this environment, because that's what actually explains the behavior in front of you.

6. I also find compelling the idea that interpretation has to come out mostly charitable — you can't coherently read a creature as being wrong about almost everything, so any decent theory of content should make massive error come out impossible or at least incoherent.

So: does this set hang together? Go through it pair by pair where it matters. Tell me which commitments are in genuine tension and how severe each clash is (rank them, don't just flag everything), which apparent tensions dissolve once you're careful, and for each real problem tell me what the cheapest fix would be and what it costs. And if there's a single revision that would clean up the most mess, name it. Don't pull punches — I'd rather hear it from you than from the society's token Davidsonian.
