I write novels for a living (three published, midlist, one small prize you've never heard of) and last month I was at a writers residency in the middle of nowhere, where after dinner two of the other fellows got into an arguement I haven't been able to shake. Partly that's because it maps almost exactly onto the book I'm stuck on right now.

Quick setup. One of them, a poet (lets call her June), lost her husband two years ago. While she was at the residency she fed his old emails and letters into one of the big AI models, then prompted, edited, and re-prompted for weeks, and ended up with a book-length piece of prose fiction in his voice. She read us some of it and it was genuinely good and genuinely disturbing. She called it a novel. The other fellow, Dan, who teaches philosophy at a small college nearby, said whatever it is, it's not that. And they were off. Roughly how it went, as best I remember:

1. Dan: just look at what the word means. A novel is an extended fictional prose narrative composed by a person, someone who meant something by every sentence. What June has is the output of a statistical process with nothing meant by any of it. It's like a forgery: however perfect the fake, a forged Vermeer isn't a Vermeer, because what a painting is is bound up with who made it and how.

2. June: you're just telling me how the word has been used up till now. The interesting question is what the concept should do for us. If this thing does everything a novel does — I read it the way I read novels, it made me weep, I've been arguing with it for weeks — then insisting it isn't one is dictionary fetishism. Concepts are tools and we revise them when the old version stops working. Astronomers did exactly that with "planet": Pluto wasn't discovered to be a non-planet, they decided the concept worked better without it.

3. Dan: Pluto cuts against you. Astronomers didn't just pick whatever was convenient, they had principled criteria tied to how the solar system actually works. And there are principled criteria here too: a novel is an act of communication, and your being moved by it depends on the felt presence of a mind behind the sentences. If you found out a sunset that moved you was actually a projection on a screen, your reaction would change, and it should. Call the manuscript whatever you like, but keep "novel" for the thing that has a whole human life on the other end of it.

4. June: two things. First, I knew exactly where it came from the entire time and it still worked on me, so drop the bit about me being fooled by an illusion. Second, notice what you just did. You opened by saying we only had to look at what the word means, and now you're telling me which features of it SHOULD matter — communication, a mind, a life. That's not reporting on the concept, that's rebuilding it around what you value. You're doing concept-revision exactly like me, you've just kept your blueprints hidden.

Dan came back with something like: picking out which features of our actual practice are essential is what analysis IS, it isn't covert revision, and June was the one proposing to change the subject while keeping the label — which is allowed, but only if you admit you're making a proposal, not settling what her manuscript really is. They were still going when I went to bed.

I need a ruling, for the book but also honestly for my own peace of mind. Who's right at each step? Specifically: (a) is June right at move 2 that "what does the word mean" and "what should the word do for us" are two separate questions, or does Dan's move 3 show they can't be pulled apart? (b) does the Pluto analogy actually help her? (c) whose analogy does more work, the forgery or the sunset? (d) is move 4 a cheap gotcha or the decisive point? And is there something both of them missed? Go move by move, not a general essay, and keep the whole thing under 600 words — I have a deadline and a short attention span.
