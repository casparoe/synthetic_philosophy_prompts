So here's something I've been chewing on. Everyone knows the Clever Hans story -- the horse in Berlin around 1900 whose owner, Wilhelm von Osten, had apparently taught him arithmetic and spelling, which he answered by tapping his hoof. He could do it in front of crowds, with strangers asking the questions. Then Oskar Pfungst ran his investigation and showed that Hans couldn't perform when the questioner didn't know the answer or was hidden from view: the horse was reading tiny involuntary cues from the humans around him, not doing math. This is usually told as a cautionary tale about experimenter bias and that is where the discussion tends to stop.

What I want is an analysis of the episode through Morgan's canon instead. The canon (from C. Lloyd Morgan's 1894 Introduction to Comparative Psychology, roughly: never explain an animal's behavior by appeal to a higher psychological faculty when it can be adequately explained by a lower one) is usually treated as a rule the Hans debacle vindicates. But I think the mapping is messier than that. Do the analysis properly: what exactly were the competing explanations for Hans's performance at the time, and which of them counts as higher or lower on Morgan's scale -- is cue-reading off a human face obviously a lower faculty than arithmetic, or does that smuggle in assumptions about the scale that the canon itself can't supply? Note that the canon says if it can be fairly interpreted lower, and ask what work fairly is doing in the Hans case: before Pfungst, could Osten fairly interpret the tapping lower, or was the lower interpretation actually invisible until the controlled tests existed? Then run it the other direction: what does the episode show about the canon? Morgan meant it against armchair anecdote, but Hans arguably shows the canon is too weak to do its job on its own -- the lower interpretation won because of an experimental method, not because of a principle of parsimony -- and Pfungst's conclusion itself attributed something fairly sophisticated to Hans (fine-grained perception of human posture that most humans can't do consciously), so the episode cuts both ways. Also say whether the canon, taken strictly, would have licensed believing in Hans's arithmetic at any point, or whether its proper application would have required exactly the experiments Pfungst did, and what that implies about the canon as a piece of methodology versus a rule of thumb.

Write it as continuous prose, like a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Don't bother with citations, I just want the reasoning.
