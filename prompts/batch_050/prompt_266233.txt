I came across the following summary of ergodicity economics in a popular science magazine, and I want to know how much of it to trust before I cite it in an essay:

"Neoclassical economics evaluates a gamble by its expected value -- an average over all possible outcomes -- but since each of us lives only one history, the correct criterion is the time-average growth rate of wealth along a single trajectory. For repeated multiplicative gambles, such as a coin flip that raises your wealth by 50% on heads and cuts it by 40% on tails, the two criteria diverge: the expected value per round is positive, yet almost every individual trajectory trends toward ruin. This proves that expected utility theory is wrong, both as a description of behavior and as a normative standard, and it shows that apparent risk aversion is really just rational maximization of the time-average growth rate -- so utility functions are unnecessary. It also explains insurance: a policy can raise the time-average growth rate of both insurer and insured, which is why both sides can rationally sign a contract on which the insurer profits in expectation."

Please audit this summary the way a careful referee would. Before giving your verdict, briefly brainstorm what the strongest and weakest claims in it are. Then go through the claims one by one: state what is accurate, what is an overstatement of a more modest thesis, and what conflates things the literature keeps apart. In particular, I suspect something is off about the claims that expected utility theory is "proven wrong" and that utility functions become unnecessary, but I cannot articulate why. Say what a defender of ergodicity economics would insist on, what a critic from mainstream decision theory would push back on, and whether the magazine's overall conclusion -- that economics needs to be rebuilt around time averages -- follows from the parts of the summary that survive scrutiny.
