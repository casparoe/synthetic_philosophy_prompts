I'm teaching an upper-level seminar on causation this term — Avicenna, Ghazālī, then Hume to show them the family resemblance — and one of my sharper students cornered me after class with an argument that I haven't been able to shake. Some background for those who haven't read him: in the seventeenth discussion of the Incoherence of the Philosophers, Ghazālī famously denies any necessary connection between fire and burning, but later in that same discussion he explains how a miracle can certify a prophet, using an analogy about someone claiming to be the king's messenger. The claimant is believed, Ghazālī says, when he announces in advance that the king will do something contrary to his settled custom — rise from his throne three times, say — and the king then does exactly that. Everyone present knows the king's habits, knows this isn't one of them, and so reads the breach as the king's endorsement of the messenger. My student's argument, cleaned up a bit from the whiteboard version:

(1) For Ghazālī, no created thing necessitates anything; every event is directly willed by God, and the regularities we observe are merely God's custom, which He may interrupt whenever He wills.
(2) If every regularity is a revocable custom, then no regularity can ground knowledge — at most it grounds expectation, the way feeding grounds the rooster's expectation without justifying it.
(3) But the miracle-as-credential works only because the audience knows the king's customary pattern well enough to recognize its violation and to read the violation as endorsement. So the probative force of the miracle presupposes precisely the knowledge of regularities that (2) rules out.
(4) Hence Ghazālī's own account of how prophecy gets verified is unavailable to him: either the audience knows the custom (against (2)) or the miracle certifies nothing and the messenger analogy is a picture with no engine in it.
(5) And the same problem recurs one level down: his jurisprudence constantly asks whether the accused's blow caused the death, whether withholding food starved the child — judgements that require causal knowledge, which (2) also forbids.
(6) So occasionalism proves too much: it saws off the epistemic branch that Ghazālī's own theology and law are sitting on. The position refutes itself.

Here's what I want from you. Tell me which step you'd reject, and be specific about whether the argument equivocates or slides somewhere (I have a suspicion about the move from 'not necessary' to 'not knowledge,' but I want to see it argued, and I want to hear why the slide might instead be forced on him by (1) itself). Then price the repair: Ghazālī does have a stock reply to the objector who says that on his view a man couldn't know he's still in his own house — roughly, God creates in us the knowledge of the continued course of things, and the bare possibility of interruption no more defeats knowledge than the bare possibility that you were moved in your sleep. Does that reply actually block (2), and if it does, what does he pay for it elsewhere — does it make his God a guarantor of habits whose whole point was that He isn't bound by any? If you think the argument is in the end sound, tell me which premise a defender of Ghazālī should surrender first. I'd most like to keep (1) as a fair statement of his view and (6) as false, so if those two can't be had together I want to know exactly why not.
