I work as a software engineer, mostly on recommender systems, and I know temporal discounting mainly from reinforcement learning, where an exponential discount factor is a standard design choice and anything else is treated as a mild exoticism. A friend who reads a lot of pop psychology lent me a book that uses hyperbolic discounting to "solve" the ancient philosophical puzzle of weakness of will, and before I cite it in a blog post I'm writing about procrastination on engineering teams, I want to know how much of the summary to trust.

The book's argument, reproduced as best I can in my own words, goes roughly like this. Philosophers since Socrates have puzzled over akrasia — doing something while judging that one ought to do otherwise — and some went so far as to declare it impossible, on the grounds that a rational agent always does what it judges best. Behavioral economics has now shown where they went wrong. The brain discounts future rewards hyperbolically rather than exponentially, which means preferences genuinely reverse as a tempting option draws closer: at 9 p.m. you prefer being rested tomorrow over watching one more episode, but at 11:30 p.m. you prefer the episode, so when you keep watching you are doing exactly what you then judge best. Weakness of will is therefore not a paradox at all but the predictable output of an inconsistent time-preference function; the person on the couch is not acting against their better judgment in any interesting sense, because their judgment has simply changed. The ancient puzzle dissolves once you model the agent correctly.

Something here feels too tidy to me — in particular the step from "preferences reverse over time" to "so the agent isn't really acting against their better judgment" — but with no philosophy background I can't articulate the objection, and for all I know the book is reporting a settled result. Could you audit this summary and separate what is accurate from what is a misrepresentation? Specifically: which parts would a philosopher who works on akrasia accept, which parts misstate what the puzzle was ever supposed to be, and does the conclusion — that hyperbolic discounting dissolves the puzzle rather than merely redescribing it in temporal terms — actually follow from the accurate parts? I would also like to know whether the book's implicit notion of "better judgment" is the one philosophers use, because I suspect that if there is an equivocation hiding anywhere, it is there.
