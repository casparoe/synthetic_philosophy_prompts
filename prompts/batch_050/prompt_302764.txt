I am a retired experimental physicist who, after four decades in the laboratory, has spent the last few years reading philosophy with the seriousness I once reserved for my own field. This term I am assisting with a university extension seminar on metaphysics, and the convenor has asked me to mark the students' written answers. I should be grateful for a second opinion on one of them before I return it. I will give you the question exactly as it was set, then the student's answer in full, and then tell you what I need.

The question was this:

A sculptor buys a lump of clay on Monday. On Tuesday she shapes it into a statue. On Wednesday, dissatisfied, she rolls it back into a ball. During the statue's existence, the statue and the lump occupy exactly the same region, are composed of exactly the same matter, and share their mass, shape, and every ordinary observable feature. Are the statue and the lump one thing or two? Set out the strongest argument for the claim that they are not identical, and the strongest objection to that argument. Then discuss at least two positions in the literature on material constitution and persistence, and defend a verdict of your own.

Here is the student's answer:

On Tuesday the statue and the lump share all their parts and coincide exactly, yet they seem to differ. The lump existed on Monday; the statue did not. The lump would survive being rolled into a ball; the statue would not. By Leibniz's Law, if two things are identical they share all their properties, so it follows that the statue and the lump are not identical, since they differ in their historical and modal properties.

The identity theorist's strongest reply is that this argument moves too quickly. The properties alleged to differ, such as being able to survive squashing or having existed on Monday, are not straightforwardly possessed by the object in front of us; modal and historical predicates are implicitly relative to the sortal under which the object is considered. There is one object, which qua lump would survive squashing and qua statue would not. The defender of identity must therefore reject Leibniz's Law as applied here, since one and the same object both would and would not survive the squashing depending on how it is described. This seems to me the weak point of the identity view, since Leibniz's Law is close to non-negotiable.

Constitutionalism instead embraces the conclusion that there are two objects: the statue is constituted by the lump without being identical to it. Constitution is asymmetric, and the two objects have different persistence conditions because they fall under different sortals, which fix what changes each can survive. The main cost is the coincidence problem: we now have two spatially coincident objects, exactly alike in mass, shape, and microphysical composition, differing only in modal and historical respects, and it is hard to say what grounds the difference between them, since nothing in their shared matter does.

Four-dimensionalism offers a different diagnosis. On the perdurance or worm theory, objects are extended through time as well as space, and ordinary objects are aggregates of temporal parts. On this view the statue and the lump are numerically identical for as long as they coincide — throughout Tuesday and Wednesday morning they are one and the same object — but distinct before and after, since the lump's temporal parts extend further in both directions. This gives the four-dimensionalist an elegant middle course, though at the price of accepting that identity can be temporary. The rival stage variant avoids even that cost by taking ordinary objects to be instantaneous stages linked by temporal counterpart relations rather than sums of temporal parts.

On balance I side with the constitutionalist. The Leibniz's Law argument goes through once we insist that the differing properties are genuine: the statue really is such that it could not survive squashing, and no merely verbal relativization removes that fact about it. The coincidence worry is a genuine oddity but not a contradiction, and every rival pays a comparable price — the identity view strains our modal judgments, and the worm theory, as shown, requires temporary identity.
