I'm a journalist who covers machine learning and cognitive science, and I'm drafting a long piece on whether the classic psychology of concepts anticipated, or was quietly replaced by, the modern machine-learning toolkit. My own fluency runs in the ML direction: nearest-neighbor methods, centroids and cluster means, kernel machines, mixture models, structured generative models, embeddings, and the distinction between a discriminative classifier and a model that can generate or explain data.

Here is what I need. Take the three classic theories of concepts from cognitive psychology -- prototype theory, exemplar theory, and the theory theory -- and restate each one, one at a time, in the vocabulary of statistical learning just listed. For each theory, do three things: (1) give the cleanest translation of its core claims about how categories are represented and how categorization judgments are produced; (2) identify what has to be distorted or quietly dropped to make the translation work -- for example, claims about typicality gradients, about the role of causal-explanatory structure, or about how concepts change across development and across cultures; and (3) name whatever residue remains that the statistical-learning vocabulary simply cannot express, such as anything about explanation, normativity (what makes a categorization correct rather than merely likely), or the difference between possessing a concept and deploying a classifier.

Then, the part I most care about: give me your verdict on the residue. In each case, does what fails to translate point to something the ML framework is missing -- something a learning system would need in order to genuinely have concepts -- or does it point to claims in the psychological theories that were vaguer than they appeared and that the translation exercise exposes as such? Please keep the translation honest: don't smuggle psychological assumptions into the ML vocabulary and call the result a translation.
