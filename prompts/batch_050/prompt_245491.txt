After forty years as an experimental physicist, I retired and, somewhat to my own surprise, became a devoted auditor of a metaphysics seminar at the nearby university. The seminar leader, a philosopher whose judgment I have come to respect, has spent the term on Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles, and I have kept a careful record of her rulings on the cases we have discussed. I would like your help predicting her ruling on the next case, which I intend to raise myself, since it comes from my own field.

Her verdicts so far:

First, Black's spheres: a universe containing nothing but two perfectly identical spheres, some distance apart in an otherwise empty space. Her verdict: two spheres, not one. Qualitative duplication, she said, does not collapse a numerical plurality into a unity.

Second, eternal recurrence: a cosmos in which history repeats itself exactly, cycle after cycle, forever. Her verdict: infinitely many cycles and infinitely many qualitatively identical events, not a single event.

Third, classical duplicates: two billiard balls that are perfect replicas of one another, differing only in position and motion. Her verdict: two balls, discernible by their trajectories; no philosophical difficulty here at all.

Fourth, the helium atom: the two electrons of a helium atom in its ground state, which share the same orbital and, as I confirmed for the seminar, all intrinsic quantum state properties. Her verdict: two electrons. She remarked that each stands to the other in the relation of having opposite spin, a relation neither bears to itself, and she pronounced that enough to keep them apart.

Fifth, the appeal to thisness: asked whether the spheres of the first case could be told apart by properties of the form being this very sphere rather than that one, she ruled such properties inadmissible, on the grounds that they would render the principle true by stipulation and therefore empty.

The case I plan to raise next week is this. In a Bose–Einstein condensate, a large number of atoms occupy precisely the same quantum state, and the same holds for the photons of a laser in a single mode. These are bosons, and unlike the electrons of the fourth case, they offer nothing analogous to the opposite-spin relation: so far as I can tell, no property and no irreflexive relation separates one member of the condensate from any other. And yet the physics counts them. Quantum mechanics assigns the condensate a definite occupation number, and its total mass and energy scale with that number.

My questions are these. How do you expect her to rule — one or many? Which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog to this case, and which is the most misleading one to lean on? Please set out the rules that fit her track record equally well, state what each of them predicts about the condensate, give me your best guess at her actual verdict together with your reasons, and tell me which further case — or which question I might put to her in seminar — would best discriminate between the competing rules. If her record genuinely underdetermines the verdict, I would rather be told so plainly, along with an account of what principle she would be committing herself to under each possible ruling. Please write your answer in American English.
