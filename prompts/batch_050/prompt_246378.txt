I've been reading LessWrong and the surrounding decision-theory literature on and off for close to a decade, and there's something I've watched happen with updateless decision theory that I want diagnosed rather than relitigated. When UDT was first floated, the framing was ambitious: don't condition on your observations at all, just choose the global policy you'd most want your future selves and counterparts to be implementing, and you get the right answers in Newcomb's problem, the transparent-box variant, Parfit's hitchhiker, and the counterfactual mugging, all cases where causal and evidential decision theory were thought to give embarrassing answers. But here we are years later, and as far as I can tell the debate has quietly stalled rather than resolved. The critics keep pointing out that UDT never specified how an agent is supposed to reason about logical counterfactuals — what it even means to consider what happens in worlds where the algorithm you're currently running outputs something different from what it in fact outputs — and that without a working theory of that, the whole framework is a promissory note. The defenders respond, roughly, that this is a general problem about logical uncertainty that every adequate decision theory will face, not a defect specific to UDT, and that the framework's performance on the canonical test problems shows it's pointed in the right direction even if the foundations aren't finished. Both of these moves have been on the table for years and nobody seems to have updated.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement isn't really about UDT's merits at all but about what a decision theory is supposed to be: one side wants a fully specified, implementable procedure and counts an undefined notion of logical counterfactual as disqualifying, while the other side treats decision theories as sketches of the right policy-selection criterion and counts performance on test problems as evidence of correctness even when the formalism is incomplete. If that's right, the two sides aren't even disagreeing — they're applying different success criteria to the same object. But I might be wrong: maybe there's a substantive disagreement hiding underneath, about whether the test problems themselves are the right benchmark, or about whether logical counterfactuals are the sort of thing that admits a principled solution at all.

What I'd like is a committed diagnosis, not a survey. Pick the characterization of this impasse you actually think is correct — verbal, methodological, a disguised technical dispute, a disagreement about which problems are canonical, or something else — and defend it against the alternatives using what each side actually argues. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me concretely what would count as progress: a specific formal result, a new problem case that would discriminate between the views, or an argument that one side's success criterion is wrongheaded. If your honest assessment is that no progress is to be expected because the dispute rests on irreconcilable starting intuitions about what decision theory is for, say that and defend it — I'd rather have a defended pessimism than a diplomatic both-sidesism.
