I'm in an undergrad seminar on paradoxes of rationality and we just did the self-torturer (the one I think is due to Warren Quinn): a device with 1000 settings wired into you, each click up adds a literally imperceptible amount of pain, and you get paid $1,000 per click, money you keep forever, pain you keep forever too. It broke my brain a little, so I've been polling my own intuitions on related cases and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can report them:

1. The dial: the guy who clicks all the way to 1000 for a million bucks and then would pay everything to get back to setting 1 did something irrational. But I cannot for the life of me say which click was the mistake -- each one trades an undetectable pain increase for $1,000, which seems obviously worth it at the moment you make it. Also, my own preferences over the states would cycle (each next setting better than the current one, but setting 1 better than setting 1000), and honestly that doesn't strike me as irrational, it strikes me as just what the case is like.

2. The wine collector: a friend prefers bottle A to B, B to C, and C to A, and would happily pay a dollar to swap each time. Him I *do* judge irrational -- someone could walk him around the circle and take his lunch money, and the preferences themselves seem to be the problem.

3. My roommate's "one more episode": every night, each extra episode costs her a barely noticeable slice of next-day functioning, and she ends every week wrecked. Here I want to say she's being irrational and the fix is that she should just pick a stopping point, any stopping point, and hold it -- rationality can demand you stop somewhere even when no particular stopping point is special.

4. The grading cutoff: I TA a course and the A line is 90.0. A student with 89.9 is, ability-wise, indistinguishable from a 90.0 student, and my honest view is that drawing a consequential line between indistinguishable cases is arbitrary and indefensible -- any such cutoff is institutional convenience, not something reason could justify.

5. The savings account: I put aside $5 a day; each day the sacrifice is negligible and the end state is great. No irrationality anywhere, obviously -- but note this has the same "each step trivial, huge cumulative effect" shape as cases 1 and 3, so whatever principle you give me has to say why this one is fine while those aren't.

Some of these are in tension with each other (I suspect 1 vs 2 and 3 vs 4, but I'm not sure), so please act as a philosopher who works on rational choice theory and vagueness-adjacent puzzles, and think carefully -- maybe brainstorm a few candidate principles before settling on one. What I'm after: a precisely stated principle (or two serious candidates) that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, an explanation of which clause does the work for each case, an honest statement of which verdicts it has to overturn, and what it predicts for two cases I haven't made my mind up about: (a) a version of the dial where, before anything starts, you're offered a one-time chance to precommit to a stopping number, and (b) the frequent flyer who reasons that each individual trip he takes adds an undetectable amount to the climate problem. End your response with a last line starting "Bottom line:" that states the principle in one sentence and lists the numbers of any verdicts it can't accomodate.
