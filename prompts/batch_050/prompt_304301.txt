Exam question: Reconstruct the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the person who dies, making its premises explicit. Then present what you take to be the strongest objection to it and assess whether that objection succeeds, including whether it can adequately handle the question of when death could be bad for its victim. Finally, consider whether the argument comparing the time before one's birth with the time after one's death adds anything to the original argument, or merely restates it.

Student's answer: The Epicurean argument runs as follows. First, death is the permanent annihilation of the person: after death there is no surviving subject who has any experiences. Second, Epicurus assumes a hedonist theory of wellbeing, according to which the only things that can be good or bad for a person are their own felt pleasures and pains. Third, death removes all sensation, and hence all pleasure and pain, from the one who has died. It follows that being dead can be neither good nor bad for the person who dies — it is, as Epicurus puts it, nothing to us. Note that the argument is about being dead, not about the process of dying, which can of course be painful and therefore bad; Epicurus explicitly concedes this.

The strongest objection is the deprivationist response associated with Thomas Nagel. On this view, death is bad for the person who dies not because being dead is a painful or unpleasant state, but because it deprives the person of the goods of life they would otherwise have enjoyed. Crucially, Nagel argues that a person can be harmed without being aware of the harm: his example of the intelligent adult who is reduced to the mental condition of a contented infant, and who is clearly worse off even though he does not and cannot register the loss, shows that harm does not require experienced suffering. So the deprivationist rejects Epicurus's second premise. It should be noted that this response commits the deprivationist to abandoning hedonism about wellbeing, since it denies that badness for a person consists in what the person feels.

The deprivationist, however, cannot escape the timing problem. Death cannot be bad for me before it happens, since it has not yet deprived me of anything, and it cannot be bad for me after it happens, since I no longer exist to be harmed. The standard deprivationist answer locates the harm at the moment of dying itself, but at that moment the person is still alive and therefore not yet deprived of anything, so the timing problem remains fatal to the objection.

Epicurus's second argument, the symmetry argument, adds something genuinely independent. It observes that the period of nonexistence before our birth seems exactly parallel to the period of nonexistence after our death; we regard the former with indifference, so consistency demands we regard the latter the same way. Against this, Parfit argued that our bias toward the future is rational — we care about future goods in a way we do not care about past goods — which refutes the symmetry argument and vindicates the deprivationist's asymmetric treatment of the two periods. The two arguments therefore work in tandem: the first establishes that death cannot be experienced as bad, and the second blocks the thought that future nonexistence is special.
