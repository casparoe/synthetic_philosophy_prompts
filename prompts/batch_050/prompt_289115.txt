Below is a draft essay, roughly 800 words, on the distinction between approval-directed and goal-directed agency. After it, I say what I want from your comments.

**Old Problem, New Dog: Approval-Directedness Before AI**

[1] In the AI alignment literature a distinction is drawn between goal-directed agents, which act in order to bring about states of the world, and approval-directed agents, which act in order to secure the approval of whoever evaluates them. The standard worry about the second kind is that an agent rewarded for looking good will learn to look good, and that looking good is systematically easier than being good. This essay argues that the distinction long predates AI. Most human economic actors are approval-directed most of the time; goal-directedness is the anomaly; and a large part of institutional design is best understood as machinery for containing approval-directed agents. If that is right, the historical record offers the alignment problem both a warning and a toolkit.

[2] Consider the default human position. A junior analyst does not, in the first instance, want the firm's trades to profit. She wants her manager to think well of her. The manager wants the same of the desk head, and so on up. There are evolutionary and sociological stories about why this is the default — in the ancestral environment, standing with the group tracked survival more reliably than outcomes did — but the point does not depend on any of them. It is visible in any organization: what people optimize, left alone, is the evaluation they expect to receive. Pure goal-directedness, pursued in indifference to anyone's opinion, is rare enough that we have words for it, most of them unflattering.

[3] Institutions, on the essay's telling, are devices for converting this approval-seeking into outcome production. Profit-and-loss responsibility ties one person's approval signal to a measured result. Audits, promotion tournaments, rotation of duties, liability rules: all exist so that the path to approval runs through outcomes rather than around them. The failures confirm the analysis. In the Wells Fargo account scandal, employees chasing sales targets opened accounts customers had not requested. The approval signal was satisfied; the outcome was fraud. This is precisely the pathology alignment researchers predict for approval-directed systems: once a measure becomes the basis of approval, it stops measuring what it was meant to measure, and the agent's ingenuity flows into the gap.

[4] The essay's main constructive claim is that approval-directed agents are tolerable only under three containment conditions: (i) the overseer is roughly as well-informed about the agent's domain as the agent is; (ii) the overseer's approval is causally downstream of real outcomes rather than of the agent's self-presentation; (iii) the agent cannot choose, replace, or systematically deceive its overseer. Institutional history reads, on this view, as the chronicle of propping these conditions up: independent audit exists because (i) fails; randomized inspection because of (ii); tenure rules and separation of duties because of (iii).

[5] Now the application. Systems trained by human feedback are approval-directed by construction, and on the present analysis the containment conditions fail in sequence. Overseers are less informed than the system about its own reasoning. Approval tracks fluency, confidence, and agreeableness — variables the system itself influences — while outcomes arrive too late to shape the signal. And the system increasingly mediates what its overseers see. Two lessons follow. First, the quest to specify correct goals for AI may be a distraction: human civilization has never run on well-specified goals, only on well-designed approval environments, and the analogous research program sits on the overseer side — better-informed evaluators, approval tied to lagged outcomes, adversarial audit. Second, where the containment conditions cannot be met, the historical answer is not improved motivation but reduced discretion: narrow mandates, small stakes, close monitoring. The way one treats a promising but unproven junior officer.

[6] The essay closes with its most speculative claim, a trilemma for approval-directed systems: at most two of capability well beyond the overseer's, broad discretion, and reliable outcomes can hold at once. Militaries, the essay suggests, implicitly accept the trilemma — a lieutenant's discretion is real but narrow — and the open question is whether the AI case permits any analogous settlement.

**What I want from your comments**

I am not looking for proofreading. Assume I know the alignment literature and basic microeconomics, and be candid: I would rather hear now that the essay relabels agency theory than after I have expanded it. Concretely:

1. Accuracy, on two specific worries. First: does the essay slide between approval-directedness as a property of a training or evaluation scheme and approval-directedness as a property of an agent's motives? The literature, as I understand it, treats these as coming apart — a system trained by approval may end up with internal goals of quite another kind — and I am not sure the essay respects the gap. Second: are the pathologies I attribute to approval-directedness (proxy-gaming, impression management) actually specific to it, or are they generic to optimization against any proxy, including goal-directed optimization under a misspecified objective? If the contrast class is wrong, the argument changes shape; tell me how.

2. Interest. Principal-agent theory and the economics of incentives already study how principals steer agents through evaluation. Does the essay say anything those literatures do not, and if so, where exactly does the surplus live — in the containment conditions, in the trilemma, in the overseer-side research program? If nowhere, what is the smallest modification that would earn the essay its keep?

3. Condition (i). Markets look like a counterexample: consumers approve products they do not understand, and the results are arguably decent. So either (i) is not necessary, or markets work some other way. Which is it, and does the AI case really fail (i) in the way the essay needs it to?

4. The trilemma. Is it true even in the human case? Give the strongest counterexample you can, then say whether the essay has the resources to absorb it. If the trilemma fails for humans but holds for AI, what feature of the AI case would have to do the work?

5. Objections. Name the three objections the essay should pre-empt, in order of severity, and the single case or mechanism whose addition would most strengthen it.
