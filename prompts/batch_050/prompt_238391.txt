Write a poem of sixteen lines, in two stanzas of eight, built on the classic Adams pair of conditionals: the indicative "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did," which we accept, and the counterfactual "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have," which we have no comparable reason to accept. The two stanzas should be near-mirrors: each line of the second stanza should rewrite the corresponding line of the first with the smallest change you can manage -- ideally only a shift in tense and mood -- so that the poem's very form stages the puzzle, namely that a grammatical wobble between "didn't... did" and "hadn't... would have" can carry a conditional from near-certain to baseless. Let the first stanza inhabit the indicative's confidence, grounded in evidence about the actual world (someone fired the shot; the evidence fixes that much), and let the second betray the counterfactual's overreach into an unactualized possibility. A reader familiar with the literature on conditionals should be able to point to the exact word where the epistemology turns.
