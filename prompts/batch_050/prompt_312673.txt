Office-mate question. I share a corridor with a metaphysician who does the possible-worlds thing, and for months I've been polling her on cases over coffee. Her verdicts so far, and she insists they're all consistent:

- Could Humphrey (a senator who narrowly lost an election) have won? Yes, obviously.
- Could you have had a wildly different life -- raised in another country, different first language, different career, different personality -- and still been you? Yes.
- Could you have been born from a different sperm and egg, to different parents, and still been you? No. That's just somebody else, no matter how similar.
- Could the oak table in her office have been made with slightly different wood, a few different planks swapped in? Yes, same table.
- Could that table have been made from entirely different planks? No. That would be a different table, even if it looked and felt identical.
- If in some physics setup you swap one electron for a different electron in exactly the same state, has anything changed? She says no, and goes further: there's no fact of the matter about "which" electron it was. There's nothing to an electron over and above its role and properties.
- Could Aristotle have lived out his whole life as a fisherman and never philosophized? Yes.

Last week I put two new cases to her and she had to go think, so now I'm curious what she'll come back with:

(a) Anna and Bea are identical twins, same zygote, split a few days after conception. Anna became a dentist, Bea became a pilot. Could Anna have lived Bea's exact life instead -- and if we describe a world where someone with that origin becomes the pilot, is that person Anna or Bea?

(b) Could Humphrey have been twins?

Predict her verdicts on (a) and (b), and tell me which framework she's actually running. The candidates as I understand them: things literally show up in many possible worlds and some of their features are non-negotiable; or nothing shows up in more than one world and "could have" talk works via similar-enough stand-ins, where what counts as similar enough depends on the conversation; or maybe something else entirely. Her verdicts seem consistent with more than one of these, so which ones pin down what? And where does the electron answer fit -- it seems to cut against how she'll need to answer (a), or does it?

One more thing: am I right that the flat "no" on the sperm-and-egg case sits oddly with the easy "yes" on the totally-different-life case? She says the line between them is principled and obvious. Where exactly is the line, and if (a) and (b) don't settle which view she holds, what further case would?
