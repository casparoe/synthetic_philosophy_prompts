Ok, here's something that's been bugging me for a while and I want a proper diagnosis, not another defense of one side. The literature on the epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis feels genuinely stuck to me, in a way that debates about, say, the halting problem never were. Everyone agrees the thesis is true (or at least nobody serious doubts it), but after ninety years people still can't agree on what kind of claim it even is. You've got one camp treating it as a theorem that just needs the right formalization of "effective calculation by a human following a routine" to be provable -- Gandy-style or Sieg-style axiomatizations of what a computor does, then derive Turing machines. You've got another camp treating it as an empirical claim about what's physically doable in our universe, one that could in principle be refuted by some hypercomputing setup. You've got people treating it as a stipulative sharpening, a definition we adopted because it works, so asking whether it's "true" is confused. And then there's the reading where it's not about physics or stipulation at all but about what we mean -- something you establish by analyzing the concept of calculation itself, closer to a conceptual truth that nonetheless isn't provable in the formal sense.

What I can't tell, and what I want you to figure out, is what the disagreement actually consists in. Is it a factual disagreement hiding under a verbal one -- i.e., the camps have quietly different target notions (human clerk vs. machine vs. physical process) and would all agree once the targets were disambiguated? Is it a disagreement about what would count as evidence or proof for a claim like this at all, where the sides just have different standards? Or is it something more like bedrock: different intuitions about whether meaning-analysis can be rigorous, which neither side can really argue the other out of? My own suspicion, offered as a hypothesis only: the provability camp and the empirical camp aren't disagreeing at all, they're each right about their own target, and the real live dispute is only between both of them and the conceptual-analysis reading, which refuses to let the thesis be about anything as thin as what a machine or a universe can do. But I might be wrong, and I'd love to be shown where.

So: diagnose the impasse, say what kind of disagreement it is, and then -- this is the part I actually care about for a grad seminar I'm planning -- tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis. A distinction? A formal result? Or should we expect none, and is that itself the interesting conclusion?
