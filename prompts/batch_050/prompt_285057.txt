I am a novelist in the final stages of a book narrated in the first person by a philosophical zombie: a man molecule-for-molecule identical to an ordinary person, indistinguishable in speech and behaviour, yet with nothing it is like to be him on the inside. My publisher has asked for a short poem to stand as the epigraph, and I should like it to be a villanelle spoken by this narrator. Nineteen lines, two refrains, the closing quatrain — the usual architecture. But I want the form to carry the philosophy rather than merely decorate it. One refrain should voice the premise of the conceivability argument: that such a being can be imagined without contradiction. The other should voice its alleged conclusion: that if so, consciousness must be something over and above the physical facts. Let the refrains return as the argument itself returns — physicalism answers it, and it rises again regardless — and let their meeting in the final two lines stage the collision at the heart of my novel: the narrator pleading his case in perfect English while the reader wonders whether there is anyone home to do the pleading. Someone who knows the debate — Nagel's question about what it is like to be a bat, Chalmers and the zombie argument, the reply that conceivability is no reliable guide to possibility — should be able to tell which refrain is which and why they can only meet at the very end. Someone who knows none of it should simply find the poem quietly horrifying.
