Article 5(3) of the Treaty on European Union (consolidated version) provides:

"Under the principle of subsidiarity, in areas which do not fall within its exclusive competence, the Union shall act only if and in so far as the objectives of the proposed action cannot be sufficiently achieved by the Member States, either at central level or at regional and local level, but can rather, by reason of the scale or effects of the proposed action, be better achieved at Union level.

The institutions of the Union shall apply the principle of subsidiarity as laid down in the Protocol on the application of the principles of subsidiarity and proportionality. National Parliaments ensure compliance with the principle of subsidiarity in accordance with the procedure set out in that Protocol."

In practice, this test is applied as a comparative exercise: the Commission publishes subsidiarity assessments arguing that a given objective (say, uniform product standards or emissions limits) would be frustrated by divergent national action and is therefore "better achieved at Union level"; national parliaments may issue reasoned opinions objecting to a proposal on subsidiarity grounds, triggering the so-called yellow-card procedure; and the Court of Justice, though formally competent to annul legislation for breach of subsidiarity, has in practice been extremely reluctant to second-guess the legislator's judgement that Union action is warranted. Meanwhile, the very same article insists, under the principle of conferral, that competences not conferred upon the Union "remain with the Member States".

Write an essay, in British English and of roughly 900 to 1,200 words (excluding the passage above), addressing the following. What view of legitimate political authority does this subsidiarity test presuppose? In particular: the test appears to allocate authority on purely instrumental grounds — whichever level achieves the objective best should act — which sits oddly with the treaty's simultaneous suggestion that Member States hold competences by default, and with the choice of national parliaments, whose authority rests on democratic self-government rather than efficiency, as the test's watchdogs. Reconstruct the implicit theory of when authority over a domain rightly migrates from a smaller political community to a larger one. Consider carefully what the test must assume to be even applicable: that "the objectives of the proposed action" are fixed and shared across levels before the comparison is made; that success at different levels is measurable on a common scale; and that some institution occupies a standpoint neutral enough to judge whether Member States "sufficiently achieve" an objective — even though every actual judge sits at one level or the other. Distinguish, for each presupposition you identify, whether the practice strictly requires it or whether several rival theories (an outcome-based theory, a theory of democratic self-government, a presumption in favour of proximity to the citizen) would license the same arrangements. Assess whether the combination of conferral, an efficiency-based subsidiarity test, and parliamentary policing is coherent, or whether it presupposes two inconsistent theories of where authority originates. Finally, describe concretely what would have to change — in the treaty's wording, in the Commission's assessments, or in the yellow-card procedure — if the efficiency test were replaced by a principle that authority belongs to the smallest self-governing community capable of voicing an interest in the matter.
