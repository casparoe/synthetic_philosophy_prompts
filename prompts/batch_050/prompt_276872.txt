Had a stupidly long argument with a friend last night about trolley cases and I want to settle something. The basic setup everyone knows: runaway trolley, five people on the main track, you can pull a lever to divert it onto a siding where one person is stuck. My friend says diverting is fine because you don't need the one person to get hit — if he somehow survived or the trolley stopped on its own, great, his death isn't doing any work. It's just an unfortunate side effect of saving the five.

Fine. But then I changed the case and she got squirmy. Imagine the siding actually loops back around and rejoins the main track just ahead of the five. So if you divert, the trolley goes onto the loop, hits the one guy — and here's the thing: hitting his body is what slows the trolley down enough that it never gets back around to the five. If the guy dove out of the way at the last second, the trolley would complete the loop and kill the five anyway. So now his getting hit isn't incidental, it's literally the mechanism that saves them. If you're being honest, you should be a little disappointed if he survives, because then your plan failed.

My friend wanted to keep saying it's still just diverting a threat, the death is still a side effect, you don't want him dead per se. And ok, technically if he got hit and miraculously survived but his body still stopped the trolley, you'd take that — so his death isn't strictly "needed." But something clearly changed between the two cases and I can't put my finger on what it is. In the first one the harm is downstream and useless; in the second one the harm is load-bearing, even though nobody wishes for it for its own sake.

Does this difference have a name? Is there an established way to mark the distinction between "bad thing happens on the way to the good outcome" and "bad thing is what produces the good outcome, even though we'd happily take the good outcome without it if we could"? My friend says this is all just the old foresight vs intention thing, but that doesn't seem to capture it, since in both cases you foresee the hit and in neither case do you want it for itself. And if there's a literature on the loopy version specifically I'd love to know — not just a bare label, I want to know where people actually think the line is drawn, and whether the load-bearing-ness is really what does the moral work or whether that's a red herring.
