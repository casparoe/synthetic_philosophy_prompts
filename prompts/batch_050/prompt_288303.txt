So I retired two years ago after four decades in experimental physics, and I've been falling down a philosophy-and-economics rabbit hole ever since. Lately it's information cascades and rational herding, and honestly it makes me a bit grumpy, because in the lab the first thing you learn is that you never let one observer read the other observer's notebook before writing down their own number. Independence of measurements is the whole ballgame. Meanwhile the entire modern internet seems designed to make sure everyone sees everyone else's estimate first.

Here's what got me going. I was reading about Francis Galton's old county-fair story, the one from his 1907 piece "Vox Populi" in Nature. Galton describes a weight-judging competition at a livestock show where roughly 800 people guessed the dressed weight of an ox, and he found that the middlemost guess landed within about one percent of the true weight. He's quite tickled by this and takes it as evidence that democratic judgment is more trustworthy than you'd think. Fine. But what everyone seems to forget is that those guesses were written down privately, on seperate tickets, before anyone saw anyone else's. Galton's crowd was wise precisely because nobody could herd.

So here's what I'd like. Write me the landing page plus a short FAQ for a fictional startup -- call it "HerdSight" -- that sells crowd-sourced estimation (weight of livestock, project completion dates, quarterly earnings, whatever) and whose marketing leans hard on the Galton ox story as its founding myth, while every single feature of the product quietly destroys the conditions that made Galton's result work. Guesses are submitted publicly and in sequence, with a live running average displayed before you commit. Users earn "Early Mover" badges for guessing first and "Consensus" badges for matching the herd. A premium tier shows you what verified top estimators guessed. The FAQ should answer questions like "Why did all 10,000 guesses land on exactly the same wrong number?" and "Your estimate moved the average -- does that mean you were wrong?" in perfectly cheerful, perfectly deadpan corporate language.

The comedy should come from getting the mechanism right: people rationally discounting their own private information once they've seen enough prior guesses, the first few estimators effectively deciding the outcome, convergence being marketed as accuracy, and the whole edifice being weirdly fragile -- one well-placed new guess with a confident note attached and the herd stampedes the other way, which the FAQ celebrates as "responsiveness." A specialist in this stuff should be able to nod along and see that the writer understands why Galton's setup worked and why HerdSight's doesn't, without the copy ever admitting a thing is wrong. Please don't have the piece wink or break character -- no sarcasm in the narrator's voice, just a company that has sincerely mistaken an echo chamber for an instrument.

About 600-700 words, formatted as the landing page (headline, a few feature blurbs, maybe a testimonial from a satisfied livestock fair) followed by three or four FAQ entries. Start with the piece itself -- headline first, no preface, no note explaining the joke to me afterwards.
