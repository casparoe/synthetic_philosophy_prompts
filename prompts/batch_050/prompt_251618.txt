I'm the standards editor at a mid-sized regional news outlet, and part of my job is making the "is this a conspiracy theory?" call -- reader tips, freelancer pitches, claims going around local facebook that someone wants us to debunk. It comes up maybe fifteen, twenty times a month, decided fast, usually by whoever's on the desk. We've burned ourselves both directions: two years ago we waved off a tip about the water utility sitting on bad test results because it smelled conspiracist, and it turned out to be true, and last year we slapped the conspiracy label on a pitch that was actually a legit FOIA story and the reporter (understandably) went ballistic. So I want to stop relying on vibes. What I'd like from you is an actual decision procedure my desk can run on any candidate claim: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in "label it," "investigate it," or "not ours to call," and where it has to hand off to an editor because the remaining question is one of judgment, not checklist.

Here's the conceptual raw material I want operationalized, but I'm not precious about it if you think parts don't survive translation. Conspiracy theories clearly aren't false by definition -- real conspiracies get exposed all the time (COINTELPRO, Tuskegee, the NSA stuff pre-Snowden would've sounded unhinged) -- so the procedure can't treat "posits a secret plot" as disqualifying; it has to target *unwarranted* conspiracy belief specifically. The markers I keep seeing in the lit: whether the theory has to explain away "errant data" that would otherwise sink it; whether it absorbs every counterexample as further proof of the plot's reach (i.e. it's unfalsifiable in practice, a degenerating research program rather than a live hypothesis); whether the number of people who'd have to keep the secret is compatible with how long secrets of that size actually last (there's that 2016 back-of-envelope model suggesting plots involving thousands of conspirators collapse within a few years, which seems load-bearing for the "everyone's in on it" flavor); and the triple assumption that nothing is as it seems, everything is connected, and nothing is off-limits to them. On the other side, official stories genuinely do lie, so the procedure needs a symmetric test and can't just defer to institutional denial as exculpatory. Tell me which of these distinctions have to survive the simplification and which I can drop without the procedure misfiring constantly, and be honest about the failure modes -- where it'll flag legit investigative journalism as conspiracy, or wave through something rotten because it's small enough to be plausible.

Then run the finished procedure on three real-ish cases from my inbox: (1) a viral claim that the county's new voting machines were quietly "audited" pre-certification by a firm tied to a big donor, (2) a claim that the hospital system paid confidential settlements to families of kids hurt by a specific vaccine lot and bound them with NDAs, and (3) a reporter's pitch that half the city council takes its talking points from a regional developers' association. I'm fairly sure at least one of these is going to produce a verdict I won't like -- my guess is the procedure tells me to treat the NDA story as investigate-don't-label, which my publisher will hate because it feels like both-sidesing a public health topic -- so don't flinch, just show the reasoning and note where the discomfort comes from. End with a short section on what this procedure structurally cannot do (time limits on the desk, no subject-matter experts on staff, claims where the warrant question is genuinely open), because I'd rather hand my team an honest tool than a comprehensive-looking one.
