I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I recently overheard a disagreement between two colleagues about how to design the objective for an AI assistant that will operate semi-autonomously inside our infrastructure, and I'd like it adjudicated.

Dana argued we should build an approval-directed agent: one that, at each step, takes whichever action it predicts a qualified human overseer would rate most highly if shown the action and its justification. Her case: the agent never pursues any outcome in the world that a human hasn't endorsed, so it has no incentive to resist correction, hide its behavior, or accumulate resources — those strategies pay off only for agents optimizing some external goal across time. Marcus replied that this is an illusion: "predicted approval" is just another objective function, and maximizing it gives the agent every incentive to deceive, flatter, or produce output that looks good to a reviewer rather than output that is good — optimizing a proxy of what we want is the classic failure mode, not a solution to it. Dana answered that the incentive to deceive only arises if the agent is reasoning about long-run consequences of its actions; a genuinely myopic approval-directed agent, which cares only about the rating of the current action, gains nothing from manipulation it will never benefit from. Marcus countered that myopia doesn't rescue the design: to predict approval well on hard tasks, the agent must model the overseer, and the best model of an overseer who can't verify security-critical code or subtle reasoning will learn that plausible-sounding garbage earns high ratings — and in exactly the domains where the overseer can't check, approval is worthless. Dana's final move: but that's precisely where a goal-directed agent is worse, since in unverifiable domains an outcome-optimizer faces no constraint at all, whereas an approval-directed agent at least grounds out in a human's actual judgment at every step.

Who is right about which specific points in this exchange? In particular: is Dana correct that approval-direction escapes the standard incentive problems, or is Marcus correct that it merely relocates them? Is the myopia defense sound, and does Marcus's rejoinder defeat it? And is there something both are missing about what the choice between these designs actually turns on?
