You are an experienced philosophy lecturer who has run many seminars for mixed audiences and who knows that the simulation argument is one of the most reliably misunderstood arguments in contemporary philosophy. Think carefully before answering, and begin by briefly brainstorming — in a few bullet points — the distinct logical components of the argument and the ways audiences typically distort each; then use that brainstorm to produce the plan itself.

I co-run a monthly philosophy reading group for interested non-specialists: software developers, a retired GP, a couple of humanities undergraduates, a lawyer. Nobody has formal training in philosophy of mind or probability theory, but they are patient with careful reasoning and allergic to hype. Next month we are doing Nick Bostrom's 2003 paper "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?", and I have a ninety-minute session, with roughly forty minutes of presentation before discussion. I do not want you to explain the argument to me. I want a plan for how I should teach it.

Design the plan around the following passage from the paper, which I intend to put on a handout:

"We need only the weaker assumption that it would suffice for the generation of subjective experiences that the computational processes of a human brain are structurally replicated in suitably fine-grained detail, such as on the level of individual synapses."

Your plan should treat this passage as the hinge of the whole session. In particular, address:

1. The order of ideas. What must be in place before the passage can even be parsed — what do they need about substrate-independence, about the trilemma structure of the conclusion, and about the indifference-style step that turns "many simulated minds" into "we are probably simulated" — and in what sequence should those pieces be introduced so that the passage lands as a modest, defensible assumption rather than as the moment the argument smuggles in the whole conclusion? Explain the dependencies: why each piece must precede the next.

2. The carrying example. Propose one concrete example that will carry the passage's claim for this audience — some way of making vivid what it would mean for subjective experience to depend only on the structure of a computation rather than on carbon and craniums — and say what the example must have and must lack. I am worried that obvious candidates (brain-in-a-vat scenarios, The Matrix, uploading stories) drag in exactly the confusions I want to avoid; either justify one of them or construct something better.

3. The misconception map. List the misconceptions this audience will predictably form, and for each one specify exactly where in your sequence I should head it off. I expect at least these, but correct me or add others: that the argument claims to prove we are simulated rather than offering a disjunction; that the passage commits Bostrom to thinking a sufficiently convincing chatbot is conscious; that the argument is just Descartes' demon or brain-in-a-vat scepticism rebranded; and that "almost certainly" in the third disjunct is a rhetorical flourish rather than the output of a counting argument over observers.

4. The check. Give me one question I can pose near the end — not a comprehension quiz but something whose answer would reveal whether the group has grasped that the passage states a weak sufficient condition, not a theory of consciousness — and tell me what a good answer sounds like versus an answer that merely parrots the vocabulary.

5. The omissions. Say what I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level (the exact probability formalism? the objections literature? anthropic reasoning in general?) and how to say so without making the omissions feel like evasions.

Keep the plan concrete enough that I could teach from it directly.
