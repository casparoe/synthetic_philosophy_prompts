After several years of moving back and forth between classical utilitarianism and various suffering-focused views, I've settled on a version of tranquilism: nothing is intrinsically good in the sense of adding positive value to a life or a world; states of consciousness are valuable only insofar as they are free of craving, disturbance, and suffering, and what we ordinarily call happy or blissful experiences are good only instrumentally, as means of relieving or preventing such disturbances. On this view, a perfectly untroubled mind is not missing anything that a euphoric mind has, and there is no positive value that would be lost if no sentient beings existed at all. I'm aware this has bite-sized counterintuitive consequences, and I accept some of them.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure. What would count against tranquilism, concretely? Distinguish, first, the intuitive cases that merely look bad but that a committed tranquilist can absorb without damage (and say why they can absorb them), from the cases that genuinely threaten the view -- for instance, if there turned out to be states of positive wellbeing that are not analyzable as the absence of disturbance, what would that do to the view, and is that even an empirical question or a purely introspective one? Second, consider whether tranquilism actually delivers the asymmetry it is often motivated by (that creating suffering is bad but failing to create happiness is not), or whether it quietly needs extra premises to get there, such that a rival view with the same extra premises would be equally well off. Third, I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one, indicate whether a thoughtful defender of the view would concede it as a genuine cost or dismiss it as confusion -- and in the latter case, what their best dismissing move is and whether it works. Finally, tell me what I, as someone who finds the introspective phenomenology of craving persuasive but has no strong intuitions about non-existence, should regard as the most persuasive single objection.
