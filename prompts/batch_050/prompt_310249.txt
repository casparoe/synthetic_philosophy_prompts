In my reporting on philosophy of language I keep running into a dispute that has been stuck for decades: epistemicists, following Sorensen and Williamson, insist that vague words like "heap" and "bald" have perfectly sharp boundaries that we are in principle unable to locate, while supervaluationists hold that such words admit many equally legitimate sharpenings, with truth amounting to truth on all of them. The standard moves are all old — the incredulous stare at an unknowable cutoff on one side, the collapse of "definitely" into higher-order vagueness on the other — and neither camp has budged. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about vagueness at all but about what a semantic theory is permitted to postulate: one side treats meaning as a fact that can outrun any possible access to it, the other treats meaning as exhausted by use. Assess that diagnosis against what each side actually argues, and tell me whether any conceivable evidence would discriminate between them — say, a psycholinguistic finding that speakers treat borderline verdicts as low-confidence guesses rather than as encounters with genuine indeterminacy, or a formal result about the logic of definiteness — or whether the impasse instead shows that the question of whether there is a sharp boundary was never a single question to begin with.
