I am planning a literary novel about public memory and need its central moral dilemma to be philosophically exact rather than merely evocative. Construct a thought experiment in which it is at least defensible to say that a nation remains guilty of a historical atrocity even though no living citizen is personally guilty of it.

The scenario must satisfy these conditions:

- The atrocity occurred more than a century ago and was deliberately carried out through durable state institutions, not merely by a mob or by officials acting privately.
- Every person who ordered, executed, knowingly facilitated, or was legally punished for the atrocity is dead.
- The present population includes immigrants, descendants of the victims, and people whose ancestors opposed the atrocity, so the case cannot trade on guilt by bloodline or ethnic continuity.
- All traceable financial gains have been repaid, surviving property claims have been settled, and the official historical record has been corrected. Call this restitution saturation.
- The present state is not continuing the original policy, suppressing the evidence, or denying the established facts.
- Nevertheless, the state actively claims continuity with the earlier nation when honoring its founders, celebrating its achievements, and presenting itself on the world stage, while insisting that apology for the atrocity would be meaningless because nobody alive committed it. Call this selective continuity.
- Include one concrete act available to the current government—an official apology, a renunciation of honors, a constitutional acknowledgment, or a comparably public institutional act—that would not compensate anyone materially.

Make the scenario realistic enough to anchor a novel: no surviving secret perpetrator, no discovery that changes who committed the atrocity, and no speculative political theory. Keep the narrative portion under 250 words.

After presenting it, demonstrate that the construction works. Distinguish personal moral guilt, inherited guilt by descent, benefit-based responsibility, and corporate or political guilt. Then give the verdicts produced by at least three positions: an individualist view on which only persons can be guilty; a collective-agency view on which institutions can remain morally answerable across complete demographic turnover; and a beneficiary view on which descendants may owe repair without inheriting guilt. Identify the exact feature that makes these positions diverge, and say what changes if selective continuity is removed.

Finally, give the strongest objection to describing the nation itself as guilty rather than merely saying that its current officials have a present duty of acknowledgment. Conclude with your own verdict, including whether official apology would express inherited guilt, institutional responsibility, or only respect for the victims.
