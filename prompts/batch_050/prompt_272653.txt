I teach an undergraduate seminar on rational choice and politics, and every year, without fail, a student raises their hand during the week on the paradox of voting and says: but Florida in 2000 proves every vote counts -- Bush won the state, and with it the presidency, by a certified margin of 537 votes out of roughly six million cast. I want to give this objection the serious treatment it deserves rather than the dismissive one I usually improvise, so I'm asking for a careful analysis of the 2000 Florida recount as a test case for the paradox of voting. Take as the theory the standard Downsian pivotal-voter calculus and its best-known repairs: the Riker--Ordeshook civic-duty term, expressive voting, minimax regret, and the altruistic or social-multiplier model on which a tiny pivot probability is multiplied by the enormous stakes for the whole electorate. Take as the episode the familiar public facts: an extremely close election, saturation media coverage of the recount, litigation ending in a Supreme Court decision, and turnout nationally of only about half the eligible population despite all of it.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, not a sermon about civic virtue. Start with the theory itself, then do the mapping honestly: who is the decision-maker in the model, what the act of voting is supposed to buy, and -- here is the part my students always get wrong -- what the pivot probability in Florida actually was, given that a single ballot decides an election only when the margin is zero or one, so that even in a 537-vote race no individual voter was in fact pivotal. Then run the analysis in both directions: what the episode confirms about the theory (for instance, that closeness at the state level does not rescue the instrumental calculus, and that the truly consequential actors turned out to be election officials, lawyers, and judges rather than voters), what it misses or misdescribes (did the perception of a near-tie change behavior in ways the model can or cannot capture, and does the altruist model survive contact with the actual turnout numbers?), and finally what the episode shows about the theory -- anomaly, confirmation, or a case it was never designed to cover.

Please structure your response under exactly these headings, in this order: "The theory in brief" (about 150 words), "The mapping: voters, options, and pivot probability" (about 250 words), "What the episode confirms" (about 150 words), "Where the model breaks" (about 250 words), and "Verdict: does Florida rescue 'every vote counts'?" (about 200 words, and take a definite position, including what I should say to the next student who raises their hand).
