I recently joined a small AI policy institute where the default framing for transformative AI is the nuclear weapons analogy: Manhattan Project, arms race, nonproliferation treaties. At next week's internal seminar I will argue that the printing press is the better historical analogy for how advanced AI's effects will actually unfold — a general-purpose technology whose large consequences arrived slowly, indirectly, and mostly unchosen by its inventors — and that the nuclear analogy should be demoted to a secondary case. The room will be about half historians of technology, who are generally allergic to reasoning from single historical cases, and half policy staff who have built their mental models on the nuclear frame and find it actionable.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. List the eight or so objections I will actually get from this mixed audience, ordered by how much damage each would do, and phrase each the way that half of the room would put it: a historian's worry about selection effects or about the contingent, centuries-long diffusion of print sounds different from a policy person's worry that the analogy offers no lever to pull. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply, what I should concede up front, and where the reply is weakest. Finish with the objection I am hoping nobody asks — the one that targets the whole enterprise of historical analogy for AI rather than my choice between these two cases.
