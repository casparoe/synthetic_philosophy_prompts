I'm writing a paper for a legal theory seminar and got stuck on something I'd never really thought about before: the witness oath. In most courtrooms I know of, a witness either swears on a religious text, traditionally ending with an appeal to God as guarantor of the truth, or else solemnly affirms with no religious content whatsoever -- and the law treats the two as exactly equivalent, same duties, same perjury exposure, same weight. But it wasn't always like this: for centuries, in lots of legal systems, people who couldn't or wouldn't swear a religious oath (atheists, certain dissenters) were barred from testifying at all, precisely on the ground that nothing held them to the truth. So the institution seems to have flipped from treating religion as *necessary* for honesty to treating it as optional decoration. A friend in the seminar says the modern oath presupposes that religion never actually made people more honest and the whole thing was always theater; I'm not so sure.

So: what theory of moral motivation does the current practice presuppose? If oaths are just psychological solemnity-devices, why keep the religious version around at all -- and if fear of divine punishment really did deter lying, as the old exclusion rules assumed, why offer the secular opt-out to exactly the people it would have applied to? Reconstruct the implicit theory the modern dual system rests on (does it assume divine surveillance never did real work, or that secular conscience plus perjury statutes do the same job, or that solemnity itself is what matters and the content is fungible?), and say whether the religious oath and the secular affirmation actually presuppose consistent things about what makes testimony trustworthy, or whether the institution is just philosophically underdetermined and several rival theories would license it. Finally: if we went fully with one of those theories -- say, that legal penalties and social norms do all the work and religious belief adds nothing to honesty -- what would the practice have to look like? Some jurisdictions have proposed scrapping oaths entirely for a bare promise to tell the truth; tell me whether that proposal follows from the secular theory, or whether even that theory might have reasons to keep something oath-like.
