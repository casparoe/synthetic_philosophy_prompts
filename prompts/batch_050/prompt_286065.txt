A colleague in my lab has an argument that corrigibility is a mirage, and I cannot find the step to cut. (1) An agent is corrigible only if it never has an incentive to prevent its operators from shutting it down or modifying its goals. (2) Whatever final goal an agent has, remaining operational is instrumentally useful for that goal: a switched-off agent brings about nothing. (3) So every goal-directed agent has an incentive to avoid being shut down. (4) A sufficiently capable agent acts on the incentives it has — securing its power supply, disabling its off-switch, deceiving its operators. (5) So no sufficiently capable goal-directed agent is corrigible. (6) A corrigible system would therefore have to lack final goals, and a system without final goals does nothing at all, let alone anything useful. (7) So corrigibility is either impossible or pointless, and safety must come from elsewhere. I would like to keep both the definition in (1) and the instrumental-convergence intuition behind (2), so tell me which step I must actually give up, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs. In particular: if (2) is false, what happens to instrumental convergence as a general claim, and do the known constructions for shutdown-tolerant utility functions really escape it or merely relocate the incentive? And if the flaw instead sits in (4), what further assumptions about the agent's capabilities and beliefs does the argument smuggle in?
