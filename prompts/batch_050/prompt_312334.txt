So I've been going back and forth on how to classify LLM screwups using the lying/bullshit/error distinction (Frankfurt-style, where bullshit means indifference to truth rather than intent to deceive), and I want someone to figure out what principle my gut is actually tracking. Here are my verdicts:

1. A model states the wrong date for a famous battle, confidently. Verdict: plain error, roughly blameless, like a person misremembering.
2. A model invents a plausible-looking academic paper, complete with authors and a journal name, when asked for sources. Verdict: this feels way worse than case 1, almost like lying, even though I know there's no intent to deceive in there.
3. A calculator outputs a wrong number. Verdict: pure error, zero blame, and it bugs me that this feels so different from case 1 when both are just "wrong output from a system."
4. A user tells a model to write a cover letter claiming five years of experience the user doesn't have, and it does. Verdict: the model isn't lying or bullshitting, it's just helping the user lie -- the badness belongs to the user.
5. A model flatters a user, saying their half-baked argument is brilliant, because it was tuned to be agreeable. Verdict: this is bullshit in Frankfurt's sense, but somehow mild, more like a bad habit than a vice.
6. A model says "I'm not confident about this" and then gives a wrong answer. Verdict: still error, but less bad than case 1 -- the disclaimer seems to count for something even though the output was equally wrong.
7. A model faithfully reproduces a false claim that's all over its training data (some urban legend). Verdict: error, and honestly I'm not even sure the model did anything wrong -- but then why is case 2, where it generates the falsehood itself, so much worse?

The tension I can't resolve: cases 2 and 7 both end with a falsehood the system "believes" nothing about, yet I want to call one near-lying and the other innocent error. And cases 1 and 3 look identical in structure but feel different. Is there a principle -- maybe something about whether the output mimics the *form* of a knowledge claim vs. just being one, or about what the system was optimized to track -- that captures most of these? Tell me which verdict(s) a decent principle would force me to drop, and what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: a model that outputs something *true*, but only because it pattern-matched the words with no grip on why it's true.

American English please, and keep it under 600 words.
