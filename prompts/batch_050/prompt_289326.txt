Ahead of a graduate seminar I am teaching on Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life, I have drafted a short statement of my own interpretive and normative commitments for the students to attack. Before circulating it, I would like an audit of whether the positions actually cohere.

The statement is organized around a particular stretch of the essay's treatment of monumental history (in section 2), which I take to be the pivot of Nietzsche's whole case. Paraphrasing rather than quoting: Nietzsche says there that monumental history deceives by means of analogies, that through seductive similarities it tempts the courageous into rashness and the enthusiast into fanaticism, that it achieves its effect only by suppressing the differences between past and present circumstances and motives, and that it is in this way a standing danger to the powerful and active who make use of it. I take the important feature of this passage to be that his complaint against monumental history concerns what it does to those who consume it, not any failure on its part to get the past right.

My commitments, as drafted, are these:

1. The three modes of history Nietzsche distinguishes — monumental, antiquarian, and critical — answer to three distinct needs of life, and no mode is correct or mistaken in itself. The legitimacy of a mode is wholly indexed to the condition of the individual or culture that deploys it.

2. Correspondingly, the worth of any historiography is exhausted by its service to life. Questions of accuracy or fidelity to the past enter only instrumentally, insofar as misrepresenting the past happens to impair life.

3. On precisely those grounds, the monumental public history currently being revived in various nationalist movements is to be resisted: what discredits it are its fabrications and distortions of the historical record.

4. Our own age suffers from the hypertrophy of historical knowledge that Nietzsche diagnoses. The historiography we should therefore want is a standing, institutionalized critical scrutiny of our inherited past — permanent commissions, curricula, and public rituals devoted to passing judgment on it.

5. The unhistorical — the capacity to forget, to act within a bounded horizon — is no less necessary to the health of a culture than any of the three historical modes; and this sits comfortably with my commitment that critical history is what our age most needs.

Tell me which pairs among these are in genuine tension and which only appear to be. For each genuine tension, say how severe it is, trace exactly what commits me to what — including where the passage paraphrased above bears on the conflict — and identify the cheapest revision that would remove it. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and conclude by naming the single revision that would clean up the most, together with what it costs.
