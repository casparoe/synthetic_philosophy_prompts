I teach a mixed undergraduate course called Evidence and the Deep Past (about 18 students, second-year level, one 90-minute session), and I want a classroom activity built around an episode in Herodotus. In the Histories, Book II, chapter 143, Herodotus recounts that when the writer Hecataeus visited Thebes and boasted of a family line reaching back to a god in the sixteenth generation, the priests of the temple answered him by leading him into a hall containing hundreds of wooden statues, each one representing a successive chief priest, son succeeding father, and counting them off: so many generations of mortal men, they argued, and not a god among them — the implied conclusion being that Hecataeus's divine genealogy collapsed against an unbroken, physically inspectable series of records. Herodotus pointedly adds that he himself was shown the same demonstration and had made no such claim. Please do not quote this passage back to me or build on any specific translation; take the paraphrase above as the text we are working from.

Design an activity in which my students do not hear this story up front but instead reproduce its structure themselves: they must try to establish or refute a claim about an event supposedly several decades before they were born, using only an evidence pack I hand them, and the decisive move available to them should be the priests' move — an appeal to a continuous, countable series of records that outlasts memory — so that when I finally read them the Hecataeus episode afterwards, they recognize what they did. The evidence pack should be designed so that the activity also generates the complications a source critic cares about: at least two of the written accounts should secretly derive from one another rather than being independent, the most precise and confident source should be the least trustworthy, and the apparently continuous series should contain one gap that students must decide how to treat. Give me concrete roles or team structure, the exact contents of the evidence pack, rules for how students may interrogate and combine sources, a scoring or verdict procedure that rewards calibrated confidence rather than boldness, and a timeline for the 90 minutes.

Then provide the debrief plan: how each moment of the activity maps onto the Herodotean contrast between seeing for oneself and reporting what is said, onto the difference between independent attestation and mere copying, and onto the seduction of precise numbers (a count of 345 statues feels like proof — should it?). Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I suspect students will rank the oldest document as the most reliable and will miss the planted contamination entirely — and how to handle it. Be frank about which feature of the real historiographical problem the activity cannot reproduce (I assume the true temporal distance, and the fact that real ancient historians never know what evidence has simply vanished, are candidates) and what I must supply by lecture instead. Finally, since I am asking for a design rather than a verdict on a scholarly question, do the nearest equivalent of stating your epistemic position: indicate how confident you are that each component will produce its intended effect, and single out the one element of the design you are least sure will work as planned.
