I'm TAing a philosophy of psychology seminar this semester (it's on delusion, mostly the two-factor theory stuff and the question of whether delusions are even beliefs). My professor has this habit of running case polls where she gives her own verdict after we argue, and I want to predict what she'll say about a new case before class. Here are her verdicts so far, as best as I've recorded them:

1. Classic Capgras: patient insists his wife has been replaced by an impostor, moved out, changed the locks, filed for seperation, gets visibly distressed when she visits. Prof: yes, that's a belief. A delusional one, but a belief.

2. Cotard-ish case: patient says he's dead and doesn't exist, but keeps eating three meals a day, complains the hospital food is bland, shows up to group therapy on time. Prof: not a belief. Those are just words coming out; nothing downstream cares about them.

3. A milder Capgras variant: patient says his wife "feels like a stranger" and that something is wrong, but when pressed says she must be his wife, she has all her memories, it just doesn't feel right. Prof: not a belief, that's someone honestly reporting an experience, not committing to a claim.

4. Korsakoff's patient who tells the doctor they met at the pub last week, tells a great story about it, then two minutes later cheerfully agrees he's never left the ward and has never seen the doctor before. Prof: not a belief. Nothing sticks long enough to count as holding anything.

5. Non-clinical case she threw in: an uncle who insists the moon landing was faked, brings it up at every family dinner, buys the books, argues down every objection, but it changes literally nothing else in his life. Prof: belief. Ordinary beliefs are allowed to be idle.

The new case I want to spring on her: a Capgras patient who has maintained the impostor claim for over a year, defends it against every objection with increasingly elaborate stories (she had plastic surgery, she's a government plant), never wavers in asserting it — but still lives with the "impostor," eats dinner with her every night, celebrated their anniversary, shows zero fear or distress, and when asked why he's so calm says "well, what am I supposed to do about it?" Stable and defended like case 1, but behaviorally and emotionally flat in a way case 1 wasn't.

What I want from you: your best prediction of her verdict and why, the closest analog among her earlier calls, and the rule you think she's actually running. But also: her verdicts seem compatible with more than one rule to me (e.g. something like "maintained and defended = belief" vs. something more demanding about integration with the rest of your psychology), so give me at least two candidate rules that fit the track record, say what each predicts for the new case, and tell me which of those two rules you'd bet she's using. Also, what future case poll result would most cleanly tell the two rules apart?

And since I'm asking you to basically guess a person's dispositions from five data points, please flag what would change your mind: which earlier verdict would have to have gone the other way for you to flip your prediction, or where your reconstruction of her rule is most likely to be off.
