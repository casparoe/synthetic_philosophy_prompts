I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and my dissertation keeps running aground on the same problem: I can't work out whether my intuitions about the aggregation of small harms cohere with one another. Here are my verdicts on four cases. I want you to tell me what principle, or small set of principles, captures them — stated precisely enough that I could apply it mechanically to new cases — and to be honest about which of my verdicts, if any, have to go.

Case 1. Either one person is severely tortured for fifty years, or an astronomically large number of people — more than could ever actually exist, say 10^80 — each receive exactly one dust speck in the eye, once, with no knock-on effects whatsoever. My verdict: the dust specks should happen. Moreover, no finite number of specks would change my answer.

Case 2. Either one person suffers debilitating chronic back pain for a decade, or 10^25 people each endure a single miserable hour, roughly as bad as a cramped overnight flight with a headache. My verdict: here the many win. The aggregate of bad hours outweighs the decade of chronic pain.

Case 3. Either a million people each receive one dust speck, or one person receives a million dust specks spread evenly across a lifetime, never compounding. My verdict: these are equally bad. Whether tiny harms are distributed across lives or stacked within one makes no moral difference.

Case 4. Either one specified person certainly gets a migraine tomorrow, or each of a billion people independently faces a one-in-a-billion chance of a migraine tomorrow. My verdict: equally bad. When it comes to risk, expected disvalue is what matters.

My own tentative principle is a lexical threshold: harms below some intensity level never aggregate, in any number, against harms above it. But I can see that Cases 1 and 2 together force the threshold to sit somewhere on a continuum of intensities running from dust speck to bad hour to chronic pain to torture, and any cutoff on that continuum looks sorites-vulnerable — adjacent intensities differ imperceptibly, yet the threshold must fall between two of them. Meanwhile Case 3 seems to rule out person-affecting or contractualist repairs, and Case 4 seems to sit awkwardly with any anti-aggregationist treatment of certainty.

So: test my threshold principle against all four cases and repair it if you can; if you can't, replace it. Then tell me what your preferred principle implies about two cases I haven't listed: (a) one person suffers the pain of a badly broken leg versus 10^18 people each suffering one paper cut; (b) a population in which half receive a dust speck and half receive a bad headache, versus one person tortured. Finally, if no principle saves all four of my verdicts, tell me which one you would actually abandon — I want your committed answer with reasons, not a survey of the available options.
