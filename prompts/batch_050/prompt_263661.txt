I am a novelist, and a literary magazine has asked me to contribute a long essay on identity in fiction -- how characters come to be who they are, and how much of that is written by the world around them rather than by themselves. Before I commit the thing to print, I want a philosophical audit of the commitments I find myself making across the draft, because I have a nagging sense that I am arguing with myself in places and can't locate where. Here is roughly what the essay commits me to. (a) Race is not a natural kind: there is no biological fact of the matter that underwrites racial classification, since human biological variation simply does not carve where the racial categories carve. (b) Race is nonetheless entirely real -- real in the way that borders and recessions are real, because a society organized around it gives it causal powers over who gets hired, followed, housed, and believed. (c) Money is socially constructed through and through, and pointing this out in no way diminishes its reality or its authority over us; constructed things can bind us completely. (d) Gender is also socially constructed, but here I catch myself deploying the claim differently: in the political passages of the essay I treat recognizing gender's constructedness as a deflationary move, as though seeing that a category was made shows it needn't be obeyed. (e) Underlying all of this, I assume there is one general account of construction that covers all three cases: social kinds are produced and sustained by collective acceptance, by enough people representing a thing in the same way. (f) Finally -- this is the novelist in me -- I insist that the best characters are those whose inner lives exceed and resist the categories their society applies to them; there is something in a person that the available labels fail to capture, and fiction's job is to honor it.

Tell me whether this set hangs together. Which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be? For each genuine tension, say how severe it is and what the least costly repair would be. I am particularly interested in whether the thing I say about money and the thing I say about gender can both be true as I use them, and whether my one-size-fits-all theory of construction in (e) actually fits race as I've described it in (a) and (b) -- a category that persists despite widespread collective rejection of it, and that no individual can escape simply by declining to accept it. If some of these tensions dissolve on inspection, show me the dissolution rather than flagging everything equally, and if one single revision would clean up the most, name it. I would rather cut a paragraph from the essay than publish an incoherent one, but I'd like to know which paragraph it has to be.
