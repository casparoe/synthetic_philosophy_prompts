Just had a three-hour argument with my friend Dan and I need a tiebreaker. We got onto cases where a belief seems to get "confirmed" over and over but only because the believer is doing the confirming. His example: he believes people generally like him, so he walks into rooms relaxed and friendly, people respond warmly, and he takes that as more evidence they like him. My example: my aunt believes everything happens for a reason, and whatever happens she finds the reason afterwards, so in her experience the belief never fails. In both cases the belief kind of manufactures the experiences that count as its own evidence. It feels different from a self-fulfilling prophecy (that term seems to be about making predictions come true, not about generating your own evidence), but maybe it isn't?

Dan says this is exactly what William James meant by truth. He dug up this bit from Lecture VI of Pragmatism: "They lead us, namely, through the acts and other ideas which they instigate, into or up to, or towards, other parts of experience with which we feel all the while—such feeling being among our potentialities—that the original ideas remain in agreement. The connexions and transitions come to us from point to point as being progressive, harmonious, satisfactory. This function of agreeable leading is what we mean by an idea's verification."

So my questions: does the phenomenon I described have its own name, and is it actually what James is describing here? Because reading it again, "the acts... which they instigate" sounds like it could cover Dan's case but I'm not sure James would count my aunts case, since her belief doesn't instigate acts so much as interpretations after the fact. If these are two different phenomena, say so and tell me where James's "agreeable leading" draws the line -- or where it should, if he's being more permissive than he should be.
