Question:
Suppose that tonight an unexplained but painless event makes every human being permanently infertile. No one now alive will die sooner or suffer physically as a result, but no further people will be born. Using Scheffler’s afterlife conjecture, explain why this prospect could still matter deeply to people now, what it suggests about the role of humanity’s continuation in present valuing, and why the conjecture should not be taken to show that only permanent or transmissible goods matter.

Student response:
Scheffler’s collective afterlife means the continuation of human life after one’s own death, not personal immortality. His conjecture is that the expectation of such continuation normally operates as a tacit background condition of valuing much of what we value. Art, scholarship, scientific research, conservation, political reform, and the maintenance of institutions are undertaken partly on the assumption that other people will receive, use, or continue them. If everyone became infertile, each living person could still complete a normal lifespan, and no merely possible person would be killed. Nevertheless, many people might experience grief, lose motivation, or find that ambitious projects now seem pointless. That reaction suggests that concern for the human future is not just concern that future people have pleasant lives; it is woven into the significance of many present commitments.

Strictly speaking, the event is bad for present people because it frustrates their desire that humanity continue, in the same way that learning that a bequest will fail is bad for the person who made it. Scheffler’s conclusion is therefore that avoiding extinction benefits the current generation by preserving the value of its projects. This also gives merely possible people a claim to be brought into existence, since without them our projects would have no inheritors. On that point, the argument leads to total utilitarianism, according to which outcomes are better when they contain more total happiness.

The conjecture should not be overstated. It is mainly a psychological diagnosis about what sustains much human valuing, not a proof that something matters only if it lasts forever or is handed down. Present relationships, relief of suffering, and some experiences can be valuable even when they produce no later effects, and people differ in how strongly their purposes depend on posterity.
