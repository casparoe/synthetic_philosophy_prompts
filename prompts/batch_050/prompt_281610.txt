I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a long piece on how societies weigh many small burdens against a few catastrophic ones — everything from drug-pricing policy to disaster triage keeps coming back to this. In reading up for it, I've been testing my own moral instincts against the standard worry about contractualist moral theories: that if right and wrong turn on whether any single individual could reasonably reject a principle, then it seems mysterious how the sheer number of affected people could ever matter, since each person's complaint is considered one at a time. I want to know whether my verdicts below can be unified by some principle, or whether I'm just inconsistent. Here they are. A rescuer can save one person from drowning or five strangers from drowning elsewhere, and can't do both: she should save the five, and it would be wrong not to. A rescuer can save one person's life or spare a hundred thousand people a week of moderate headaches: she should save the life, and I don't think the number of headaches changes that. A broadcaster can either continue a World Cup transmission that millions are watching or interrupt it to rescue a worker electrocuted in the transmitter room: obviously rescue the worker. A health board with a fixed budget can either fund a treatment that saves the lives of three patients or a treatment that cures permanent paralysis in three hundred: I find myself wanting to fund the paralysis treatment, even though death is surely a worse fate for each of the three than paralysis is for each of the three hundred. And finally: if the choice is between curing one person of paralysis and curing ten million people of a sore throat that lasts a day, I genuinely can't settle on a verdict — some mornings I think the sore throats must win at some number, other mornings I think that's a rationalization.

Is there a principle — or a small family of principles — that captures most of these verdicts and tells me what to do with the ones it can't? I'd like it stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing the work in which case: for instance, whether the relevant notion is a threshold of seriousness below which claims simply don't register, a pairwise comparison with some aggregative tie-breaker, or something else I haven't thought of. In particular, tell me what the principle says about the paralysis-versus-lives case, since that's the verdict I'm least confident about, and about a case I haven't listed: a government choosing between preventing one accidental death and preventing a million people from losing an hour to airport security delays. If no principle can save all my verdicts without collapsing into case-by-case ad hocery, say which verdict you'd overturn and why, rather than quietly rescuing them all with epicycles.
