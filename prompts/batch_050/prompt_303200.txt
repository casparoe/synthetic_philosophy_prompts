I'm in a graduate seminar on assertion and self-knowledge, and our professor has spent the semester building a taxonomy of Moore-paradoxical sentences — sentences that may well be true, and that other people can utter without any impropriety, but that are somehow absurd for the speaker to assert, or even to think, in their own case. Over the term she has ruled on the following eight cases:

1. "It is raining, but I do not believe that it is." — paradoxical.
2. "It is raining, but I believe that it is not." — paradoxical.
3. "It is raining, but I doubt it." — paradoxical.
4. "It is raining, but you do not believe it." — perfectly fine.
5. "It was raining, but I did not believe it at the time." — perfectly fine.
6. A woman silently thinking to herself, while watching the rain through the window, "It's raining, but I don't believe it." — paradoxical.
7. "It is raining, but nobody believes it." — perfectly fine.
8. "I do not believe it is raining, but it is." — paradoxical.

Next week she has promised to rule on two new cases, and a few of us have a small wager riding on the outcome. The first: "It is raining, but I do not know that it is." The second: "If it is raining right now, then I do not believe that it is," said by someone ruefully reflecting on their own miserable track record of failing to notice rain.

Please predict her verdict on each of the two new cases. More important than the predictions themselves: tell me what general rule she appears to be running — some principle about where the absurdity lives, whether in the content believed, in the act of asserting or judging, or in what a speaker represents about herself — that would generate all eight verdicts above. Then give me at least one alternative rule that fits all eight verdicts equally well but delivers the opposite verdict on at least one of the new cases, and explain why nothing in her track record so far excludes it. Tell me which of the eight earlier verdicts does the most work in constraining the choice between these candidate rules, and why. Finally, propose one further test sentence, one she has not yet ruled on, whose verdict would most cleanly discriminate between the two most plausible rules. I am less interested in the correct theory of Moore's paradox than in how much of her view is actually fixed by her track record — and how much of it we have merely been assuming.
