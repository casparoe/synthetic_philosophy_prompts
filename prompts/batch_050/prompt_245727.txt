I've never studied philosophy formally, but for the past year or so I've been obsessing over a puzzle I first ran into in a newspaper column, and I'd like your help turning my scattered thoughts into something organized. The puzzle is a rescue case. Imagine you're on a cliff and you can throw a rope either to five strangers trapped on one ledge or to one stranger trapped on another; there's no time to do both, and nothing else distinguishes the two groups. My question is what you ought to do, and more importantly why.

From thinking on my own and reading around a bit, I've collected a handful of answers people seem to give. Some say you simply save the five, because more good is better than less and that's the end of it. I have a vague memory that a philosopher named Taurek once argued something closer to flipping a coin, so that each of the six people gets an equal chance, though I may be misremembering the details and I'd rather you correct me than trust me. I've also come up with two ideas of my own that I haven't managed to place. The first is a ticket system: give every trapped person one ticket, draw a ticket at random, and save whichever group the winner belongs to, so the five get five times the odds of the one but the one isn't written off entirely. The second is a threshold rule: when the numbers are close, say five against four, flip a coin, but past some ratio, say a hundred against one, just save the larger group outright. I'm also unsure how the answers should shift when the rescuer knows one of the trapped people personally, or when the rescuer is the one who caused the danger in the first place.

What I'd like from you is a decision tree that starts from someone facing the basic case and leads, through a series of yes-or-no questions, to a definite position. Each question should be about the situation itself rather than about theory, so that a person could answer it without knowing any labels: questions like whether losing one life is the same kind of loss whether it's suffered alone or alongside four others, or whether giving everyone an equal chance is itself a way of respecting people equally. Each endpoint of the tree should carry the name of the position if philosophers actually defend one there (and please say plainly when a combination of answers lands somewhere nobody defends, along with your best guess as to whether that's because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored). For every endpoint, add one sentence on the most awkward consequence of holding it. Please also tell me where my ticket system and my threshold rule end up on the tree, whether anyone actually defends either of them, and flag any pair of endpoints your tree can't genuinely separate. One more thing: I want to send the result to a friend as a long letter, so write the whole thing as continuous prose, walking the reader through the tree question by question, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
