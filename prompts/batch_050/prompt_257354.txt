So I retired two years ago after forty years as a chemist, and having far too much time on my hands I've started working through a popular book on set theory. Lovely stuff. But I've got properly stuck on something and the answer I keep getting fobbed off with is a slogan, and I'd like to know what the slogan actually means.

Here's my problem. The book shows me the standard von Neumann way of building the numbers out of sets, where each number is the set of all the ones before it, so 2 = {0, 1} and 4 = {0, 1, 2, 3}. On that construction, 2 is literally a member of 4. Fine. But then the book mentions, in a footnote, an older construction (Zermelo's, I think?) where each number is built differently, and on that one 2 is NOT a member of 4. Both constructions apparently do all the work arithmetic needs. So I asked at my philosophy reading group: which is it, really? Is 2 an element of 4 or not? It seems like there should be a fact of the matter.

The response I got from the two philosophy PhD students who run the group was basically a shrug plus "mathematics is the science of structure", said in the tone of voice that settles things. When I pressed them, they said my question was 'ill-posed' and changed the subject.

What are the different things that slogan could actually be claiming? I suspect it ranges from something harmless (mathematicians mostly care about structural facts) to something much stronger (numbers have no identity beyond their position in a structure, so my question is meaningless) and maybe stronger still. Lay the readings out as a list, from the trivial to the radical, tell me which ones are actually defensible, and crucially which of them would justify dismissing my question rather than answering it. I want to go back next thursday with something to push back with.
