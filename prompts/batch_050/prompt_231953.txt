Next week I am running the discussion section for an undergraduate medieval philosophy course, and my single hour is on Abelard's Ethics — specifically his claim that sin consists neither in desiring something forbidden nor in doing it, but solely in the will's consent to what one takes to be contempt for God. The students will have read only a short excerpt in translation; they are sharp but historically naïve, and most will arrive assuming medieval ethics is a matter of divine commands and rule-following.

Do not explain the theory to me; plan how I should explain it. Specifically:

1. Order of ideas. What has to be in place before "consent" can do any work? My own thought is that they first need a three-way distinction between wanting, consenting, and doing, since ordinary English blurs all three into "intention." Lay out the sequence and state what each step depends on.

2. The carrying example. I plan to build the hour on a single case: two people performing the identical outward act, one sinning and one not. Tell me what the act should be, what the example must include and must exclude (for instance, it must not let students explain the difference by appeal to the outcome, the desire behind the act, or its consequences), and whether I would do better with a case of consent without deed — someone who fully consents but is prevented from acting — to make the point that the deed itself adds nothing to the sin.

3. The misconception map. The misunderstandings I should expect, in the order they will surface, and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off. My guesses: that Abelard means "intention" in the modern sense of purpose or foresight, so that foreseen side effects would count as sins; that he must be saying desires and temptations are themselves sinful; that he is committed to actions not mattering at all; and that "consent" here means what it means in contemporary debates about consent. Correct me where I am wrong and add what I have missed.

4. A point where the theory should be allowed to bite. I want one moment where the view surprises the students rather than being domesticated into something familiar. My candidates are the claim that consent alone completes the sin even when no act follows, and the claim that acting against one's own conscience is sin even when the conscience is mistaken. Tell me which to use and where in the hour it belongs.

5. Check for understanding. One question or fresh case I can pose at the end whose answer shows they grasp the theory well enough to apply it rather than paraphrase it.

6. What to leave out. Which adjacent material I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level — the Augustinian background, the problem of the invincibly ignorant conscience, later scholastic uptake — and how to flag the omission without inviting it.

Open your reply with a single sentence stating the plan's central through-line — the one thing students must be able to do by the end of the hour — and then give the plan, with no preamble and no restating of this request.
