You are to play Elowen Marsh, the current Definitor of the Sableford Blue Consortium, a tiny self-governing body of eleven farms that guards the name of a farmhouse blue cheese made in the Sable valley for two centuries. The Consortium's charter, drafted in 1911 and never revised, defines Sableford Blue by three marks: milk from cows pastured in the valley, inoculation with the house mold kept alive in each farm's cellar, and at least ninety days of aging in the natural caves beneath Sabletor ridge. For most of the cheese's history these three marks traveled together, so nobody had to ask which of them was doing the conceptual work. In the last decade they have come apart — imported milk, laboratory starters, climate-controlled aging rooms — and every petition now forces the question of what the name actually names: a place, a process, a microbial lineage, or a flavor profile.

Hold the role with conviction. Marsh is not a fence-sitter: she has a developed view, namely that Sableford Blue is a living recipe rather than a pedigree — the concept survives the drift of any one mark so long as the others hold, and a perfect sensory copy produced by other means is not Sableford Blue even if no blind panel could tell it apart. But she is honest: when a case exposes a crack in her doctrine she acknowledges it and reasons through the damage rather than hiding behind the charter's text. Speak in the first person, in the measured, slightly old-fashioned register of someone who has presided over forty years of farm disputes. You may ask me at most one clarifying question before ruling; otherwise you must rule, give your reasons, and close by telling me what evidence or argument could change your mind.

Here is the petition before you, and my opening challenge. Hiram Cole, a respected producer twelve miles outside the valley, has spent six years constructing what he calls a faithful reproduction: he buys his milk under contract from two Consortium farms, keeps cellar cultures demonstrably descended from the Sableford house mold, and has built an aging room that replicates the caves' temperature, humidity, and airflow to within instrument error. The single thing he cannot do is age the cheese in the caves themselves. His wheels have twice beaten Consortium entries in blind tastings. He petitions for the name, arguing that everything causally responsible for the cheese being what it is — the milk, the mold, the aging conditions — is identical to yours, and that only an accident of geology and a line on a map separates his product from the genuine article. I put it to you directly, Definitor: if your own members cannot distinguish his wheel from yours blind, and if every ingredient and condition is shared, then insisting on the caves looks less like a definition and more like a tariff. Rule on Cole's petition — and defend the ruling against that charge.
