Hey, so I help run a monthly rationalist-adjacent meetup (call it 8-14 people, mix of grad students and software people, we get a room for about 90 minutes) and I want to run a session on whether "generations" are actually a thing. You know the genre of discourse: endless thinkpieces about Gen Z's work ethic, Boomers ruining housing, millennials and avocado toast, and every time it comes up in our group someone says "generations aren't real, it's all astrology for people who read the Atlantic" and someone else says "no wait, obviously growing up through 2008 / COVID / smartphones did something." Both takes feel lazy to me and I want an activity that breaks both of them a little.

Here's the wrinkle I most want people to feel in their bones, not just hear in a debrief: the age-period-cohort problem. Roughly: if you observe that some attitude correlates with when someone was born, you can't cleanly tell whether it's (a) an aging effect (everyone gets more X as they get older, regardless of cohort), (b) a period effect (the times changed everyone at once, e.g. 2008), or (c) a genuine cohort effect (people who were 15-25 during event E carry a permanent mark). And the brutal part is that these three are mathematically entangled -- since your age at time T is just T minus your birth year, no amount of cross-sectional data on age, year, and cohort can fully separate them. That's a really underappreciated point and I don't want to lecture it at people; I want them to walk into the wall themselves.

So: act as if you're a sociologist who moonlights designing classroom games and structured activities. Think carefully and brainstorm a couple of different possible formats before settling on one -- feel free to show me the brainstorm briefly so I can see why you picked the winner.

What I want designed:

1. A runnable activity for ~10 people, 90 minutes, no materials beyond paper/whiteboard. Ideally in phase one the participants themselves construct a generational scheme -- they get some plausible data (you should invent it, and it should be constructed so that it LOOKS like it supports clean generational kinds) and have to draw the boundaries, name the cohorts, and make predictions. Then in phase two something should go wrong for them: they get new data or a new task (e.g., asked to determine whether a trend is aging vs. cohort from a table) and discover the identification problem by failing at it, before I ever name it. The failure has to be genuinely forced by the structure of the data, not by me tricking them.

2. Constructed data that actually does this. Please include the actual tables/numbers they'd work with, and double check that the numbers really are ambiguous between the hypotheses -- I'd be embarrassed if the group could legitimately crack it in five minutes.

3. A debrief guide that maps specific moments of the activity onto the concepts: nominalism vs. realism about social kinds, the impressionable-years idea as the best candidate mechanism for cohort realism, period vs. lifecycle effects, and the identification problem itself. Including what a "generation" would have to be like to count as a REAL kind rather than an arbitrary bin (I'm thinking something like: clusters that hold together because of a mechanism, not because Pew drew a line at 1981).

4. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I already foresee one guy saying "just get panel data," so tell me how to handle that and two or three other failure modes.

5. An honest note about which feature of the real problem the activity can't reproduce. I suspect the big one is that in the activity I control the data-generating process and real researchers never do, but maybe you see a deeper gap.

One constraint: no slides, no lecture up front. They shouldn't hear the phrase "age-period-cohort" until the debrief. If some part of the design requires me to fib to participants (e.g., implying the data is real survey data), flag it and tell me how much deception is actually needed.
