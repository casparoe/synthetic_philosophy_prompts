I'm working through the nonidentity problem — the difficulty that arises for choices affecting who will exist (Parfit's cases of the fourteen-year-old girl and the choice between conservation and depletion): the people who end up badly off cannot say they were made worse off, since they would not have existed at all had the alternative been chosen, and their lives are worth living.

The approaches to the problem that I already know and do not need repeated, in the form in which I understand them:

1. Dissolution by person-affecting strictness. Accept that no wrong is done: an act harms or wrongs someone only by making that person worse off than he or she would otherwise have been, and existence with a life worth living is never worse than nonexistence. The counterintuitive verdicts are simply to be accepted.

2. Impersonal replacement (Parfit's Principle Q and its relatives). Keep a comparative framework but relativize it to outcomes rather than persons: an outcome is worse if the people who live in it are worse off than the different people who would have lived instead, at least when the same number live either way. The wrongness is explained by outcome-badness, not by harm to anyone.

3. De dicto wrongness (Hare). The wrong is done to whoever exists, de dicto: the property of being wronged by the choice attaches to the role of the person who will exist rather than to a fixed individual, so it is no objection that different individuals would fill that role under different choices.

4. Wronging without harming (Shiffrin, Kumar). Comparative harm is not necessary for wrongdoing: one can wrong a person by creating them in a way that imposes serious burdens for inadequate reasons, or by violating a duty owed to them concerning the conditions of their creation, even though they are not worse off than under any alternative.

5. Non-comparative harm (Harman). Being made worse off is not the only way of being harmed: causing someone significant pain or disability harms them whether or not the alternative for that very person was nonexistence. The wrong in the standard cases consists in imposing such harms for trivial gains.

As far as I can tell, these cover the standard moves, so I am asking for something genuinely new: a sixth approach whose structure differs from all five — in particular, not another impersonal-badness principle with different details, not another account of non-comparative harm, and not a hybrid of two items above. State the approach precisely enough that its application to the fourteen-year-old case and the depletion case is determinate; show that the verdicts it yields in those cases follow from its structure rather than being stipulated; and conclude with an honest assessment of whether it survives the most obvious objection to it.
