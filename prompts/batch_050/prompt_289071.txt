Folks, need help untangling something before I teach it next week. Pretend you're a decision theorist who's spent years on intention and practical rationality -- think carefully, and feel free to brainstorm a couple of candidate diagnoses before you commit to one.

Quick context: Kavka's toxin puzzle. An eccentric billionaire will wire a million dollars into your account tonight if, at midnight, you sincerely intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin won't kill you, but it will make you violently ill for a day. The payout depends only on whether you hold the intention at midnight -- come morning the money is yours to keep whether or not you drink, and the billionaire is indifferent to whether you actually drink. Standard take: you can't form the intention, because you know full well that once the money's in the bank you'll have every reason not to drink, and you can't rationally intend to do what you already know you won't do.

Fine. But I wrote the following argument on the whiteboard in my grad seminar and it seems to prove way too much. It generalizes the toxin point into a claim that can't possibly be right, and none of us could pin down where it breaks. Here it is:

(1) Rationally intending to φ requires believing, at least with decent confidence, that you will in fact φ when the time comes.
(2) A rational agent will φ at a time only if, at that time, they take themselves to have sufficient reason to φ right then.
(3) So you can rationally intend to φ only if you expect that, at the moment of action, you'll have sufficient reason to φ. (from 1, 2)
(4) What you have reason to do at the moment of action is fixed by what φ-ing would then achieve or cost -- not by the fact that you earlier intended to φ. An intention can't manufacture a reason for its own fulfillment, otherwise any resolve, however daft, would bootstrap itself into rationality.
(5) In the toxin case, drinking tomorrow achieves nothing and costs a day of misery, so come afternoon you'll have decisive reason not to drink. By (3), then, you can't rationally intend to drink tonight. That's just Kavka's conclusion, and I want to keep it.
(6) But nearly all of everyday planning has the toxin shape. At the moment of action the local reasons almost never line up with the plan's point: skipping this particular dessert achieves nothing measurable (health comes from the pattern, and one slice won't show up in your bloodwork); getting up at 5:30 this morning buys you one groggy hour; grading tonight rather than tomorrow morning makes no difference anyone will ever notice; keeping a promise when breaking it is undetectable adds nothing, since per (4) the promise itself isn't an extra reason. The benefits attach to having the stable policy or disposition, not to performing the individual act.
(7) So by the same reasoning, you can never rationally intend, in advance, to do any of these things. Rational advance planning is possible only where each act is independently worth doing at the moment of performance. And that's absurd -- we manifestly make and carry out such plans all the time, and a theory of intention that rules them out has definately gone off the rails somewhere.

So: where does the argument actually go wrong? Name the premise or step you'd reject, explain why it looked plausible (it fooled all of us for a good twenty minutes), and tell me what rejecting it costs -- if you go after (4), what you owe me about bootstrapping; if you go after (2), what you owe me about akrasia; if you go after (1), what you owe me about intending things you doubt you'll manage. One constraint: I want to hold onto (5)'s verdict in the toxin case if at all possible, so if your fix forces me to say the intention to drink is perfectly available after all, say that plainly instead of hiding it in a footnote.

And be honest about your epistemic position here: tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis overall, and single out the one step or claim in your own answer you're least sure of, with a sentence on why. I'd rather get a tentative answer that knows where it's shaky than a confident one that doesn't.
