Spent like three hours last night arguing with a friend (she's doing a phd in south asian philosophy, I'm just the person she tests arguments on) about Jain anekāntavāda, and she walked me into a corner I still cant find my way out of. She claims the doctrine refutes itself. Her argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Anekāntavāda says every positive claim about reality is true only from some particular standpoint (a naya), which is why the Jains prefix everything with syāt, "in some sense": in some sense the pot exists, in some sense it doesn't, in some sense it's inexpressible, and so on through the seven predications.

(2) The claim "every claim about reality is true only from a standpoint" is itself a claim about reality.

(3) So it too is true only from some standpoint — and from some other standpoint it's false, i.e. there are claims that hold unconditionally.

(4) But ruling out unconditional one-sided (ekānta) claims is the entire point of the doctrine. That's the mistake every other school is supposed to be making.

(5) So the doctrine faces a dilemma. Assert itself unconditionally and it's self-undermining the same way crude relativism is. Assert itself only conditionally and the Jains have to grant that, from some standpoint, some one-sided view is just plain true — which hands the game to their opponents.

(6) And the obvious escape — saying "in some sense it is, in some sense it isn't" never actually contradicts itself because the syāt qualifier relativizes everything to a respect or standpoint — just makes the doctrine trivial, since literally everyone, including the most dogmatic realist, already agrees that claims hold from some perspectives and not others.

(7) So anekāntavāda is either self-refuting or trivial.

I want to keep (1) and (4) — I'm fairly confident those are just accurate descriptions of the doctrine — so if something gives, it's in (2)–(3) or (6), but I honestly can't see where. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically: if (6) is the weak point, what is the doctrine actually saying that my friend's "trivial" reading leaves out — because she's right that the bare phrase "true from some perspectives" is something nobody denies. And if the reply is that the doctrine governs object-level claims about reality but not itself, why isn't that just an ad hoc exception carved out to save it?
