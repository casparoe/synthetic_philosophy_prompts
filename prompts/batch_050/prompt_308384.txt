So I've been going down a rabbit hole about how the asylum system actually works (long story, started with an argument at a dinner party), and the more I read the more philosophically weird it seems, and I want someone to help me figure out what its underlying theory even is. Here's what strikes me as odd. If a refugee manages to physically reach your border, you're basically stuck with them in a moral and legal sense: the postwar treaties say you can't send them back to somewhere they'll be persecuted (the famous non-refoulement rule), and they get an individualized hearing where they have to prove their personal case. But if that exact same person is still sitting in a camp abroad, you owe them essentially nothing, and states in fact spend enormous effort making sure people never arrive in the first place — visa requirements, fining airlines that fly in undocumented passengers, offshore processing deals, and "safe third country" rules where you can hand someone off to whatever country they happened to pass through on the way. And then there's who counts at all: you qualify if you're persecuted for specific reasons like race, religion, or politics, but fleeing famine, poverty, climate disasters, or often even generalized war doesn't get you in the door, no matter how badly off you are.

Okay, so here's the actual request. Please take on the role of a political philosopher who also knows their way around migration law, and treat this whole patchwork of practices as a text to be interpreted: what theory of responsibility and obligation does it presuppose? I'd like you to think carefully and brainstorm a few candidate views first before committing to a reading. Some things I'd especially like you to weigh in on: what kind of view makes mere *presense* at a border morally load-bearing (is this a Samaritan-style duty that falls on whoever happens to be there?); what the persecution requirement implies about whether claims are triggered by need or by being wronged; and what safe-third-country rules assume about whether responsibility is fungible — dischargeable by any adequate protector — as opposed to attaching to whoever caused the displacement. Also, please distinguish what the system *strictly* presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and flag where it's genuinely underdetermined (i.e. where several rival theories would license the same practice). Then tell me what would concretely have to change if we swapped the implicit view for (a) a causal-accountability view where states whose actions produced a refugee flow owe those people more, or (b) a straightforward needs-based cosmopolitan view. Bottom line at the end: is there one coherent philosophy under all this, or is the system an unstable compromise between two or three incompatible ones?
