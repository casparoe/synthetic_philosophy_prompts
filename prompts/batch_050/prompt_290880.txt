I teach an early-modern philosophy seminar for upper-level undergrads, about 18 students, and we have one 75-minute session coming up on al-Ghazālī’s occasionalism. The textbook summary makes it sound too much like “things look connected, but God secretly does everything,” and the students either nod too quickly or dismiss it as obviously unscientific. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel the more interesting epistemic pressure behind the view: we observe one event reliably following another, but never observe a causal power literally passing from the first event into the second. Ideally, the activity should begin as a prediction or coordination game in which they become confident that certain moves cause certain outcomes, then reveal that the outcomes were being fixed according to a deeper rule or by an outside “sustainer” of the order. Please give concrete rules, materials, round structure, and any payoff or scoring system; no expensive props or prior knowledge of al-Ghazālī.

I’d also like a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the activity onto the distinction between constant conjunction, necessary connection, and direct divine causation. Include the complication that occasionalism need not mean chaotic arbitrariness: God can reliably act according to an established habit or custom, so prediction and ordinary practical reasoning still mostly work. Have students articulate what is lost if we say fire only accompanies burning rather than causes it, and what is gained if every event has the same ultimate metaphysical explanation. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it--for example, students treating the reveal as a cheap trick, confusing occasionalism with determinism, or assuming the point is that miracles happen all the time. Don’t lecture them with the terminology at the start; they should encounter the problem through the game first.
