Here's a bunch of cases about fakes and originals, plus my gut verdicts on each. What I want from you is the principle behind them, if there is one.

1. Someone makes a molecule-for-molecule perfect copy of a famous painting. No human could ever tell them apart by looking. I'd still pay far more for the original, and I think that premium is rational, not just snobbery.
2. Van Meegeren's fake Vermeers were admired as masterpieces until they were exposed in the 1940s, after which their value collapsed, even though not a single brushstroke changed. I think the collapse was appropriate.
3. But: imagine a forgery that hangs in a major museum for two centuries and is loved by millions of visitors. If it's exposed tomorrow, I don't think all those people were cheated out of real aesthetic pleasure. The experiences they had were genuine and stay genuine. Exposure changes what we know, not what they saw.
4. A signed first pressing of a record vs a later identical reissue: I'd pay extra for the first pressing, but here I actually suspect it IS just snobbery, and I'd like to be talked either into or out of that.
5. A bronze cast made from Rodin's original molds by his estate decades after his death, vs an unauthorized cast that is visually indistinguishable: the authorized one feels more genuine to me, though I honestly can't say why.
6. Restorations: if a conservator repaints 5% of a painting it's obviously still the original. If 80% gets repainted it starts to feel like a copy of itself. There's a line somewhere and I don't know where it is or what draws it.
7. Music: I have literally zero preference for the original master tape over a good digital copy, and a great cover version can be just as valuable to me as the original recording. Provenance seems to matter not at all here.

So: is there one coherent principle about what makes originals valuable that delivers my core verdicts (1–3 and 5) while also making sense of 6 and 7, including why authenticity seems to matter for paintings but not for recordings? If no single principle can do it, tell me which verdict I should give up and why that one specifically — my guess is the pressure is between 2 and 3, but maybe I'm wrong about where the tension is. Then tell me what your principle predicts for cases I haven't listed: a performance piece that only exists as re-enactments by people other than the artist, and an author's handwritten manuscript vs a perfect photocopy of it. State the principle precisely, say which of my cases motivates which clause, and be blunt if the real answer is that my intuitions are just inconsistent and I should swallow the cost on one of them.
