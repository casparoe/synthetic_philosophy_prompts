Here's my transcription of the key stretch: "Cantor's diagonal argument shows that the real numbers are uncountable: no list, however long, can contain all of them. But the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem tells us that if the axioms of set theory have any model at all, they have a model whose domain contains only countably many objects. So there's a countable structure that nonetheless makes the sentence 'the reals are uncountable' come out true -- even though, looking at the structure from outside, we can enumerate everything in it. Skolem's own conclusion was that this deflates Cantor's result: uncountability isn't a genuine fact about collections of things, only a reflection of which mappings we happen to have catalogued, so the reals are 'uncountable' just relative to an incomplete inventory of bijections. The standard way logicians resolve the paradox is to say that the model is simply mistaken -- it believes its reals are uncountable because it can't perceive the enumeration that we, standing outside it, can plainly see. The broader lesson many logicians draw is that first-order logic is demonstrably incapable of capturing notions like finiteness and cardinality, which is why serious set theorists these days conduct their work in second-order logic."
