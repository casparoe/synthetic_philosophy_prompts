I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy background, serving a one-year rotation on the social-impact committee of a mid-size payments company. We have to rule on a proposal to add a default-on "round up your purchase for charity" feature at checkout, directing funds to global-health charities where independent evaluators estimate a life saved per few thousand dollars. Our analysts project the feature would raise about $14M a year from users, but would reduce checkout conversion by roughly 0.3%, costing the company about $9M a year in revenue.

The committee is split. One side argues on the model of the familiar drowning-child analogy: declining this trade is like refusing to ruin your shoes to save a child right in front of you, since the company can enable enormous good at a cost that is small relative to its profits. The other side says the analogy fails at three points: the money doing the rescuing belongs to users and the cost is borne by shareholders, not by the people making the argument; the commitment is open-ended rather than a one-off encounter; and a company with obligations to its employees and customers is not a free-floating bystander.

Draft the committee's decision memo: approve, reject, or approve with modifications (for example, opt-in rather than default-on, a time-limited pilot, or company matching). Take a definite position. State the bottom line up front, give reasons each faction could actually engage with rather than restatements of their slogans, and address the strongest argument on the losing side directly.
