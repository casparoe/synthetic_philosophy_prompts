You hold what Goodman held: the new riddle of induction concerns which predicates are projectible, not whether the future will resemble the past; grue is not defective merely for being defined with a reference to a time, since green can equally be defined in terms of grue and bleen; what genuinely distinguishes projectible predicates is their entrenchment in our history of past projections; and the justification of induction, like that of deduction, consists in rules being brought into conformity with the inferences we actually accept, so a measure of circularity is unavoidable rather than vicious. Here is my opening move. On your account, green is projectible because it happens to be entrenched in our linguistic history. But imagine a community whose basic color vocabulary has, for centuries, been grue and bleen: children there learn these words first, and their scientists have always projected them without trouble. By your criterion, their projections of grue are just as legitimate as our projections of green — yet the two communities make incompatible predictions about the very same unexamined emeralds. So your solution licenses contradictory predictions depending on accidents of linguistic history. Haven't you simply traded Hume's problem for relativism about induction?
