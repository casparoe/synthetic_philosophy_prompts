My two roommates got into a surprisingly heated argument last night about which animals are conscious, and they both told me to ask someone impartial, so here goes. Maya's position: consciousness is probably distributed way more broadly across the animal kingdom than people think, and the sensible default is to treat any animal with a nervous system and flexible behavior -- bees, octopuses, crabs, even fruit flies -- as likely conscious, because the cost of wrongly treating a conscious animal as a mere object (torture, basically) hugely outweighs the cost of wrongly treating an unconscious one as conscious (wasted empathy). David pushed back that she's smuggling in a false binary: for any specific animal the realistic options aren't "conscious or not" but "probably conscious in some thin sense" -- and a thin sense might not carry the moral weight she's assuming. A bee might have experiences, he said, without having anything like suffering worth worrying about, in which case her precautionary math collapses, because you can't weigh harms against wasted empathy if you don't even know the harms exist in the relevant form.

Maya answered that this just moves the problem one step back: how would we ever know whether a bee's experience includes suffering, if not by looking at the same behavioral and neural markers (nociception plus trade-offs, self-directed behavior, global activity patterns) that got us to "thinly conscious" in the first place? David said that's the problem with markers -- they were developed by noticing what correlates with consciousness in us and our close relatives, so of course they light up for animals that are behaviorally similar to us, and it's circular to then point at them as independent evidence. He then made what I thought was his strongest move: he said the real question isn't where the cut-off line is (fish yes, worm no, etc.) but whether there's a line at all. If consciousness fades gradually as you go down the tree of life, then at some point you're not deciding whether an animal is conscious, you're deciding how much to care about vanishingly dim consciousness, and that's a values question dressed up as a science question. Maya said fine, but even a values question needs an answer, and precaution is the only answer that doesn't require knowing things we can't know.
