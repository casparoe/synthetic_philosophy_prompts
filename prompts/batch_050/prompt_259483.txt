I serve on the approval panel of a regional children's authority. Roughly three times a month we decide whether to approve prospective adoptive and foster parents, and occasionally whether to recommend intervention in an existing family where a referral alleges that a child's upbringing is being harmed. The statute gives us broad welfare standards and little else. Our chair has proposed grounding our practice in two ideas from philosophy: Joel Feinberg's principle that a child holds a right-in-trust to an open future, such that certain adult options must be kept available until the child can choose among them, and Hugh LaFollette's argument that if society licenses drivers and doctors, it ought in principle to license parents. I am not persuaded that either idea survives contact with a Tuesday-afternoon hearing, and I want you to do the translation work.

The chair's draft guidance rests on a distinction she has coined: between 'option-removing' practices, which make some future impossible (failing to teach a child to read; a medical refusal that permanently forecloses a capacity), and 'option-loading' practices, which leave futures open but raise the cost of pursuing them (immersing a child in a demanding religious life; an all-consuming athletic training). The draft proposes that we may withhold approval or recommend intervention for the first kind but never for the second. My suspicion is that this distinction collapses under pressure — load enough cost onto an option and you have removed it — and that the interesting question is where it collapses, and what residue of judgement remains when it does.

Please write, in British English, a decision procedure the panel could actually run in a forty-five-minute hearing, by members who are social workers, teachers and lay volunteers rather than philosophers. Set out the questions in the order they should be asked, what would count as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure terminates with a decision and where it must stop and hand the matter back to a person's judgement because the question is one of values rather than findings. Be explicit about which features of Feinberg's principle and LaFollette's analogy survive the simplification and which you had to abandon, with reasons. Then apply the procedure to three cases: a couple seeking to adopt who intend to raise the child within an insular religious community, withdrawing from formal schooling at fourteen for work inside it; a culturally Deaf couple who decline cochlear implantation for their deaf toddler and intend a signing-only upbringing; and prospective foster parents who plan twenty hours a week of competitive gymnastics training for the child. Include at least one application where the procedure returns a verdict the panel will find uncomfortable, and flag it as such. Conclude by stating what the procedure cannot do — which classes of case it will systematically get wrong, and what a panel member should do when they suspect they are sitting in one. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need something that works in the room.
