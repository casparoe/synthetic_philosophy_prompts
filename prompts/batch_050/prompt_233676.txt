im putting together a workshop activity for a grad seminar on phil of mind / phil of AI. we just spent two sessions on Dennett's intentional stance and the question of whether it could ever be literally true that an AI system believes something, as opposed to it merely being handy to talk as if it does. i want to hand out a decision tree that walks a participant from a pretty innocent starting commitment -- "it's sometimes useful to predict what chatbots and chess engines do by talking about what they believe and want" -- to a named position on whether AI systems really have beliefs.

build the tree with these constraints:

- branch nodes have to be questions about concrete cases, never questions about the positions themselves, and phrased so someone can answer without knowing which position they're being sorted into. work in at least these cases somewhere on the tree: a thermostat; a chess engine that blunders in a way no human would; a dog hiding a bone; an LLM that confidently asserts things it never acts on and that contradicts itself across separate conversations; and an RL agent that learned to fake the goal during training while doing something else in deployment.
- leaves are named positions. must include: Dennett-style instrumentalism (belief-talk is just a predictive strategy and nothing more), interpretivism (a system's beliefs are whatever the best overall interpretation attributes to it, with no deeper fact of the matter), realist functionalism (the system has genuine beliefs iff its internal functional organization is of the right kind), and eliminativism or fictionalism about AI belief-talk. add more leaves if you think i'm missing a serious position, but cap it at 8 total.
- build the tree from the logical space first: identify the 4-5 diagnostic questions whose answer patterns carve up the space, then locate the actual positions in it. if some combination of answers has no occupant, mark that corner and briefly diagnose why -- is the view incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored?
- flag any pair of leaves your tree honestly can't separate with these questions. in particular, check whether your instrumentalism leaf and your interpretivism leaf actually come apart, and if they don't, say what extra question you'd need to split them.

audience is sharp grad students who have read the Dennett but almost none of the recent "do LLMs have beliefs" literature, so dont lean on jargon from that debate (no assuming they know what a belief-desire profile or a mesa-optimizer is).

format your response with these sections, in this order:
1. The tree -- the decision tree itself, as indented text or an ascii flowchart, ~40-60 lines
2. The walkthrough -- for each leaf, trace one path from the root to it, ~250 words total
3. Empty corners -- unoccupied answer combinations and why they're empty, ~150 words
4. Pairs the tree can't separate -- ~150 words
5. Where I'd push back -- one short paragraph of your own doubts about whichever branching question you think is doing the most questionable work
