I've been going around in circles with my roommate about population ethics (neither of us is a philosopher, he's an econ major and I'm in a bioethics class that touched on this for like one week) and I want to nail down what I actually believe. So here are my verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I'd like someone to tell me what principle, if any, is behind them.

Case one: a couple has a kid they know will have a good, happy life, at some modest cost to their own wellbeing. I say the world gets better, full stop, the kid's existence is a net positive addition. Case two: same couple, but they somehow know the kid would have a life of constant low-level misery -- not torture, just reliably bad, the kind of life where nonexistence would have been better. Creating that kid makes the world worse, and I feel this one more strongly than case one, which already feels like an asymmetry but whatever. Case three: world A has a billion people all living excellent lives; world B has the same billion people slightly less well off plus an extra five billion people with decent but not great lives. I'm tempted to say B is fine, maybe even better, but it doesn't feel like a clear improvement the way case one does. Case four: world C has a trillion people whose lives are just barely worth living -- like, every day is a tedious slog but technically net positive -- versus world A again. I strongly prefer A, and I refuse to accept that C is better just because there's more total happiness. Case five: starting from world A, you add a bunch of new people with pretty good lives, which slightly lowers the welfare of the original billion (say through resource strain) but nobody's life becomes bad. I think this is... neutral to mildly good? Definitely not a catastrophe. But here's the thing my roommate pounced on: if you iterate case five enough times, don't you march straight from world A to world C, which I said was way worse? So somewhere my verdicts have to give, and I don't want to give up case four.

So: is there a principle or small family of principles (total utilitarianism, average views, critical-level stuff, person-affecting views -- I've skimmed these terms but don't trust my grasp of them) that captures most of my verdicts, stated precisely enough that I can see which case motivates each clause? Tell me which of my verdicts has to be sacrificed on the simplest coherent view, and whether the case-one/case-two asymmetry can be principled rather than just a mood. Also tell me what your best candidate principle says about a case I haven't listed: a policy that would make everyone currently alive somewhat worse off but prevent a large number of future people from having lives not worth living. Please answer in American English.
