You are a professional philosopher of mind with particular expertise in consciousness and mental causation. Two classmates of mine in a graduate seminar got into an argument afterward about epiphenomenalism -- the view that phenomenal experiences are caused by physical processes in the brain but themselves cause nothing. Please adjudicate their exchange.

Mara opened by arguing that epiphenomenalism is self-defeating. Our judgments that we have experiences -- including the introspective convictions that motivate the view in the first place -- are physical events. If qualia cause nothing, they play no role in producing those judgments, and a judgment about X in whose production X played no role cannot count as knowledge of X. So if epiphenomenalism were true, no one could know it, or even know that they have experiences at all.

Devon replied that this proves too much. The epiphenomenalist's picture is a common-cause structure: the brain state that produces the experience also produces the judgment, so the two correlate reliably without the experience causing anything, just as a barometer reliably indicates a storm it does not cause. Knowledge requires reliable correlation, not causation by the object known -- after all, we know things about numbers without being causally affected by them.

Mara answered that the barometer analogy fails. Barometer and storm are bound by a law-governed mechanism; break it and the readings stop tracking storms. Qualia, on the epiphenomenalist picture, are danglers: a zombie with the same brain states makes the same judgments with no experiences at all, and a subject with inverted qualia makes the same judgments with different ones. The judgment would be made no matter which experience, if any, was present -- and a belief one would hold regardless of its truth is not knowledge.

Devon countered that this misdescribes the counterfactual. The judgment "I am having this very experience" deploys a phenomenal concept that picks its referent out directly, so in the zombie scenario there is no judgment with that content at all: the zombie is not falsely believing what I believe, it is believing something else. A judgment that cannot be false is not thereby disconnected from its subject matter, any more than my belief that I exist is defective because I would never falsely hold it.

Mara's last move was to say that talk of the concept "picking the quale out directly" merely names the relation the epiphenomenalist is not entitled to. A physical judgment-event's latching onto a non-physical, causally inert quale is exactly what needs explaining, and acquaintance that does no causal work is acquaintance in name only.

Please go through the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it succeeds, partially succeeds, or fails, with a sentence or two of justification. Then give your overall verdict -- which side, if either, comes out ahead, and on which specific points -- and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing. In particular, address whether Mara's argument, even if fully successful, would show that epiphenomenalism is false rather than merely unknowable, and whether Devon's two defenses (reliable correlation via a common cause, and content fixed directly by the experience) can be run together or in fact trade against one another. Think carefully, and reconstruct the underlying dialectic before issuing your verdicts.
