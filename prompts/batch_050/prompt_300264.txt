I've been in a reading group on nationalism studies for about two years now and the argument about how old nations are has completely frozen, and I want a diagnosis rather than another round of it.

The setup, roughly: the modernists (Hobsbawm, Anderson, Gellner and friends) say nations are recent -- products of print capitalism, standardised state education, conscription, and traditions that were deliberately cooked up in the 18th and 19th centuries while being dressed up as ancient. The other camp, the ethnosymbolists and perennialists around Anthony Smith, say nations are modern in form but grow out of much older ethnic communities with their myths, memories and sacred territories, so the modernist story massively overstates the rupture. Both sides have been citing the same cases at each other for decades -- France, England, Armenia, the Greeks -- and as far as I can tell nobody has ever switched sides because of a new historical finding.

My hypothesis, which I'd like tested rather than accepted: the disagreement is really about criteria of identity over time, and it's stuck because both camps quietly use different ones. When the ethnosymbolist says the Armenian nation is old, they mean there's a continuity of myth, memory and self-ascription running back centuries. When the modernist says nations are new, they mean mass political mobilisation, vernacular literacy and the claim to popular sovereignty are new. These could both be true of the same population! It's a bit like the species problem in philosophy of biology, where "species" picks out different things under different legitimate concepts and some disputes just dissolve once you notice. Under this reading the debate is mostly verbal, but with a real factual core buried in it somewhere.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is there a hidden empirical question -- e.g. how much pre-modern mass identification with the ethnic community there really was, versus identification with village, church and dynasty -- that would settle it if the archives could answer it? Or is it a disagreement about which continuities count as relevant, which is conceptual and can't be settled by archives at all? Or are the two sides answering different questions under one name, one about political legitimacy and one about social psychology? Please engage with what each side actually argues rather than giving me a survey of positions, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis -- a distinction everyone should adopt, an agreed test case, a different question -- or argue honestly that no progress should be expected and why.

One more thing: please write your answer in American English, even though I'm not. And if my species analogy is doing more work than it can bear, say so.
