My two closest friends from grad school are getting married next month and they've asked me to give the toast at the reception. Here's the catch they insisted on: they met in a seminar on early Chinese thought, and she wrote her dissertation on the Mohists, so they want the toast built around the dispute between Mozi's impartial care (jian ai) and the Confucian defense of graded love -- not as flavor, but as the actual spine of the speech. Their joke, which they think is hilarious, is that a wedding is the most aggressively partial thing two humans can do. You're standing up in public and declaring that this one specific person gets a kind of love that no one else gets, forever, deliberately. Mozi diagnosed partiality (bie) as the root of harm in the world -- people, families, and states harming each other precisely because each cares only for its own -- so on his view the couple is basically celebrating the disease. The Confucian answer is that love naturally has gradations, that ren begins with those nearest to us and extends outward from there, and that demanding equal care for stranger and kin is psychologically impossible and maybe a violation of what love even is. The couple wants the toast to let Mozi's indictment land seriously for a while -- guests should feel the sting -- and then have the Confucian case genuinely earn the wedding back, rather than just patting Mozi on the head and moving on.

Practical constraints: around six minutes, so 700-800 words. The audience is split. One table will be philosophy PhDs who will visibly wince at any caricature, so please don't render Mozi as a proto-utilitarian with a spreadsheet or reduce graded love to "family first, everyone else gets the crumbs" -- the Mencian move is about extending a natural feeling outward, not about licensing favoritism, and the speech should respect that. Everyone else -- grandparents, work friends, the groom's college roommates -- has never heard the name Mozi and needs the whole thing to track as a love story with one weird detour, not a lecture. The groom's parents are devout Catholics, so avoid framing impartial benevolence as a rival faith that lost; I just want it handled as a philosophical challenge, not a religious one. No inside jokes that exclude more than a few people, and the toast has to end on the couple themselves, not on who won the debate.

Since the whole thing depends on a particular reading of this dispute, at the end of the toast give me a short note saying how confident you are in how you've represented each side, and single out the one spot in the speech where you're least sure you've been fair to the historical positions -- for instance, if you softened Mozi's argument to make the toast land, or leaned on a contested interpretation of the Confucian response, I want that flagged rather than smoothed over. And write it in American English, please.
