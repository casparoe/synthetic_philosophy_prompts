Act as a philosopher of mind specializing in functionalism. Think carefully, begin by identifying several possible principles, and only then settle on the best one. I want to know what principle captures my judgments about these cases:

A silicon system that reproduces the causal organization of my brain has beliefs and pains. An exact neuron-by-neuron simulation running a million times more slowly is also conscious, because only the pattern of causal relations matters. A vast lookup table that would produce every response I would give throughout my life, without any internal process connecting its states, has no mind. An alien whose pain-like state is realized by hydraulic pressure rather than neurons can feel pain if that state plays the right role in perception, learning, avoidance, and motivation. Two systems with identical actual behavior need not have the same mental states if one lacks the internal organization that would produce the right responses under changed circumstances. A planet-wide network of people exchanging messages could, in principle, realize a mind, but I remain reluctant when each participant merely follows instructions without understanding the whole.

What precise version of functionalism best unifies these verdicts? Explain how it distinguishes genuine realization from mere behavioral mimicry, why processing speed and physical composition do not matter, and what work counterfactual organization is doing. Then test the principle against two further cases: a system whose functional organization exists only because an external intelligent operator continually repairs it, and a system that duplicates my causal organization for one instant but has no appropriate past or future. Finally, identify which of my verdicts the simplest principle would have to reject, and whether saving that verdict is worth the added complexity.
