I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy (officially bioethics, though my committee is mostly value theory people) and in three weeks I'm presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on wild animal welfare. The paper defends a narrow slice of the hedonistic imperative program — not the full abolish-suffering-through-biotechnology vision, but one specific pilot: using CRISPR edits, and eventually a gene drive, on a small well-studied wild herbivore population to dampen the affective, motivational component of pain while leaving nociceptive reflexes in tact. My central analogy is pain asymbolia in humans, where people notice the injury and still pull their hand off the stove but report that it doesn't bother them. My claim is that this is morally unlike wireheading or tranquilizing the population into oblivion, since the informational function of pain survives and only the suffering gets turned down. I also argue the pilot beats the rival interventions people usually propose (contraception, habitat expansion, predator culling) on rough cost-per-suffering-reduced grounds, though my numbers there are honestly back-of-envelope.

The room matters. It's about thirty people: maybe half conservation biologists and wildlife ecologists who, near as I can tell, treat leaving nature alone as close to axiomatic and think at the level of populations and ecosystems rather than individual welfare; a quarter animal welfare scientists, the measurement people, who will zero in on how I'd ever verify reduced suffering in a free-living population; one population geneticist who works on gene drive containment and reversibility and who I know is attending; and a few philosophers who'll grant me the suffering point but are skeptical the asymbolia analogy carries over to animals whose behavior we can only read from the outside. Nobody there is a transhumanist, and I suspect invoking the hedonistic imperative by name would cost me the room.

Prep me for the Q&A. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way each faction would phrase them — the ecologists in their own vocabulary (trophic cascades, pleiotropy, predator avoidance, selection pressures), not in philosophy-speak. For each objection: a two-minute reply, what I should concede immediately to keep credibility, and where the reply is softest. Flag which objections have genuine force against the narrow pilot specifically, since I can always retreat from the full program if pressed, and which ones I can safely give ground on. And tell me the one question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks.
