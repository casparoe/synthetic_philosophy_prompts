You are a philosopher of science who also has experience advising secondary-school teachers on media literacy. Please think this through carefully rather than reaching for a generic answer.

I am a sixth-form student and I help edit our school's history society magazine. We publish two or three short pieces a term written by students, and lately we keep receiving submissions that feel like pseudohistory: Atlantis treated as a real empire, ancient-astronaut explanations of monuments, and one memorable piece claiming, in the style of Fomenko's new chronology, that several centuries of the Middle Ages were simply invented by later chroniclers. I need a decision procedure my fellow editors and I can actually run on a submission. None of us knows more than A-level history, we have about forty-five minutes per piece, a teacher must sign off any rejection, and we owe every author a short paragraph explaining the decision.

Please write the procedure as a sequence of questions to ask in order. For each question, state what counts as a satisfactory answer, whether an unsatisfactory answer stops the procedure with a rejection, and whether it instead escalates the decision to the faculty adviser. The procedure should be grounded in what philosophy of science and historiography genuinely say about demarcation — for instance, the difference between a claim that is false and a claim that is unfalsifiable, and between an unorthodox conclusion and a disreputable method. Be explicit about which distinctions must survive the simplification and which can safely be dropped for our purposes. I am especially worried about two failure modes: rejecting a piece merely because its conclusion is heterodox, and accepting a piece merely because it is heavily footnoted. Address both directly, and note where the procedure cannot eliminate editorial judgement.

Then run the procedure on these three submissions, showing each step:

1. A piece arguing that Atlantis was a real Bronze Age civilisation in the Azores, destroyed by a tsunami, treating Plato's dialogues as a reliable record and interpreting underwater ridges in satellite images as walls.
2. A Fomenko-style piece arguing that Charlemagne is a duplicated phantom of a later ruler and that dendrochronology has been quietly calibrated against the very chronology it is supposed to test.
3. A piece arguing that the conventional dating of a famous ancient monument is wrong by millennia, citing geological weathering patterns, acknowledging the mainstream dating evidence, and explaining why it finds that evidence inconclusive.

I expect the procedure to deliver at least one verdict I will dislike, so do not soften its output to please me. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do — which kinds of submission it will get wrong and why.

Format: open with a single sentence stating the core test your whole procedure boils down to, then give the reasoning and the procedure itself. No preamble, and do not restate the task. After the worked examples, include a brief list of the failure modes you considered while drafting and how the procedure guards against each.
