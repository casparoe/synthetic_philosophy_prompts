I am preparing a graduate seminar on metaphysics and literature, and our next unit pairs the presentism–eternalism debate with Kurt Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five. Please read the novel through that debate, with particular attention to the following. First, the Tralfamadorians claim to perceive all moments at once, so that every event simply is, permanently — this looks like a textbook statement of eternalism, but examine whether what the novel dramatizes is actually the metaphysical thesis or only its phenomenology: Billy Pilgrim comes unstuck in time, experiencing moments out of order, and one can hold a block universe without anyone hopping around inside it. Distinguish carefully between what eternalism says and what Billy's condition depicts. Second, the Tralfamadorians treat free will as a local Earthling superstition, and the novel's refrain after each death enforces a mood of resignation — press on whether the novel thereby commits the common confusion of running eternalism together with fatalism. Philosophically, a fixed future in a block universe is compatible with events being caused by earlier ones, so deliberation need not be futile even if the future already exists; does the novel see this distinction, or does it exploit the conflation for consolatory effect? Third, take seriously the novel's own consolation: if all moments are permanent, then the dead are not annihilated but merely located elsewhere on the timeline, and the narrator suggests attending to the good moments. Test this against the objection that grief essentially involves a sense of the no longer — that loss is constituted by something's having ceased to be — and consider whether eternalism actually dissolves grief or merely changes its object. Fourth, address the interpretive crux that everything Tralfamadorian may be Billy's coping mechanism for the Dresden firebombing: the eternalist cosmology arrives precisely when he needs it. Does that make the novel a psychological rather than metaphysical argument — a study of why a traumatized mind would want eternalism to be true — and if so, does that undermine or deepen the reading? Close by saying what, if anything, the novel contributes to the presentism–eternalism debate that argument alone does not, and where the reading has to concede that the fiction resists tidy philosophical treatment. Please lead with your overall verdict on the novel's metaphysical coherence in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
