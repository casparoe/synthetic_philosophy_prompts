I'm helping run a philosophy evening class at our local community college — ten sessions, a mix of retirees, two nurses, a guy who fixes bikes, and one very intense teenager — and I've been assigned the session on "when do numbers count." The case I want to build the whole evening around is the old torture-versus-dust-specks setup: would you rather one person be tortured horribly for fifty years, or that some astronomically huge number of people each get a single dust speck in the eye — barely noticeable, gone in a second, no lasting effects at all? I've watched this question detonate in online arguments, and what strikes me is that both answers feel monstrous once you sit with them. Picking torture seems to say tiny harms never add up to anything, which can't be right, and picking the specks seems to condemn someone to agony over irritations nobody will remember five minutes later. I don't want to argue either side in the session; I want the group to understand why the question is genuinely hard, which is harder than it sounds because most people arrive with a reflex answer and alot of them never budge.

So plan the session's explanatory arc for me — the teaching plan, not your own verdict. What has to be in place before the question even makes sense? I'm guessing at minimum they need to accept that a dust speck is a harm at all, however tiny (nonzero, not zero), and maybe also that we already aggregate small harms all the time without feeling monstrous — speed limits, vaccination campaigns, product safety rules — so the dispute isn't really about whether numbers count but about whether there's a threshold where they stop counting. Tell me what the actual dependency structure is, in order. And tell me whether the dust speck is even the right carrying example: its charm is that it's nearly consequence-free, unlike "everyone drops one piece of litter" where the harms visibly compound into something big, but maybe that makes it too easy to dissmiss as literally nothing. If a mild headache or a stubbed toe or a moment of nausea would carry the point better, say what the example needs to have and needs to lack.

Then give me the misconception map in the order I'll actually hit it. Someone will say the speck isn't a real harm so multiplying by any number still gives zero; someone else will call the whole thing a rigged advertisement for utilitarianism and refuse to play; the intense teenager will try infinity tricks; someone will quietly substitute a realistic version where the specks cause car crashes and miss the point; and someone will try to dissolve it all as a sorites paradox with no answer. For each of these, tell me where in the sequence to head it off rather than letting it fester, and how. Finally: one question or exercise for the last ten minutes that shows me whether they've actually gotten it — not which side they picked, but whether they can feel the pull of the side they rejected — plus what I should deliberately keep out of this session (population ethics, formal continuity arguments, the repugnant conclusion?) and say out loud that I'm keeping out.
