I am trying to locate the source of a disagreement with a colleague about expected-value fanaticism. My credence in the thesis below is 0.55; hers is 0.90. Please give your own credence rather than surveying positions.

Define a transparent n-bit lottery as follows. A mechanism generates n independent, fair bits through a fully specified and publicly auditable process. Option A guarantees one unit of welfare. Option B pays 2^(n+1) units of welfare if all n bits come up zero and pays nothing otherwise, so its probability of success is exactly 2^(-n). There is only one draw; the payoff is finite; there are no side effects, reputational considerations, costs of verification, or beneficiaries whose testimony motivated the offer. Welfare units aggregate linearly, without diminishing marginal value or an upper bound.

Let Finite-State Fanaticism be the thesis that, for every finite n, ideal rationality requires choosing B over A, even when n is 400, 4,000, or arbitrarily larger. The small probability here is not inferred from an implausible story: it follows directly from the mechanism. The question is therefore not whether one should trust a Pascalian mugger, but whether any nonzero probability, however small, can be outweighed by a sufficiently large finite payoff.

What is your credence that Finite-State Fanaticism is true as a principle of ideal rational choice? Give a number between 0 and 1, then decompose it into the following sub-questions, assigning a probability and a brief justification to each:

1. Whether probabilities as small as 2^(-4,000) can remain exact and action-guiding for an ideal agent.
2. Whether welfare can coherently aggregate linearly and without bound across sufficiently many lives or welfare-relevant events.
3. Whether, conditional on exact probabilities and unbounded linear welfare, expected-value maximization is rationally mandatory rather than merely one defensible rule.
4. Whether any feature of one-shot choice—such as the near-certainty that the large payoff will never occur—introduces an independent rational constraint.

Explain how these probabilities combine, while noting any dependence among them. Then identify which component carries most of the disagreement between my 0.55 and my colleague’s 0.90, which component you are least confident about, and what argument or counterexample would most change your answer. Finally, state where your reasoning is most likely to fail rather than presenting the final number as more secure than it is.
