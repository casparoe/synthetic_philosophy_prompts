You are a political philosopher whose specialty is global justice and the ethics of partiality — someone who has spent years on the nationalism-versus-cosmopolitanism literature and can distinguish, on demand, between a dozen theses that usually travel together under one phrase. Think carefully and precisely; before writing your final answer, work through a private brainstorm of the different claims at stake so that the menu you produce is genuinely exhaustive rather than the first three readings that come to mind.

Here is my situation. I am writing a chapter on whether wealthy states may legitimately prioritize domestic poverty relief over foreign aid. At a workshop last month, after I presented a draft arguing that a state's aid budget should be allocated wherever marginal welfare gains are largest — which would send nearly all of it abroad — a senior colleague ended the discussion by remarking that charity begins at home. The room treated this as decisive, and I suspect it is doing far more argumentative work than any defensible reading of it can bear. I want to take the proverb apart properly.

Produce a menu of the distinct precise theses that this proverb could be expressing. I expect the readings to range from the near-trivial to the radical, and I want them stated as theses, not gestures. At minimum, consider whether it might be making: a merely descriptive claim about human motivational psychology; an epistemic-practical claim about who is best placed to identify and meet needs; a coordination or division-of-moral-labor claim about how global duties get discharged if every community looks first to its own; an associative-duties claim that shared membership in a political community is itself reason-giving; a grounding claim that coercively imposed shared institutions, rather than mere co-membership, are what generate special claims; a weighting claim that compatriots' interests simply count for more in deliberation; and a lexical or threshold claim that obligations to outsiders arise only once domestic needs are fully met. Do not treat this list as exhaustive — add readings I have missed if they are genuinely distinct.

For each reading, do three things. First, give a verdict: is it a platitude, a defensible but substantive position, a contentious claim that needs real argument, or straightforwardly false — and why. Second, state whether that reading, if true, would actually support my colleague's conclusion that a state may redirect its aid budget inward, and identify exactly which further normative or empirical premises the inference would still require. Third, flag whether the reading is a claim about what individuals may do or what states may do, since I suspect the proverb slides between these and I want the slide exposed rather than smoothed over.

Then address the proverb's built-in vagueness directly: what work is being done by 'home' (family, locality, nation, co-citizens under shared institutions — these are not interchangeable), by 'charity' (supererogatory generosity versus enforceable duty), and by 'begins' (temporal ordering, lexical priority, or a claim about where obligations are grounded). Say which disambiguation of each term my colleague most plausibly needed for his intervention to succeed.

Conclude with a judgment: which single reading, if any, could actually carry the weight the proverb was asked to bear in that room, and if none can, say so plainly and identify the most defensible nearby position my colleague could retreat to — along with what he would have to concede to occupy it.
