i teach an upper-level phil mind seminar and we're on the week about the timing of experience -- the color phi phenomenon, Dennett's Orwellian vs Stalinesque models, the whole is-there-even-a-fact-of-the-matter business. instead of doing summary slides at the end i want to read them a poem.

Write me a Petrarchan sonnet about the color phi phenomenon, volta after the octave. Set the octave inside the experiment as the subject lives it: a red dot flashed on the left, a green dot on the right a split second later, and what you see is a single dot moving across that turns from red to green somewhere in the middle -- even though, at the time the 'change' would have to be experienced, the second dot hadn't appeared yet. The octave should hold that puzzle without solving it: how did the moving dot know where to change color?

The sestet should stage Dennett's two revisionary accounts as two rival clerks in the brain. The Stalinesque clerk stages the show trial before broadcast: the interpolation happens pre-consciously, and the experience that arrives was already fabricated seamless. The Orwellian clerk lets you genuinely experience red-then-green and only afterwards doctors the record, so your memory is rewritten and you sincerely misremember what you saw. Keep the two mechanisms distinct -- a reader who knows this literature should be able to tell which clerk is which from *how* each one does its work, not from labels.

Then the closing couplet should land Dennett's own punchline: that pressing the question "but which clerk is right, and when exactly did the color change become conscious?" assumes there's some finishing line in the brain where content arrives to be experienced, and once you give that up the two stories stop being rivals. Don't name Dennett, don't say "Cartesian theater" -- the the couplet should earn the point by showing that nothing in the subject's access could ever decide between the clerks.

Accurate enough that someone who's read the Dennett & Kinsbourne material nods along rather than winces, but still a poem, not a precis with line breaks.
