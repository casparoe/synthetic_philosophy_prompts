I'm a PhD student in history working on my dissertation prospectus, and I'm trying to put together a typology of the ways archives produce silences -- the mechanisms by which certain people and experiences end up unrecorded or unreadable -- for a chapter on methodology. My supervisor keeps saying my list is "just the Trouillot greatest hits plus digitization," which is fair, so I want to expand it. Here's what I have so far, so please don't repeat any of these:

(1) Silences at the moment of record creation -- whole populations (enslaved people, the illiterate poor, women in many periods) rarely produced documents at all, so they appear in the archive, if at all, only as objects of someone else's paperwork: tax rolls, court records, plantation ledgers. (2) Appraisal and retention -- archivists and institutions decide what counts as worth keeping, and everyday life, "ephemera," and records of marginalised groups have historically been judged not worth the shelf space. (3) Deliberate destruction -- colonial powers destroying or removing sensitive files at decolonisation, states shredding records of abuses, families burning embarrassing letters. (4) Description and cataloguing bias -- even when records survive, finding aids index them under the categories and names that mattered to the record creators, so (say) women's or indigenous peoples' materials become effectively invisible to keyword-based searching. (5) Digitization and access skew -- what gets funded for digitization, and what sits behind paywalls or in restrictive reading rooms, now shapes what actually gets researched and cited, creating a kind of second-order silence.

What I want is one more mechanism that is genuinely distinct from all five of these -- not a special case of appraisal, not "destruction but digital," not description bias in a new guise. Ideally it would operate at a different point in the life of a document than any of the ones I've listed, or operate on records that *do* exist and *are* accessible rather than on absent ones. Also tell me whether the standard remedy of "reading against the grain" would even help with the silence you identify, since that's the move my whole chapter currently rests on and I'm starting to worry it has blind spots. If you think your proposed mechanism is actually a bit weak or collapses into one of mine on closer inspection, I'd honestly rather hear that too.
