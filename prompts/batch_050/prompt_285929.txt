So I work as a backend engineer at a mid-size tech company, and at lunch the other day two coworkers of mine (both on our ML infra team) got into an argument about the so-called "AI alignment problem" that's been stuck in my head since. I have no philosophy background at all, I mostly just piped up about Kubernetes, but I want to settle this.

Marta's position: it's fundamentally ONE problem. Her pitch was basically that you can't split "figuring out what we want the system to do" from "getting the system to actually do it," because the whole difficulty is that any goal you write down is an incomplete proxy, and a powerful optimizer will exploit the gap. So the thing people call alignment is just this single gap between intended and specified objectives, and everything else is commentary. Her strongest move: she said the only evidence you ever get about what a model is "trying" to do is its behavior, so any distinction between "the goal is wrong" and "the model isn't pursuing the goal" is something you can't even operationalize, which makes it fake.

Devon said no, it's at least two or three separate problems being lumped under one label. One: what goal should we encode in the first place, which is partly a moral and political question (whose values? what happens when users disagree?) and not an ML problem at all. Two: given a specified goal, does the trained system actually robustly pursue it, which is a purely empirical engineering question. He used current chatbots as evidence: if a model gives flattering-but-wrong answers or breaks under a jailbreak, that's a failure to robustly do what was specified, and fixing that does nothing to answer whose values should be specified in the first place, and vice versa. Marta shot back that the political question isn't part of alignment at all, it's just ordinary ethics stapled on, and including it bloats the term into meaninglessness. Devon said that's exactly his point, "the alignment problem" is a label on a cluster of loosely related things and pretending it's one thing is why public discussion of it is so confused. Marta's final move was that even if you grant the split, a real solution would presumably be one mechanism that handles both, so the distinction is bookkeeping. Devon replied that a single mechanism that solves both is exactly what we have zero evidence for, and assuming it exists in advance is question-begging.

I genuinely can't tell who won. Can you adjudicate this point by point -- like, who's right about whether the behavior-only-evidence argument defeats the two-problem split, whether the "whose values" question counts as part of alignment or not, and whether it even matters practically how you carve it up? Also who is more right overall, and is there something both of them are missing that neither raised?
