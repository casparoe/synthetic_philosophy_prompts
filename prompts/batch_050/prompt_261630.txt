I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general readership, and I have landed myself in a commitment I now need help honouring. At a recent dinner with friends the conversation turned, as it somehow does, to whether infinity plus one is simply infinity, and the disagreement grew heated enough that I rashly volunteered to lead an evening of our informal reading group on the subject. There will be about a dozen of us, most of whom have not studied mathematics since school but who are curious and patient, plus one keen amateur who keeps insisting that actual infinity is a philosophical fiction and that only potential infinity — always being able to add one more — is coherent. I have ninety minutes. My hope is to take the group from Hilbert's hotel, through Cantor's conclusion that some infinities are strictly larger than others, and to end on my amateur friend's finitist worry about whether any of this describes anything real or is merely an elaborate game with symbols. I should stress that I want a plan for the session rather than the explanation itself: I can absorb the material, but I have never taught mathematics to anyone and I do not trust my instincts about sequencing.

What I would like, then, is a teaching plan that takes the dependency structure seriously. Please set out the order in which the component ideas must be introduced and why, in particular whether the group first needs to accept that one-to-one correspondence is the right way to compare the sizes of collections before the hotel does any work for them, and whether the hotel should precede or follow the move from the counting numbers to the real numbers. Tell me which single example should carry the whole evening — I am assuming Hilbert's hotel, but if some other case does the job better, argue for it — and what that example must have and must lack in order to bear the weight placed on it. Map the misconceptions the group will predictably bring or form: I am guessing these include infinity being just a very large number, all infinities being the same size, a part necessarily being smaller than the whole, and the suspicion that the whole exercise is contentless notation. For each one, indicate the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off rather than left to fester. Advise me on what to do about my finitist friend: should his objection be addressed early so it does not poison the reception of Cantor, or is it better saved as the closing open question, and does that choice change the shape of everything before it? Give me one question I can pose at the end that would show whether the group has genuinely grasped the idea of different sizes of infinity rather than merely enjoyed the paradoxes, and tell me what to leave out entirely at this level — the mechanics of the diagonal argument, ordinals versus cardinals, the continuum hypothesis — and to say explicitly that I am leaving it out.

Two further requests. First, if the arc I have sketched is pedagogically confused — if, say, ending on finitism threatens to undo the evening's work, or if Cantor simply cannot be done honestly in ninety minutes with this audience — please say so plainly and propose something better, rather than politely building a plan around a flawed premise. Where the right approach is genuinely contested, be candid about that rather than presenting one orthodoxy as settled. Secondly, please write your answer as continuous prose, in the manner of a long letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
