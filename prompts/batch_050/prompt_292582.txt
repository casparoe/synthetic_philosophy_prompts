I'm setting the final exam for my second-year course on Plato's Republic, and I need one essay question on the allegory of the cave that cannot be answered by retelling the story of the ascent. The crux I want candidates to confront is this: the prisoners take the shadows for the whole of reality, yet the released philosopher who returns to the cave has to argue with them in their own language. So the question should press candidates on whether, for Plato, ordinary belief is straightforwardly false, true but systematically about the wrong objects, or corrigible from within its own terms -- and on what the allegory's treatment of shadows versus the artifacts that cast them, the compulsion involved in the ascent, and the lower segments of the divided line commits a candidate to in answering. Candidates will have 75 minutes.

Please produce the following, in order:

1. The exam question itself, phrased so that a candidate who has merely memorized the plot of the allegory (shadows, fire, ascent, blinding, return) cannot earn a passing answer from that summary alone.

2. A model answer of about 600 words. It should defend one of the three readings -- ordinary belief as false, as true of the wrong objects, or as corrigible from within -- and it must engage the strongest single consideration in favor of one rival reading rather than ignoring it. Do not quote the text; paraphrase.

3. A marking scheme presented as a table with exactly these columns, covering five components worth 20 marks in total:
   - Component: the element of an answer being assessed
   - Marks: the value out of 20
   - What earns full marks: the specific argumentative move a top answer must make (not a topic label -- a move, such as distinguishing what the prisoners' words are actually about from what the prisoners take them to be about)
   - How marks are typically lost: the characteristic error, for example treating the cave as a compressed restatement of the theory of forms, or assuming the shadows are mere illusions the prisoners could shrug off if they paid attention

4. After the table, a short paragraph stating what separates an A answer from a B answer, naming at least two concrete moves the A answer must contain that a B answer can get away with omitting.
