I've recently become convinced of a fairly specific thesis, and I want to stress-test it rather than be talked out of time skepticism wholesale. The thesis is not eternalism in general but the Rietdijk–Putnam route to it: the argument that because simultaneity is frame-relative, whatever events are real for me now must include events that, in some other inertial frame, lie in the past of a distant observer who shares my present moment — and since that observer's present slice is as legitimate as mine, events that are still future for me must already be real simpliciter. Iterating the move across frames then seems to force the conclusion that all spacetime events are equally real, hence the block universe. I first ran into this in the form of the Andromeda thought experiment, where two walkers passing each other on the street disagree about whether a distant fleet has already launched, and I found it genuinely unsettling in a way that generic eternalist arguments never were. A friend who studied physics keeps telling me the argument is "just a confusion about reference frames," but when I press him, he can't locate the confusion precisely, which makes me trust my current conviction more than perhaps I should.

So: what would count against this argument specifically? I want a map of its exposure, not a survey of the philosophy of time. Which steps are actually load-bearing — for instance, how much work is done by treating "real relative to a frame" as something that can be aggregated across frames into "real simpliciter," and what would have to be true about the nature of temporal reality for that inference to fail? Is the argument vulnerable if spacetime structure turns out to privilege a foliation — say, if some approach to quantum gravity or cosmology ends up singling out a preferred slicing — and would that merely blunt this particular argument while leaving eternalism standing on other grounds, or does the Rietdijk–Putnam reasoning fail even in a world with a hidden preferred frame that no observer could detect? I'm also curious about the reverse direction: what would defenders of presentism or the growing block have to concede, given special relativity as we currently understand it, and which of their maneuvers (neo-Lorentzian interpretations, treating simultaneity as a mere coordinate convention with no ontological weight, restricting reality to the past light cone rather than a simultaneity slice) actually engage the argument rather than changing the subject?

Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each, say whether it's the sort of thing a thoughtful eternalist would themselves concede as a genuine threat or dismiss as missing the point. I should also flag what I'd personally find most persuasive: not intuition-mongering about the felt passage of time, but either a formal gap in the argument's structure or a physically motivated alternative account of what "now" could pick out that doesn't smuggle absolute simultaneity back in. If the honest verdict is that the argument is extremely well insulated and that the live objections are really objections to eternalism as a whole rather than to this route to it, I'd like that said plainly, along with an assessment of whether that insulation is a strength or a sign that the argument doesn't risk anything and therefore shows less than it appears to.
