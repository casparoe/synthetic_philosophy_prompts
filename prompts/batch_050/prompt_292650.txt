I’m putting together a worksheet for a philosophy of psychiatry seminar on Ian Hacking’s idea that classifications of people can loop back and alter the people being classified. My students keep treating this as just a self-fulfilling prophecy, so I’m making a taxonomy of distinct feedback routes. So far I have: a person learns their diagnosis and begins interpreting or presenting their experiences through that category; clinicians, teachers, or relatives treat the person differently in ways that elicit the classified behavior; institutions create incentives for people to fit a recognizable diagnostic profile; researchers study only already-diagnosed people and mistake effects of treatment or institutional life for features of the condition itself; and public awareness changes how common the condition appears or how it is expressed, which then leads experts to revise the category.

Give me one additional looping mechanism that is not on that list and not merely a special case or combination of those five. It has to involve a genuine feedback path from classification back to people or their behavior, not just a general claim that psychiatric categories are socially constructed. Describe the mechanism in at least two causal steps, and say which thing it changes most directly: the classified person, the evidence available to clinicians and researchers, or the boundaries of the classification itself. Also flag any respect in which your proposal might secretly collapse into one of the routes above — I’d rather have an imperfect but genuinely different mechanism than a fancy redescription of stigma or clinician bias.
