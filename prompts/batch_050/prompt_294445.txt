I work as an independent mediator — mostly dissolving small partnerships and splitting contested estates — and over the years I've developed strong gut reactions about which settlements are fair and which merely look fair. A colleague who mediates alongside me swears by a mechanical rule: choose the split that maximizes the product of the two parties' gains relative to walking away with nothing. We argue about it constantly, and I'd like to settle the argument rigorously. Please answer as an expert in the formal theory of bargaining and fair division — a philosopher-economist, if you like — and think carefully: formalize each dispute before reasoning about it, and don't offer generic advice about communication or compromise. Conventions, in thousands of dollars: in every dispute below, if talks fail both parties walk away with nothing, and contracts or binding precedent cap what each party could conceivably be awarded, so each side has a definite best case.

Here are my verdicts. Dispute 1: a pool of 120, with A's award capped at 100 and B's at 40. My colleague's rule yields 80 for A and 40 for B, and I found that hard to defend: B receives every cent of the best he could possibly do, while A gets only 80% of hers. I pushed for roughly 86 and 34, on the grounds that each side should end up at the same fraction — about 86% — of the distance from nothing to their best case. Dispute 2: the same parties, but newly discovered receivables raise A's cap to 140; the pool is still 120 and B's cap is still 40. My colleague insisted the answer shouldn't change — 80 and 40 — since everything actually available to B is exactly as before and the old deal remains on the table. I felt A was entitled to more and proposed 90 and 30. Only afterward did I notice that this puts B below the 34 I had insisted was B's due in Dispute 1, even though nothing on B's side of the table had changed, which made me worry I'm inconsistent. Dispute 3: a pool of 160, caps of 120 and 80. My verdict was 96 and 64 — 80% of each side's best case — against my colleague's 80 and 80. Dispute 4: identical numbers to Dispute 1, except that B's counsel now argues B's realistic best case is 70 rather than 40, citing aggressive precedent, though nothing enforceable has changed. Applying my Dispute 1 reasoning with a ceiling of 70 hands B about 49 and cuts A to about 71. My gut says that's absurd — a settlement shouldn't swing by fifteen thousand on the strength of one side's storytelling about its own ceiling. But that verdict plainly sits badly with the principle I relied on in the first three disputes.

My tentative principle, then, is this: a fair split leaves each side at the same percentage of the way from their walk-away outcome to the best outcome they could possibly obtain. Please (a) test this principle against all four disputes and state precisely which of my verdicts it captures and which it cannot; (b) formulate the principle — or the two or three strongest rival candidates — precisely enough that I could compute outcomes myself, indicating which dispute motivates each clause; (c) tell me honestly which of my verdicts must be abandoned under each candidate, since I suspect Disputes 2 and 4 cannot both be reconciled with Disputes 1 and 3, and I want to understand exactly why; (d) say what your preferred principle predicts for cases I haven't listed — unequal walk-away positions, say A keeps 30 if talks fail while B gets nothing, and a three-way split of the same kind; and (e) address what should anchor the "best case" — enforceable maximums only, or also credible aspirations — since Dispute 4 turns entirely on that. Reason step by step, feel free to brainstorm rival principles before settling, but end with a clear recommendation and an explicit list of which of my gut verdicts it forces me to surrender.
