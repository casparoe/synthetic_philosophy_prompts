I am trying to work out what I actually believe about the validity of consent, and I want to do it by reflecting on my verdicts in particular cases rather than by starting from a theory. Here are my verdicts, grouped by the three pressure points of deception, coercion, and competence.

Deception. (1) A surgeon, while operating on a patient's knee with consent, also removes a suspicious mole; the removal was medically sensible and cost the patient nothing. I say consent was absent — this is battery. (2) A patient is not told of a one-in-a-thousand risk of nerve damage; the risk materializes, but the patient says they would have consented anyway. I say the consent was valid; the omission was negligent but did not undo it. (3) Someone agrees to sex with a person who falsely claims to be wealthy and unmarried. Valid consent, though the deceiver behaved badly. (4) Someone agrees to sex with a person who falsely claims to be a specific famous director they admire. Invalid — this feels like the surgery case rather than the wealth case, though I cannot articulate why.

Coercion. (5) Handing over a wallet at knifepoint is not consent. (6) A supervisor makes clear that a promotion depends on sex; the employee's acquiescence is not valid consent. (7) A long-term partner says, truthfully, that they will end the relationship if the other is never again willing to have sex; the other has sex, reluctantly. I judge this valid consent, though the situation is grim. (8) Someone threatens to reveal an affair unless the spouse's lover sleeps with them. Invalid. Yet in both (7) and (8) the threatener threatens only to do what they are legally and perhaps morally entitled to do — leave a relationship, tell the truth. My verdicts diverge exactly where my reasoning does not.

Competence. (9) A lucid, enthusiastic person with moderate dementia consents to sex with their longtime partner, though they will not remember it tomorrow. Valid, on balance. (10) A mature, well-informed fifteen-year-old consents to a tattoo. Invalid, full stop — even though their grasp of the facts may exceed the dementia patient's. (11) A plainly drunk but coherent and eager person consents to sex with a sober stranger. Invalid, I think — but I would reverse the verdict if the stranger were their spouse and the two had discussed it sober the week before.

Is there a principle, or a small family of principles, that captures all or nearly all of this? My tentative candidate is: consent is valid just when the consenter understands the essential nature of what they are agreeing to and agrees free of any threat they have a right not to face. Please test that principle against my cases, repair it clause by clause where it fails, and tell me plainly which verdicts, if any, I must surrender as the price of coherence. I would also like the repaired principle's verdicts on two cases I have not settled: a person who agrees to sell a kidney while crushed by medical debt, and a participant in a clinical trial who signs the consent form without reading it.
