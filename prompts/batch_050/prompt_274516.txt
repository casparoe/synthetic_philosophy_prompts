I'm setting a 2-hour essay exam for an upper-level moral philosophy seminar and need one question on lexical threshold negative utilitarianism that can't be answered by just saying suffering matters more than happiness. Please use the stipulated theory and cases below, so students don't need outside texts or citations.

Define lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, or LTNU, this way: each outcome has a total suffering burden B and total pleasure P. There is a threshold T. If neither outcome crosses T, rank them by P − B. If exactly one crosses T, the non-crossing outcome is always better, regardless of how much pleasure the crossing outcome contains. If both cross T, rank them first by lower B and only then by higher P.

Case 1:
- Baseline: B = 99, P = 400
- Festival: B = 101, P = 1,000,000
- Clinic: B = 98, P = 0
Assume T = 100.

Case 2:
- Dispersed pain: B = 99 is spread across millions of people as brief mild headaches; P = 400.
- Concentrated pain: B = 90 consists entirely of one person's prolonged agony; P = 390.

Case 3:
A risky policy has a 98% chance of producing B = 50, P = 500, and a 2% chance of producing B = 120, P = 100,000. The safe alternative certainly produces B = 99, P = 0.

Draft the exam question around this issue: is LTNU a genuine middle path between ordinary utilitarianism and fully lexical negative utilitarianism, or is it just three different theories awkwardly stitched together? The question should require students to address:

1. what kinds of commensurability LTNU still requires even though it claims suffering can lexically outrank pleasure;
2. the cliff-edge created when B moves from 99 to 101;
3. whether aggregation hides the moral difference between dispersed and concentrated suffering;
4. whether LTNU should evaluate risky policies by expected B and P or by whether crossing the threshold is possible;
5. what the best repaired version of LTNU would look like, and whether that repair turns it into prioritarianism or classical utilitarianism in disguise.

Please provide the polished exam question, a model answer of about 750 words, and a marking scheme out of 30. The scheme should have six must-hit components, say what an A answer does that a B answer misses, and include one common way students get each component wrong.
