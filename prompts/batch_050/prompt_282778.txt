I am teaching Shirley Jackson's The Lottery in a political philosophy seminar and want to read it against Rousseau's account of the general will. The village's ritual is near-unanimous, self-administered, applies to everyone without exception, and is treated as the condition of the community's flourishing — so on the surface the lottery looks like a general will in action, and Tessie Hutchinson's stoning like a grisly case of being forced to be free. But Rousseau's own machinery supplies reasons to resist that reading: he distinguishes the general will from the mere will of all, holds that the people err when citizens vote under the sway of custom, deference, or partial interest rather than each judging the common good, and requires that legitimate law be authored by the sovereign people and apply impersonally. Work the reading out in both directions. On which of Rousseau's criteria does the lottery plausibly qualify as a general will, and on which does it fail? Give sustained attention to the awkward details: Tessie participates willingly until her family's slip is drawn, and Rousseau holds that when a lone dissenter is outvoted, she has simply misjudged what the general will is — a doctrine that seems tailor-made to describe her fate. Then take a stand: does the story show that Rousseau's test for legitimacy is empty, since nothing in it appears to rule the lottery out, or does it dramatize precisely the pathologies he diagnosed and thereby vindicate the theory? Finally, say where the reading strains against the story, and what Jackson's fiction contributes to this debate that Rousseau's arguments alone do not.
