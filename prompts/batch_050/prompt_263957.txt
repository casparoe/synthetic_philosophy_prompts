I'm a graduate student putting together a problem set for a seminar on population ethics and uncertainty, and I want a worked example that my students can't resolve by pattern-matching to the usual "shrimps and stunning" discussions that dominate the online literature. Here's what I need. Construct a thought experiment about insect sentience in which three different ways of handling low probabilities of sentience deliver three different verdicts about what to do. The three approaches are: (a) straightforward expected-moral-value reasoning, where each insect counts for its probability of sentience multiplied by some welfare weight, so that enormous populations with tiny credences can dominate the calculation; (b) a threshold or de minimis approach, under which probabilities of sentience below some cutoff are treated as morally negligible and drop out of deliberation entirely; and (c) a precautionary approach that, when the probability of sentience is genuinely uncertain rather than merely small, reasons as if sentience were likely enough to matter and prioritizes avoiding the worst-case outcome.

Constraints on the scenario: it must be realistic -- a decision an actual institution could face this decade, such as a municipal pest-control program, an agricultural policy, or a food-tech investment decision -- with no science-fictional devices and no stipulation that we somehow know the true probability of sentience. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph. The numbers should be plausible in scale (billions or trillions of insects is fine; a single laboratory colony of forty is not), but don't invent fake empirical precision; rough orders of magnitude are all the case is allowed to lean on.

After the scenario, walk through the reasoning each of the three approaches gives and show that they genuinely diverge -- one recommends acting, one recommends refraining, and one recommends an intermediate option such as choosing the least harmful method rather than abandoning the program. Then tell me which structural feature of the case is doing the work: is it the sheer population size, the contrast between a merely small credence and genuine uncertainty, or something about the asymmetry of the stakes? Say what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed or weakened. Finally, anticipate the strongest objection from a defender of each approach who wants to deny that the case forces the verdict I've claimed for them -- in particular, how the expected-value theorist resists being held hostage to arbitrarily large populations, and how the threshold theorist resists the charge that any cutoff is arbitrary.
