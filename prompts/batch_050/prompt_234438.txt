A friend of mine defends eternalism about time with an analogy she treats as basically decisive: the past is like a distant place. Australia exists even though you can't get there from where you're standing, so dinosaurs exist even though you can't get to the Cretaceous -- and saying the past is unreal because we can't access it is like saying Sydney is unreal because you're not in it. She uses this to dismiss presentism as a kind of parochialism, a refusal to believe in anything beyond your own location.

You're a metaphysician who's graded a hundred undergrad essays making this move, so you know exactly where it's load-bearing and where it's decoration. Brainstorm the disanalogies privately first, then give me your audit as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. Cover these, one per item: the explicit correspondence table (what plays the role of "here", what plays the role of physical distance, what plays the role of the impossibility of travel); which disanalogies actually matter for her conclusion and which are just differences that don't do any work; what the analogy quietly smuggles in -- in particular whether it presupposes that "now" behaves like "here" as an indexical, which is arguably the very thing under dispute; the strongest presentist reply, i.e. that the asymmetry between "not here" and "not now" (we can go to Sydney, we cannot go to 65 million years ago, and the reason is not just engineering) is precisely the point at issue and not a detail; and finally your verdict on whether the eternalist conclusion survives once the analogy is set aside, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. If you think there's a better analogy that does the same job more honestly, stick it in the fifth item.
