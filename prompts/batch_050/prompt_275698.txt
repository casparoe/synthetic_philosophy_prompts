A financial columnist defending the Kelly criterion writes that reinvested wealth is like a snowball rolling downhill: the sensible aim is not to possess the largest expected snowball at some arbitrary checkpoint, but to choose the slope along which it compounds most reliably. On that basis, she concludes that anyone with a long horizon should use full Kelly whenever the relevant probabilities are favourable, and that fractional Kelly is merely a loss of nerve. Consider a repeatedly offered double-or-nothing gamble in which the bettor wins with probability 0.55, loses with probability 0.45, and must restake the same fraction of current wealth each time. Audit the snowball analogy in detail. What corresponds to bankroll, bet size, growth rate, randomness, compounding and the investment horizon? Explain exactly how maximising expected logarithmic wealth differs from maximising arithmetic expected wealth, and whether the former is best understood as optimising the typical path, the median result, the long-run time average, or something else. Which disanalogies are harmless, and which matter once there is a finite horizon, a possibility of near-ruin, serial dependence between bets, changing odds, consumption from the bankroll, or uncertainty about the true probabilities? Does the snowball picture support the columnist’s claim that full Kelly is uniquely rational and that half Kelly is simply timidity, or are there reasons for fractional Kelly that the analogy conceals? If another analogy would better capture what Kelly betting does and does not establish, propose one.
