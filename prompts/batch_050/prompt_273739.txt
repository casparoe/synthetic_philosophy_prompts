I am a graduate student in political economy, and for the past year I have been part of a small working group on the socialist calculation debate. What strikes me is not that the group disagrees but that the disagreement never seems to move, and I would like a diagnosis of why. Please act as a philosopher of economics who is also a careful historian of this debate. Before answering, think through the structure of the disagreement, brainstorm at least three candidate diagnoses, and only then settle on the one you find best supported.

Here is where we are stuck. One faction reads Mises's original argument as a straightforward impossibility claim: without money prices for capital goods there is no way to compare alternative uses of them, and Lange's trial-and-error procedure, in which a central board adjusts accounting prices in response to surpluses and shortages, answers that claim on its own terms. This faction cites the later computational turn, in which writers such as Cockshott and Cottrell argue that modern hardware can solve the relevant systems of equations quickly enough for central planning to be feasible, and they point out that firms like Walmart or Amazon internally coordinate supply chains of a complexity comparable to a small economy without using internal markets.

The other faction says this concedes nothing, because by the time Hayek wrote his knowledge papers the argument had changed: the problem is not computing a solution to given data but generating and discovering the data in the first place -- dispersed, tacit, partly inarticulable knowledge of local circumstances, and entrepreneurial alertness that a price-adjusting bureaucracy has no mechanism to replicate. On this reading, the computational socialists are answering a question nobody was asking anymore by the middle of the twentieth century, and the internal planning of large firms is not a counterexample but the Coasean point itself: islands of conscious planning whose boundaries are set by the market prices surrounding them.

My working hypothesis is that this is at least three disagreements wearing one label: a logical question (is calculation without factor prices possible in principle), an empirical-engineering question (can existing or foreseeable computation approximate the price system's informational performance), and a conceptual question about what rational economic calculation even is (a static allocation problem versus a dynamic discovery procedure). I further suspect the impasse persists because each side's standards for what would settle it belong to different disciplines: one side treats the debate as settled by existence proofs and complexity bounds, the other treats it as answerable only through comparative institutional history, which the two sides never read the same way.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Please diagnose rather than adjudicate. In particular: Is there a hidden factual question doing real work here -- say, about how much economically relevant knowledge is articulable and transmissible -- or is the dispute genuinely about what counts as a solution to the original problem? Are the sides answering different questions under the single heading of the calculation debate, and if so, can you reconstruct, from what each side actually says, the question each is really answering? Does the a priori framing some Misesians give the argument immunize it against computational evidence in a way that makes the debate unfalsifiable, or is that framing a legitimate methodological claim about the scope of economics? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal result, a specified empirical test, an agreed taxonomy of the sub-questions, or an honest argument that no progress should be expected because the debate is at bottom about rival conceptions of what an economy is?

Two constraints on your answer. First, separate clearly what is settled among people who know this literature -- for instance, what Lange actually proposed, or what Hayek's later arguments were -- from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as consensus, including either the popular one in which Lange simply won or the popular one in which Mises was simply vindicated. Second, if my three-disagreements hypothesis is wrong or incomplete, say so plainly rather than fitting your diagnosis to it.
