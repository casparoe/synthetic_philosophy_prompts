I'm on my university's sustainability advisory board (mid-sized school, ~9k students) and we vote next month on whether to renew two policies from 2021, both under attack now that budgets are tight:

1. The endowment (about 340M) divested from fossil fuel companies, which was maybe 2% of holdings.
2. Dining halls went plant-based-by-default -- meat is still available but you have to ask, and the beef entree line was cut entirely.

A trustee who's an economist circulated a note basically saying both are pure symbolism. On divestment: every share we sell gets bought by someone else at basically the same price, so we've changed nothing except possibly making the shares marginally cheaper for someone who doesn't care. On dining: our total meat purchasing is a rounding error next to national demand, and the atmosphere doesn't notice one university's emissions either way. His conclusion is we should drop both, save the administrative costs, and stop "performing."

Two people pushed back at the last meeting and I want the memo to take them seriously:

- Our dining services director says the economist's framing works for emissions but NOT for meat purchasing. Supply isn't continuous. Regional distributors and farms make lumpy decisions -- whether to renew a contract, keep a processing line running, expand a herd -- and large institutional buyers dropping chicken or beef has a real, if probabilistic, chance of being the straw that tips one of those decisions. The expected number of animals affected per year isn't zero even if most years nothing visibly changes. She admits she can't prove which year it matters.
- A philosophy prof on the board says both sides are missing the point: the question isn't whether we move global numbers, it's whether the university should be profiting from or directly paying for practices it teaches students are wrong. Complicity, not consequences. The trustee responded to this by asking how far that logic goes -- do we audit the catering company's truck fleet? -- and honestly I didn't think she had a great answer.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote (keep it to a length people will actually read). It should give a clear recommendation for each policy seperately -- renew, drop, or modify -- and the recommendations need to follow from an actual position on when the inefficacy objection succeeds, not just vibes. Specifically: if the lumpiness argument is right, does it rescue dining but leave divestment dead, or does something analogous apply to capital markets? And say what the complicity argument, if you buy it at all, actually commits us to beyond these two policies, because the trustee's slippery-slope question deserves a real answer. Whatever you recommend, give the strongest version of the trustee's case first and don't strawman him, he's sharp and he'll be in the room.
