So I'm a novelist, working on something set in a fictional occupied city in the 1940s, and my main character is a railway clerk who schedules the freight trains, some of which carry deportees. He never hurts anyone himself, never even sees the deportees, and — this is the crucial bit for my plot — the trains would run on exactly the same schedule with or without him, because there are three other clerks who'd pick up the slack instantly. My critique partner read the draft and insists the clerk is complicit in the deportations, full stop. I've realized I actually hold the opposite view, or at least the position I've been writing from: you're complicit in a wrong only if your participation made some difference to whether it happened or how badly it went. If the outcome is fully overdetermined and the world goes exactly the same whether you show up or not, then you're maybe a coward, an opportunist, a moral free-rider, but you're not *complicit* — complicity has to bottom out in some kind of contribution, and a contribution that changes nothing isn't one.

Here's what I want: what would count against this position? Not just the obvious reply that my clerk "still participates" — I want to know which cases this view genuinely can't describe well, and which of those a defender of the view would concede are problems versus which they'd bite the bullet on. For instance, I've got a nagging feeling the view struggles with cases where someone joins a scheme *wanting* it to succeed and doing their assigned part in good faith, and it just happens to be redundant — does my position really have to say the eager joiner is off the hook while the sabotaging holdout is also off the hook, since neither changed the outcome? Also cases where the participation is expressive — where refusing wouldn't change the outcome but would change what the refuser *stands for*. Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which of them are live arguments in the literature on collective harm and overdetermination versus which are just my novel-brain inventing edge cases.

And one more thing: stress-test my position the way an unfriendly reader would. Is there a way the difference-making requirement could be *right* about causation but still leave complicity intact on some non-causal ground — like sharing the intention, or accepting the benefits, or sustaining the institution that does the harm? If those grounds work, does my view collapse into the one my critique partner holds, or does it survive as a narrower thesis about one kind of complicity? I'd rather know now, before I revise the manuscript, whether the moral architecture of my novel rests on a position that's defensible but lonely, or one that's just wrong. Also please dont just tell me what philosophers on each side say — tell me what would actually settle it, if anything could.
