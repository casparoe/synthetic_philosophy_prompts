So I'm a poll worker volunteer in California and last November a guy at my table asked me something I couldn't answer. He'd just voted on like twelve ballot propositions, and he goes, wait, I can amend the state constitution right now with a simple majority, but the legislature needs two-thirds just to raise a tax? Why is it easier to change the constitution than to change a budget line? I laughed it off but I've been chewing on it since. And then I was reading about how Switzerland does referendums constantly but requires a double majority (majority of voters AND majority of cantons) for constitutional changes, and how the Brexit referendum was just a one-shot 50%+1 on something arguably bigger than any constitutional amendment, with no quorum, no supermajority, no second vote once the actual deal was on the table.

Here's my question: what do these designs actually presuppose, philosophically? Like, what theory of popular sovereignty or legitimacy does a system have to hold for it to make sense that constitutional change is hard in Switzerland, easy-by-initiative in California, and in the UK doable by pure accident of one bad Tuesday? Does the Californian setup presuppose that "the people" are a more legitimate author of fundamental law than their representatives -- and if so, does that sit coherently with the same system making ordinary taxation *harder* for the legislature, as if the people trusted with constitutions can't be trusted with revenue? Or is the real presupposition something less romantic, like distrust of representatives rather than trust of voters? And the Brexit case seems to presuppose that a bare majority at a single moment settles a question indefinitely, which sits oddly with the fact that no one would run a jury, a legislature, or even a corporate board vote that way -- so is there some theory under which that's actually fine, or is it just philosophically underdetermined and any of several views would license it?

I'd love an answer that reconstructs the implicit theory of each practice, says which rival views it quietly rules out (deliberative vs. aggregative conceptions of democracy, consent theories, constitutionalism-as-precommitment, whatever's relevant), and tells me what would have to change in each system if you swapped in a different underlying theory -- e.g., if you really took seriously the idea that constitutions are precommitment devices against momentary majorities, does the whole California initiative process become illegitimate, or does it survive in some restricted form? Don't just tell me the designs differ; tell me what each one is secretly committed to.
