I'm a PhD student in philosophy of math, and this term I'm TAing my advisor's seminar on Benacerraf's dilemma (the causal-theory-of-knowledge vs. platonism pinch, not the identification problem, though god knows that comes up too). My advisor has this habit, over beers after seminar, of pronouncing on whether various mathematical believers actually *know* what they believe, and her verdicts have started to look like a genuine track record. So far: a grad student who believes the Pythagorean theorem because she's worked through Euclid's proof herself — knows. An engineer who believes 37 × 53 = 1961 purely because his battered old calculator says so — knows. A number theorist convinced Goldbach's conjecture is true because it's been verified up to some astronomical bound — doesn't know. A set theorist who believes CH is independent of ZFC because she read the standard textbook treatment, but who has never herself worked through a forcing argument — doesn't know (this one suprised me). Someone who believes the four color theorem, having followed the reduction argument carefully but taken the computer's case-enumeration entirely on faith — knows. And a student who believes some large cardinal is consistent because a famous guru in the field, right about such things maybe seventy percent of the time, assured him so — doesn't know.

Next week she wants to discuss a new case: a mathematician who believes a hard combinatorial theorem solely on the say-so of a theorem-proving system whose outputs have been confirmed correct in every independently checkable instance for a decade, but whose proofs are orders of magnitude too long for any human to survey. Predict her verdict on this one. But I'm less interested in the bottom line than in the reconstruction: lay out the rules about mathematical knowledge that are each consistent with all six earlier verdicts (I can gestate at least a couple — something in the neighborhood of "you must possess the proof yourself" versus "any reliable method will do" — but neither of those naive versions seems to survive all six, so the real candidates are presumably subtler, maybe something about whether the truth of the belief has to explain why she holds it, or a hybrid that treats computation and theoremhood differently). Say where the surviving rules diverge on the AI case, and which single earlier verdict is doing the most work in narrowing the field. Then tell me what her implicit rule suggests about which horn of Benacerraf she'd grab: is she quietly rejecting the idea that knowledge of abstracta needs a causal hook, or is she relocating mathematical knowledge onto facts about proofs, practices, and instruments rather than objects? Finally, give me one further case — not among the seven — whose verdict would best seperate the two most plausible surviving rules, and explain why it's the sharpest discriminator.
