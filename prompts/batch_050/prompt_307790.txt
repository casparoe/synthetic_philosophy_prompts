okay, strange request but: i've been invited to give the commencment address at a small military college next month — the kind of place where most of the graduating class commissions as officers at the end of the ceremony. the complicating detail is that i'm a pacifist. not performatively: i refused the draft in my twenties, did a short stint in jail for it, and have spent forty years arguing that organized war is something we should be abolishing rather than managing. the college knows all this and invited me anyway, which i think is to their credit, but it means i cant give the usual speech, and i also cant give the speech some of my friends are hoping for (a polite antiwar lecture), because lecturing 22-year-olds on the day their parents are watching them commission would be self indulgent.

write the address for me, about ten minutes, 1300 words or so.

heres the actual hard part, and i want the speech built around it. i genuinely admire these kids — the discipline, the seriousness, the willingness to subordinate yourself to something larger — while also believing the institution they're joining sometimes asks people to do morally catastrophic things, and i want both of those fully present in the speech rather than one of them being a fig leaf for the other. a few constraints that follow: no "war is hell, but..." rhetoric, since in my experience that phrase mostly functions to make war sound noble; no pretending i think their choice is straightforwardly fine when i dont; and no condescending "i respect your decision even though it's wrong" line, which is just an insult with good manners.

two things i do want in there. first, the point that soldiers carry moral risks the rest of us outsource to them — including the risk of being sent into a wrong war by politicians that people like me voted for — and that honesty from the podium requires me to own a share of that rather than standing there as the clean-handed one. second, briefly, the observation that willingness to die for something and the rightness of that something are independent variables, and that confusing them is how countries talk themselves into disasters. if you can make that second point land without it sounding like a logic seminar, you're doing it right.

the audience is the cadets, their families (proud, several generations deep in the military in some cases), and faculty officers including combat veterans. assume half the room is braced to dislike me and half is pleasantly curious. the speech should land as respect — not capitulation, and not a provocation dressed up as one either.

format: just continuous prose i can read aloud. no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, nothing like that — draft it the way you'd write a letter.
