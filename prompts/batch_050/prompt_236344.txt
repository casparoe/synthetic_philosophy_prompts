Before I spend more time comparing higher-order theories of consciousness in detail, I want a specification to judge them against. Write the desiderata any adequate higher-order theory must meet: the cases it has to handle (e.g., targetless higher-order states, where the representation of a mental state occurs without the state itself; states that are conscious without being attended or reportable), the distinctions it cannot collapse (being conscious of a state versus the state being conscious; a state's becoming conscious versus the creature's becoming self-aware; disposition versus occurrence), and the empirical and developmental constraints it should not flagrantly violate (non-reporting paradigms, infant and animal consciousness, the neural evidence people take to bear on prefrontal involvement). For each requirement, give the consideration behind it and whether it is a genuine adequacy condition or a commitment of one camp. The part I care most about: identify any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied -- for instance, whether a theory can both explain higher-order misrepresentation without collapsing into first-orderism and remain a distinctively higher-order view -- and say which single requirement rules out the most candidates. Then, in a few sentences only, indicate how the main variants (a thought-based view, a perception-like inner-sense view, and a self-representational view) fare against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard.
