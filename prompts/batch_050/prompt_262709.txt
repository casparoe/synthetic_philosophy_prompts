I've been thinking about what might be called *moral trade*, and I'd like help finding the principle behind my intuitions. By a moral trade I mean an arrangement in which two people each agree to do, or refrain from doing, something on moral grounds -- something the other party wants for moral reasons -- where neither would have done it absent the deal, and where the acts being traded are ones the parties regard as morally significant rather than merely inconvenient. Ordinary favors (I'll drive you to the airport if you water my plants) are not moral trades. I want to know when trading *these* is permissible.

Here are my verdicts:

1. Maya campaigns against factory farming and Jonah against deforestation. Each was planning to abstain in a referendum, but they strike a deal: Maya votes for Jonah's forest-protection initiative and Jonah votes for Maya's farm-animal initiative. This seems fine to me.

2. Priya and Sam each plan to donate $2,000 to their favorite charity. They learn of a matching arrangement whereby, if they each give to the *other's* charity instead, both donations are doubled. They do it, though each privately thinks their own charity does more good per dollar before the match. This seems fine -- even admirable.

3. Two state legislators trade votes: each votes for a bill she mildly opposes in exchange for the other's vote on her own priority bill. Both bills pass. I think this is acceptable, if a bit grubby -- ordinary legislative practice.

4. Two friends agree: I'll tell your boss you were sick last Friday if you tell mine I had a family emergency. Both lies are told. I think this is clearly wrong, even though it's structurally parallel to case 3 -- each performs an act for the other in exchange for the reciprocal act.

5. A citizen sells his vote in a mayoral election to a campaign for $500. No one is deceived, and he'll use the money well. I think this is wrong, and noticeably worse than the logrolling in case 3, even though in both cases someone votes against their judgment for a benefit.

6. A committed vegan who regards eating meat as seriously wrong agrees to eat meat at weekly family dinners for a year, in exchange for her spouse, who eats meat daily, going fully vegan. By her own lights, far fewer animals are harmed than if neither changed. She keeps the deal. This still seems wrong to me -- she's doing what she takes to be wrong, deliberately and repeatedly -- though I confess the arithmetic pulls at me.

And the case I genuinely can't settle: two activists, one who pickets abortion clinics and one who disrupts fur farms, agree to a mutual ceasefire -- each stops a campaign she regards as combating a grave moral evil, in exchange for the other doing the same. Nothing is traded *for* wrongdoing, yet each is paying for moral progress with moral retreat. I go back and forth.

Is there a principle -- or a small set of jointly necessary conditions -- that delivers verdicts 1 through 6, and what does it say about the ceasefire case? Please distinguish candidates that save all six verdicts from simpler principles that must overturn one, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts your preferred account cannot accommodate and why you think that verdict, rather than the principle, should go.

After your analysis, give me a table with columns: Case | My verdict | Principle's verdict | Status (accommodated or overturned) | Governing clause.
