I'm a teaching assistant for a criminal law seminar, and we're running a mock legislative hearing on a fictional bill I've drafted, the Pure Food Accountability Act. The bill's key provision makes it a misdemeanor for any senior executive of a food company whenever that company ships a contaminated product into commerce -- regardless of whether the executive knew about the contamination, had any reason to suspect it, or could personally have prevented it. The drafters' stated rationale is that companies respond to incentives, that proving what any particular officer knew is nearly impossible, and that the people best positioned to prevent poisoned food from reaching the public should bear the criminal risk. In our class discussion, one camp insists the provision is a straightforward corruption of criminal law, and they keep returning to a passage from Book IV, Chapter 2 of William Blackstone's Commentaries on the Laws of England, where Blackstone writes: "And, as a vitious will without a vitious act is no civil crime, so on the other hand, an unwarrantable act without a vitious will is no crime at all. So that to constitute a crime against human laws, there must be, first, a vitious will; and, secondly, an unlawful act consequent upon such vitious will."

Write a dialogue (roughly 600-900 words) between two equally sharp interlocutors preparing testimony for the hearing: one who believes the provision is defensible and one who believes it should be struck from the bill. Both should treat the Blackstone passage as common ground worth arguing over rather than as a trump card or an antique curiosity. In particular, they should contest what a "vitious will" could consist in here: the defender might argue that knowingly accepting an office whose business is feeding the public, profiting from it, and declining to build adequate safeguards is itself the relevant will, so the statute doesn't punish the will-less after all; the opponent might press that Blackstone explicitly excuses offences committed "by chance or ignorance," where "the will sits neuter," and that the conscientious executive who did everything reasonable is exactly that case. Let them genuinely answer each other -- the opponent should have a reply to the "accepting the office" argument (perhaps that it smuggles negligence in under the label of will, or punishes a role rather than a choice), and the defender should have a reply to the worry about punishing the faultless (perhaps about what the criminal label is for when the alternative is no deterrent at all, or about where the line between punishing conduct and pricing risk actually lies). Neither side should be a caricature, the dialogue should not end in easy agreement, and by the end the reader should be able to say precisely which premise about the nature of criminal fault the two characters still disagree on.
