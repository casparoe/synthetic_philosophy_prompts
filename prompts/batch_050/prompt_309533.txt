I run a small reading group on social ontology, mostly grad students, and last week one of them laid out an argument against 'generations' that I still havent found the exit from. It's clearly proving too much but I can't pin down where. Here it is, roughly as she gave it:

(1) A category is a real kind only if membership in it licenses inductions: finding out that one member of the category has some property gives you at least some reason to expect other members to have it.

(2) Membership in a 'generation' licenses almost no such inductions. Within-cohort variation on basically everything people care about — politics, religiosity, risk aversion, tech habits, when people have kids — dwarfs the between-cohort differences, and the boundaries are arbitrary lines anyway (does Gen Z start in 1995 or 1997? depends which consultancy you ask). Knowing that someone is a Millenial tells you next to nothing you couldn't already guess from their age.

(3) So 'Boomer', 'Millennial', 'Gen Z' and so on aren't real kinds. They're just labels we stick on date ranges and then mistake for explanations.

(4) But the test in (1)–(2) sinks all sorts of categories we obviously want to keep. The Europe/Asia border is an arbitrary line. 'Adolescence' has fuzzy boundaries and enormous within-group variation, yet developmental psych seems to study it fine. Plenty of species boundaries are messy (ring species, hybrid zones) and knowing an animal's species tells you little about many of its traits. Even 'the working class' fails (1) for lots of properties.

(5) If we're consistent, none of those are real kinds either, and then a big chunk of demography, developmental psychology, sociology and even biology turns out to be the study of fictions.

(6) That's absurd, so something between (1) and (4) has to give.

Her own view is that (1) is the problem, but honestly I think she picked it because it's the cheapest-looking premise, not because she diagnosed anything. Here's my bind: I'm actually sympathetic to (3) — I've long thought generation-talk is mostly astrology for op-ed pages — so I'd rather keep (3) and find the flaw somewhere that still lets me have it, rather than rescue real-kind status for adolescence and species by conceding it for Gen Z too. But maybe that preference is exactly what's blinding me.

So: which premise or step do you reject, and why did it look plausible in the first place? Price the alternatives for me — in particular, tell me what (1) would have to be weakened to in order to keep species and developmental stages as kinds while still excluding generations, and whether that weakened version is doing real work or just gerrymandered to give the verdict I wanted. If the honest answer is that there's no principled line and my instinct about generations was just snobbery, I'd rather hear that too.
