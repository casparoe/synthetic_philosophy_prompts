Two philosophers are preparing an adversarial collaboration on the question of whether psychopathic offenders know that their seriously harmful actions are morally wrong.

The first camp defends Minimal Moral Cognition. Its members argue that an offender can possess moral knowledge without feeling guilt, empathy, or any inclination to comply with morality. On their view, an offender knows that an action is wrong if he can reliably distinguish serious moral violations from mere rule violations, apply the judgment to himself as well as others, explain that the victim’s interests make the action wrong, and recognize that the action would remain wrong even if an authority permitted it or detection were impossible. Callous motivation, they insist, is a defect of concern rather than of knowledge.

The second camp defends Integrated Moral Understanding. Its members deny that fluent moral classification and rehearsed victim-centered explanations are sufficient for knowing that an action is wrong in the morally relevant sense. They argue that such knowledge requires appreciating other people’s interests as reasons that bear on one’s own conduct, and that this appreciation ordinarily involves some affective uptake: the victim must register as mattering, not merely as an object to which moral labels can be accurately applied. This camp does not claim that every wrongdoer must feel empathy, that moral knowledge guarantees obedience, or that emotional incapacity automatically excuses.

Draft the joint statement they should produce. It should contain the following sections:

1. Shared ground. State six to eight propositions that both camps could honestly accept. These should include, in some form, the heterogeneity of psychopathy, the inadequacy of treating either verbal assent or criminal conduct alone as decisive evidence, and the distinction between knowing a moral proposition and being motivated by it. Do not present contested empirical conclusions as settled.

2. Terminological agreement. Define and distinguish moral judgment, knowledge-that, understanding-why, affective recognition, motivation, and moral responsibility. Identify any earlier disputes between the camps that should be retired as merely verbal.

3. Precise disagreements. In a table, state each remaining disagreement in language that neither side would regard as loaded. For each disagreement, give the thesis endorsed by Minimal Moral Cognition, the thesis endorsed by Integrated Moral Understanding, and the practical or philosophical consequence that each side believes follows.

4. Cruxes. Identify four cruxes whose resolution would change at least one camp’s position. At minimum, consider whether affective salience is constitutive of moral knowledge or merely evidence for it; whether the capacity for moral understanding can be present without its characteristic exercise; whether recognition that another person’s suffering is a reason differs from merely reporting that others regard it as a reason; and whether an offender can fully understand a moral reason while remaining wholly indifferent to it.

5. Agreed diagnostics. For each crux, propose a hypothetical case, interview method, or behavioral test that both camps would accept as relevant, without assuming that it would be conclusive. Possibilities include unfamiliar harms not covered by memorized rules, role-reversal cases, judgments independent of authority and punishment, requests to teach a child why an act is wrong, and choices made when moral compliance is costly. Record in advance what each camp would predict if its view were correct, and specify what result would put pressure on each view rather than merely on a caricature of it.

6. Residual disagreement. End with a short paragraph that both camps could sign even if every proposed test remained inconclusive.

Do not adjudicate the debate or declare either camp victorious. The aim is a constructive document whose formulations are substantive but neutral, and which separates genuine disagreement from differences in terminology. Do not provide citations or a bibliography.
