Earlier this year, at thirty-four, I learned from a consumer DNA test that the man who raised me is not my biological father: I was conceived with sperm from an anonymous donor. I am coping with this reasonably well, except for one intrusive thought that a cousin of mine, who studied philosophy at university, made considerably worse. She pointed out that on a widely held view about the metaphysics of origins — a view she associated with Kripke's lectures on naming and necessity — a person's identity is fixed by their origins, and in particular by the particular sperm and egg they came from. Had the clinic reached for a different vial that day, she said, the child my mother raised would not have been me at all but someone else; I would simply never have existed. I found, and still find, this vertiginous: on her view, my entire existence apparently turned on a technician's inventory choice.

When I objected, my cousin reached for an analogy she plainly regarded as decisive. A river, she said, is individuated by its source: the Rhine could not have had the Rhône's source, because a river rising in different mountains would simply be a different river. Everything downstream can vary — the bends, the tributaries, the cities on the banks — while the Rhine remains the Rhine; but move the source and you have changed the subject. Likewise, she said, with a person and their originating gametes.

I would like this analogy audited rather than accepted or dismissed by instinct. Specifically:

1. Give me the explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the source, the headwaters, the main channel, the tributaries, and the mouth, in the personal case.
2. Identify where the mapping breaks, sorting the breaks into those that matter for my cousin's intended conclusion and those that do not. Worries I already have, without pretending the list is exhaustive: rivers have vague and contested sources, so the analogy may import a precision the target lacks; rivers split and merge, and early embryos do something at least superficially similar in twinning and chimerism; and we do seem able to divert or re-route a river's upper reaches while continuing to count it as the same river, which suggests the source is not doing the essentialist work she needs.
3. Say what the analogy quietly smuggles in — for instance, that origins are discrete events rather than gradual processes; that the gametes, rather than the zygote or the parents, are the relevant "source"; and that a heuristic about how we in fact trace and re-identify things has been converted into a claim about what is metaphysically necessary.
4. Judge whether her conclusion survives the audit. Even granting that some necessity-of-origins thesis is defensible, does the river picture establish the strong version she needs — that a different sperm or egg means a different person — or only something weaker, such as that we standardly identify individuals by tracing them back to their origins?
5. If the analogy cannot carry her conclusion, offer a better one, or a direct argument that could, with an honest statement of its costs.

If it falls out naturally, I would also welcome a word on whether any of this should actually change how I feel about the donor, my father, or the technician.
