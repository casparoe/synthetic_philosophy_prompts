Foreign policy people do this thing all the time where they reach for historical analogies to justify present decisions. Don't invade, it'll be another Vietnam. You have to stand up to him now, remember Munich. We're sleepwalking into 1914 all over again. Whole op-eds and Situation Room debates run on this, and it's not just pundits -- actual decision-makers talk this way, and entire subfields (applied history, "lessons of the past" courses at policy schools) exist to systematize it.

But here's what bugs me. A lot of the same people will, in other contexts, say stuff like "history doesn't repeat itself" or "every situation is unique" or roll their eyes at grand theories of historical development. And philosophers like Popper spent a whole book arguing there can't be laws of historical development, partly because history turns on things like the growth of knowledge, which we can't predict in advance (if we could predict tomorrow's discoveries, we'd have made them today).

So: what does the analogy practice actually presuppose? That there ARE lawlike regularities in history after all -- that appeasement-as-such fails, quagmire-as-such follows intervention-as-such? Or can it get by on something weaker, like loose tendencies or mechanisms, and if so what exactly makes "Munich" evidence for anything if 1938 is a sample of one? Also: is the practice even internally consistent with the "every situation is unique" line, and which of the two should give? Finally, if you fully bought the Popperian no-laws view, what would honest policy argument have to look like instead -- is there any legitimate use of historical cases left, or does the whole applied-history industry collapse?
