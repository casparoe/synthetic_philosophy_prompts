I chair my philosophy department's academic integrity panel, and since the widespread adoption of large language models we have been inundated with a new kind of case: student essays containing fabricated citations, invented quotations, and confidently stated falsehoods that no student would plausibly have come up with on their own. Our current sanctions code distinguishes sharply between deliberate deception (the most serious tier) and negligence or honest mistake (the least), and it was drafted long before anyone imagined a machine that produces fluent falsehood as a matter of course. At our last meeting, one member argued that every such case is straightforwardly lying, since the student submitted claims they knew they had not verified; another insisted the students have done nothing worse than misplace trust in a tool, and that the fault, if there is any, lies with the software. I found myself reaching for Frankfurt's analysis of bullshit — the suggestion that the bullshitter is neither lying nor merely mistaken, but indifferent to the truth-value of what they say — and I suspect the category does real work here, though I am not confident I am applying it correctly when the 'speaker' is split between a student and a model.

I would like a decision procedure the panel can actually use: a sequence of questions we should ask of each case, in order, that classifies the student's conduct as lying, bullshit, or honest error, given that the falsehood itself typically originated with the model and not the student. The procedure needs to tell us what evidence would settle each question (drafts, prompt logs, the student's own testimony about what they believed, whether they ever read the cited works), what to do when that evidence is unavailable, and — importantly — where the Frankfurt-style classification should stop mattering, since I recognize that a student's culpability may not track the speech-act category of what they submitted. Please also indicate where the procedure will misfire or underdetermine a verdict, and where the panel must simply exercise judgment about institutional values rather than apply a conceptual distinction.

To make the procedure concrete, please run it on three cases drawn from our recent docket: a student who submitted an essay containing three fabricated journal articles, and who admits she never checked them but insists she believed the model would not invent sources; a student who, when the model failed to find support for his thesis, prompted it repeatedly until it produced a plausible-sounding citation, which he then formatted into his bibliography; and a student whose essay repeats a subtle but consequential mischaracterization of a thought experiment, which the panel believes came from the model's summary of the secondary literature, and which the student appears to sincerely believe is accurate. For at least one of these, I would like the procedure to deliver a verdict I will find uncomfortable, if that is where the analysis honestly leads.
