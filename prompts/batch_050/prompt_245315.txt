I teach an intro ethics unit to high school juniors (about 24 kids, and we have a week of classes plus homework time to play with). I want to build an activity around Benjamin Franklin's self-improvement scheme from his Autobiography -- the bit where he draws up his list of thirteen virtues, makes a little book with a grid (virtues down one side, days of the week across the top), gives each week to one virtue while marking a black spot for every fault in the others, and hopes to eventually look down at a clean page. Most kids only ever hear the list itself, so I want them to actually run the scheme, not just read about it.

Design me the full activity. Roughly what I'm after:

- The rules of the student version. They can't just copy Franklin's thirteen (silence and humility will go badly in a 11th grade classroom, and I want them to feel the weirdness of having to operationalize a virtue at all -- Franklin defines order as letting everything have its place, which is doing a lot of work). Should they write their own list? How many items? Who decides what counts as a "fault"?
- The tracking mechanics. Something they can realistically keep up for 5 school days without it becoming homework theater -- where and how they record faults, whether anyone else sees the book, and how to handle the fact that Franklin scored himself, which is obviously an invitation to cheat or go easy.
- One in-class twist partway through the week that stages the problem Franklin runs into with Order -- he basically admits he could never get the hang of it and ended up concluding that chasing flawlessness too hard can make you ridiculous to other people (the whole speckled axe business). I'd like the kids to hit that wall themselves, e.g. by realizing the scoring system rewards gaming it or that the virtues conflict with each other, before I tell them Franklin hit it too.
- A debrief plan that maps what happened onto the actual philosophical questions: is moral perfection even a coherent goal, is virtue the kind of thing you can get by habit-tracking, what does the self-scoring problem say about self-knowledge, and was Franklin's project really about being good or about being seen as good.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with teenagers, and how to handle it.
- One honest paragraph on what the activity can't reproduce -- I'm guessing a week vs. Franklin doing this for years is part of it, and the fact that he tied the project to his religion and his career in ways my students won't, but tell me what I'm missing.

Keep it practical -- actual materials, timing per class period, and worksheets described concretely enough that I could make them. The kids should come away suspicious of self-help optimization culture, which I will defintely not say out loud to them.
