I serve on the seven-member purchasing committee of our student-run campus food co-op, and I'm also enrolled in an environmental ethics seminar this term, which is partly why I've been asked to write up our decision properly. Next month we vote on a member proposal to remove all beef from our shelves. The case I'm stuck on is the inefficacy objection, and I want the memo to take it seriously rather than wave at it.

Here is the wrinkle that makes our case odd. We order through a regional distributor that buys from ranchers in fixed lots: a lot is only commissioned once the distributor's aggregate weekly orders from all its client stores pass a trigger quantity, and the trigger is not public. Most weeks our beef order is a small fraction of the distributor's total. So on any given week, the co-op's order probably changes nothing: the same lots get commissioned either way. But occasionally our order is the one that tips aggregate demand across the trigger, and then a whole additional lot exists because of us. One committee member argues that since the probability of tipping is roughly our share of total demand, the expected effect of our order is exactly proportional, so the objection fails on the objection's own terms. Another member responds that this reasoning would vindicate literally any purchase of any size, which she finds suspicious -- and she adds that even if the expected-impact math is right, our actual decision is about what to stock, and the co-op is a collective agent, so the individual-inefficacy framing may be the wrong lens entirely.

Draft the committee's decision memo. It should: recommend a concrete policy (remove beef, retain it, or some conditional arrangement such as removing it from regular stock while allowing member special orders), so the committee has a motion to vote on; state whether the lumpy supply chain and the trigger probability actually defeat the inefficacy objection, or merely relocate it; address whether collective framing -- the co-op deciding as an institution rather than as a sum of shoppers -- changes what reasons apply; and consider whether non-causal considerations (not wanting our institution to be complicit in, or to signal endorsement of, a harmful practice) can bear weight if the causal argument turns out weaker than my colleague hopes. Give the strongest version of the losing side whichever way you come down: if you recommend removal, take seriously the member who says we are paying a real cost -- lost revenue, members who shop elsewhere for beef anyway -- for a symbolic gesture; if you recommend retention, take seriously the charge that the co-op's entire charter commits it to acting as if institutional choices matter even when individual ones don't. Roughly 800-1200 words, written so the committee can adopt it.
