QUESTION: You're driving home late on a highway that has two exits. The first exit puts you on a slow route home (payoff 0). The second exit takes you straight home (payoff 4). If you sail past both, you're stuck driving until morning (payoff 1). The catch: you're absent-minded. Each time you arrive at an exit, you can't remember whether you've already passed one, and the two exits look identical to you. Before setting off you can commit to a probability p of driving past any exit you find yourself at, and because of the absent-mindedness, whatever rule you settle on is the rule you'll end up applying at each exit.

(a) What value of p maximizes your expected payoff, calculated before you set off? Show your work.

(b) Now imagine you're actually at an exit. Given your answer to (a), what probability should you assign to this being the first exit? Does the plan from (a) still look like the right thing to do from the driver's seat? Explain the tension in a few sentences.

And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

STUDENT'S ANSWER:
(a) Say I continue with probability p at each exit. Then I exit at the first exit with probability (1−p) and get 0; I pass the first exit and take the second with probability p(1−p) and get 4; and I pass both with probability p² and get 1. So expected payoff = 4p(1−p) + p² = 4p − 3p². Setting the derivative equal to zero: 4 − 6p = 0, so p = 2/3. The expected payoff there is 4/3. So the optimal policy is to continue with probability 2/3 and exit with probability 1/3.

(b) When I'm sitting at an exit, it could be either one of the two, so I should assign probability 1/2 to each. Exiting right now gets me (1/2)(0) + (1/2)(4) = 2 in expectation, which beats the 4/3 from the plan, so once I'm actually at the exit I should just exit for sure rather than randomize. The ex ante calculation was made in ignorance of where I was, but at the exit I have new information (namely, that I'm at an exit), so the plan is obsolete and I should recompute from scratch. This is basically the same lesson as Newcomb's problem: evidential reasoning beats the naive causal policy, since the causal policy tells you to stick with the plan even when you have evidence that deviating does better.
