Please give me your credence in the following proposition, together with a decomposition of the reasoning that produces it.

Proposition: There are possible evidential situations in which a fully rational agent is required — not merely permitted — to hold an imprecise credence toward some proposition. That is, situations in which every sharp (point-valued) credence would constitute a rational defect, and only an interval- or set-valued credal state is rationally adequate.

For concreteness, anchor your answer in this test case. An agent is told that a coin is either strongly biased toward heads (90%) or strongly biased toward tails (90%), and receives no information whatsoever about which. Consider the agent's rational credence that the next flip lands heads. Symmetry seems to force 0.5 — yet this sharp value seemingly misrepresents the evidential situation, since the agent has no idea whether the true chance is 0.1 or 0.9, unlike an agent who has seen a fair coin flipped ten thousand times. Note further that the sharp-0.5 agent's state is subject to what is sometimes called dilation: conditioning on almost any new evidence about the bias causes the estimate to spread apart, whereas the well-informed agent's 0.5 is robust. The question is whether this contrast shows that a sharp 0.5 here is rationally defective, or merely that the same credence can be held on better or worse evidence.

Decompose your credence into at least the following sub-questions, assigning a rough probability to each with one or two lines of justification:

(a) Uniqueness: the probability that, for any total body of evidence, there is exactly one rational credence function. If this thesis holds, the proposition is false, so the operative term is the probability that uniqueness fails.
(b) Given that uniqueness fails, the probability that the failure is best modeled by imprecise or set-valued credences rather than by rational permissiveness among several distinct sharp credences — i.e., that the correct response to evidential thinness is a thick credal state, not a disjunction of thin ones.
(c) The probability that the higher-order reduction objection fails: the objection that any interval-valued state can be replicated by a sharp first-order credence combined with sharp higher-order uncertainty about the chance, so that imprecision is never strictly required but only an optional bookkeeping convention.
(d) The probability that the requirement claim survives the symmetry argument in the test case — i.e., that an agent who violates symmetry by adopting a non-0.5 interval's edge, or who adopts the full interval, is doing something rationality demands rather than something symmetry forbids.

State how your terms combine (they need not be independent; note any dependence you assume). Then tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to, which term you are least confident in, and what argument, result, or formal theorem — for instance, concerning whether accuracy-dominance reasoning extends to imprecise credences, or concerning the behavior of set-valued states under updating — would most change that term.

Finally: my own credence in the proposition is about 0.6; a colleague's is about 0.1. On the basis of your decomposition, identify the sub-question where our disagreement most plausibly lives, and say what each of us is probably assuming that the other is not.
