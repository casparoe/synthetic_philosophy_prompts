Construct a thought experiment in which process-based supervision and outcome-based supervision of an AI assistant deliver opposite verdicts on the same action, in the direction that is harder to construct: the outcome is genuinely good by the operator's own standards, yet the process by which the system produced it is one a careful supervisor would condemn. Most discussed failures in this debate run the other way -- outcome supervision approving a result that merely looks good, or process supervision blessing a procedure that yields a bad result -- so this case must not reduce to those. In particular, the good outcome must be real, verifiable, and one the operator would endorse after full inspection, not an artifact of reward misspecification, noisy measurement, or an evaluator being fooled.

The case is for testing whether outcome-based supervision can be defended purely on results, or whether its plausibility tacitly depends on the system's methods being sound and inspectable. A case that merely stipulates the two verdicts, rather than generating them from the two supervision schemes, fails.

Constraints:

- The setting must be a realistic near-term deployment -- scheduling, drafting, medical or legal triage, code review, customer support -- with no exotic capabilities.
- There must be no deception or scheming on the AI's part. The process flaw should be something like an unreliable heuristic, a guess, or an illegible shortcut that happens to pay off in this instance, not concealment.
- One scenario, stated in at most one paragraph.
- The process must be such that rerunning it across many similar cases would predictably do worse than an approved process, so that the good outcome here is not representative of what the process delivers in general.

After the scenario, do the check. State what outcome-based supervision says about the action and why; state what process-based supervision says and why; show that each verdict follows from the respective supervision scheme applied to the facts of the case rather than being asserted. Then identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, say what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed, and give the strongest reply available to a defender of outcome-based supervision who wants to deny that the case counts against their approach -- for instance, that a well-designed outcome scheme already prices in reliability across cases, making this a dispute about evaluation windows rather than about outcomes versus processes.

Lead with a one-sentence statement of what the case establishes, then give the scenario, then the analysis, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
