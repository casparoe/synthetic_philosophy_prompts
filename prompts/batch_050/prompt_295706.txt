Last night I got into a long and rather heated argument with a friend of mine who is a political theorist and a great admirer of Rousseau. I work in mechanism design, so my native vocabulary is preference profiles, aggregation rules, social welfare functions, incentive compatibility, equilibrium implementation, and the like. My friend kept insisting that Rousseau's general will is not just an aggregation of individual preferences -- that it is distinct from what Rousseau calls the will of all, that a majority vote can fail to express it, and that someone who is outvoted and made to comply with the law anyway is, in Rousseau's notorious phrase, being forced to be free. I pushed back that once you strip away the rhetoric, either the general will is some function from preference profiles to social outcomes (in which case Arrow's theorem and the Gibbard-Satterthwaite result tell us it will have all the usual pathologies), or it is not a function of individual preferences at all (in which case it is unclear whose will it is and how anyone, including the citizens themselves, could know it). My friend accused me of flattening the view by translating it into a framework that begs the question against it. We parted without resolution.

So here is what I would like. Restate the Rousseauian position -- the general will, its contrast with the will of all, the claim that citizens can be mistaken about their own general will, and the forced-to-be-free doctrine -- in the vocabulary of social choice theory and mechanism design, as faithfully as you can. I am fully fluent in this framework, so do not simplify. Then give me a residue report: which elements of the position translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in a vocabulary built out of preferences, aggregation rules, and equilibria. For each piece of residue, I want your honest judgment on whether it marks a genuine blind spot in my framework -- something social choice theory cannot express but a theory of political legitimacy needs -- or whether it is a sign that the original claim is less coherent than my friend believes.

Two ground rules. First, keep the translation honest: do not quietly build Rousseauian conclusions into the target vocabulary by, say, defining the common good into the social welfare function and then calling the result a translation. Second, I am particularly interested in the case of the outvoted dissenter. In my framework, a person whose preferred alternative loses is simply someone whose preferences were not satisfied at the social outcome, and there is no obvious sense in which coercing them realizes their own will. If Rousseau's claim about such a person genuinely cannot be expressed in preference-and-aggregation terms without collapsing into paradox or paternalism, say so directly -- and if it can be expressed, show me the formulation and tell me what it cost.
