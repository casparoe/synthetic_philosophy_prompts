Here was the exam question (short answer, ~300 words):

"Explain the difference between theoretical equipoise and clinical equipoise. Which does Freedman take to be the appropriate ethical standard for randomized trials, and why? Raise one objection to equipoise as a requirement for ethical randomization and say whether you find it convincing."

And here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Randomizing patients sits uneasily with the physician's duty of personal care, since randomization means not directly choosing what seems best for the patient in front of you. Equipoise is the standard meant to resolve this tension. Freedman distinguishes two versions. Theoretical equipoise requires the evidence for the two arms to be exactly balanced, so that the investigator is in a state of perfect indifference; Freedman argues this is so fragile that a hunch or an anecdote disturbs it, and it is almost never satisfied in practice. Clinical equipoise, which he endorses, is instead a property of the medical profession: it obtains when there is genuine, honest disagreement within the expert community about which arm is superior. For equipoise in this sense to hold, expert opinion has to be roughly evenly divided -- if a clear majority of specialists already favors one treatment, the requirement fails and randomizing would be unethical. An individual investigator's personal hunch that one arm is better does not by itself disturb equipoise, since hunches are not evidence, so a physician with a hunch may still ethically enroll patients. Because equipoise lives at the level of the profession rather than the individual, the natural way to check whether it holds is to survey the investigators actually conducting the trial; if they are genuinely uncertain, the requirement is met. Informed consent, while necessary, cannot substitute for equipoise. One objection is that equipoise is unstable, since evidence accumulates over the course of the trial, but this overstates things: the trial is precisely the instrument for disturbing equipoise, and it remains ethical until the evidence would convince the expert community. I find this response convincing, since otherwise no trial could ever be completed."
