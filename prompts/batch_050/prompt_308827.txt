I’m a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep seeing the same argument about Gödel’s incompleteness theorems and minds/machines. It goes in circles every time.

One side says something like: Gödel showed that any sufficiently powerful consistent formal system contains true arithmetic statements it cannot prove. A computer program is basically a formal system, but a human mathematician can understand why the relevant Gödel sentence is true. So human mathematical thought can’t just be computation.

The other side replies that this only works if we already assume the human mathematician is consistent, can survey every mathematical belief they’d ever accept, and is not itself implementable as some larger or messier formal system. A program can also accept the Gödel sentence if it is given the right extra assumption, such as the consistency of the original system. According to this side, the theorem shows a limitation on particular formal systems, not a special non-computational power of human minds.

My suspicion is that the debate is stalled because people are sliding between several different notions of proof, truth, belief, and computation, but I’m not sure. Is this ultimately:

- a technical disagreement about what Gödel actually establishes;
- an empirical disagreement about whether human mathematical abilities are computable;
- a conceptual disagreement about what would count as “following a formal system”;
- or a stalemate caused by each side idealizing human mathematicians differently?
