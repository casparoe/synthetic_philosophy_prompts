At our school's student council meeting last week, we were debating how to split a one-time grant between the robotics lab and the drama program. I said we should at least try to weigh the benefits of each against each other, and another student shut me down with the line that you can't compare apples and oranges — as if that ended the matter. It clearly doesn't end it, since the money has to go somewhere, but I couldn't articulate what exactly was wrong.

My request: treat that saying as if it were a philosophical thesis and reconstruct the different precise claims it could be making, ordered roughly from most trivial to most radical. For example, I can already see at least three candidate readings: that comparing the two is merely difficult or imprecise; that there is no single unit or scale in which both benefits can be measured; and that the two options genuinely stand in no evaluative relation at all — not better, not worse, not even equally good. I suspect there are further readings in between that I haven't thought of, and I'd like the full menu.

For each reading, please do three things. First, state whether it is even plausible that the reading applies to choices like ours. Second, test it against this scenario: suppose the drama program's funding were slightly increased, so that option became a little better along every dimension anyone has mentioned. If the choice between the increased drama funding and the robotics lab is still genuinely hard, what does each reading predict, and what does the outcome of that test show about which reading we were actually dealing with? Third, say whether the reading would actually justify my classmate's conversational move — that is, whether it licenses stopping the comparison rather than continuing it with less precision. My suspicion is that the readings strong enough to justify his move are false, and the true readings are too weak to justify it, but I'd like that shown carefully rather than asserted.
