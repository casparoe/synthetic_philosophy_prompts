I do scheduling for a clinic, so I spend all day moving small pains around: one patient waits longer so three urgent cases get in, one nurse loses a lunch break so nobody misses meds, that kind of thing. A guy I game with heard me complain and said utilitarianism has this famous seperateness of persons problem, and then he turned it into an argument that feels like it proves way too much. His version goes, in one breath: harms and benefits are entries in personal ledgers, not soup in one big pot; an entry can be cancelled only inside the same ledger, by a later gain to that very person, or by consent, or because the person made themselves liable; a clinic policy or tax or vaccine rule that gives A a real loss so B, C, D and E get bigger gains is moving entries between ledgers that do not transfer; therefore aggregation can never justify even a small uncompensated burden on one person; but if that is right then jury duty, airport noise, ambulance sirens at 3am, progressive taxes, quarantine, even asking one roommate to take the trash out so the whole place is not gross are all forbidden unless every burdened person consents or ends up better off; that is absurd, so the separateness of persons is just an accounting mood and we should go back to adding up total welfare.

I do not buy his final jump, but I also cannot say exactly which step is the bad one. I want to keep the idea that people are not little cups you can pour into one jug, because that seems like the whole force of the objection to utilitarianism. But I also want to keep normal coordination, where sometimes one person gets stuck with a cost for everyone else and we do not always track down perfect repayment.
