I serve on the five-person selection committee for our mathematical society's biennial research prize, which honors a single result published within the last six years. One of this year's finalists has landed us in a genuine dilemma, and the committee has asked me to draft the opinion we will vote on at our next meeting. I would like you to draft that opinion. (The names below are anonymized; please treat the case on its internal merits rather than trying to identify the real parties.)

The facts. Four years ago, Dr. A published a proof of a conjecture that had stood for roughly twenty years: that every structure in a certain well-studied class admits a particular kind of decomposition. The proof was celebrated, survived refereeing, and is the work our nominators cited. Eighteen months ago, a referee for an unrelated journal constructed an object that belongs to the class as the conjecture was originally stated, and showed that the proof's central lemma fails for it. Strikingly, the conjecture's conclusion still holds for that object — it does have the decomposition — so the theorem may well be true. What was refuted is the lemma: the proof, not (yet) the claim.

Dr. A has since published a revised paper. It does not repair the lemma. Instead, it redefines the class by adding a coherence condition, proves the theorem for the redefined class, and argues that the excluded objects are degenerate edge cases that were never the conjecture's real subject matter. The condition is not ad hoc on its face: it has independent uses elsewhere, and several senior figures say the redefined class is the one everyone should have meant all along. Against that: nobody wrote the condition down before the counterexample appeared, and the excluded objects had been studied as members of the class, without complaint, for years. The referee who found the counterexample is an early-career researcher who, under our current practices, would receive no recognition from any outcome.

The committee is split. Half hold that this episode is simply how serious mathematics advances: the original proof contained the essential insight; the refutation forced a clarification of what the problem was really about; the revised theorem is deeper and better understood than what was conjectured; and prizeworthiness should track insight rather than the literal statement first submitted. The other half reply that the citation would credit a proof of the conjecture, and no such proof exists; that the revision converts a refutation into a terminological victory; and that awarding it announces to the field that the way to secure a claim is to redraw its boundaries after a counterexample appears.

Please draft the committee's written opinion. It should recommend one of three courses — award the prize for the revised result under an accurately worded citation, decline to award this cycle, or postpone one cycle to see whether the original statement can be proved — and it must include: the reasoning for the recommendation; a fair statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side and why it does not prevail; a proposed principle, in two or three sentences, that the committee would be endorsing for future cycles; and, if you recommend awarding, a proposed wording for the citation and some gesture toward recognizing the referee. The opinion should be one the whole committee, including the eventual dissenters, can acknowledge as honest.
