Construct a thought experiment in which a machine -- a perfectly ordinary formal, mechanical system -- manages to come to know the truth of its own Gödel sentence, i.e. the very thing Lucas-style anti-mechanist arguments say no machine can ever do but any human mathematician can. Constraints: no oracles, no halting-problem magic, no quantum gravity in microtubules (Penrose can stay home for this one), and the machine must be describable in a page or less as an ordinary piece of software, like a proof verifier running on a laptop. The loophole you're allowed -- in fact the one I want you to build the case around -- is that the machine need not assert its own consistency: it can hedge, revise earlier outputs, or issue conditional claims. What the case is for: testing whether the consistency assumption is genuinely load-bearing in Gödelian anti-mechanism, or whether it's smuggled in.

After giving the scenario, do the following: (a) walk through the standard Lucas move step by step and pin point exactly which premise your machine escapes and how; (b) say whether a human reasoner placed in the machine's exact epistemic position could do the same thing, and if so whether this shows the Lucas argument slides between two different senses of "seeing that G is true" -- something like recognising its truth versus being entitled to assert it unconditionally; (c) identify the single feature of your case doing all the work, and say what happens if you remove it (does the scenario collapse back into the standard dialectic where the machine stalls and the human supposedly doesn't?); and (d) give the strongest reply a defender of Lucas or Penrose would make -- presumably that your machine never really knows G, it only knows G-on-the-assumption-of-its-own-consistency, which any theorem prover trivially does -- and say honestly whether you think that reply defeats the case or concedes the interesting point. Don't just stipulate that the machine knows G: the scenario has to generate that conclusion from how Gödel's theorems actually work, or it's cheating.

Please answer in British English. End your response with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by one short phrase stating whether the case shows the Lucas-Penrose argument requires an extra premise (and if so, which one).
