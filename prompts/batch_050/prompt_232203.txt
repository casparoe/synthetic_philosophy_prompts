I am a PhD student in public policy, and my dissertation examines how climate-model ensembles should inform long-lived infrastructure decisions — concretely, whether a coastal water utility should size its capital investments around the multi-model mean of regional precipitation and temperature projections or instead hedge against the tails of the distribution. At a recent workshop, a senior climate scientist defended the standard practice with an analogy I have since heard repeated many times: a multi-model ensemble such as CMIP is like a panel of independent expert forecasters, so the ensemble mean deserves our trust in the way a crowd's average does, and the inter-model spread measures our uncertainty in the way disagreement among experts does. The analogy is doing real argumentative work — it is being used to license both the mean-as-best-estimate convention and the spread-as-uncertainty convention — and I want it rigorously audited rather than repeated once more.

For context, here is what I take the target of the analogy to actually look like. The models share code, parameterization schemes, and calibration targets; all are tuned against substantially the same historical record; the ensemble is assembled opportunistically from whatever modeling centers happen to contribute rather than sampled by design; and each model outputs a simulation rather than a stated probability. My own tentative repair is to treat the ensemble as a family tree and to weight models by their estimated shared-code ancestry, thereby restoring independence statistically — but I am genuinely unsure whether this fixes the analogy or merely renames its central problem.

Please structure your reply as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the strongest genuine correspondence the analogy captures; (2) the disanalogy that most threatens the mean-as-best-estimate inference, and whether that inference survives on independent grounds; (3) the disanalogy that most threatens the spread-as-uncertainty inference, and whether it survives; (4) whether my ancestry-weighting proposal repairs the analogy or only relabels the failure; and (5) a more apt analogy for an ensemble of opportunity, together with which of the two conventions, if either, that better analogy would actually support.
