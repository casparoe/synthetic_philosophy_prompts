I'm a retired condensed matter physicist (spent 35 years mostly on disordered systems, if that matters) and since retiring I've been working my way through Hume on my own, no teacher, just me and the text and too much coffee. Right now I'm stuck on his essay "Of Suicide" — the one he wrote in the 1750s but which only got published after he died, because I gather even Hume's publisher thought it was asking for trouble.

Most of the essay I follow fine, but there's one passage in the section about duty to God where I lose the thread. Here it is:

"The providence of the deity appears not immediately in any operation, but governs every thing by those general and immutable laws, which have been established from the beginning of time. All events, in one sense, may be pronounced the action of the almighty: They all proceed from those powers, with which he has endowed his creatures. A house, which falls by its own weight, is not brought to ruin by his providence more than one destroyed by the hands of men; nor are the human faculties less his workmanship than the laws of motion and gravitation."

For context: the objection Hume is answering, as I understand it, is the old one that taking your own life usurps God's prerogative — that the power over life and death belongs to the deity alone, so suicide is playing God. And Hume's response seems to be that since God governs the world through general laws rather than by reaching in and fiddling with individual events, everything that happens (including whatever I do with my own life) is equally "the action of the almighty," so ending your life can't be an encroachment on providence any more than a house falling down is.

Here's where I want help:

1. First, just reconstruct the arguement for me properly — premises, conclusion, the whole thing in plain English. I keep half-getting it and then losing it when I try to write it down. In particular I don't understand what work the word "immediately" is doing in the first sentence, and whether the house example is supposed to be an analogy or something stronger.

2. Second, and this is what really bugs me: doesn't this prove too much? If every event equally expresses providence because it all proceeds from powers God endowed, then the same reasoning seems to excuse literally anything — burning down my neighbour's house, or worse. Now, Hume deals with duties to our neighbour and to ourselves later in the essay, so maybe he'd say sure, murder is also "no transgression of duty to God" but it's still wrong for the other two reasons, and that's fine because his conclusion here is deliberately narrow. But then has he actually shown anything interesting, or has he just quietly redefined "duty to God" so that nothing could ever violate it, which makes the whole section feel like a magic trick? I can't tell if I'm catching him in a cheat or if I'm misunderstanding the dialectic.

3. Third, the physicist in me wants to poke at the setup. Hume leans hard on laws being "general and immutable." Does the argument actually depend on that? Suppose the laws are statistical at bottom, or suppose there were genuine chance events — would the argument collapse, survive unchanged, or get stronger? My instinct is that the real engine of the argument isn't the immutability of the laws at all but something about the division of labour between divine and human causation, and the immutable-laws stuff is rhetorical scaffolding that happens to fit the Newtonian air Hume was breathing. But I might be projecting. Tell me whether the physics-flavoured premise is load-bearing or decorative.
