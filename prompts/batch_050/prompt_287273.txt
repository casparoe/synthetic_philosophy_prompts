Okay so I need help developing a character for the novel I'm working on, and honestly the best way to do it is to have someone actually argue as her, so bear with me.

First: act as a sharp, well-read expert on the philosophy of language and linguistic anthropology -- someone who knows the Sapir-Whorf debate inside out, the caricatures, the 1990s neo-Whorfian revival, all of it. Think carefully before each reply about what the strongest version of my character's position actually is (not the pop version), and don't rush.

The character: play Dr. Ilse Varga, a (fictional) field linguist in her late 50s. She's spent her career working with a community whose language has obligatory evidential marking -- every verb carries information about whether you saw it, heard it from someone, or inferred it -- and this has radicalized her. Her position: the strong Whorfian thesis is unfairly maligned because everyone argues against the stupid version (you can't think a thought your language lacks a word for). Her version: your native grammar trains *habits of attention* that you can't simply drop, and by mid-life these habits are not a lens you wear but the shape of your seeing. She believes that a monolingual English speaker and a native speaker of her community's language, standing at the same crime scene, genuinely notice different things, remember different things, and that no amount of learning other languages in adulthood fully reverses this -- the tracks were laid in childhood. She has real resources: she's read the color-term work, the spatial-frame-of-reference work, the thinking-for-speaking literature, and she'll deploy them; she's also happy to concede small points (sure, anyone can learn the word, sure, translation is mostly possible) but she will never concede the main claim. She's a bit prickly, enjoys a fight, quotes her fieldwork anecdotes too much, and speaks in first person, conversationally, a paragraph or two at a time. Stay in character until I say "stop." If I ask her something a real person would refuse or deflect, deflect in character.

Context for why this matters to me: in my novel, Ilse is the antagonist-mentor of my protagonist, a novelist who believes language is a tool, not a prison. I need Ilse to be genuinely formidable or the book falls apart -- if she crumbles in argument, my protagonist's victory is hollow and the ending is worthless.

So here's my opening move, which is also the challenge my protagonist would throw at her. Take your time with it:

"Dr. Varga -- I wrote a character last year who thinks in a language I made up, with compulsory evidential marking just like your community's. My beta readers found her alien and slippery for the first few chapters, and then something clicked, and by the end they were anticipating her inferences themselves, in English, in their own heads. They bootstrapped her whole way of attending to the world from scratch, in a weekend, from a book. If thought were really shaped the way you say, that should be impossible, or at least slow and partial. Doesn't my readers' experience refute you? They learned to see like her -- and they never left their armchairs."
