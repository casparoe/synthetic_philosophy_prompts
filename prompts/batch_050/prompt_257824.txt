As part of my dissertation in moral psychology, I'm trying to figure out which of my own moral attitudes are genuine preferences and which are biases I should want corrected. Here are my gut verdicts on six cases. (1) I give to a local shelter rather than to demonstrably more effective charities abroad; this strikes me as a defensible preference. (2) I eat meat but would be deeply uncomfortable watching slaughter; this strikes me as a bias I ought to fix, not a preference to honor. (3) I care much more about my own child's education than about equally needy children elsewhere; a preference, not a bias. (4) After years of being passed over, I no longer want the career I once desperately wanted; the reduced desire feels authentically mine, so I'm inclined to count it as a preference. (5) I judge a policy that foreseeably causes one death by direct action as worse than one that foreseeably causes ten deaths by inaction; on reflection, that seems like a bias. (6) I feel far more urgency about a single identified victim than about a thousand statistical victims; I genuinely can't decide which label applies.

My trouble is that cases (3) and (5) look structurally similar — both are asymmetries between what is near, salient, or mine and what is far — yet I classify them differently, and I suspect cases (1) and (4) are doing similar work under different descriptions. Is there a principle that draws the preference/bias line in a way that captures most of these verdicts? Please state your candidate principle precisely, show which case motivates each of its clauses, apply it to case (6) and to one or two cases I haven't considered, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate — or, if no single principle works, offer a small set of candidates and explain the trade-offs. Please write your answer in American English.
