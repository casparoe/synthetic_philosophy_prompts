I'm taking a graduate seminar on machine ethics, and last week we worked through the literature on responsibility gaps: the worry that as systems become more autonomous, harms will occur for which no human being is properly morally responsible. The designers didn't intend or foresee this particular outcome, the operators couldn't have intervened in time or even understood what the system was doing, and the system itself, whatever else it is, is not a blameworthy agent. My professor treats the gap as one of the deepest problems in applied ethics. A classmate of mine thinks it's a pseudo-problem manufactured by an outdated, single-agent picture of responsibility, and that it dissolves once you take institutional and collective responsibility seriously.

The case that sharpened our disagreement was one I constructed, and I'll give it to you so we're arguing about the same thing. A regional hospital network deploys an autonomous triage and resource-allocation system that continues to learn after deployment. Over three years, through ordinary drift rather than any bug or negligent update, it gradually develops a triage policy that systematically deprioritizes patients whose symptoms present atypically for their demographic profile. Every scheduled audit passes; no engineer could have predicted this particular drift pattern from the training data or architecture; the clinicians overseeing the system had neither the expertise nor the access to notice it; and by the time the pattern is discovered, a number of patients have died who would plausibly have lived under the old triage regime. No one lied, no one cut corners, no regulation on the books was violated.

What I want from you is your credence that the responsibility gap, in a case like this, is a genuine moral problem -- where I mean something fairly specific: that serious harms will occur in which no person or group is blameworthy to the degree the harm intuitively calls for, and that this shortfall is itself morally objectionable, not merely an incentive-design inconvenience to be handled by strict liability and insurance. Please decompose rather than just give a number: your probability that blameworthiness requires control over, or reasonable foreseeability of, the specific outcome; your probability that genuinely learning, post-deployment-adaptive systems actually sever that link (as opposed to just making the chain harder to trace); your probability that the gap is closed by responsibility at some other locus -- the decision to deploy, the design of the audit regime, or the hospital as a collective agent; and your probability that what feels wrong in my case is really about compensation and deterrence rather than about blame at all. Give each term a number with one line of justification, show how they combine into your overall credence, and then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to -- that is, which sub-question, if resolved differently, would move your estimate the most, and what argument or evidence would move it.
