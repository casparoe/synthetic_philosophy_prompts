I’m giving a 20-minute department talk defending a limited version of performance legitimacy, and I need a Q&A prep-sheet.

My thesis is that reliably producing safety, health, education, and broadly shared prosperity can give an authoritarian government provisional legitimacy, but never an unlimited or permanent right to rule. I call this constrained output legitimacy. On my view, China is the hard central case and Singapore is the comparison case, though I’ll acknowledge upfront that calling Singapore straightforwardly authoritarian is contestable. I’m explicitly not arguing that growth cancels repression, only that political legitimacy can come in degrees and that consent-based procedures are not the only possible source of it.

The talk starts from this passage in John Stuart Mill’s Considerations on Representative Government:

“To find the best form of government; to persuade others that it is the best; and, having done so, to stir them up to insist on having it, is the order of ideas in the minds of those who adopt this view of political philosophy. They look upon a constitution in the same light (difference of scale being allowed for) as they would upon a steam plow, or a threshing machine.”

My interpretation is that Mill is identifying an instrumental view of government, but not necessarily endorsing the crude claim that governments should be judged only by outputs. I want the objections to pressure both my reading of Mill and my application of it.

The audience will contain: two democratic theorists who think equal political agency has intrinsic value, not merely instrumental value; one Rawlsian who will push on public justification and reasonable disagreement; one quantitative comparativist who distrusts legitimacy surveys and counterfactual claims about what democracy would have delivered; and one historian of modern China who will be alert to uneven benefits, coercive state capacity, and the danger of treating “China’s performance” as one undifferentiated thing. Nobody is a tankie, but nobody thinks “authoritarianism is bad, QED” is an argument either.

Please give me the eight objections I’m most likely to get, ordered by how damaging they would be if I handled them badly. Phrase each the way the relevant audience member would actually ask it, not as a strawman. For every objection include:

1. my best 90-second answer;
2. what I should concede;
3. what I should firmly refuse to concede;
4. where my answer is weakest;
5. a distinction that might help — e.g. legitimacy versus justice, legitimacy of a regime versus legitimacy of particular decisions, or output legitimacy versus justification to the governed.

Also tell me which objection I’m secretly hoping nobody asks, and draft a calm answer to it anyway. Please don’t give me a generic list about human rights and economic growth; make the objections turn on Mill’s machine metaphor, feedback under censorship, the counterfactual baseline, distributional blindspots, and whether successful rule tends to disable the mechanisms needed to judge that success.
