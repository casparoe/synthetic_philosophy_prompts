I'm writing a piece of film criticism and want a philosophically serious reading to build on. Read Spike Jonze's Being John Malkovich through Daniel Dennett's essay "Where Am I?", in which Dennett imagines his brain removed and kept alive in a laboratory vat while his body, radio-linked to the brain, wanders about in the world — and imagines, further, that the brain can be replaced at the flick of a switch by a computer duplicate of it, with no difference he can detect from the inside. The film literalises much of this apparatus: a portal behind a filing cabinet deposits the visitor inside John Malkovich's head for roughly fifteen minutes, seeing out through his eyes, before ejecting him beside the New Jersey Turnpike, while the visitor's own body slumps vacant on the office floor. Dennett's moral, as I read him, is that "where am I?" has no tidy answer — neither the brain, nor the body, nor the point of view will serve as the self's address — and I would like the reading to test that moral against the film, and the film against the moral.

In particular, the film seems to me to distinguish four modes of occupation that Dennett's thought experiment runs together: visiting (the fifteen-minute rides, where the occupant is a mere passenger, with a point of view but no control), puppeteering (Craig's learned control of Malkovich's body, where control is achieved but the occupant remains a parasite on a life that is not his), residence (Lester's collective scheme of riding the "vessel" into immortality), and entrapment (the ending, where Craig is locked inside Emily, seeing but never driving). I would like the essay to ask whether Dennett's framework even has the resources to register these distinctions — whether it can say what goes wrong, metaphysically and morally, when control is achieved without ownership — and, in the other direction, where the film gets the philosophy wrong, for instance in treating a person's head as a location that one can literally be in. Two scenes I especially want pressed: Lotte's single ride, after which she becomes convinced she should have been a man — does the film suppose that a point of view is gendered, and what would Dennett make of that? — and the moment Malkovich himself crawls through his own portal and finds a world populated entirely by his own face. Please reach a verdict: does the film vindicate Dennett's conclusion that the self is not the sort of thing that has a location, or does it quietly rely on precisely the old-fashioned occupant-and-vehicle picture of persons that Dennett was trying to demolish?
