I am a retired population ecologist who has lately been reading philosophy of biology, and I keep running into a dispute whose stakes I cannot quite pin down. The old objection against Darwinian explanation — that "survival of the fittest" is vacuous because fitness is defined by survival, so the principle amounts to "survivors survive" — is, as I understand it, supposed to be dissolved by the propensity interpretation, which redefines fitness as a dispositional property: an organism's expected reproductive success given its traits and environment, rather than its actual number of offspring. That move seems to me to fix one problem while quietly generating several others. If fitness is an expectation rather than an outcome, then a fitter organism can die childless and a less fit one can leave ten offspring, and natural selection stops explaining the actual distribution of traits in any population — which was, I thought, the whole point of invoking it. I have also encountered the suggestion that fitness differences do not *cause* evolutionary change at all but merely *summarize* it statistically, the way an average summarizes a population without pushing any individual around.

Could you explain, as clearly and precisely as possible, how the propensity interpretation is meant to escape the tautology charge, whether it genuinely succeeds, and what new difficulties it inherits? In particular, I would like you to distinguish the charge that the theory is circular from the charge that fitness is merely a redescription of the data rather than a cause of it, to say something about the tension between defining fitness as an expectation and explaining actual reproductive outcomes, and to indicate where the causal-versus-statistical dispute about selection leaves a working biologist who just wants to say that the faster gazelle outcompeted the slower one because it was fitter. Please keep the whole response under 550 words.
