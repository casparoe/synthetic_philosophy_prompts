I'm a novelist (midlist literary stuff, nothing you'd have heard of) and I also run an evening class at our local arts centre for amateur writers. Lately half the class is using AI tools to brainstorm, and every session slides into the same argument: does the machine actually understand the stories it helps them write, or is it just shuffling words around very convincingly? So I've decided to give one whole evening — 90 minutes, whiteboard, no laptops aloud — over to the Chinese Room thought experiment as the spine of that discussion.

Plan the session for me. Specifically:

- The order the pieces have to come in. My instinct is they need the distinction between following rules for manipulating symbols and knowing what those symbols mean before the room itself will land — but what else has to be in place first, and what should wait until after the room has done its work?
- The one running example to build the room around. Should it be a story version (a woman locked in a room answering letters in a language she can't read, using a huge instruction book?) or something more abstract, and what does the example need to include vs. what must I keep out of it so it doesn't smuggle in the conclusion or get bogged down in logistics?
- The misconceptions they'll bring or form on the spot, roughly in the order they'll surface. I'd guess: "the woman would just learn the language eventually," "a faster room with a better book would understand," "this is supposed to prove computers can never be creative," and the sneaky one — "fine, the woman doesn't understand, but the room as a whole does." Tell me where in the sequence to head each one off, and which of them deserve to be taken seriously rather than defused.
- How I'll know by the end whether they've actually grasped the difference between producing convincing text and understanding it, and haven't just picked a team in the AI wars.
- What to openly leave out at this level — consciousness, the official menu of replies, whatever — and how to flag that I'm leaving it out without making the argument seem weaker than it is.

Keep in mind these are writers, not philosophers: comfortable with metaphor, allergic to jargon, and at least two of them have strong personal feelings about their AI tools that I don't want the evening to turn into a referendum on.
