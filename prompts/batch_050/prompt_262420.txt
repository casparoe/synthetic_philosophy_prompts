A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the procreation asymmetry — the idea that bringing into existence a person who will suffer badly is a moral wrong, while declining to bring into existence a person who would have been happy is not a missed good, because there is no one in the second case whom the omission harms. We were talking about it concretely, in relation to whether I should have a child, and we kept getting stuck on two wrinkles that neither of us could resolve.

First, we disagreed about whether the asymmetry survives when the decision is probabilistic rather than certain. Suppose a couple learns that any child they conceive has, say, a ten percent chance of developing a condition involving chronic severe pain, and a ninety percent chance of an ordinary, flourishing life. My friend argued that under the asymmetry, the expected-happiness calculation cannot simply average the outcomes, because the downside registers as a genuine moral cost while the upside registers as morally neutral — so the gamble looks worse than a naive expected-value comparison suggests. I replied that once you reason this way, almost every act of procreation counts as a net moral risk, since every child faces some probability of a bad life, and that this seems to prove too much.

Second, we clashed over how the asymmetry interacts with a timing decision. If I can either have a child now who would have a merely decent life, or wait several years and have a different child who — because of changes in my circumstances — would very likely have a much better life, the asymmetry appears to imply that waiting is not better, only less risky, since the unborn better-off child cannot be wronged by my not waiting. My friend found this conclusion absurd and took it as a reductio of the whole framework; I suspect it is instead a bullet worth biting.

Please describe the most important implications of the procreation asymmetry for how I should think about my own decision whether and when to have a child. In particular, address whether the asymmetry really does make risky procreation look systematically worse than expected-value reasoning suggests, whether it genuinely collapses the moral difference between "decent life now" and "better life later," and what someone who accepts the asymmetry should say to existing people — including me — who would be made worse off by forgoing parenthood, since those are harms to people who definitely exist.
