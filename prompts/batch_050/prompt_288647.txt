So I'm a novelist, twenty years in, mostly literary fiction, and I keep circling this question about whether being as morally good as possible is actually a good way to be. I read a summary of Susan Wolf's thing about "moral saints" -- the idea that a person who devotes everything to being maximally good ends up kind of... unappealing? bland? missing the stuff that makes a person a person? -- and I had this jolt of recognition, because it sounds EXACTLY like a craft problem I deal with constantly: characters who are too good are dead on the page. Readers don't beleive in them, don't love them, don't remember them.

So here's what I want. Take Wolf's argument (as best you understand it) that maximal moral goodness is not a desirable ideal, and restate it entirely in the vocabulary I actually work in -- the vocabulary of fiction craft. Character arcs, flaws, reader identification, likability vs. believability, stakes, interiority, the "save the cat" beat, why readers forgive a bastard with a wound but skim past the nice guy. Be as precise as you can: which of her claims maps onto which craft principle.

But the part I care about most is the residue report. I want to know what DOESN'T survive the translation:

- which of her claims translate cleanly into craft terms (and basically become something a good creative writing teacher would say),
- which ones you had to fudge or distort to fit, and be honest about the fudging,
- and which ones have no counterpart at all in fiction vocabulary.

And then the verdict: when something doesn't translate, is that because my craft vocabulary is missing something real (novels aren't lives, after all, and maybe "would this person be fun to read about" just isn't the same question as "is this a good way to live"), or is it because Wolf's argument was partly trading on an aesthetic intuition about people that's weaker than it looks once you separate it out? I have a suspicion the whole argument is secretly a claim about narrative, and I want to know if that suspicion holds up or if I'm just seeing everything through my own hammer-and-nail situation.

Format, roughly, in this order:

- "The Translation" (~350 words): Wolf's position stated in fiction-craft vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage
- "What Survives" (~200 words): the clean mappings
- "The Fudges" (~200 words): where you had to bend things, and how
- "The Residue" (~250 words): what has no expression in craft terms at all
- "Whose Problem Is It" (~200 words): your verdict on whether the residue is a gap in my framework or a weakness in hers

Don't be diplomatic in that last section. I can take it.
