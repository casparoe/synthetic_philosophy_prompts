I’ve read rationalist discussions of coherent extrapolated volition, reflective equilibrium, and idealized preference for years, but I still do not know what work extrapolation is supposed to do beyond correcting obvious factual mistakes. I’m trying to infer my own criterion from cases rather than begin with a formula. Consider the following. Mira buys a plane ticket because she falsely believes an important meeting is tomorrow; if she knew it had been cancelled, she would not want the ticket. My verdict is that her extrapolated preference is not to buy it. Theo loves a piece of harsh modernist music, fully understands why others dislike it, and continues to love it after reflection. My verdict is that extrapolation should preserve this preference even though there may be no deeper justification for it. Noor wants a cigarette at this moment but has stably and lucidly endorsed quitting; my verdict is that the extrapolated preference overrides the craving. Amos, raised in a strict religious community, freely and reflectively places great terminal value on obedience; he understands alternative lives and still chooses the monastery. My inclination is to respect that preference. Lea’s devotion to a charismatic political leader was produced through prolonged deception and psychological conditioning. She now knows how she was manipulated, yet the induced devotion has become stable, coherent, and wholehearted. My gut says her extrapolated devotion should not automatically inherit authority merely because the fully informed and internally coherent future Lea would still endorse it.

Amos and Lea are the pair I cannot easily reconcile. If stability, information, coherence, and reflective endorsement are sufficient, both preferences seem authoritative; if a suspect causal history can disqualify Lea’s devotion, it is unclear why Amos’s religious formation should not also be suspect. I also have a third case that pressures the usual demand for coherence: Ilya has two genuine terminal values, personal independence and loyalty to his family, which conflict over whether he should emigrate. My intuition is that extrapolation should not flatten this into a single utility score, manufacture a lexical ranking, or select a lukewarm compromise merely to achieve consistency. It should preserve the conflict while perhaps helping him understand which considerations are truly at stake. In other words, I want coherence among beliefs and instrumental plans, but I am not convinced that coherence among final values is always an improvement.

What principle, or small set of principles, best captures these verdicts? I am especially interested in a formulation that distinguishes correcting ignorance from replacing a person’s evaluative outlook, distinguishes first-order desires from reflectively endorsed ones without simply letting whichever desire is calmest win, and prevents extrapolation from laundering manipulation into authenticity. Please identify which case motivates each clause, state what the resulting account implies about new cases I have not listed, and be explicit about which of my verdicts must be abandoned if no elegant principle accommodates them all. Avoid merely saying that the ideal agent would know more, think longer, and be more coherent; specify what kinds of changes the idealization is permitted to make and how much it may change someone before the resulting preferences cease to be that person’s. At the end, say which part of your proposal is least secure, what its strongest objection is, and what argument or counterexample would most plausibly change your verdict.
