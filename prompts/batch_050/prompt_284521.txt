In everyday life I have noticed that our judgments of blame shift dramatically when we learn that someone has a medical diagnosis, and I am trying to understand what view of responsibility this practice commits us to.

A few observations about how it works. If a colleague is repeatedly curt and dismissive, I resent him; if I later learn he was in the middle of a severe depressive episode, the resentment typically dissolves, even retroactively -- the past incidents themselves feel different, though nothing about them has changed. A teenager's screaming outburst in a quiet ceremony provokes anger until we learn she has Tourette syndrome, at which point the anger is not merely suppressed but seems no longer appropriate at all. Yet the excusing power of diagnosis has strange limits. We do not stop blaming a manipulative fraudster on learning that he scores high on psychopathy measures, even though that condition is presumably no more his fault than depression is the depressed person's fault. And sympathy has a reverse gear: the irritable colleague is excused while untreated, but if he refuses to take medication that reliably controls the irritability, we blame him again -- not, apparently, for the outbursts themselves, but for the refusal, as though responsibility had been relocated rather than removed.

So my questions are these. What theory of moral responsibility does this informal practice presuppose? Does it assume that blame is appropriate only when the person could have acted otherwise, or is it tracking something subtler -- perhaps whether the behavior expresses what the person really stands for, or whether they are a fit target for the particular emotional responses that blame consists in? Second, is the practice's internal line-drawing coherent: is there a principled basis on which depression exempts and psychopathy does not, and on which we excuse symptoms while holding people responsible for managing their own condition, or is this a patchwork that no single theory can rationalize? Third, how much does the practice strictly require as opposed to merely fit comfortably with -- could several rival accounts of responsibility license roughly the same pattern of excusing? Finally, what would have to change in our ordinary reactions if we concluded that no one is ever responsible for having the dispositions they have?
