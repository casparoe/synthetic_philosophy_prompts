I'm on the advisory board of a mid-sized climate philanthropy that is considering dedicating roughly its entire three-year grantmaking budget -- enough to fund maybe eight to ten researchers -- to conceptual and normative work on solar radiation management governance, rather than to atmospheric science itself. At a recent retreat, our program officers kept circling around a proposal one of them sketched, which we have been informally calling a "continuity escrow": states participating in a deployment program would each pay a substantial, non-trivial sum into a common fund every year that deployment continues, and the accumulated funds would be disbursed to affected populations only in the event of an abrupt, uncoordinated termination -- the idea being to make the termination shock financially self-punishing for whoever causes it, and thereby to make the deployment commitment credible in a way treaties apparently cannot. We found ourselves unable to agree on which conceptual questions would need to be resolved before anyone could responsibly design or evaluate such a mechanism, and that disagreement made us realize we have no principled way of deciding what to fund at all.

So here is what I'd like from you. Identify the open questions in this area -- spanning the moral hazard problem, the politics and legitimacy of deployment decisions, and the termination shock problem -- that you think matter most for a funder in our position, and then pick the three you would actually prioritize, in order. I am specifically not asking for a survey of the debate or for a list of everything that is unresolved; I want a defended agenda. For each of your three, explain why it is upstream of other work (that is, how many other design and policy questions cannot be answered until this one is), how tractable it is for philosophers, political theorists, and economists as opposed to climate scientists, and how neglected it currently seems relative to its importance. Where your criteria conflict -- say, a question that is hugely important but probably intractable, or one that is tractable but already crowded -- tell me how you are trading them off.

Two further requests. First, stress-test the continuity escrow idea itself as you go: if one of your prioritized questions would, if resolved a certain way, show that mechanisms of this kind are conceptually confused or normatively illegitimate (for instance, because they amount to hostage-taking by the deploying states, or because they presuppose an answer to the "who decides" question that no one is entitled to give), say so -- a ranking that quietly assumes the mechanism is fine would be much less useful to us than one that might kill it. Second, for each prioritized question, tell me what evidence, argument, or real-world development over the next two or three years -- a regional deployment experiment, a breakdown of a particular international negotiation, a formal result in game theory -- would move it up or down your ranking, so that we can revisit the agenda rather than locking it in.
