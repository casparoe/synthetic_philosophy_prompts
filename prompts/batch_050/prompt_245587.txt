I sit on the works council of a regional freight company, and on Friday we must vote on management's proposal to fit long-haul drivers with headbands that read scalp EEG and vibrate when the signal pattern indicates the onset of drowsiness, typically a minute or two before drivers themselves notice anything. The proposal followed a near-miss involving a close colleague of mine, and the safety case is genuinely strong: our insurer will cut premiums, and the drivers' own survey shows most of them want the alerts. The dispute concerns the data pipeline. Management wants the raw signal retained and scored so that supervisors can flag "chronically inattentive" drivers for route reassignment, and a minority on the council holds that any employer recording of neural data is categorically off-limits, however crude the measurement, because brain activity is the last private interior a person has and consent extracted under employment pressure is not consent. The majority replies that the device reads nothing like thought content, no more intimate than a heart-rate strap, and that whether a driver is falling asleep at the wheel of a forty-tonne truck is not a purely private fact. Draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote: it should recommend approval, rejection, or approval with specified conditions, and it should take seriously the strongest version of the losing side, including whether mental privacy can extend to states of one's own mind one has not yet become aware of, and whether a categorical ban on neural monitoring could survive the same reasoning applied to accepted workplace biometrics.
