I spent my career as an experimental molecular biologist and, since retiring, have been reading philosophy of science in the evenings. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher with expertise in evolution, computation, and the history of biology, and to help me think through something that has been nagging at me. Please do not answer immediately. First, brainstorm: list several candidate ways the case below could be mapped onto the theory, several ways it might resist the theory, and the strongest objections to each reading. Then compose a structured final answer drawing only on what survives that scrutiny. Think carefully and write for a scientifically literate reader with no philosophical training.

Here is the theory, as I understand it. Daniel Dennett argues that Darwin and Turing each performed an inversion of the usual order of explanation. Before Darwin, it was assumed that competence requires comprehension: that intricate, well-designed biological machinery must trace back to a comprehending designer. Darwin showed that a mindless, mechanical process of variation and selection can generate competence on that scale without any comprehension anywhere in the chain. Turing performed a parallel inversion for reasoning itself: where it had been assumed that getting the right answers requires understanding the meanings of one's symbols, he showed that a mechanism blindly following formal rules can be reliably competent at tasks we regard as intellectual, without grasping anything. Dennett's slogan for the upshot is competence without comprehension.

Here is the episode. In 2020, DeepMind's AlphaFold entered the CASP protein-structure-prediction competition and produced predicted structures approaching experimental accuracy on many targets, in a field where I watched talented people struggle for decades. Earlier, in 2016, its sibling system AlphaGo defeated Lee Sedol, and its move 37 in the second game was described by top professionals as creative, even beautiful, and as something no human would have played. In both cases the systems' designers could not tell you, in the vocabulary of the domain, why a given output was right.

Analyze this episode through the lens of the inversion idea, in both directions. First, the mapping: where exactly is the competence here, and what would comprehension amount to in this case — understanding organic chemistry, understanding what a fold is for evolutionarily, understanding the concept of a prediction? Be precise rather than gesturing. Second, does the episode confirm the inversion, or does it strain the competence–comprehension distinction — is there a category the theory does not anticipate, such as prediction that works without any mechanistic story a scientist could tell? Third, the other direction: what does the episode show about the theory? Is comprehension a single thing or a bundle, and if a bundle, does the inversion lose its bite? Finally, I spent my working life assuming that in science, genuine competence — the kind that earns credit and trust — requires comprehension. Tell me honestly what the episode does to that assumption, including where the theory is simply silent and I should not force it to speak.
