I sit on the technical advisory panel of a regional flood-forecasting agency, and at our next meeting we must recommend which of two early-warning systems to procure for the coming decade. The first is a compact, physically interpretable hydrological model with a few dozen calibrated parameters; the second is a large neural network trained on decades of gauge and satellite data, which has beaten the compact model by a modest but consistent margin on held-out historical floods, though nobody can fully explain what it has learned. Last night I had a long argument about this with a friend who teaches philosophy of science. She insisted that preferring the simpler model is not mere tidiness but has a principled justification: Solomonoff induction shows that an ideal Bayesian reasoner assigns prior weight roughly 2^-K to hypotheses, where K is Kolmogorov complexity, so Occam's razor falls out of probability theory itself, and minimum-description-length reasoning is the practical shadow of that result. I pushed back that we never actually compute Kolmogorov complexity, that the answer depends on an arbitrary choice of reference machine and description language, that the theorem is incomputable in any case, and that in our actual decision the "simpler" model is only simple relative to a notation we happen to like. She answered that this is a counsel of perfection -- the finite, messy version of the argument still licenses a real, if defeasible, preference for parsimony -- and we parted without agreeing on what, if anything, simplicity buys you when the more complex option is currently winning on predictive score.

I would like you to draft the decision memo I will circulate to the panel before the meeting. The memo should take an actual position -- recommend one system, or one system with binding conditions -- and it should treat the simplicity debate as decision-relevant rather than decorative: the panel has members on both sides, and the memo needs to tell them what the Solomonoff/Kolmogorov line of argument does and does not entitle us to conclude in a concrete procurement case like ours, where interpretability also matters because engineers must diagnose failures and defend them in public after a disaster.

Please write the memo in American English, structured in the following named sections, in this order: "Recommendation" (one short paragraph stating the bottom line up front); "The Decision Before the Panel" (roughly 120 words summarizing the two systems and what is at stake); "The Case for the Parsimonious Model" (roughly 150 words); "The Case for the High-Capacity Model" (roughly 150 words, giving the strongest honest version of this side, whichever way the recommendation goes); "What Simplicity Arguments Actually Establish" (roughly 200 words, engaging the Solomonoff and minimum-description-length justification for Occam's razor and its known limitations, in terms a mixed technical panel can follow); and "Conditions and Next Steps" (roughly 100 words, with concrete requirements attached to the recommendation).
