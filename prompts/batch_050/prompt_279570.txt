You are a philosopher with expertise in corrective justice, reparations, and population ethics. Please think carefully before answering; it may help to first sketch informally how each of my commitments is standardly motivated, and only then to examine the set for joint tenability.

I have been asked to contribute a personal position statement to an interdisciplinary volume on reparations for historical injustice, and before I finalize it I want an independent audit of whether my views actually hang together. Here is where I currently stand:

1. The United States federal government is a persisting corporate agent, and as such it can owe debts for its own historical wrongs. Its obligation to make reparations for slavery and for the Jim Crow regime did not lapse when the officeholders responsible for those policies died.

2. The ground of this obligation is counterfactual harm. Descendants of enslaved people are worse off today than they would have been in a world without slavery and its aftermath, and reparations should be calibrated to close that gap.

3. I accept the non-identity problem in full generality: an act or policy cannot harm a person if that person would not have existed had the act or policy not occurred. This is why I think wrongful-life claims fail, and why I think our climate obligations cannot be grounded in harm to particular future individuals whose existence depends on which policies we choose.

4. No one is guilty of, or blameworthy for, wrongs committed before they were born. Moral responsibility attaches to one's own agency, and I reject any framework that treats present-day Americans as sharing in the guilt of slaveholders.

5. Where wrongful gains from slavery can be traced — to institutions, endowments, or family fortunes — the beneficiaries owe a larger share of the reparative bill than the general public, and those gains should be disgorged before general taxation is used.

6. Reparations are a matter of corrective justice, owed to the specific successors of specific victims. They should not be conflated with, or replaced by, forward-looking anti-poverty policy aimed at whoever currently happens to be worst off.

My questions:

First, which pairs of these commitments are in genuine tension, and which pairs merely appear to be? I want you to discriminate rather than flag everything. For each genuine tension, rank its severity, state precisely what the problem is, and identify what would have to give. For each merely apparent tension, explain what distinction dissolves it.

Second, for each genuine tension, identify the cheapest available revision — the smallest change to the set that restores consistency — and say what that revision costs me in terms of claims I would have to abandon.

Third, if I could revise only one commitment, tell me which single change would do the most to clean up the position, and why.

I am not asking for a literature survey or for citations; I want the audit itself, conducted on the merits of the views as stated. I also have no preferred escape route in mind, so please do not assume I am more attached to any one commitment than to the others.
