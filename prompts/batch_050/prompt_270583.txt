A colleague pressed me with the following argument, and although I'm sure it goes wrong somewhere, I can't locate the step. (1) For any robust behavioral pattern, a resourceful theorist can devise a plausible scenario in which that pattern would have enhanced fitness in the ancestral environment — and, just as easily, one for its exact opposite: if parents invest heavily in offspring, children were scarce and precious; if they neglect them, offspring were cheap and numerous. (2) A hypothesis that can be made to fit any possible outcome, including its negation, is confirmed by no outcome; a fit that survives whatever you observe carries no evidential weight. (3) Therefore adaptive explanations of human behavior have no evidential standing; evolutionary psychology is storytelling. (4) But the recipe in (1) works just as well for physiology: for any organ one can spin a scenario in which its structure served fitness, including rival scenarios — the heart's beat as courtship display rather than as byproduct of pumping. (5) So by the same reasoning, adaptive explanations in physiology have no evidential standing either. (6) That conclusion is absurd; functional anatomy is among our best-confirmed science. I want to keep (2), which looks like straightforward confirmation theory, and I want to keep much of (3)'s spirit — I suspect a good deal of the field really is under-evidenced — without swallowing (5). Which step do you reject, why did it look innocent, and what does rejecting it commit you to? If your answer is that behavior and physiology differ in testability, specify what concrete evidence could discriminate between rival adaptive stories about a behavior, the way comparative anatomy and experiment do for organs. Reply in at most 500 words, leading with the step you reject.
