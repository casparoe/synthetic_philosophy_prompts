I'm a literary novelist, not a philosopher, but I've been asked to guest-host three episodes of a university philosophy podcast while their regular presenter is on sabbatical, and my final guest is a philosopher of mind who has spent much of her career defending the reality of qualia against eliminativist critiques -- she's written a new book arguing that the qualitative character of experience is both real and irreducible, and she is famously unflappable in interview settings. My interest in the topic is not purely professional: the novel I'm currently drafting has a protagonist who works as a professional coffee taster, and who comes to suspect, over the course of a decade, that while the coffee still tastes exactly the same to her, her capacity to enjoy it has quietly drained away -- or possibly the reverse, that the taste itself has changed and her fondness is intact. When I read Daniel Dennett's 1988 essay "Quining Qualia" while researching this character, I was struck by how directly it speaks to her predicament, especially the Chase and Sanborn passage, and also by this claim from the essay's opening section:

"Qualia are supposed to be special properties, in some hard-to-define way. My claim--which can only come into focus as we proceed--is that conscious experience has no properties that are special in any of the ways qualia have been supposed to be special."

I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher of mind with a specialization in consciousness studies and some experience preparing journalists for technical interviews. Before drafting anything, please brainstorm: first, reconstruct the strongest version of my guest's likely position; second, list the points at which Dennett's essay actually bites rather than merely provokes; third, identify the three or four deflections she is most likely to reach for when pressed. Then, drawing on that groundwork, draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions. The sequence should open by having her explain, in terms a novelist's readers could grasp, what the "specialness" in the passage above is supposed to consist in, and then tighten progressively: I want at least one question on the Chase and Sanborn disjunction -- whether my fictional taster's predicament involves a change in the coffee's taste-quale or merely in her reactive attitudes toward an unchanged quale, and how she would even in principle tell the difference in her own case; at least one on whether Dennett's explicit concession that conscious experience has properties quietly hands the qualia realist everything she needs; and at least one on Dennett's burden-shifting insistence that appeals to private, subjective properties be treated as, in his phrase from the same section, "guilty until proven innocent" -- whether she accepts that framing or thinks it begs the question. For every question, give me the answer she will most plausibly give and a follow-up keyed specifically to that answer, so I'm not left stranded when she deflects. Close the sequence with a question that puts my coffee-taster scenario directly to her and asks what it would take, observationally or otherwise, for her view to be wrong about a case like that. Please think carefully and avoid generic questions about her career or what drew her to the topic; every question should turn on the actual content of the dispute.
