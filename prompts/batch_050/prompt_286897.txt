I am preparing the final examination for my upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, in which we spent two weeks on Daniel Dennett's three stances (physical, design, and intentional) and on his defense of a mild realism about patterns. In past years I have asked students to define the stances and apply them to familiar cases such as thermostats and chess computers, but I have concluded that such questions reward memorization: a student can recite the definitions, note that the intentional stance is cheap and predictive, and earn full marks without ever confronting what is philosophically at stake. I would like you to draft a single essay question, answerable in a two-hour sitting and worth 20 marks, built around a case the students will not have seen: a deterministic cellular automaton whose behavior can be derived exactly from its update rule only by laborious step-by-step simulation, but which admits a compact, highly reliable description in intentional terms (it "seeks" stable configurations, "avoids" certain regions of the grid, and so forth). The question should force candidates to take a position on what, if anything, would make the intentional description of this system true of the world rather than merely useful to us: is it sufficient that the pattern affords a compression of the data that no purely physical description can match without intractable computation, or must the pattern also play some role in producing the system's behavior, and not merely in our predictions of it?

The question should also push candidates on the observer-relativity worry that shadows any compression-based realism: if a pattern is real because it is indispensable to some predictor, does that make reality hostage to the predictor's cognitive limitations and interests, and can the observation that physically very different observers would converge on the same pattern defuse the worry without smuggling in mind-independence by the back door? Along with the question, please supply (i) a model answer of roughly 600–800 words written at the level of a strong undergraduate; (ii) a marking scheme organized around five must-hit points, stating what each point requires and how many marks it carries (totaling 20); (iii) for each point, a one-line diagnosis of how weaker candidates typically fumble it — for instance, conflating predictive usefulness with explanatory reality, or treating the design stance as a halfway house rather than a distinct commitment; and (iv) a description of one type of answer that sounds sophisticated and will be common among well-read students, but that should nonetheless be capped at 12 out of 20, together with a justification of the cap.
