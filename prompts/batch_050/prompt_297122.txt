I design distributed systems, and I am fluent in that field’s vocabulary: autonomous agents, local state, message passing, coordination protocols, consensus, commitments, failure detectors, partitions, retries, and incentives. Restate three leading accounts of collective intentionality—Bratman’s account of shared cooperative activity, Searle’s account of we-intentions, and Gilbert’s account of joint commitment—in those terms, one at a time. For each account, identify what corresponds to agents, intentions, beliefs, mutual knowledge, obligations, coordination, and the group’s capacity to act. Then give a residue report: which parts translate cleanly, which can be expressed only by adding assumptions or changing their meaning, and which have no counterpart in distributed-systems vocabulary. In particular, determine whether the supposed difference between a collection of interlocking individual intentions and a genuinely plural subject is merely a difference between non-atomic and atomic system-level descriptions, or whether it marks a normative or phenomenological phenomenon that the engineering vocabulary cannot capture. Conclude by saying whether the untranslated residue exposes a limitation of distributed-systems concepts or suggests that some philosophical claims about group agency are under-specified.
