Is there an established name for the following pattern, which I keep noticing and can't place? A person creates a false impression not by saying anything false, and not even by saying anything that would be misleading if considered on its own, but purely by choosing which true things to say over time. Two examples of what I mean. First, a manager is asked for a general account of an employee's year. He mentions four projects the employee completed, all accurately described. He doesn't mention the two that failed. No single thing he says is objectionable; if you asked him about any one of the four projects in isolation he would say exactly the same words, and there was never a moment at which some specific question was dodged or some specific omission was glaring. The false impression lives in the selection, not in any utterance. Second, a friend, whenever I ask how things are going with her partner, tells me a true recent story about something thoughtful he did. Every story is true. She never once says the relationship is going well. But after a year of this I hold a firm belief that it is going well, and it is in fact collapsing. Again, no individual report implied more than its content; the false belief was produced by the pattern as a whole.

The obvious nearby categories don't seem to fit. Lying is out, since everything said is true. Lying by omission is closer, but that seems to describe cases where a particular piece of information was called for in a particular context and withheld; here there is no particular omission to point to, only a running selection policy. Cherry-picking is usually a charge against an argument or a data set, not against an ongoing pattern of interpersonal remarks. I gather there is also a literature on misleading through true but suggestion-laden statements, but the cases discussed there seem to involve individual utterances that would mislead a hearer on their own, whereas my phenomenon requires aggregation: examine any one utterance and there is nothing to object to at all.

So my questions are these. Does this selection-only way of creating false beliefs have an established name, or at least an established home in some literature? How does it relate to the standard distinction between lying and misleading, given that it seems to be a third thing: not false assertion, and not misleading implicature either, since no implicature ever occurs? And where does it fall morally? People disagree about whether ordinary misleading is as bad as lying or a lesser wrong, but this case seems to test the disagreement in a new way, because the speaker can truthfully say of every utterance that it was accurate and unforced, and yet the overall effect may be exactly the false belief a liar would have aimed at. If there is no existing concept that covers this, I'd like you to coin a term, define it precisely, and mark its edges: does it require an intention to deceive, or does negligent or even accidental unrepresentativeness count; does it require an extended series, or can a single utterance be misleading in this way; and how does it differ from ordinary, inevitable selectivity, since everyone always says only a fraction of the true things they could say?

Please answer in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
