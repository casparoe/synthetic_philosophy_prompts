Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a committed one-factor theorist and a committed two-factor theorist of delusion formation, written so that both could sign it. The one-factor theorist holds that a delusion is an attempt, continuous with ordinary abductive reasoning, to explain an anomalous experience -- on this view, the patient with Capgras syndrome who receives anomalous affective or familiarity signals upon seeing his wife is reasoning about as well as anyone could when he concludes she is an impostor, and the pathology lies in the experience, not the inference. The two-factor theorist holds that an anomalous experience alone cannot explain a delusion, because people with comparable experiential anomalies often fail to form delusions, and so a second deficit -- in belief evaluation, hypothesis testing, or the balance of prior against evidence -- must be present as well.

The statement should contain four sections. First, a numbered list of claims both parties can endorse, including what each syndrome actually shows (for instance, what the contrast between Capgras and anosognosia for hemiplegia does and does not establish, and the agreed phenomenology of Cotard, mirrored-self misidentification, and the persecutory delusions of schizophrenia) -- the hard part is phrasing these so they commit to more than the data but less than either theory. Second, a table of residual disagreements, each formulated as a single proposition that one party affirms and the other denies, with the wording vetted by the affirming side (for example, whether the one-factor theorist must deny that any second cognitive factor exists, or only that none is necessary). Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one party to revise, distinguishing empirical cruxes from conceptual ones about what counts as an explanation of an experience at all. Fourth, for each crux, the observation, experiment, or case -- actual or merely possible -- that both parties agree bears on it, with each party's prediction recorded in advance in one sentence, so that neither can accommodate the result after the fact.

Write in American English. Keep the tone of a document prepared for a professional audience of philosophers of psychiatry and cognitive neuropsychologists, and flag in a brief closing note any formulation where the two parties still suspect they are agreeing about words rather than about the world.
