I'm a second-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of mind course, and my professor has asked me to design and run one of the weekly discussion sections while she's at a conference. The section has about 18 students and lasts 75 minutes, and the topic for that week is Searle's Chinese Room argument. Most of the group has read the standard presentation but finds it flat on the page -- they either nod along or dismiss it as an intuition pump without being able to say which intuition is being pumped. I'd like you to design a structured activity that makes them feel the force of the argument themselves, staged rather than lectured: one student should actually occupy the role of the rule-follower inside the room, working from a finite rulebook that maps incoming symbol strings to outgoing ones, while the rest of the class submits inputs and judges the outputs, so that the group runs into the gap between fluent-looking behaviour and the rule-follower's total incomprehension before anyone names the argument. Please specify the rules, roles, and materials in enough detail that I could run it cold (including how to construct a rulebook simple enough to operate in real time but rich enough that the outputs look intelligent for at least a few rounds, and how to handle the fact that some students may read Chinese -- assume I'll use an invented script if needed). I'd also like the activity to have a second and third phase that deliberately stage the two most familiar counter-moves rather than leaving them to me to explain: one phase where the class is forced to treat the whole setup (rule-follower, rulebook, and slips of paper) as the candidate understander, in the spirit of the systems reply, and one where the room is coupled to real events in the classroom so that the symbols are caused by things in the environment, in the spirit of the robot reply -- each phase engineered so the class discovers for itself what the move gains and what it gives up. Then give me a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the session onto the argument's actual logical structure (what exactly the conclusion is, why it's about sufficiency rather than simulation, and where the other-minds reply bites), warns me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and is honest about which feature of the argument the activity cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply by other means -- I'm especially unsure whether anything staged in a classroom can convey whatever work the sheer speed and scale of the imaginary rulebook is doing in the original thought experiment.
