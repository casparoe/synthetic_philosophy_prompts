I have spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and most debates about epistocracy versus democracy seem to stall at the same exchange: one side points to widespread voter ignorance, while the other asks who gets to design and control the competence test. I would like a more disciplined treatment.

Draft the joint statement that could emerge from a serious adversarial collaboration between the following two people:

- Mara, a competence-weighting epistocrat. She believes that political influence over a decision should vary with demonstrated competence concerning that particular decision. She does not defend rule by credentialed experts, general intelligence tests, or restrictions based on education. Her central claim is that equal voting power has no intrinsic moral priority when some citizens can reliably understand a policy’s mechanisms and consequences better than others.
- Leon, a legitimacy-first democrat. He believes that equal voting power is partly constitutive of equal political standing and helps authorize coercion over people who dissent. He supports deliberation, civic education, sortition, and institutional expertise, but rejects systems in which officials formally assign some adults greater electoral weight because of their judged competence.

They are evaluating a proposed institution called Decision-Specific Influence Weighting:

1. All adult residents retain one vote in every referendum.
2. For selected high-impact referenda, residents may take an optional assessment covering what the proposal changes, its principal causal mechanisms, foreseeable tradeoffs, major sources of uncertainty, and which groups bear its costs and benefits.
3. The assessment does not test names, dates, ideological loyalty, or agreement with expert policy conclusions. Its questions come from a large public item bank, may be retaken, and are graded anonymously.
4. A passing resident receives two votes rather than one.
5. After three election cycles, a randomly selected citizens’ assembly reviews disparities in passage rates, effects on turnout, evidence of capture or manipulation, and measures of policy performance. It may recommend modification or repeal.

Write the document Mara and Leon could both sign without thereby agreeing to adopt the proposal. It should contain:

A. At least ten numbered agreed claims. Separate empirical, normative, and institutional claims. Avoid vacuous formulations such as saying merely that both knowledge and legitimacy matter.

B. A table of their remaining disagreements. For each disagreement, state the precise question, Mara’s position in language she would accept, Leon’s position in language he would accept, and whether the disagreement is empirical, moral, conceptual, or about acceptable risk. Identify any apparent disagreements that are actually differences in the meanings of competence, equality, expertise, or legitimacy.

C. At least six cruxes: sub-questions such that resolving them would materially change at least one participant’s view of the proposal. For each crux, specify a study, historical comparison, pilot outcome, or thought experiment that both would accept as relevant. Record in advance what result Mara predicts, what result Leon predicts, and how each should update if the other’s prediction proves correct.

D. A jointly acceptable five-year pilot design. Include its scope, eligibility rules, safeguards against item-bank capture, demographic auditing, measures of decision quality and perceived legitimacy, predefined success conditions, predefined failure conditions, and provisions for repeal. Do not simply average the two positions; explain why each safeguard addresses a risk that at least one participant considers serious.

E. A final section listing at least four statements that neither participant would sign, with a brief explanation of the distortion or hidden premise in each.

The tone should be that of a jointly authored public document: exact, restrained, and intellectually charitable. Mara should not be portrayed as favoring technocratic rule in general, and Leon should not be portrayed as indifferent to voter knowledge. Do not adjudicate the debate or end by declaring a winner. No bibliography or citations are needed.
