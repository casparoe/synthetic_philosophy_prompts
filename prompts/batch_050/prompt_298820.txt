My roommate took a logic class last semester and cornered me with this argument last week, and I keep losing the argument even though the conclusion is obviously false. The conclusion is basically that there are no hard math problems, only long ones. Here it is, numbered:

(1) If you rationally believe some premises, you ought to believe anything that's one obvious inference step away from them. That's just what it is to be rational about your own commitments.

(2) Each step of a formal proof is one obvious inference step. That's the entire point of writing proofs in small steps -- a proof is just a chain where every link is something a competent reasoner can check at a glance. Nobody doubts this about real proofs; it's why we trust peer review of them.

(3) If you ought to believe A, and A is such that you ought to believe B if you believe A, then you ought to believe B. Norms chain through.

(4) So if you rationally believe the axioms of Peano arithmetic (which you should, they're as secure as anything), you ought to believe every theorem of PA.

(5) Obviously no finite head contains all infinitely many theorems, so read (4) the sensible way: anyone who accepts the axioms is already committed to every theorem, and the theorems are all epistemically on a par for them. The only thing seperating you from any theorem is bookkeeping.

(6) So there is no epistemic difference between 2+2=4 and Fermat's Last Theorem for anyone who accepts the axioms. "Discovering a proof" is really just retrieving something you already owned. Hardness is a psychological fact about us, like how long division is hard in your head -- it says nothing about the epistemic status of the result.

(7) But that's insane. Wiles did something. He didn't spend seven years tidying his mental filing cabinet.

My gut says (3) is the culprit, it smells like a sorites -- each step looks free but the chain isn't. The problem is that if I deny that oughts chain through, I seem to lose the right to ever say "you accepted the premises, so you have to accept the conclusion" in a multi-step argument, which is like 90% of all arguing. And (1) feels like the bare minimum of what rationality even means, I'd really like to keep it. So which step do I actually reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? If the trouble is in how (2) glosses "obvious," then tell me what "obvious" has to mean for each step of a real proof to be checkable, and whether that same reading still works once it's chained into (4).
