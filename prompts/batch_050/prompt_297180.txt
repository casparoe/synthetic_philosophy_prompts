Three months ago I was diagnosed with a degenerative condition that will progressively limit what my body can do, and nothing I or anyone else does will change that trajectory. A friend pressed Epictetus on me, and I have since been working through the Handbook and the modern Stoic revival literature with the mixture of need and suspicion you would expect. What I want now is not consolation but a stress test of the idea I am being asked to lean on, and I want it run on the hardest case the tradition offers.

So: analyze James Stockdale's seven and a half years as a prisoner of war in North Vietnam — shot down in 1965, held in the prison the inmates called the Hanoi Hilton until 1973, tortured repeatedly, and serving as senior officer among the American prisoners — through the dichotomy of control. I know the broad facts of the story; the analysis is the deliverable.

Structure it roughly as follows. First, the mapping, and be precise about it: enumerate what was and was not up to him in the terms of the dichotomy, and resist the lazy version that just says his judgments were up to him. Tell me which specific judgments and responses the famous episodes of his captivity actually required — the self-inflicted disfigurement to render himself unusable in propaganda footage, the covert communication network he built among the prisoners, the code of resistance he promulgated to men he could not enforce it upon. Second, the strains, examined rather than smoothed over. I see at least two. (1) Stockdale devoted enormous effort to controlling outcomes manifestly not up to him: the conduct of other prisoners, the camp's policies, the terms of his own exploitation. Is a Stoic permitted to fight that hard for externals, and does the doctrine of preferred indifferents do the needed work here or merely patch the hole? (2) The version of the story that circulates in business books — Jim Collins's Stockdale paradox, the pairing of unflinching confrontation with brutal facts and unwavering faith in eventual release — seems to make an emotional commitment to a future external load-bearing. Is that compatible with the dichotomy, or has Collins preserved Stockdale's psychology at the price of his Stoicism? Third, run the analysis in the other direction: what does this episode, honestly treated, show about the dichotomy itself? Is it vindicated as a discipline for the worst cases, or is it exposed as something only a rare character in a structured role — rank, duty, an audience of subordinates whose survival depended on him — could actually live? And if the latter, what follows for someone in my position, with no rank, no role, and no one watching? Finally, use the case to assess the modern revival: does its focus-on-what-you-can-control mantra descend faithfully from what Epictetus meant by "up to us," or has something essential — the normative weight of virtue, the stakes of treating externals as genuinely indifferent — been quietly dropped to make the doctrine livable for the healthy?
