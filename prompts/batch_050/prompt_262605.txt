Ok so I'm in this American pragmatism seminar and we just did the Peirce vs. James unit on truth, and instead of a normal final paper our prof said we could do a "mock adversarial collaboration" -- like, write the joint statement that a committed Peircean and a committed Jamesian could both actually sign after arguing it out in good faith. I want to draft this properly, so help me produce the document.

Here's my rough understanding of the two sides (fix me if I'm strawmanning either): Peirce takes truth to be whatever belief inquiry would settle on if pushed to its ideal limit, so what's true is fixed independantly of what's comforting or useful to anyone right now. James ties truth to whether a belief works -- whether acting on it leads you into satisfactory experience -- so truth can in some sense get made, or at least only exists in the actual run of experience rather than at some ideal endpoint.

What the statement needs to contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims both parties sign. Stuff like: both reject truth as a static mirroring between words and a ready-made world, both think truth has to be understood through the consequences of holding beliefs, both think inquiry matters. But push for the strongest agreements you can find, not just platitudes.
2. A disagreements table. Each row: the precise point of disagreement, with the Peircean position phrased in words a Peircean would accept and the Jamesian position in words a Jamesian would accept. No slanted wording in either direction.
3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions where settling one would force at least one side to update. For each crux, give a concrete case both sides accept as bearing on it, and write down each side's verdict on the case in advance.

Please include these test cases (add more if you think of good ones):

- The self-fulfilling leap: a climber stuck on a mountain ledge whose only escape is a jump they'll only manage if they believe they'll manage it. Is "I'll make this jump" true at the moment they commit to believing it? I suspect James has something interesting to say here and Peirce has something awkward to say, but work it out.
- The bank run: everyone comes to believe a bank is insolvent, and purely because they act on that belief, it becomes insolvent. Was the belief true before the run started?
- The untraceable past: some event that left no evidence and never will -- like whether a particular leaf fell off a particular tree on a particular afternoon two million years ago. Is there a truth about it even though no inquiry, however ideal, could ever settle it? I think this one cuts against a different side than the first two, which is exactly why I want all three in there.

Two more things. If some part of their dispute turns out to be verbal -- e.g. one of them using "true" for something the other would file under a different concept -- say which part and why, in the statement itself. And somewhere address whether Peirce's move of renaming his own position to distance it from what James and co. were doing reflects a genuine philosophical split or mostly a turf/personality thing.

Tone of the document: precise but not stuffy. The whole grade rides on whether the formulations are things both guys would actually put their names to, so if any row of the table would make a real Jamesian go "that's not what I think," redo it.
