I'm setting the final for my upper-level undergrad seminar on self-locating belief and I need one essay question on the self-sampling assumption vs the self-indication assumption, doable in about 75 minutes. Last year I asked a straight "explain SSA and SIA and apply them to the doomsday argument" question and got thirty identical wikipedia-flavored recaps, so this year I want something they can't answer from memory.

Here's the angle I want. Build the question around a case roughly like this, which you should sharpen and make precise: two cosmological theories that predict the *same total number of observers*, but differ in what fraction of those observers are in exactly your evidential situation -- say, on one theory half of all observers see a red sky like yours, on the other only one in a million do. The point is to force students to figure out whether SSA and SIA even come apart when observer totals are equal, and to expose who actually understands that SIA conditions on your evidence vs who just thinks it means "theory with more observers wins." Then a second part should push them into the presumptuous philosopher: is the presumptuousness result a reductio of SIA or just a bullet to bite, and is SSA's doomsday-style reasoning really less embarrassing -- especially once they notice that SIA's prior boost to observer-rich worlds can cancel a doomsday update.

So: write the question itself (tightening my case so the numbers actually work), a model answer of around 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 built on five must-hit moves. For each move give the marks and a one-line note on the specific way weaker answers fumble it -- e.g. treating the reference class problem as a tiebreaker rather than a threat to SSA itself, or definining SIA without the conditioning on one's own evidence. The scheme should name what separates an A from a B in terms of moves made, not general impresson of sophistication.
