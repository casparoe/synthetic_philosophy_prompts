Last week a close friend and I argued for three hours about whether there is any rational justification for discounting a future cost or benefit simply because it is in the future -- not because it is less certain, not because money will be worth less, not because our tastes will change, but merely because it happens later. I defended the view (which my friend kept calling the Sidgwick position, the idea that a rational person should care equally about all the moments of their own life) that pure time preference is irrational. My friend insisted it is not only psychologically unavoidable but perfectly defensible, and that I was confusing a moral aspiration with a fact about what reasons a person actually has. We ended the evening agreeing on nothing except that neither of us could get the other to feel what we felt.

So I want to settle it differently, or at least stage it better. I co-run a university philosophy reading group -- about 14 participants, mostly second- and third-year undergraduates who have done some ethics but no decision theory -- and I have a single 75-minute session. Design a structured in-class activity in which the participants enact the dispute over pure time preference rather than discuss it. The design I have in mind would have them make a sequence of real choices with small genuine stakes (snacks, priority for picking next term's readings, points exchangeable for something modest at the end of term -- propose whatever works), such that: (a) some choices are made now about tradeoffs between now and later, and some choices made weeks ago by a previous cohort are revealed and executed in the room, so participants experience being on the receiving end of their own earlier scheduling decisions; (b) at least one built-in reversal point forces a subset of the group to discover, mid-session, that they now prefer what they previously declined, so that dynamic inconsistency surfaces before anyone names it; and (c) the design cleanly separates pure time preference from its rational lookalikes -- uncertainty about whether the future good will arrive, diminishing marginal utility of the good itself, and changing tastes -- so that when a participant defends a discount, the group can test which of these is actually doing the work.

Please give me: the full rules and materials, including the payoff structure and how to keep stakes real without money; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes; the exact point at which the reversal is sprung and how to handle participants who see it coming; and the preparation required in prior sessions, if any. Then the debrief: how to map specific moments in the activity onto the dispute -- the prudential argument that mere temporal location is not a reason, the reply that my present self has special authority over my present resources, the reply that I am arguably less psychologically connected to my distant future self than to my near one, and the consistency argument that hyperbolic patterns, unlike exponential ones, generate regret by construction. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (I expect at least one participant to game the stakes and one to refuse the reversal on principle), and be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce -- for instance, whether anything staged inside an hour can really bear on discounting across decades, and what the facilitator must simply say rather than show. My friend will be in the room, and I would like the activity to be fair enough that they cannot reasonably call it rigged.
