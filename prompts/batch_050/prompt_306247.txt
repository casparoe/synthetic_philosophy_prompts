A friend who writes a newsletter on rationality summarized Aumann's agreement theorem for me as follows: "Aumann proved that two Bayesian agents who start from the same prior and update on the same evidence must end up with identical beliefs. So if both agents are honest, and each knows the other is rational, any disagreement between them can only last until they exchange views, at which point their estimates are guaranteed to converge. Aumann's conclusion, then, is that persistent disagreement among rational people is impossible — which is why agreeing to disagree is epistemically illegitimate: if you and an epistemic peer still disagree after talking a question through, at least one of you is failing to be a proper Bayesian." I suspect the spirit of this is roughly right but that some of the details are off, particularly the jump from the theorem to what real-world disagreements show. Which parts correspond to what Aumann actually proved, which are conflations or overstatements of it, and does the normative moral about agreeing to disagree survive once the theorem is stated carefully?
