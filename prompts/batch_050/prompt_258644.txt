so there's this recurring fight at my work (research institute, we review grant proposals) and I keep getting dragged in as the tiebreaker. Last week a reviewer, Dina, gave a proposal an 8/10 and wrote up a long justification. Then our calibration data came out -- she's in the batch where we track reviewers against eventual outcomes, and it turns out reviewers with her profile systematically overrate proposals in exactly this subfield by about a point and a half, mostly because of a novelty bias thing. Dina saw the data, said "noted", and kept her 8. She said she'd actually engaged with the proposal, the calibration study didn't, and you can't correct a judgment you made with your own brain by deferring to statistics about brains like yours -- that would just be outsourcing the evaluation to people who never read the thing.

My two coworkers then went at it over whether Dina did anything wrong. Marta's position was: Dina is being flat-out irrational. The calibration data IS evidence, it bears directly on whether her 8 is trustworthy, and ignoring evidence because it's about you rather than about the proposal is just special pleading. She gave the comparison of a pilot who feels totally fine but whose test shows impairment -- nobody thinks "but I checked my own alertness and it seemed good" is a response. You bracket your judgment when you have reason to think the instrument is off, full stop. And she said it's the same structure as when two equally careful reviewers disagree -- you can't just privilege your own assessment because it's yours.

Jules pushed back. He said there's a huge difference between the pilot case and belief. The pilot can decide not to fly -- that's an action, and you can act on statistics. But Dina can't believe "the proposal is a 6.5" on behalf of the calibration study; beliefs have to be based on her actual assessment of the actual proposal, and hers, done carefully, says 8. He said if higher-order evidence could just override your first-order judgment wholesale, then any time someone showed you data that "people like you" are biased you'd have to abandon your own reasoning, and since the only tool you have to evaluate that very data is the same allegedly-compromised reasoning, the whole thing eats itself. You'd never be entitled to trust anything, including the calibration study.

Marta came back with: that's an overreaction. Nobody said override wholesale -- the point is the evidence should move the number. Keep your reading of the proposal, but deflate toward the base rate. Refusing to move at ALL is what's indefensible, and Dina refusing to budge even a little shows she's just defensive about her track record. She also said the self-defeat worry cuts both ways: if Dina's reasoning is so fragile it can't incorporate known information about its own failure modes, that's an argument for trusting it less, not more.

Jules's last move was to distinguish two things he said Marta was mashing together: Dina should obviously report the calibration issue and the committee should weight her score accordingly -- that's institutional design, fine. But as a matter of what Dina herself ought to think, "my judgment is probably distorted" plus "here's my undistorted-looking judgment" is a genuinely weird position to occupy, and acting like she can just subtract 1.5 from her own sincere assessment is pretending you can steer your beliefs like a thermostat, which you can't. You believe what seems true to you. Marta said that's exactly the problem -- seems-true is corruptible and grown-ups correct for it.

They both think the other one is confusing action and belief, or evidence and self-image, or something. Adjudicate for me: go through the actual moves (the pilot analogy, the bracketing point, the self-defeat argument, the "move the number but keep your reading" compromise, the thermostat point at the end) and say who's right about each specific point and who's more right overall. One thing I really want: be clear about which parts of this are basically settled among people who work on higher-order evidence and epistemic rationality, and which parts are live disputes where smart people genuinely land on both sides -- don't present a contested position like it's the agreed answer. If it turns out Marta and Jules are both half-right but talking past each other on different questions, say that plainly instead of crowning a winner.
