In three weeks I'm giving the keynote at the rededication of Founders' Square in the small industrial town where I grew up, and I'm struggling with what the occasion even is. The square is named for Josiah Hale, the nineteenth-century mill owner who paid for the town's hospital, its library, and its waterworks, and whose private guards broke the 1891 weavers' strike, in which three workers died. After years of argument, the council has adopted an unusual compromise: Hale's statue stays in place, but the old plaque is replaced by a dual inscription that names the three dead strikers alongside his benefactions, and -- this is the genuinely novel part -- the dedication now carries what the town calls a review clause. Every twenty-five years, the town must publicly re-argue whether the dedication stands, and the plaque itself states the values on which it rests, so that future residents can judge the monument by the town's own declared reasons rather than having to guess at them. I am, to make this harder, Hale's great-great-granddaughter, which is why I was asked to speak; the audience will include descendants of the strikers, ordinary residents who are tired of the whole controversy, and a handful of historians and philosophers invited for the first scheduled review hearing.

Please write the speech, roughly ten minutes. I don't want a verdict delivered from on high -- I'm an interested party and everyone in the square will know it. What I want is a speech that does the conceptual work the occasion demands. It should take seriously the question of what a rededication ceremony does: whether keeping a statue in its original place, on its original plinth, with a ceremony around it, is unavoidably an act of honoring rather than mere historical preservation, or whether the dual inscription and the review clause genuinely convert it into something else -- and if so, what that something is. It should grapple honestly with the review clause from both directions: whether building revision into a dedication is a rare piece of civic honesty, since all commemoration rests on present values pretending to permanence, or whether a dedication that schedules its own reconsideration has quietly ceased to be a dedication at all, the way a vow with a scheduled review date is no longer quite a vow. And it should avoid the two failures the audience is braced for: a descendant laundering her ancestor, and a descendant performing her own enlightenment at his expense. The people whose grandparents died in the strike and the people whose grandparents were saved in Hale's hospital will be standing in the same square, and the speech has to be addressable to both without pretending their claims are symmetric.
