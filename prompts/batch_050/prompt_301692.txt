I help run a small effective-altruism reading group, and for our session on invertebrate welfare each of us has been asked to write down our working position so the others can attack it. Here is mine, as honestly as I can state it. First, I think the right way to reason about where our moral concern should go is something like expected moral weight: the probability that a kind of creature is sentient, multiplied by roughly how much it can suffer, multiplied by how many individuals there are. Second, I think insects are substantially less likely to be sentient than, say, chickens or fish — I'd put it at least an order of magnitude lower, on the grounds that their nociception looks largely reflexive and isn't clearly integrated with flexible learning the way it is in vertebrates. Third, I think we are in no position to assign anything like precise probabilities to sentience in any nonhuman animal; the science of consciousness is simply too undeveloped, and anyone who claims a figure like 0.4% or 8% for insects is dressing up a gut feeling. Fourth, I think industrial insect and shrimp farming is a genuinely serious moral problem that deserves real resources now — things like humane slaughter methods for farmed shrimp — not just further research. Fifth, I think cross-species comparisons of how much suffering matters are basically intractable; we have no principled scale on which to weigh a bee's bad experience, if it has any, against a pig's.

Before the group meeting, I'd like an audit of this set. Tell me which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, ordered by how severe the tension is; for each one, explain exactly what the conflict is and what the cheapest revision would be to dissolve it, including what that revision costs me. Also flag any pair that looks like a tension but actually dissolves on inspection, so I don't waste the group's time defending a consistency that was never threatened. Then tell me which single change to the set would buy the most overall coherence. One more request: throughout, please separate what is reasonably settled among people who work on animal sentience from what is genuinely contested or speculative — I don't want a contested empirical claim about insect pain, or a contested philosophical claim about moral aggregation, presented to me as though it were the consensus.
