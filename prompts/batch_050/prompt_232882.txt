A statistics blogger I follow explains the replication crisis like this: 'Psychology's replication crisis is, at bottom, a frequentist crisis. A p-value of 0.04 does not mean there is only a 4% chance the null hypothesis is true, but generations of researchers were trained to treat it that way, so the literature filled up with noise dressed as discovery. Bayesian analysis fixes the problem at its root: instead of asking how surprising the data would be under a null hypothesis nobody believes, it tells you directly how probable each hypothesis is given the data, and a Bayes factor of 10 means the effect is ten times more likely to be real than not. Because Bayesian inference builds in prior plausibility, implausible claims automatically require stronger evidence, and because it conditions on the data actually observed rather than on hypothetical repetitions of the experiment, practices like optional stopping and peeking at the data lose their power to corrupt conclusions. The obvious policy upshot is that journals should require a Bayesian reanalysis before accepting any empirical finding.' Something about this feels too triumphant, but I can't locate the problem.
