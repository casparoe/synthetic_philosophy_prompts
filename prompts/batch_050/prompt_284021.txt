There's a debate in formal epistemology about updating on uncertain evidence that I can't tell whether it's alive or stuck. Strict conditionalization only covers the case where you become certain of something, so Jeffrey proposed his rule: when your experience shifts your credences over a partition (say you see the jellybean by candlelight and go 70% that it's red), you update everything else by averaging over the partition cells weighted by the new credences, keeping the conditional credences given each cell fixed (the "rigidity" assumption). The debate, as I see it: critics say rigidity is just assumed, not argued for, that the rule is non-commutative (order of updates matters, which seems wrong if learning is supposed to be truth-tracking), that it's sensitive to how you carve up the partition, and that cases like van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin show that specifying new credences on a partition underdetermines the rational update -- different update rules that agree on the evidence give different posterior credences about the hypothesis you care about. Defenders (Wagner, for instance) reply that rigidity isn't an extra assumption but part of what it means for your learning experience to be *just* that shift and nothing more -- if rigidity fails, you learned something extra, and then you were never in the situation the rule describes. And this defense has been on the table for like twenty years and the two sides just keep restating it.

My hypothesis, which I'd like pressure-tested: the dispute has stalled because there's no shared, independently-motivated account of what a "learning experience" is, so each side's rule bakes in its own answer -- Jeffrey's defenders individuate experiences by their full rigidity profile (making the rule trivially correct), while critics individuate them by observable credal shifts (making rigidity an unjustified add-on). If that's right, the disagreement is partly verbal, or at least one level down from where it's being fought.

So: is that diagnosis right, or is something else doing the work -- e.g., disagreement about what would even count as justifying an update rule (dutch book arguments? symmetry principles? fitting intuitions about cases?), or a hidden empirical question about what actual perceptual learning is like?
