Last week I had a long argument with a close friend about a pattern in how I make decisions, and I would like a more systematic treatment than either of us managed.

Here is the pattern, in two instances. First: last year I rented an apartment. Before searching, I wrote down my criteria — a price ceiling, a commute under forty minutes, two rooms. My plan was to take the first place that met them. But every viewing changed the list. The first apartment taught me that natural light mattered more to me than floor space; the third taught me I could not live above a bar; by the sixth I had quietly dropped the commute requirement and added a criterion about the general feeling of the stairwell that I could not fully articulate. My rule — stop as soon as the criteria are met — never fired, because the criteria were never stable long enough to be met. I did choose eventually, and well, but I cannot say the place satisfied my requirements, because my requirements at the end were neither the ones at the start nor the ones at any single point in between.

Second instance: I help run hiring at a small firm. We write a job specification, interview against it, and find that each serious candidate revises our idea of what the role is. A strong candidate with an unexpected profile makes the original specification look naive rather than making the candidate look unsuitable. We have never once hired someone who fit the posted description, yet nearly every hire has worked out.

My friend insists this is a known, named phenomenon: some economist or psychologist, he said, described choosing by a threshold of "good enough" and letting that threshold adjust with experience. But what he describes is the threshold rising or falling — standards moving along dimensions already fixed. In my cases, it is the dimensions themselves that move; the search does not calibrate my standards so much as replace the questions I am asking. I don't think his concept covers this, but I may be wrong.

So: does this pattern — criteria being constituted by the very search that was supposed to test candidates against them — have an established name? If so, I want more than the label: how exactly does it differ from the threshold-adjusting account my friend gestured at, from ordinary indecision, and from the documented tendency of people to be paralyzed by too many options? If there is no established name, coin one, give it a precise definition, and mark its edges with cases that fall just outside it.

In that final section, state specifically which argument or consideration would lead you to conclude instead that the pattern is merely a variant of the threshold-adjusting account, and identify where in your own reasoning the carving is most likely to fail.
