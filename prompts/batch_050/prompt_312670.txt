I'm a distributed systems engineer by training, and I keep colliding with the game theorists' notion of common knowledge — first in the Two Generals problem, which is native territory for me, and then in the philosophical literature where it does considerably more work. I've been reading about common knowledge of rationality and the way epistemic game theorists use it to justify solution concepts like backward induction: in the centipede game, say, the claim is that if it's common knowledge that both players are rational, the first player should end the game immediately, however long the game is. I've also come across the muddy children puzzle, where the children deduce their own states in successive rounds of silence, and it struck me that this looks remarkably like a protocol converging over rounds of message exchange. But I think I'm probably pattern-matching too loosely, so I'd like a proper translation.

Here is what I'd like you to do. Restate the epistemic game theorists' position — that common knowledge of rationality underwrites backward induction reasoning in extensive-form games, and that the muddy children puzzle illustrates how iterated public announcements can generate higher-order knowledge — in the vocabulary I actually work in: nodes, message passing, synchronous versus asynchronous networks, reliable versus lossy channels, crash failures, Byzantine failures, rounds of communication, protocol states, and consensus. Be as faithful as you can. Tell me what the analogue of a rational agent is when my nodes are presumed to follow their protocol rather than choose anything; what the analogue of a public announcement is; whether n-level mutual knowledge corresponds to what a system can achieve in n rounds; and whether the coordinated attack impossibility result is the same fact as the game theorists' claim that no finite depth of mutual knowledge is common knowledge, or merely a cousin of it.

Then, and this is the part I care about most, give me a residue report: which components of the original position translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I suspect the normative element — that players ought to act on their rationality, and that rationality is something an agent can fail at, the way a node fails only by crashing or going Byzantine — is where things get lost, but I want that assessed rather than assumed. Where something doesn't survive, tell me whether that shows my vocabulary is missing something important (some notion of agency or justification that engineering simply doesn't need) or whether it shows that part of the philosophers' disagreement was less substantive than it appeared. I'd also like your verdict on whether k rounds of achieved mutual knowledge is, for any practical purpose, as good as genuine common knowledge, given that real systems never attain the latter. Please answer in British English.
