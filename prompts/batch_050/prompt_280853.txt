I am a novelist by profession, and I also sit — as the designated lay member — on the curatorial council of our country's new Museum of the Twentieth Century, which opens in eighteen months. At its next meeting the council must settle the organizing principle of the museum's largest space, the Hall of Mass Violence, which will cover the century's major atrocities: the Holocaust, the Holodomor, the Gulag, the Cambodian killing fields, the Rwandan genocide, the Bengal famine, and several others, among them one atrocity committed on our own soil against a minority whose descendants are numerous, organized, and watchful.

The museum's director proposes that the hall be organized around a composite index of gravity, developed by our historical advisory panel, which scores each event on total deaths, deaths as a share of the affected population, duration, and degree of organizational intent, and arranges the galleries as an explicit descent from highest to lowest score. A bloc of council members — several representing survivor communities — finds the very idea of a ranking obscene and proposes instead a suite of equal-sized, self-contained rooms, each presenting a single event on its own terms, with no comparative apparatus whatsoever. A third faction proposes thematic rooms (famine, camps, mob violence) that cut across events and therefore compare constantly, only implicitly.

Two facts complicate the decision. First, under the index our domestic atrocity ranks third, below two events with no descendant communities here, and the minority's representatives have hinted that they will withdraw their artifact loans and lobby against our public funding if their catastrophe is placed third. Second, the ranking is less stable than the director admits: the historians on the advisory panel dispute the intent weighting, and the famine scores shift dramatically depending on how much foreknowledge counts as intention.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the council before the vote. It should recommend one of the three organizing principles, or a precisely specified hybrid, and state the recommendation plainly enough that it could be moved as a motion. The reasoning should engage seriously with the strongest argument on whichever side I do not choose: if I recommend against ranking, the opinion must answer the charge that refusing to compare is itself a covert ranking — that equal-sized rooms assert equal gravity — and that the public is owed an honest picture of scale; if I recommend ranking, it must answer the charge that commensurating suffering is a morally different act from describing it, and that the index's appearance of objectivity merely launders contestable judgments about intent. Since the council knows me as a novelist, I would also value a frank treatment of a point from my own trade: the rooms will have an order whatever we decide, and every exhibition is a narrative whether it confesses to being one or not — so the real question may be which arrangement is most honest about the choices it cannot avoid making. Keep the opinion to a length busy council members will actually read.
