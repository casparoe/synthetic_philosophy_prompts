Next spring my family is relaunching my late grandfather's fishing boat after a restoration that, in hindsight, replaced nearly everything: over nine years in my uncle's boatyard we swapped out every plank, the keel, the mast, the engine, and the wheel, keeping only the brass nameplate and one drawer of his charts. My cousin, who studied philosophy and has needled us about this the whole time, has been loudly maintaining that we are not restoring his boat at all but building a replica with a pedigree, and he has hinted that the box of original planks in the barn is the real heir. The launch is in six weeks, and I have agreed to give the short christening speech before we break the bottle. The audience is mixed: my cousin and two of his philosophy friends will be listening for whether I concede or fight, while most of the crowd are relatives and harbor people who simply want to hear about my grandfather and what the boat meant to him, and who would find an abstract lecture on persistence through time tone-deaf at a launch. Write the speech as exactly five clearly marked sections, each one or two sentences of framing guidance followed by the actual lines to be delivered, with no introduction or conclusion outside the five sections. The sections should move from memory of my grandfather, through an honest naming of the puzzle of what makes this his boat, to a resolution the boatyard crowd can feel rather than merely follow, ending with the christening line itself. The identity puzzle has to do real work: it should shape what I can sincerely claim about continuity, inheritance, and stewardship, and it should let me answer my cousin with warmth on his own terms rather than pretending the objection away or winning an argument at a family occasion.
