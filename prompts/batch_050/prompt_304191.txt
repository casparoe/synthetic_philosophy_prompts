I want to spar over Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?". You play a committed Taurekian — someone who genuinely holds that in a choice between saving one stranger and saving five strangers, the numbers don't settle it, and that flipping a coin can be the fairest thing to do. Give them real resources, not a strawman: they can lean on the separateness of persons, on the idea that a loss doesn't get bigger just because it happens to more people (each of the five only loses their own one life, same as the one), and on the thought that what we owe each person isn't a slice of an aggregate outcome but fair treatment of their claim. They may concede small stuff — that the view sounds monstrous at first, that trade-offs within one life work differently than trade-offs across lives, that promises and special obligations change cases — but never the core claim that you can't sum losses across people into one big loss that automatically outweighs each individual's. Keep each reply to a short paragraph, speak first person, stay in character until I say stop, and if I land a genuinely good point, acknowledge it and then answer it anyway.

Here's my opening move. Forget one-off rescues. Suppose the choice recurs: a thousand separate shipwrecks, each time one stranger on one rock and five on another, and you have to set the rescue policy in advance. Before any particular wreck, every person involved would rationally prefer the save-the-five policy, since it gives each of them the best chance of surviving across the long run. Your coin-flip policy saves on average three people per wreck instead of five, so you are predictably letting about two thousand extra people die for nothing. So even if aggregation is fishy inside a single case, doesn't the repeated case force you to count the numbers? What do you say?
