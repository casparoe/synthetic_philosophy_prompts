I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but my work increasingly depends on how I answer what I gather is called the "stochastic parrots" debate: whether LLMs genuinely understand language or merely produce statistically apt text. I don't ask this out of pure curiosity. I design systems that delegate real tasks to these models — summarizing contracts, triaging bug reports, drafting customer-facing explanations — and my engineering decisions keep silently presupposing an answer. For instance: when a model fails, should I treat the failure like a misunderstanding (fixable with better context, the way I'd brief a new hire) or like a slot machine misfire (fixable only with guardrails, retries, and verification scaffolding)? Those are very different architectures.

Here is a distinction I've been turning over, which I haven't seen discussed but which frames my question. Call a system *transactionally grounded* if its use of a term has, at some point in the system's history, been causally answerable to the thing the term refers to — even if only through narrow channels, like a model fine-tuned on the outcomes of its own database queries, or trained against the success and failure of code it proposed. A model trained only on human text would be grounded at best vicariously, through the humans who produced the text. It seems to me there are at least three positions one could take: (a) transactional grounding is necessary for understanding, so today's pure text models cannot understand even in principle; (b) vicarious grounding suffices, because a symbol can inherit meaning from a community's usage the way my use of "quark" inherits meaning from physicists I couldn't imitate; (c) the whole grounding question is a distraction, and understanding is a matter of having the right internal functional organization, whatever its causal history.

My request: treat these as the live candidate fault lines in the debate — the necessity of transactional grounding, the sufficiency of vicarious grounding, and the functionalist dismissal of grounding — plus any fault line I'm missing that you consider more important. Which of these is most worth resolving *first*, given that my goal is not to settle consciousness or machine mentality in general but to make sound engineering judgments about when to trust, verify, or constrain model output? I'd like a defended ordering rather than a survey. For each fault line: what practical engineering choices actually hinge on it (and which choices people *think* hinge on it but don't), whether it is tractable at all for someone in my position — someone who can probe models behaviorally and read interpretability research but can't run training experiments — and whether it's upstream of the others in the sense that settling it would collapse or dissolve the rest.

Two further constraints. First, please be explicit about the criteria driving your ordering — decision-relevance, tractability, upstreamness, or whatever you actually weight — and flag where the criteria pull in opposite directions. Second, tell me what would change your mind: which empirical finding (say, a particular result about internal representations, or a pattern of compositional failure) or which argument would most force you to reorder your ranking, and where your reasoning is most likely to be smuggling in an assumption — about what understanding even is — that a philosopher from a different tradition would reject on sight.
