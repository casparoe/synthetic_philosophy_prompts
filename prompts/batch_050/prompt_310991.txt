I coordinate a monthly reading group on political ideas for colleagues in my civil service department — economists, statisticians and policy analysts, none of whom studied philosophy — and it is my turn next month. I have chosen to do a session on Hobbes's argument that life without a common power to keep everyone in awe collapses into a war of every man against every man. I do not want to lecture from the text; I want to lead them to the argument. My worry is that this audience, encountering the state of nature for the first time, will hear it either as a falsified piece of speculative anthropology (people in pre-political societies plainly cooperate) or as a drearily pessimistic claim about human nature (Hobbes thought everyone was selfish), and once either of those framings sets in, the actual argument — that conflict arises even among reasonable, moderately decent people once there is no trusted enforcer — will never get a hearing.

Please plan the session's explanatory arc for me. Work out the dependency structure first: which ideas have to be in place before the war-of-all conclusion can even look plausible (I suspect rough equality of vulnerability, uncertainty about others' intentions, and the rationality of anticipating a threat each need separate staging, but tell me the right order and why), and whether the conclusion should be reached before or after the remedy of covenanting to erect a sovereign is introduced. Second, help me choose the one carrying example the session will be built around. My instinct is to use an arms race between two rival states, since my colleagues know the genre, but I want you to tell me what the example must have (no trusted third party, no assured repetition of dealings, gains from pre-emption) and what it must lack (anything that smuggles back in the very trust the scenario is meant to do without), and whether a state-of-nature framing between individuals should come before or after the interstate case. Third, give me the misconception map: the objections this particular audience will raise in roughly the order they will raise them. I already anticipate 'this is just a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and we know cooperation evolves' and 'real anarchic orders, like the international system or medieval Iceland, manage tolerably well', and I would like to use their game-theoretic reflexes rather than fight them — so say where the prisoner's-dilemma mapping genuinely illuminates Hobbes and where it distorts him, and at what point in the sequence each predictable objection should be headed off rather than allowed to fester.

Finally, tell me two things a session planner usually forgets: how I can check at the end that the argument has actually landed — something like whether they can explain why Hobbes's reasoning threatens even a population of well-intentioned people, and can state what would have to be true for anarchy to remain stable — and what I should deliberately leave out at this level and say that I am leaving out (I am inclined to skip the laws of nature, the distinction between commonwealths by institution and by acquisition, and the question of whether the sovereign is a party to the contract, but check my judgement). The deliverable is a teaching plan, not the lecture itself.
