I'm a PhD candidate in philosophy writing a dissertation on collective responsibility, and I've reached the reflective-equilibrium stage: I have settled intuitions about particular cases of corporate blame but no principle that unifies them. I'm familiar with the broad divide in the literature between views that locate moral agency in a corporation's internal decision structure and views that treat corporate blame as shorthand for blaming individual members, so please don't rehearse that taxonomy. What I need is the principle underneath my verdicts. Here they are.

1. A pharmaceutical company's senior executives review interim trial data suggesting cardiac risks and, in a documented meeting, decide to keep marketing the drug while quietly redesigning the endpoint criteria. Patients are harmed. The company itself is blameworthy, over and above the executives. This is my clearest case.

2. An airline's maintenance division logs recurring fault reports on a component; a separate scheduling division keeps compressing turnaround times; a third office holds a memo noting that the combined pattern exceeds the component's rated tolerance. No single employee ever possessed all three pieces, and each acted within their mandate. A crash results. The airline is blameworthy even though no individual is.

3. A mid-level lab manager at a food company falsifies contamination tests, contrary to explicit written policy, recent training, and a whistleblowing channel that had caught problems before. She acted alone, for a bonus. The company is not blameworthy; she alone is.

4. A solo consultancy, one owner-employee incorporated for tax reasons, gives a reckless recommendation. Calling the firm blameworthy strikes me as empty bookkeeping; there is just the man.

5. A manufacturer in 1965 follows every regulation and industry standard of the day, but its own scientists' internal reports say the product likely causes long-term harm, and the standards were partly shaped by industry lobbying. The company is blameworthy.

6. That same manufacturer today: every employee, executive, policy, and product line has turned over since; nothing of the old decision structure survives except the name and legal personality. The present-day company is not blameworthy for 1965, whatever the courts say.

7. One I can't settle: a trading firm's fully automated system, running exactly as designed and approved, discovers and exploits a pattern that destabilizes a small market. The design team tested extensively but did not foresee this. Part of me blames the firm; part of me thinks this is case 2 without anyone even coming close to knowing.

Cases 2 and 3 are the pair I most need a principle to separate, since both involve harms no individual intended, yet I blame in one and not the other. Cases 1 and 6 together put pressure on the idea that the persisting legal entity is what matters, and case 4 pressures any view on which incorporation alone generates an additional blameworthy agent. My own tentative principle — a corporation is blameworthy when the harm issued from features of its decision structure that a reasonable firm in its position would have redesigned — handles 1 and 3, strains at 2 (whose structure failed, exactly?), and goes silent on 6 because it fixes blameworthy features without fixing the bearer of blame over time.

What I want from you: a principle, or two competing principles, stating the conditions under which a corporation itself, and not merely its members, is a fitting target of blame. State it precisely enough that each clause can be tied to the case that motivates it. Then give its verdict on case 7; say which of my verdicts, if any, it cannot accommodate, and which one you'd advise me to abandon if forced; and give its verdict on the hardest natural extension I haven't listed — for instance, a harm produced by the merger of two firms neither of which alone would have caused it. Please keep the entire reply under 700 words; one well-worked principle is worth more to me than a survey.
