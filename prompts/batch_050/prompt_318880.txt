Since retiring from a thirty-five-year career in experimental chemistry, I have devoted much of my time to reading moral philosophy, and I have lately been preoccupied with an axiological position known as tranquilism. On this view, the only thing of intrinsic value is the absence of craving, distress, and dissatisfaction: experiences are good not because they contain pleasure or joy, but insofar as they are free of any felt need for things to be otherwise. The tranquilist thus denies that adding pleasure to an already untroubled mind makes anything better, while insisting that suffering and frustrated desire are straightforwardly bad. This stands in contrast to what I shall call the symmetric view, on which positive experiences have value in their own right and can, at least in principle, outweigh or compensate for suffering.

Please write a dialogue of roughly 900 to 1200 words between two equally able philosophers — one a convinced tranquilist, the other a defender of the symmetric view — who meet at a colloquium dinner and attempt to locate the precise point at which their views diverge. Neither interlocutor should be a caricature: each must respond to the other's actual arguments rather than recite prepared positions, and each should concede points where concession is honestly due.

To ensure the exchange goes beyond well-worn ground, please have the two work through the following three stations in the course of their conversation:

First, the case of the unbothered gardener. Have them consider a person who lives a quiet, entirely contented life tending a garden — no cravings, no sorrows, but also no intense joys, no exhilarating discoveries, no rapturous loves. The tranquilist presumably holds that nothing is missing from this life; the symmetric theorist should press hard on whether this commits the tranquilist to saying that there is no reason at all, not even a weak one, to give the gardener a single afternoon of genuine delight. The tranquilist should be given the resources to answer this without simply repeating the original thesis.

Second, the creation question. Have them consider whether there is any moral reason to bring into existence a new person whose life would be perfectly tranquil — or, alternatively, a person whose life would contain great joys purchased at the price of recurrent intense cravings that are eventually satisfied. Ask them to explore whether the two views actually disagree about cases of this kind, or whether the disagreement evaporates once one distinguishes between making a person happy and making a happy person.

Third, the dreamless sleep objection. The symmetric theorist should argue that tranquilism, taken literally, cannot explain why a world of serene meditators is any better than a world of stones or of permanently dreamless sleepers, since stones and sleepers lack craving too. The tranquilist should respond in a way that clarifies whether tranquilism values the absence of craving as such, or the felt state of undisturbed experience, and the two should examine whether that clarification forces the tranquilist to concede that positive phenomenology matters after all.

By the end of the dialogue, the two need not have reached agreement, but they should have isolated a single, crisply stated proposition on which their disagreement ultimately turns. Please close the dialogue with each character stating, in one or two sentences, what observation or argument would move them to the other side.
