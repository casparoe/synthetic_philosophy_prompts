I sit on a child-welfare panel that advises family courts on when the state may override parental decisions — mostly schooling, medical treatment, and religious upbringing. I want you to take the idea of the child's right to an open future and turn it into a procedure we can actually run on a case: an ordered sequence of tests ending in intervene / don't intervene / intervene in a limited way. The hard bit is that every upbringing forecloses some futures (nobody is raised speaking twelve languages), so the procedure can't just ask "does this close options?" — it needs a principled way of sorting acceptable foreclosures from unacceptable ones. Here's a distinction you could build in, though improve on it if you can: some foreclosures are reversible (you can learn Welsh at thirty) and some aren't, or aren't without real cost (you can't un-live a childhood cut off from the wider society). Work it through at least these cases: parents refusing all standard schooling in favour of purely religious instruction; parents refusing a blood transfusion for their child; and raising a child inside an isolated community whose language and skills don't transfer outside it. Mark the steps where the procedure is really just judgement dressed up as checkboxes, and say whether applying it consistently would amount to a stealth form of parental licensing. Also, be honest about what's genuinely contested versus settled — whether the open-future principle rules out immersive religious upbringing at all is a live dispute, not a finished result, so don't present one side of that fight as the philosophical consensus.
