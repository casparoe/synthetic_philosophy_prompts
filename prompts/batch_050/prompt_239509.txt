I'm a high school student writing an essay for my ethics class, and I've gotten stuck on what seemed like a simple question: how do you tell the difference between a mere personal preference and an actual bias? I have strong gut reactions to particular cases, but when I try to state the principle behind them, it keeps collapsing. Could you help me find it, or tell me which of my reactions I have to give up?

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. I like tea and dislike coffee. This is obviously just a preference; no one owes anyone an explanation for it.

2. I tend to strike up friendships with people who share my taste in music. This also seems fine. It shapes my social circle, but nobody is wronged by it.

3. On the committee picking our school's debate team, I notice I warm to candidates whose speaking style resembles mine -- measured, a bit dry. When I catch myself doing this, it feels like a bias I should fight. But structurally, isn't this the same as case 2? In both cases I'm drawn to people who are like me in a trivial respect. Yet 2 feels innocent and 3 doesn't.

4. If I could only save one person from danger, I'd save my little brother over a stranger, and I think that's not just excusable but right -- morality should leave room for this.

5. At the same time, I catch myself instinctively trusting someone who grew up in my hometown more than a newcomer, and that feels like a bias. But my brother is also just "one of mine." I can't articulate why loyalty to him is legitimate partiality while the hometown thing is prejudice.

6. A friend of mine is a vegetarian and says the smell of meat physically disgusts her, and for her that disgust counts as evidence that eating meat is wrong. I suspect her disgust is doing no real moral work -- it feels like a preference wearing the costume of a principle -- but I can't say why her disgust is less trustworthy than, say, my disgust at cruelty to animals, which I'd want to stand by.

My tentative principle is this: a reaction is a bias rather than a legitimate preference if you would want to get rid of it upon learning where it came from. My hometown trust fails the test because, knowing it was installed by mere familiarity, I'd wish it gone; my love of tea survives because learning that I acquired it from my grandmother changes nothing.

The problem: my love for my brother also has an origin I didn't choose -- I didn't pick him, and I wouldn't wish that love away even knowing its history is pure accident. So my principle seems to classify the very partiality I want to defend as a bias.

Is there a repaired principle that handles all six cases, or at least most of them? Please state it precisely, show me which case motivates each part of it, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save. I'd also like to know what it predicts for two cases I haven't made up my mind about: a teacher who unconsciously calls more often on students who remind her of her younger self, and someone whose commitment to fairness was itself drilled into him by his parents and who would not want to lose it even so.
