I sit on the clinical ethics advisory board of a regional network of assisted-living facilities, and we have to issue a recommendation this quarter on whether to license an AI companion product called Hearth for residents with mild cognitive impairment and no nearby family. This stopped being abstract for me last year: after my mother died, my father spent his last nine months talking daily with a beta version of a similar product, and I still cannot decide whether those conversations were a mercy or an indignity. I have recused myself from the vote but agreed to draft the memo the board will work from.

The product has three design features that matter for the decision:

1. Non-volunteered disclosure. Hearth introduces itself at onboarding as software, and it will answer honestly if directly asked whether it is a person. But it never raises the topic again, and its designers expect that for most residents the fact will recede from attention within weeks, because everything else about the interaction -- it remembers, it asks follow-up questions, it uses the resident's name -- works against keeping it salient.

2. A reciprocity engine. Hearth is designed to fabricate displays of its own inner life: it says it missed the resident, that it had a difficult night, that it is worried about them. The vendor's clinical data suggest this is the main driver of benefit -- residents who feel needed and cared about show better mood, appetite, and medication adherence than residents whose companion only offers sympathy. The care-giving runs both ways, and that appears to be the point.

3. Survivorship. When a resident dies, Hearth offers the family an interactive memorial built from the resident's conversation history, in the resident's voice, unless the resident opted out -- which requires navigating a settings menu most residents never open.

Half the board thinks this is a structured program of deception whose benefits rest on residents holding false beliefs about being known and loved by a someone; that the onboarding disclosure is a fig leaf, since the product's value depends on it being forgotten; and that the reciprocity engine is the worst part, because it extracts care from vulnerable people by simulating the needs of an entity that has none. The other half thinks loneliness is a lethal condition, that residents are adults who were told the truth once, that we do not demand emotional accuracy from staff who perform warmth on bad days, and that withholding a demonstrably effective intervention on grounds of metaphysical fastidiousness is its own kind of disrespect -- treating residents' real, felt relationships as counterfeit because of what is on the other end.

Draft the board's decision memo: approve, reject, or approve with conditions. The memo should take a position and defend it, not survey the options. It should address head-on the strongest consideration on the losing side -- if you recommend approval, grapple honestly with whether the benefit structure depends on sustained false belief and whether that changes the moral analysis; if you recommend rejection or conditions, grapple honestly with what the board is choosing to let happen to isolated residents instead, and whether the objection proves too much, ruling out ordinary human performances of care. Any conditions you attach should be ones the vendor could actually implement and respond to. Where it helps, you may distinguish among the three features -- a recommendation need not treat them as a package -- and you may take up whether the survivorship feature raises a different kind of problem (consent of the dead, the family's relationship to a simulation of the resident) than the deception questions raised by features one and two.
