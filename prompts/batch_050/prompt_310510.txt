I run a monthly philosophy evening at our local library — none of us has any formal training, we just like working through arguments carefully. For the next session I've chosen the white horse dialogue from ancient China, attributed to Gongsun Long of the School of Names: the text that defends the thesis usually rendered as "a white horse is not a horse." I'll have about thirty minutes to present it before open discussion. I've pieced the argument together myself from two translations and some essays, but my audience will be adults with no background in Chinese thought and no logic beyond everyday reasoning, so I want a plan for the presentation rather than the presentation itself. Specifically:

First, the order of the pieces and the reason for that order. Something has to come first — my instinct is that the distinction between "is identical to" and "belongs to the class of" has to be in place before the dialogue makes any sense, but I may have the dependency backwards, and I want to know what else the audience needs before each move lands.

Second, one carrying example built out of ordinary English material. The original turns on features of classical Chinese phrasing that don't survive translation, so I need a homegrown case — something like an everyday classification dispute — that makes the same point. Tell me what such an example must contain and, just as important, what it must not contain, since a badly chosen one will make the thesis look trivially true or trivially false.

Third, the misconceptions I should expect and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. I anticipate at least these: that the whole thing is a trick of words and nothing more; that Gongsun Long must be doing something like Plato's forms; and that since a white horse plainly is a horse, the text can be dismissed in one line. If there are others you expect this audience to form, add them.

Fourth, a single check for understanding — a question I could pose to the room whose answer would show the idea has landed rather than been memorized.

Fifth, what I should openly refuse to cover at this level: Mohist logic, the text's later use by Han Feizi, comparisons with Aristotle — whatever you'd list — and say why depth there would cost more than it buys in thirty minutes.

Finally, scholars disagree about whether Gongsun Long is equivocating or making a genuine point about how compound terms refer. State which reading your plan commits to, identify the step in the sequence where that commitment becomes load-bearing, and tell me what in the text, if it turned out to support the rival reading, would force you to reorder the whole plan.
