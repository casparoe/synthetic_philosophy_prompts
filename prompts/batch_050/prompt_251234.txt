For the past two semesters I've been running a decision theory discussion group, and we keep circling back to Pascal's-mugging-style cases without anyone changing position. The setup we use: a stranger offers you a deal in which, in exchange for five dollars, they will produce some astronomically good outcome, with a probability so small that the expected value calculation still favors paying, at least if you take the stated numbers at face value. The group has settled into three stable camps. The expected-value maximalists bite the bullet and say you should pay, arguing that any refusal principle strong enough to reject the deal either smuggles in an arbitrary probability threshold or amounts to ignoring high-stakes possibilities in a way that cannot be defended without special pleading. The bounded-utility camp says the payoff cannot actually grow without limit once you specify it concretely, so the case never gets going. And the third camp -- which I find myself in -- says the problem is upstream of the arithmetic: nothing about the stranger's testimony gives you a reason to assign the probability the deal requires, because the probability you should assign depends on the process by which such offers get generated, not on the stipulated numbers. Our maximalists reply that this last move is just refusing to engage with the thought experiment as posed, and that if you grant the premises the conclusion follows.

My working hypothesis, offered tentatively, is that the debate is stalled because it is not really about decision rules at all but about what one owes a thought experiment. The maximalists treat the stipulated probability and payoff as fixed data and ask what rationality demands given them; the third camp treats the case as a question about how rational agents should assign credences to claims whose content scales in ways engineered to swamp any discount rate, and suspects that any such claim defeats itself. On my hypothesis, both sides are answering different questions under one name, which is why every round ends with the maximalists accusing the others of changing the subject and the others accusing the maximalists of confusing a formal exercise with a real deliberative situation. The bounded-utility camp, meanwhile, seems to be making an empirical-ish claim about what goods are actually available, which feels like it should be resolvable but somehow never resolves anything.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of this disagreement, not a verdict on who is right. Is the impasse substantive -- do the camps differ about real facts or genuine normative commitments -- or is it partly verbal, with "rational," "probability," or "the case as posed" doing equivocal work? If you think my hypothesis is wrong or only half right, say where and why. And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a sharper distinction between kinds of credence, a formal result about decision rules under tiny probabilities, a convention about how thought experiments should be read, or an argument that the case is simply ill-posed and the debate should be dissolved rather than settled?
