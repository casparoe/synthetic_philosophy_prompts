So here's my situation. I'm a retired organic chemist and since hanging up my lab coat I've been auditing philosophy courses at the university in town, and this term it's a seminar on practical rationality. The instructor, who I've become friendly with over coffee after class, let it slip that she's swamped and dreading writing the final, and in a moment of overconfidence I offered to help draft one of the questions. Now I have to actually deliver. The topic I got assigned is Warren Quinn's self-torturer puzzle — the one where a device has a thousand settings, each notch up pays you a chunk of money but raises your pain by an amount too small to notice, and yet the endpoint is agony you'd pay a fortune to escape. The students will all have read Quinn and a couple of follow-ups, so a question they can answer by reciting the setup and saying "aha, intransitive preferences" would be a waste of everyone's time.

What I'd really like the question to probe is something I found genuinely slippery when I worked through it myself: whether the puzzle actually *needs* intransitive preferences to get going, or whether it works even for an agent whose preferences are perfectly transitive but who can't perceptually distinguish adjacent settings. In other words, which premise has to give — the stepwise rationality of each individual trade-up, the transitivity of preference, or the assumption that you can only be required to respond to differences you can actually detect? And relatedly: is this really a paradox about *preferences* at all, or is it a sorites paradox wearing a decision-theory costume, and does the standard money-pump diagnosis actually apply to the self-torturer or does it quietly assume something the puzzle denies? My instinct as a scientist is that there's a confusion of variables here, like blaming the thermometer for a broken furnace, but I can't quite pin it down.

So: please write one essay exam question, answerable in about 75 minutes by a strong upper-level undergrad, that forces students to take a stand on which premise should be abandoned and to defend it against the best alternative. Then give me a model answer of around 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 points that names the specific moves an A answer has to make versus a B answer, including the most common way students botch each point. One more thing, since I've been burned by this in my own reading: please be upfront in the model answer about which parts of this are basically settled among people who work on the puzzle and which parts are genuinely contested — I don't want to hand my friend a marking scheme that treats one contested resolution as the official received view, because she'll catch it and I'll never hear the end of it.
