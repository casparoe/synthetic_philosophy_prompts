I’m a novelist revising a historical novel set partly in Regency England and partly during the Atlantic slave trade, and I need help settling an argument between two friends who read the same chapter. I’m not taking a side here—I mostly want to know whether I’m accidentally making the history too neat.

In the chapter, an aging duellist refuses a challenge after his daughter shows him a pamphlet arguing that courage should mean self-command, not being willing to kill or die over an insult. My friend Marcus says the scene gets moral revolutions backward. Philosophical arguments against dueling had been around forever, he said, but the practice only really collapsed once states monopolized violence, courts became usable substitutes, and young men had career reasons not to ruin their lives in a duel. In other words, dueling ended when the interests changed, not because anyone finally noticed the arguments were good.

Priya said that’s too crude. The same material changes can affect a norm differently depending on what people think the norm means. If honor stops attaching to accepting challenges and starts attaching to restraint, then arguments can cause the change precisely by reshaping what counts as honorable. A court is only a substitute for a duel if people believe that using it doesn’t make you a coward.

Marcus replied that this still makes honor the real engine. People don’t abandon a practice because it’s irrational; they abandon it because defending it no longer brings status. He pointed to a scene where my abolitionist merchant publicly defends slavery for years, then converts after his business partners start boycotting slave-produced goods. Marcus said I should stop pretending the merchant was convinced and make it clear that the boycott changed his incentives.

Priya answered that Marcus was confusing the moment of conversion with its explanation. A boycott can get someone to examine arguments he had been avoiding, and the arguments can still be what convince him. She also said my merchant should be harder to read: maybe he first pretends to believe abolitionism for profit, starts defending it in public, and eventually comes to believe it because he cannot keep seeing himself as a fraud. That would make interests, honor, and arguments all causally involved without reducing any one to another.

Marcus made one more point: if Priya is right, then “moral progress” is a misleading label, because people may become morally better only as a side effect of status games and economic pressure. Priya said progress doesn’t require the people making it to have pure motives. A norm can improve even if the route to its collapse is psychologically messy. Marcus said that may work for judging societies, but not for judging individuals, since the merchant still doesn’t deserve much credit.

Please adjudicate the disagreement step by step: who is right about dueling, about the abolitionist merchant, about the difference between cause and rational justification, and about whether moral progress needs morally admirable motives. Also tell me what both of them are missing. Give your verdict in American English.
