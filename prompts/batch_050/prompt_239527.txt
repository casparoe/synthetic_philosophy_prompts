I am drafting a paper on the limits of knowledge and have been cornered by the following argument, which I will state as carefully as I can.

(1) Knowability: every truth is knowable. For every proposition p, if p is true, then it is possible that someone knows p.
(2) Ignorance: at least one truth is never in fact known. Let q be such a truth: q is true, and nobody knows q.
(3) The conjunction (q and nobody knows q) is therefore itself a truth.
(4) Applying (1) to that conjunction: it is possible that someone knows (q and nobody knows q).
(5) Distribution: whoever knows a conjunction knows each of its conjuncts.
(6) Factivity: whatever is known is true.
(7) In the possible situation countenanced in (4), someone knows q (by (5) applied to the first conjunct); and someone knows that nobody knows q, so by (6) and (5) applied to the second conjunct, nobody knows q. Contradiction.
(8) Whatever yields a contradiction by logic alone is impossible; so (4) is false. Since (4) followed from (1)–(3), and (3) from (2), premises (1) and (2) cannot both hold: if every truth is knowable, then every truth is known.

I am unwilling to conclude that every truth is known, and unwilling to give up (2). I would also strongly prefer to keep (5) and (6), which strike me as close to definitional. A colleague tells me I should not be troubled at all, because the argument proves too much: steps (4)–(8) draw on nothing about knowledge beyond factivity and distribution over conjunction, so the schema applies to any operator with those two features. Consider the Book of Truth, a ledger in which only truths are ever inscribed. The same reasoning shows that if every truth could in principle be inscribed in the Book, then every truth is inscribed in it. That conclusion, my colleague says, is a triviality about ledgers rather than a paradox; the original argument is therefore a logical curiosity that teaches us nothing about knowledge specifically, and the anti-realist who preached (1) was simply confused about modal logic.

Please do the following. First, assess my colleague's deflationary reading: does the fact that the schema generalizes to every factive, conjunction-distributing operator discredit the argument, or does it instead state a correct general theorem — and if the latter, does that make the conclusion about knowability more disturbing or less? Second, say whether the derivation contains a defective step; if it is valid as it stands, identify the premise you would surrender, explain why that premise appeared innocent, and state the cost of surrendering it. Third, price these exits in particular: restricting (1) so that it does not apply to propositions that themselves embed knowledge claims, and whether that restriction is principled or merely gerrymandered to dodge the proof; denying distribution, and what an epistemology without it would look like; conducting the reasoning in intuitionistic logic, so that the argument establishes only that there is no unknown truth rather than that every truth is known, and whether that resting place is stable; and accepting the conclusion outright. Finally, if you recommend the restriction on (1), say whether the paradox can be reconstructed without quantifying over propositions that mention knowledge, so that the restriction fails to block it.
