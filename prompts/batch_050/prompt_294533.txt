For the final exercise in my upper-level undergraduate seminar on metaethics, I would like a satire that makes students take evolutionary debunking arguments seriously while exposing a common abuse of them. Begin from this passage in chapter IV of Charles Darwin’s *The Descent of Man*:

"It may be well first to premise that I do not wish to maintain that any strictly social animal, if its intellectual faculties were to become as active and as highly developed as in man, would acquire exactly the same moral sense as ours. In the same manner as various animals have some sense of beauty, though they admire widely different objects, so they might have a sense of right and wrong, though led by it to follow widely different lines of conduct. If, for instance, to take an extreme case, men were reared under precisely the same conditions as hive-bees, there can hardly be a doubt that our unmarried females would, like the worker-bees, think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers, and mothers would strive to kill their fertile daughters; and no one would think of interfering.[6] Nevertheless, the bee, or any other social animal, would gain in our supposed case, as it appears to me, some feeling of right or wrong, or a conscience."

Write a deadpan parody in the form of the founding charter and compliance manual of a fictional university office called the Institute for Genealogical Moral Hygiene. The institute’s trustees, having read Darwin, conclude that every normative conviction with a contingent causal pedigree must be quarantined until the institute has certified that pedigree as epistemically respectable. Treat Darwin as the warrant for the institute’s anxieties, not as an unqualified supporter of its programme. The immediate target is the slide from “our moral sense could have developed otherwise” to “our moral judgments are therefore groundless,” not evolutionary ethics or Darwin himself.

The humour should arise from conceptual precision rather than from making the institute stupid. In particular:

- Dramatize the difference between explaining where a moral belief came from, showing that the belief is false, and showing that the process producing it is unreliable; the institute should repeatedly profit from confusing these tasks.
- Include the strongest serious challenge behind the satire: if our moral beliefs were produced primarily by fitness-sensitive causes, and if those causes need not have been responsive to mind-independent moral truths, then any correspondence between our beliefs and such truths may appear accidental. Let the institute state this worry clearly before bureaucracy turns it into a farce.
- Give the institute a selective audit policy. It suspends judgments about compassion, reciprocity, promise-keeping, or partiality because of their possible adaptive functions, while inventing reasons to exempt logic, mathematics, scientific reasoning, and the institute’s own principle that genealogically contaminated beliefs ought to be distrusted. This should expose the risks of global skepticism and self-defeat without turning the piece into a lecture.
- Preserve Darwin’s final concession that the counterfactual social animal would still possess some sense of right and wrong or conscience. The institute should find this concession administratively troublesome: is a radically alien conscience a rival source of moral evidence, a defective version of ours, or merely evidence that some normative capacity was inevitable?
- Incorporate Darwin’s hive-bee case in a ruling or classification exercise. Do not present the imagined bee morality as automatically false merely because it horrifies us, but do not pretend that its mere possibility settles the question either.
- Include one instance in which causal genealogy may genuinely support moral learning rather than undermine a belief—for example, a judgment that changed because people acquired better evidence, encountered affected perspectives, or escaped coercive social arrangements.
- Satirize a practice and an argumentative slide, not any named philosopher, living or dead.

Use the following sections in this order:

1. **Founding Charter** — approximately 250–300 words. An official statement of the institute’s mission, standards, and vocabulary. It should sound plausible enough that the reader initially understands why intelligent people might establish it.
2. **Compliance Procedures** — approximately 350–400 words. A numbered set of auditing rules, exemptions, evidentiary requirements, and appeal mechanisms. The procedures should become increasingly revealing as their distinctions multiply.
3. **Adjudication of a Hard Case** — approximately 125–175 words. A formal committee ruling on Darwin’s hive-bee scenario, including at least one dissenting procedural note. The ruling should bring out the distinction between the contingency of a conscience’s content and the apparent inevitability of some conscience-like capacity.
4. **Satire Key** — approximately 200–250 words. Step outside the parody and explain how its principal jokes correspond to the Darwin passage and to genuine issues raised by evolutionary debunking arguments. State plainly which sophisticated version of those arguments the parody does not refute.

The total creative piece should be approximately 725–875 words, excluding the Darwin passage and the **Satire Key**. It should be intelligible to a student who has not previously encountered evolutionary debunking arguments, while rewarding a reader who knows the surrounding metaethical debate.
