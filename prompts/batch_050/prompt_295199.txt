I'm currently enrolled in a seminar on the sociology of work, and we just finished the unit on Max Weber's analysis of the Protestant ethic and its role in shaping the spirit of modern capitalism — the idea that ascetic Protestantism, especially in its Calvinist and Puritan strands, turned diligent labor, frugality, and the systematic pursuit of one's calling into a moral duty rather than merely an economic necessity. In our last session, a classmate raised a question I haven't been able to stop thinking about, and I'd like your help developing it before I propose it as my term paper topic.

Weber famously worried that the religious foundations of the work ethic had eroded, leaving behind an iron cage in which people toil without the beliefs that once gave the toil meaning — specialists without spirit, as he put it. My question is: what would Weber likely have made of two developments that postdate his lifetime? First, the French 35-hour workweek and, more broadly, the European push toward shorter working hours and legally protected leisure, often defended on the grounds that a good society should free people from work rather than sanctify it. Second, the Japanese phenomenon of karoshi — death from overwork — which seems to show a work ethic that has become literally lethal, yet emerged in a culture without the Calvinist theological background Weber treated as the ethic's historical engine.

Please reconstruct, as carefully as you can, how Weber would have analyzed each case given his actual framework: would he see the 35-hour week as liberation from the iron cage, as evidence that the spirit of capitalism has finally exhausted itself, or as something else entirely? And would karoshi strike him as a perverse fulfillment of the ascetic ideal, as proof that a work ethic can persist and even intensify without any Protestant roots, or would it force him to revise his genealogical story? I'm less interested in a definitive verdict than in a well-grounded argument about which reading best fits his theory, so please flag where Weber's own texts push in competing directions and where the evidence is genuinely ambiguous.
