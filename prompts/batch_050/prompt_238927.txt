I'm preparing a position statement for a graduate reading group on the ethics of deception, and before I circulate it I want to stress-test whether my commitments are jointly tenable. The group has spent the term on the lying–misleading distinction, and I find that my considered views come from two or three different directions at once, which makes me suspect they don't fit together as cleanly as I'd like. Here is where I've landed. First, I hold that lying is pro tanto morally worse than merely misleading, even when we hold fixed the false belief the hearer ends up with, the speaker's intention to produce it, and all downstream consequences. Second, my explanation for this is that assertion involves an explicit undertaking: the speaker stakes their credibility and invites the hearer to rely on their word, whereas a merely misleading speaker supplies true material from which the hearer draws their own inference, and that inferential step means the hearer shares in the responsibility for the resulting belief. Third, as a general principle about culpability for deception, I think how blameworthy a speaker is tracks how deliberately and foreseeably they bring about the false belief — responsibility for another person's epistemic state is a matter of what one knowingly and controllably produces in them. Fourth, a case verdict: a doctor who flatly tells an anxious patient that the scan is clear, knowing it is not, acts worse than a doctor who carefully selects a true statement designed to lead the patient to that same false conclusion — and I want to say this holds even in a version of the case where the doctor is more certain the true statement will mislead than the lie would be. Fifth, in the classic case of the murderer at the door asking whether the intended victim is inside, I think misleading him is permissible but lying to him is not. Sixth, I don't think the moral line between lying and misleading should shift around depending on the accidental conventions of one's linguistic community — it shouldn't turn, for instance, on whether some evasive phrasing happens to be locally understood as a coded commitment.

Please audit this set rather than lecture me on the literature. Which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be? I'd like the genuine tensions ranked by severity, and for each one, tell me exactly what would have to give — which commitment must be revised or abandoned, and what the cheapest revision costs me elsewhere in the set. In particular, I'm unsure whether my general principle about culpability can actually deliver my verdict in the doctor case, and whether my trust-based explanation of why lying is worse can accommodate my verdict about the murderer at the door, but I don't want you to assume in advance that these are the real problems rather than the apparent ones. If one single revision would resolve the most tensions at once, identify it and state plainly what I'd be losing. Conversely, if some pair of commitments looks inconsistent on the surface but the appearance dissolves once the commitments are stated more carefully, show me the dissolution rather than counting it against me.
