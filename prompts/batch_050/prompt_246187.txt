My reading group has been working through Kripke's Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language, and since a few members found it rough going, one of them circulated a chapter from a popular philosophy-of-language book that purports to give the whole argument in two pages. Here is its summary:

"Kripke, developing Wittgenstein, raised what he called a skeptical paradox about meaning. Every use you have ever made of a word like 'plus' involved only finitely many numbers, and infinitely many deviant functions — Kripke's example is 'quus,' which agrees with addition on every case you have actually encountered but diverges beyond some bound — fit your entire past history equally well. Since nothing in your past behavior, instructions, or inner states singles out addition rather than quaddition, there is simply no fact of the matter about what you meant, and hence no fact of the matter about what anyone ever means by any word; meaning talk is an illusion we would do better to drop. Kripke's own way out is to relocate meaning from the individual to the community: you mean addition because your linguistic community would accept your answers and correct your deviations, and the community's agreement, which needs and admits no further justification, is what makes it true that you mean one thing rather than another. It follows that a speaker in total isolation, such as a Crusoe with no one to check him, cannot coherently be said to mean anything at all by his words."

Several of us felt this was roughly right but somehow too tidy, and I have a nagging memory that Kripke does something with assertability conditions rather than truth conditions, which the summary's last two sentences seem to ignore. Please audit the summary component by component: which parts a careful reader of Kripke would accept, which are defensible compressions, and which misrepresent the argument. In particular, I would like to know whether the summary is right to frame the paradox as a curve-fitting problem about past data, or whether something else (normativity?) is doing work it omits; whether "an illusion we would do better to drop" accurately conveys Kripke's skeptical conclusion and his response to it; and whether the closing claim about the isolated speaker actually follows from the parts of the summary that are accurate. Also flag any point where the popularizer has attributed to Kripke a crude position that Kripke himself considers and rejects.
