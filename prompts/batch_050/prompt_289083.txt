I write literary thrillers, and for my current manuscript I have fallen into the habit of running the knottier scenes past a friend — a decision theorist by training — who rules on whether my characters' choices count as rational. His verdicts have been consistent enough that I believe I discern his pattern, but my latest scene has stumped me, and I would like your prediction before I trouble him again. His prior rulings: (a) A character learns from a flawless medical study that nearly everyone who takes a certain harmless, mildly pleasurable drug carries a gene that will independently bring them grief in later life; the drug itself causes nothing. He ruled her rational to take it — the bad news concerns who she is, not what her choice brings about. (b) A character plays a single prisoner's-dilemma-style game against her exact psychological duplicate, raised in isolation, with coordination impossible. He ruled defecting rational, since her choice does nothing to the other's. (c) A nearly infallible predictor has already arranged a reward on the basis of what it foresaw; my character may open one box or two. He ruled she should open both, the prediction being settled and the second box costing nothing. (d) A character acts against her narrow interest purely to produce an effect that her act itself causes in a watching party; he ruled that rational when the payoff warranted it, since there the choice genuinely does the work.

The new scene: my heroine, in Damascus, meets a figure — call him the Collector — who informs her that he will come for her tomorrow in whichever city she occupies, that he has already made his arrangements for tomorrow based on his prediction of her movements, and that he has never once been wrong about where his quarry will be. She believes his record entirely. She may remain in Damascus or take the night caravan to Aleppo; the journey is wretched but survivable if he is not waiting at its end. She cannot hide, fight, or bargain; the only variable is her location. One of my draft endings has her, in despair of deliberating, simply flip a coin at the city gate — and I note that his fearsome reputation rests on foreseeing what people conclude, since no one has ever tested him against a coin.

Please predict my friend's verdict on the scene: would he pronounce her rational to flee, to stay, to flip the coin, or would he say something stranger — that this is a situation in which no choice can be rational? In answering, identify which of the four earlier rulings is the closest analog, state the rule you take him to be running, name at least one rival rule that fits all four verdicts but delivers a different verdict here, and describe the further scene that would distinguish the two rules. Kindly keep the entire reply under 350 words.
