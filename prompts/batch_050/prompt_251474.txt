I'm putting together the materials for a graduate seminar on the ethics of deception, and our next meeting takes up the standard view that lying is, other things equal, morally worse than merely misleading -- that there is something specially objectionable about asserting a falsehood, as opposed to saying something literally true while intentionally inviting a false inference. My students grasp the asymmetry easily when the cases are stacked in its favor, so I want to challenge them with a case stacked the other way, and my attempts to construct one keep failing in instructive ways: either the misleading turns out worse only because of smuggled-in consequences, or the lie is so trivial that the comparison is rigged from the start. So I'd like you to build the case properly, to order.

Construct a realistic, everyday scenario -- a workplace, a friendship, a family, a landlord-tenant relationship, or similar; no courtroom oaths, no murderers at the door, no spies -- in which two speakers, A and B, deceive the same kind of hearer about the same proposition, with A telling an outright lie and B uttering something literally true that is designed to generate the same false belief by implicature. The constraints: the scenario itself should be a single paragraph; A and B must stand in the same relationship to their hearer, act from the same motive, and produce the same downstream consequences, so that neither special trust nor harm can explain any verdict difference; and yet, on reflection, B's misleading should come out morally worse than A's lie. The case will not count if the verdict is simply stipulated -- the asymmetry has to fall out of recognizable features of lying and misleading, not be asserted by the narrative voice.

After the scenario, do the demonstration. State exactly what A asserts, what B asserts, and what B implicates, and how the implicature is generated. Then take the two or three standard explanations of why lying is supposed to be specially wrong -- that assertion carries a warranty of truth that implicature does not, that lying breaches a norm constitutive of the practice of assertion, that the liar invites a kind of trust the mere misleader technically does not -- and show why each of them, applied to your case, fails to vindicate the usual ranking and in fact cuts the other way or goes neutral. Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work of reversing the verdict, and tell me what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse back into the standard ranking, or into moral equivalence? Finally, give the strongest objection available to a defender of the orthodox view that lying is always pro tanto worse -- the move by which they would try to show that my seminar case has been gerrymandered -- and say honestly whether you think the case survives it.
