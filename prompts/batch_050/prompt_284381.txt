I serve on the grants panel of a small foundation that funds "civic reasoning" education -- basically programs that are supposed to make college students and community members better at thinking through contested public issues. We have about $120k to give out this cycle and one finalist proposal is splitting the panel. It's a semester-long workshop series called Clear Thinkers, run by two philosophy PhDs, and their pitch is that the main obstacle to good public reasoning is conceptual sloppiness: people throw around words like "evidence," "bias," "misinformation," and "expertise" without knowing what they mean. Their curriculum is therefore almost entirely definitional -- students memorize precise definitions of about forty key terms, drill them with flashcards, learn to spot when a speaker is using a term loosely, and the program's success metric is a pre/post test where students rate their own confidence with each term plus a multiple-choice definitional quiz. Half the panel loves how crisp and assessable it all is. The other half thinks it's training people to *feel* sophisticated without making them any better reasoners, and one of them keeps waving around this passage from C. S. Peirce's "How to Make Our Ideas Clear": "merely to have such an acquaintance with the idea as to have become familiar with it, and to have lost all hesitancy in recognizing it in ordinary cases, hardly seems to deserve the name of clearness of apprehension, since after all it only amounts to a subjective feeling of mastery which may be entirely mistaken."

Here's my problem as I prep for Friday's meeting. I'm not sure the passage actually condemns the proposal, or only its way of measuring success. Peirce seems to be describing the lowest grade of clearness -- mere familiarity -- and complaining it's inadequate, but he goes on to allow that abstract definitional distinctness is a genuine further achievement, just still not the whole story, since he ultimately wants concepts cashed out in terms of their conceivable practical effects. So does the Clear Thinkers curriculum even reach the second grade, or is it stuck at the first? And does a fair reading of Peirce support funding the program with redesigned outcome measures (say, tasks where students have to actually *use* the concepts to predict, decide, or criticize real arguments, rather than define them), or does his whole picture of inquiry imply that definition-first pedagogy is backwards and we should ask them to rebuild the curriculum around practice from day one? The defenders on the panel also have a serious point I don't want steamrolled: fluency with shared terminology plausibly is a precondition of productive disagreement, their confidence-rating data might track something real, and the Peirce passage is about grades of *clearness of ideas*, which may just be the wrong lens for judging a teaching program.

Draft the decision memo I'll circulate before the vote: recommend fund, decline, or fund with conditions, with the conditions specified concretely enough that the applicants could actually revise and resubmit next cycle. Interpret the passage carefully in the body of the memo -- say what it does and doesn't license -- and give the strongest version of the pro-funding case before answering it. Around 600-800 words is fine, plain memo format, since half the panel won't read anything longer.
