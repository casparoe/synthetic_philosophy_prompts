I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine and host its podcast. For a special episode we are interviewing Charles Darwin — played by a historian of biology who answers strictly in character, drawing only on what Darwin wrote or could plausibly have known by 1859. The episode is built around a passage from Chapter II of On the Origin of Species (1859): "Practically, when a naturalist can unite two forms together by others having intermediate characters, he treats the one as a variety of the other, ranking the most common, but sometimes the one first described, as the species, and the other as the variety."

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having him restate his theory. I want the sequence to press where this passage is genuinely vulnerable: whether it quietly demotes "species" from a real division of nature to a bookkeeping convenience, and if so what work the concept still does in an argument that is supposed to explain the origin of species; why frequency, or the sheer accident of which form was described first, should settle rank — and whether he would accept the consequence that a variety which came to outnumber its parent would thereby become the species; and how his handling of intermediate gradations would cope with cases he never faced, such as populations that interbreed freely with their neighbors at one end of a long chain but not at the other, or organisms that do not cross at all. Close with a question about whether he would rather abandon the word "species" than keep repairing it. For each question, supply a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give — and make sure one follow-up anticipates the reply that his theory predicts borderline cases, so the messiness of classification is evidence for him rather than an embarrassment.
