I work as a software engineer, and part of my job these days involves integrating a large language model into a customer-facing product. When the model outputs confident falsehoods, my team keeps reaching for vocabulary borrowed from human speech and finding that none of it quite fits. I have no formal philosophy training, but I read a summary of Harry Frankfurt's account of bullshit and it got me thinking. Here are the candidate classifications of LLM falsehoods that I already know and do not need repeated:

1. Lying. On the standard picture, lying requires asserting something the speaker believes to be false with the intent to deceive. Since it is at least contentious whether an LLM has beliefs or intentions at all, calling a false output a lie seems to smuggle in a theory of machine minds rather than describe the output.

2. Bullshit, in Frankfurt's sense: speech produced with indifference to its truth value, where the speaker's goal is something other than conveying accurate information (impressing, persuading, filling the conversational role). This is the most popular classification, and it has some intuitive pull, since the model is optimized for producing plausible, well-received text rather than for truth tracking. But it arguably inherits the same problem as "lying": Frankfurt's bullshitter has a recognizable psychological attitude of indifference, and it is unclear what the analogue of that attitude is in a next-token predictor.

3. Confabulation, by analogy to the neuropsychological phenomenon: the model fluently generates plausible content to fill a gap in what it "knows," without registering the gap as a gap. This seems descriptively better, but one could object that the analogy only works if there is something like a deficit the model is compensating for, whereas for the LLM the generation process is identical whether the output is true or false -- there is no special failure mode.

4. Plain error or malfunction, the way we would describe a buggy function returning wrong results: no speech-act vocabulary at all, just a system producing incorrect outputs under conditions it was not designed or trained to handle. This is the framing my engineering colleagues prefer, but it seems to lose something, because the outputs are not garbled -- they are rhetorically competent falsehoods that invite trust in a way a stack trace does not.

Here is what I would like from you: propose a fifth classification that is genuinely distinct from all four of the above -- not a rewording of one of them, not a compromise position between two of them, and not merely a more precise version of the same idea. Ideally it should be grounded in something about how LLMs actually work (training on text distributions, next-token prediction, RLHF-style optimization for human approval), since classifications that ignore the mechanism tend to float free. After presenting your candidate, do two things: (a) explain precisely where it diverges from whichever of the four it is closest to, so I can check that the novelty is real, and (b) give your honest assessment of whether it actually improves on the four candidates or whether it just rebrands one of them. If you conclude that the existing taxonomy is already exhaustive -- that any fifth option collapses into one of the four on inspection -- then say so and show the collapse, rather than inventing a spurious fifth entry to satisfy the request.
