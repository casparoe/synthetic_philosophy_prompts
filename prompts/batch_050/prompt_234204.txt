I'm in a seminar on value alignment and we've spent like three weeks on idealized-preference theories (full-information accounts, coherent extrapolated volition, that whole family) and every reading just gestures at the idea of what someone would want if they were smarter, better informed, more the person they wish they were, and then moves on. Before we start evaluating specific proposals against each other I want the actual spec. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of idealized preference has to deliver, given that the job is to tell an AI system what to do on someone's behalf, not just to analyse the concept for its own sake. For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, and mark whether it's non-negotiable or just a preference of one camp.

Some cases I think have to be on the list, though you should push back if any of them is actually a bad test:

1. The adaptive preference case: someone shaped by deprivation or oppression to want very little. The theory needs to be able to override this without collapsing into paternalism where the theorist's own values just get smuggled in.
2. Preferences constituted by ignorance: wanting to solve the proof yourself, wanting the surprise to be a surprise. Fully-informed-you already knows the answer, so does idealization destroy these preferences, satisfy them trivially, or what?
3. A case I keep coming back to and haven't seen discussed: someone whose deepest commitment is that no machine should compute what they would want. They'd rather live with their flawed actual choices than be governed by their extrapolated self. Does a decent theory respect this preference or quietly launder it away, and can it do either consistently?
4. The divergence problem: two extrapolation procedures that both seem reasonable (different orderings of what the person learns first, different amounts of reflection time) converge on different answers. Does the theory owe us a fact of the matter about which is right, or an account of why the difference doesn't matter?

The part I most want done carefully: which pairs of desiderata can't be jointly satisfied. My suspicion is that taking actual current preferences as the sole source of authority conflicts with overriding preferences produced by manipulation, and that wanting a procedure-independent answer conflicts with pretty much any concrete extrapolation procedure, beacuse the details of how you idealize end up doing the normative work. If the list itself turns out to be unsatisfiable that's a result I'd want to know about.

Then briefly score the main contenders against the list: ideal-advisor/full-information views, CEV-style extrapolation, and plain preference-satisfaction with a laundering filter bolted on. Keep the scoring short -- the criteria are what I actually need for the paper.
