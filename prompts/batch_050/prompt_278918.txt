I'm taking a bioethics seminar this semester and for my final paper I have to take a position on where the line between therapy and enhancement should be drawn (in medical contexts, not sports or whatever). Before I commit to any account I want to figure out what an account of the distinction actually has to DO to be good enough.

So: can you write me the desiderata a criterion for the therapy/enhancement distinction must meet — which cases it has to classify correctly, which pressures it has to withstand, which distinctions it must respect — with the motivating case behind each? Some situations my prof kept returning to that I want the list to cover:

- Interventions that overshoot: e.g. a cochlear implant that restores hearing but also lets the person perceive frequencies outside the normal human range. It's one procedure that seems partly therapy, partly enhancement. Does a good criterion have to split it in two, or is it allowed to call the whole thing one or the other?
- The shifting baseline problem: suppose some cheap safe supplement raises the average memory of everyone who takes it by 15%, and most of the population takes it. Now the people who didn't are "below normal" and insurers start calling them impaired. Is giving them the supplement therapy or enhancement? Any criterion tied to statistical normality seems to give wierd verdicts here, so maybe that's a desideratum: the verdicts shouldn't flip just because everyone else got treated.
- Prevention beyond the species-typical: a gene edit that makes someone more malaria-resistant than any unmodified human. Preventing disease feels like core medicine, but the result is a person who's better than normal. Is disease prevention its own category or does the criterion have to absorb it?
- Positional goods: height enhancement where the whole point evaporates if everyone gets it. Should a therapy/enhancement criterion even care about distributional effects, or is that a separate question?

Also tell me which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just the hobbyhorse of one camp, and flag any subset that can't be jointly satisfied. I have a hunch that "the line must track normal human functioning" and "the line must be stable over time" collide once medicine itself moves what counts as normal — if that's right it seems like the actual finding of the paper.

Scope: the criterion only needs to be good enough to guide decisions about what a public healthcare system should fund, not to settle the metaphysics of disease. You can briefly score the usual contenders (normal-functioning views, welfarist views) against the list at the end, but keep that short — the criteria are what I actually care about.
