I work as a machine learning engineer, mostly on reinforcement learning systems, and I have no background in philosophy. While reading about the death penalty on my own, I came across this passage from Cesare Beccaria's An Essay on Crimes and Punishments (Chapter XXVIII, "Of the Punishment of Death"):

"It is not the intenseness of the pain that has the greatest effect on the mind, but its continuance; for our sensibility is more easily and more powerfully affected by weak but repeated impressions, than by a violent but momentary impulse. The power of habit is universal over every sensible being. As it is by that we learn to speak, to walk, and to satisfy our necessities, so the ideas of morality are stamped on our minds by repeated impressions. The death of a criminal is a terrible but momentary spectacle, and therefore a less efficacious method of deterring others, than the continued example of a man deprived of his liberty, condemned, as a beast of burthen, to repair, by his labour, the injury he has done to society. If I commit such a crime, says the spectator to himself, I shall be reduced to that miserable condition for the rest of my life. A much more powerful preventive than the fear of death, which men always behold in distant obscurity."

My instinct is that Beccaria is making a point I already know in other terms: a sparse, high-magnitude punishment signal is worse at shaping a population's behavior than a dense, durable one. Please restate his argument as precisely as you can in the vocabulary of reinforcement learning — punishment as a training signal, deterrence as shaping the policies of agents who learn from observing others, salience, discounting, credit assignment — and tell me what in his psychology that framing gets right. Then give me the residue report: which of his claims translate cleanly, which you had to distort (the spectator's compassion and indignation, for instance), and which parts of the wider death penalty debate simply have no counterpart in this vocabulary — I am thinking especially of retribution, of the idea that an innocent person's execution is a wrong to him rather than merely a corrupted training example, and of his remark elsewhere that no one can hand the state a right over his own life. Finally, I want your judgment on the residue: does it show that the reinforcement learning lens leaves out something real, or that part of what Beccaria and his opponents say is less clear than it looks?
