In the clinical reasoning course I teach to third-year medical students — twenty of them, one ninety-minute session, printed materials only — I want to run an activity that puts the mechanics of an informational cascade under their skin before they ever hear the term. Handoffs are the natural vehicle: in real ones, each clinician reads the previous clinician's impression and then examines the patient, and I want my students to discover, as participants rather than as an audience, how a tentative early impression can travel down a chain, override later private evidence, and leave a group with a consensus containing less information than its members collectively held — even though everyone in the chain reasoned correctly.

Design the activity in full. Specifically:

The simulation. Each participant receives a piece of imperfect private evidence (a test result, an examination finding) about an underlying diagnosis, plus the one-line impression written by the person before them, and must commit to a one-line impression of their own to pass on. Specify how the evidence is generated and calibrated — give the actual numbers — so that a single private signal is genuinely outweighed by two concordant prior impressions, meaning that from some point in the chain onward, discounting one's own finding is the rational move rather than a failure of nerve. I want a run in which the majority of private signals point one way while the chain converges the other; tell me how to arrange the materials so this is guaranteed without deceiving anyone about the rules, since chance alone won't guarantee it for me.

Incentives. Points, not money, are at stake in this course. The payoff scheme must make accuracy the only thing that pays; otherwise students will either grandstand by contradicting the chain or conform to seem agreeable, and the exercise collapses into a claim about personality rather than about information. Say how the instructions and scoring foreclose both.

The second phase. Once the cascade has run, break it in front of them: introduce one item of public information — for instance, that one participant's private test is known to be far more reliable than the rest — and let them watch the consensus dissolve. Design this so the fragility is experienced rather than asserted.

The debrief. Put the chain of written impressions next to the full set of private results so the information loss is visible on paper, then map what happened onto the theory: the difference between a cascade (private information stops being used) and mere unanimity; why each step was rational though the endpoint was wrong; where the lost information actually went. Force them to distinguish what they just did from deference to seniority, from anchoring on a first diagnosis, and from peer pressure. And end with them proposing structural fixes for real handoffs rather than resolving to think more independently — a resolution the exercise should have shown to be beside the point.

Close with what will predictably go wrong the first time — I especially expect students to forward their raw results out of helpfulness, destroying the private/public structure the design depends on — and a candid account of which features of real cascades the simulation cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in discussion.
