My wife and I are trying to decide whether to have a second child, and the question has become stranger the longer I sit with it. Setting aside what we want -- assume the personal side is a wash -- I keep circling a more abstract claim: that bringing into existence one more person whose life goes well makes the world better than it otherwise would have been, other things equal. Some of my friends treat this as obvious; others insist the comparison is meaningless because, in the no-child world, there is no one who is worse off for never existing.

I would like your actual credence in that claim, stated as precisely as you can make it: given a couple in stable circumstances whose child would, on reasonable projections, have a life well worth living and would impose no extraordinary burdens on others, does the world containing that child outrank the world without her, morally speaking?

More important than the number is the derivation. Please decompose your answer along whatever fault lines you take to be load-bearing, assigning a rough probability to each component and showing how they combine. I would expect the decomposition to engage at least the following, though you should add terms if I have missed any: the probability that comparisons between outcomes differing in who exists are well-formed at all, as opposed to the person-affecting intuition that an outcome can only be better if it is better for someone; conditional on such comparisons being well-formed, the probability that existence confers the relevant kind of value (i.e., that total or critical-level views survive the objections that push people toward averaging or person-affecting alternatives); the probability that the standard objections to total views -- the repugnant conclusion most obviously, but also the asymmetry between our seeming duty not to create miserable lives and our seeming lack of duty to create happy ones -- are decisive enough to defeat the verdict in this ordinary, non-extreme case; and an empirical term for the likelihood that a particular child in our position would in fact have a net-positive life and net effects on others.

Then tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or consideration, if you encountered it, would move that term most. Finally: if the decomposition reveals that the claim I asked about is not quite the claim my decision actually turns on -- if, say, the practically relevant question is narrower or different in kind -- say so, and restate the question I should be asking.
