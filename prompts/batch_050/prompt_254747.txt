I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about the ethics of insecticides and pest control, and I'd rather start from my reactions to concrete cases than from any theory. Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can report them:

1. Crushing the aphids on my tomato plants by hand: fine.
2. Setting out ant bait that workers carry back to the nest, poisoning the queen and collapsing the entire colony: fine.
3. Glue traps for mice, which hold the animal until it dies of stress or dehydration over hours or days: not fine; a snap trap that kills the same mouse instantly: fine.
4. Releasing factory-reared sterile male mosquitoes, so that females mate fruitlessly and the local population crashes within a few generations: fine.
5. Releasing mosquitoes carrying a gene drive that spreads a sterility trait through an entire species until it goes extinct worldwide: not fine — though I concede this would likely prevent a great many malaria deaths, which is what makes me uneasy about the verdict.
6. Spraying a field with a broad-spectrum insecticide that kills the bees and butterflies passing through along with the target pest: not fine; spraying a narrow-spectrum compound at doses that spare them: fine.
7. Poisoning a locust swarm that would otherwise consume the crops a village depends on for the year: fine, and not even a close call.
8. Releasing parasitoid wasps whose larvae consume a caterpillar pest from the inside over the course of several days: I go back and forth, but I lean toward not fine — even though this is precisely what organic agriculture does in place of spraying, and the spray may not be any gentler.
9. Tilling a field in spring, which foreseeably kills enormous numbers of soil insects as a side effect: fine, or at least not something any reasonable person should lose sleep over.

My tentative principle is roughly this: killing insects is permissible in proportion to what is at stake for human beings, provided the method minimizes the suffering involved, and provided no one deliberately aims at eliminating an entire species. But I can already see it struggling — case 7 licenses mass killing on the strength of human stakes while case 5 forbids it on exactly the same strength, and case 9 lets through more incidental death than case 6 forbids as bycatch.

Please test my principle against these cases, and repair or replace it. What I would like is a precise principle, or a small set of candidates, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it; an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and has to overturn; and its verdicts on cases I haven't considered — for instance, releasing mosquitoes infected with a bacterium that blocks disease transmission without killing anyone, and a farmer's choice between a crop engineered to repel pests and one engineered to poison any insect that bites it. Finally, say something about whether the principle should treat killing very many individuals and ending a lineage or population as morally different acts, since my verdicts on cases 2, 4, and 5 seem to require that it does, and I'm not sure that requirement is defensible.
