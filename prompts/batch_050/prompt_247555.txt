For the final meeting of my graduate seminar on the evolution of mind, I would like to close with a poem rather than an argument. Write a villanelle — nineteen lines, five tercets and a concluding quatrain, built on two refrains in the usual pattern — that takes up the idea of free-floating rationales: reasons that exist without reasoners. The cuckoo's egg is colored for a reason (it must pass as the host's own), the eye is organized around light for a reason, and yet no mind ever formulated, intended, or comprehended those reasons. Natural selection tracks the why of things without anyone thinking it; the rationale floats free of any reasoner who might hold it.

The two refrains should embody the two halves of the thesis. One should state a rationale plainly, as if someone meant it — the reason itself, given its due. The other should insist that no one means it: that the reason exists unheld and unthought. Across the tercets, let the refrains alternate so that the reader keeps feeling the reason and the absence of a reasoner pass each other without touching. Concrete cases may give the tercets their bodies — the cuckoo's egg, the bee's dance, the moth's wing darkening with the soot of its century — but each case should be chosen so that its reason is genuinely explanatory, not merely as-if. In the closing quatrain, where the refrains finally stand side by side, let their juxtaposition alone, without commentary, show what the thesis claims: that there can be a why without a who.

The poem must get the view right. A reader who knows the position should recognize that these reasons are real and do explanatory work, and should see in the form itself why the two commitments — reasons are real; no one has them — keep circling one another and meet only at the end. A reader who knows nothing of the debate should still find it a good poem. Provide the poem alone, with no gloss or commentary.
