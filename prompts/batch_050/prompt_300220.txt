At a philosophy workshop last week I listened to two graduate students argue about prioritarianism -- the view that a benefit of a given size has more moral value the worse off its recipient is -- and its supposed immunity to the leveling-down objection. Neither convinced the other, and I'd like a referee.

(1) Sofia opened by saying the leveling-down objection decisively favors prioritarianism over egalitarianism. If inequality were bad in itself, then making the better-off worse off without helping anyone -- her example was destroying part of a rich region's harvest so it matches a poor one -- would be good in at least one respect, which she called absurd. Prioritarianism avoids this entirely, since lowering the better-off raises no one's priority-weighted score, so the view is strictly superior.

(2) Leon replied that prioritarianism escapes the letter of the objection but abandons something real along with it. Because it attends only to absolute welfare levels and weights, it is wholly non-comparative: it cannot register any difference between an inequality that arose through plain bad luck and an otherwise identical inequality that arose through one group being cheated. Yet we do judge these differently, which suggests comparison matters in a way prioritarianism structurally cannot capture.

(3) Sofia answered that whatever is doing the work in Leon's case is a judgment about injustice, not inequality as such, and a prioritarian can simply add an independent principle against cheating. Building comparison into the fundamental currency of value, she said, is exactly the move that generates leveling-down absurdities, whereas a pluralist view keeps each value where it behaves sensibly. She added that our intuition that inequality itself is bad is probably just misplaced concern for the worse-off: when we scrutinize cases, what troubles us is always their absolute condition.

(4) Leon closed by denying that the objection targets the view egalitarians actually hold. The plausible claim was never that inequality simpliciter is bad, but that undeserved or uncompensated inequality is bad -- and leveling a deserved gap down is not an improvement even in one respect on that view. So the leveling-down objection refutes a position nobody serious defends, and prioritarianism's alleged dialectical advantage over its main rival is illusory.

Please adjudicate: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating; say who is more right overall and on which specific points; and identify anything important both speakers are missing. Also indicate how confident you are in your verdict, and single out the one assessment you are least sure of, explaining why.
