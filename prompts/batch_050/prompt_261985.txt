I sit on the medical standards board of a national passenger rail operator. One of our drivers, recovering from an eye condition, underwent a new corrective procedure; it worked so well that his tested acuity now exceeds both his pre-illness vision and the standard our regulations define as normal, and there is credible evidence he now responds to trackside signals faster than before he fell ill. Our rules permit procedures that restore normal function but forbid "elective enhancements," a category we have never defined. Word has spread: a second driver, whose mediocre-but-passing vision has never needed treatment, has requested the identical procedure, and the union is pressing a parity argument — if the board blessed the overshoot as therapy in the first case, on what basis can it deny the same procedure now? Draft the board's decision memo: approve the second request, deny it, or adopt a general policy under which both cases resolve consistently. The memo should (i) commit to a principled therapy/enhancement line by naming the baseline it relies on — species-typical function, the patient's own prior function, or the function the job actually requires — and defend that choice; (ii) confront the awkward fact that if the procedure counts as enhancement, it is also a safety improvement the board would seem perverse to prohibit; and (iii) state the strongest consideration favoring the recommendation the memo rejects, and explain why it does not carry the day.
