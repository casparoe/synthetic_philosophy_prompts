So my friend and I got into this huge argument at the pub last night that went til closing and we basically agreed to disagree, but it's still bugging me. I'm going to lay out the back-and-forth as neutrally as I can (not saying which of us held which side lol) and I want you to referee.

It was about the is-ought gap. The exchange went roughly like this:

1. Side A: Hume's point stands and people keep forgetting it. No amount of facts about the world gets you a single ought without a value premise doing the work. So claims like "we ought to cut emissions" are never established by climate science alone — the science gives you the "if you want X" part, and the wanting X has to come from somewhere else.

2. Side B: But we bridge is and ought constantly without anyone batting an eye. "Your brakes are shot, so you ought to get them fixed before the drive down." Nobody hears that as missing a premise. The ought follows fine once the situation is described.

3. Side A: There IS a suppressed premise there — something like "you ought to do what your ends require" or just that you want to survive the drive. That's not a counterexample to Hume, that's Hume's whole point: the ought was smuggled in through the premise. Hypothetical imperatives don't refute the gap, they illustrate it.

4. Side B: OK but then epistemic oughts are in the exact same boat. "The evidence supports the hypothesis, so you ought to believe it." If no ought can come from an is, then rational belief is just as ungrounded as morality. And that's self-undermining for A, because A's whole arguement is "you ought to accept the gap, because the logic compels it" — an epistemic ought resting on the very bridge A says doesn't exist.

5. Side A: There's an asymmetry though. Epistemic norms might be constitutive of belief itself — to believe something just is to take it as true, you literally can't believe while repudiating the norm of truth. But you can act while repudiating morality; the amoralist is at least coherent. So the two cases come apart, and no self-undermining.

6. Side B: Two problems. First, whether some norm is "constitutive" of belief is itself a factual claim about what belief is — an is-claim — so A is now deriving an ought-asymmetry from an is-fact, which violates A's own rule. Second, it's not obvious the amoralist is coherent either; maybe intentional action involves taking your reasons as normative in the same way belief involves taking things as true. A just asserted the asymmetry instead of showing it.
