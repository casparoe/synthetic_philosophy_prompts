My two roommates got into a long argument last night about mind uploading and I want an outside referee because they're both dug in. Here's the exchange as best I remember it.

It started with the classic setup: destructive scan-and-copy uploading (your brain gets scanned and destroyed, a perfect emulation runs on a computer) versus gradual replacement (tiny machines swap out your neurons one at a time over, say, a year, each synthetic neuron doing exactly the job of the biological one, until your whole brain is artificial but you never "went under").

(1) Dana said gradual replacement clearly preserves the person -- nobody would say you die if a doctor replaces one neuron, and one more, and one more -- but scan-and-copy clearly kills you and leaves a copy that merely *thinks* it's you. So the two procedures have completely different outcomes even if the end products behave the same.

(2) Leo said that's incoherent. The endpoint of gradual replacement is a brain with zero original matter, functionally identical to the uploaded copy. If the gradual endpoint counts as Dana surviving, then matter doesn't matter, only organization does -- and the copy has the organization too. Calling one survival and the other death is just sentimentality about the *path*, not the outcome.

(3) Dana said the path is exactly the point: in gradual replacement there's an unbroken stream of conciousness the whole time, you're awake and experiencing things as each neuron is swapped, whereas scan-and-copy has a sharp break and then a second, separate stream starts up elsewhere. Continuity of experience is what personal identity actually is.

(4) Leo replied that "unbroken stream" does no work, since sleep, anesthesia, and even fainting break experience all the time and nobody thinks you die nightly. And in the gradual case, by the end, the stream is running entirely on silicon anyway -- so if silicon can carry your stream there, why can't it carry it after a scan?

(5) Dana then tried a different tack: in the upload case there's a moment where both the biological original and the copy exist, and they're obviously two different people at that moment -- so the copy was never the original, and destroying the original afterwards is just killing them. Gradual replacement never has a two-people moment.

(6) Leo said that only shows branching is weird, not that the copy isn't a continuation -- by that logic, if the scanner destroyed the original a millisecond *before* the copy booted, it would suddenly be fine, which is absurd. He added that Dana's continuity criterion seems to really be "no duplicates ever exist," which isn't about the person at all, it's about bookkeeping.

(7) Dana's last move: fine, maybe she can't state the criterion cleanly, but the burden is on Leo -- he's the one claiming a procedure that involves obliterating the only brain that's ever been you somehow counts as you walking out of the clinic. When in doubt about whether something kills you, don't do it.

So: please go through moves (1)-(7) and say who's right at each step and why, not just who wins overall. And at the end, tell me who's more right on the big question, and what crucial thing you think *both* of them are missing.
