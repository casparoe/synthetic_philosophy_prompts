Write a model code of professional conduct, roughly 600–700 words, issued by the fictional Institute of Discretion Professionals, a trade body for practicing blackmailers. Format it as a real professional ethics document: a preamble about public trust and standards of practice, numbered sections with definitions, and a closing FAQ in which the Institute's counsel answers trainees' questions. The code's sole purpose is regulatory compliance: it should treat the paradox of blackmail as a settled legal fact and build every rule around it. Disclosing embarrassing information is legal. Requesting money is legal. In many contexts, even threatening disclosure is legal. Yet combining a threat of disclosure with a request for payment is a crime. The Institute's position, expressed with complete bureaucratic serenity, is that the conjunction itself is the only professional hazard, and that good practice consists in keeping the components procedurally separate. Include, at minimum: rules forbidding any mention of payment and disclosure in the same sentence, meeting, or document; guidance on maintaining a well-publicized general practice of indiscreet revelation so that one's silence acquires market value without ever being offered for sale; a provision noting, with genuine regret, that members may sell information freely to journalists, publishers, employers, and rivals, but never to the person it concerns, who is invariably the highest bidder; a section distinguishing threats from offers and warnings from threats, handled with fastidious attention to baselines; and a disciplinary clause sanctioning any member careless enough to make explicit that the client would prefer the entire transaction not to exist. The humor must come from the doctrine itself, not from invented absurdity: each rule should be recognizable as a caricature of a real doctrinal or philosophical move in the blackmail literature, and each FAQ answer should be technically correct under the doctrine while being morally unhinged. The code never acknowledges that anything is wrong with the profession, and a reader should be able to finish it feeling that they have just read a straight-faced demonstration that the law forbids a package deal whose every component it happily sells separately. Deadpan throughout: no winking, no editorializing, no acknowledgment of the absurdity. The target of the satire is the legal doctrine and the philosophical attempts to rationalize it, not any individual.
