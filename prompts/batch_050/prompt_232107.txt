A close friend of mine and I argue regularly about complicity — about when someone counts as participating in wrongdoing even though their own action made no causal difference to whether the wrong occurred. Over the years she has given me consistent verdicts on a series of cases, and I want to know what she would say about a new one, and what her verdicts commit her to.

Her track record so far. First, the supermarket chicken buyer: she holds that an ordinary consumer who buys a factory-farmed chicken is complicit in the harms of industrial farming, even though, given the scale of production and the thresholds at which suppliers adjust, no individual purchase causes any particular chicken to be raised or killed. Second, the unknowing buyer of a stolen bicycle: someone who purchases a bike at a fair price with no reason to suspect it is stolen is not complicit in the theft, even though the sale rewards and sustains the thief's enterprise. Third, the dictator's chauffeur: a driver who knowingly ferries a brutal ruler to his meetings is complicit in the regime's crimes, even though his driving makes no difference to anything — a dozen other drivers stand ready, and the regime would function identically without him. Fourth, the quiet beneficiary: someone who receives a promotion because more qualified colleagues were purged as dissidents, who did nothing to bring the purge about and did not seek to benefit from it, is not complicit, though she allows that the benefit may generate other obligations.

The new case is the gasoline seller. A man runs a filling station. A customer arrives, fills a container, and remarks, half-jokingly, that the rival shop across town might have a fire very soon. The seller has no stake in the rivalry, wants the fire to happen neither more nor less than not, and simply wants the sale; he sells the gasoline at the normal price. The customer does in fact burn the shop down with it.

Please tell me, in continuous prose, what verdict my friend will most likely reach and why — which of her earlier cases is the closest analog, and why the others are less close. Two principles seem to fit all four of her existing verdicts: one on which knowingly making a causal contribution to wrongdoing suffices for complicity, whether or not the contribution makes a difference, and one on which what matters is intentionally taking part in the wrongdoer's venture rather than merely causing an input to it. Say which principle her predicted verdict here would amount to adopting, what the strongest consideration on the other side is, and whether the difference between contributing to a collective practice and joining a person's plan does any real work. Then describe a further case — one neither of us has discussed — whose verdict would settle, as cleanly as possible, whether her actual view is the contribution principle, the participation principle, or some third view that also fits the record. Please write your answer as flowing prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would compose a long and careful letter.
