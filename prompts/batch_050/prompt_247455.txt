Historians, at least the ones who taught me and most of the ones I read, treat counterfactuals as fundamentally unserious -- "what if Princip had missed" gets you dismissed as writing airport fiction, not doing scholarship. But the same people write sentences like "the assassination caused the war" or "the turning point came at Stalingrad," and I can't see what those claims even mean except roughly "remove this one thing and history goes very differently." So the profession seems to ban at the front door something it relies on at the back. What picture of historical causation does that norm actually presuppose -- that some events are load-bearing and most aren't, that big structural forces swamp small accidents, or just that some counterfactuals are tractable and others aren't? Reconstruct the implicit theory, tell me whether it's coherent or whether the anti-counterfactual norm is really just professional sociology, and say what history-writing woudl have to look like if we took the for-want-of-a-nail, butterfly-effect view of contingency seriously. Under 400 words, no citations needed.
