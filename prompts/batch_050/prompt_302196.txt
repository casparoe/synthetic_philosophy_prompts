So I write a monthly column for a general-audience science magazine about dead and discredited science — phlogiston, miasmas, caloric fluid, the luminiferous ether, that whole graveyard. The pieces do fine with readers but my editor keeps scribbling "whiggish" in the margins and I can't fully argue with her. I do tend to write the past as a bunch of people stumbling around in the dark until someone finally gets it right, because honestly that's the version that makes a good story. I know the term comes from Herbert Butterfield and I get the basic complaint — judging the past by how well it anticipated us, treating the winners as the plot and everyone else as an obstacle — but knowing the complaint hasn't stopped me from committing it.

What I want is a self-editing procedure I can run on every draft before it goes to her, something that takes an hour or so on a 3,000-word piece. Give me the tests in order, each one concrete enough that I can answer it about a specific sentence or paragraph, with what counts as passing and what counts as a rewrite. I want it to handle the tricky distinctions, like: sometimes the past genuinely did lead to the present, and saying so isn't automatically a sin; sometimes a historical figure really was treated unfairly; sometimes "X had not yet discovered Y" is just a true sentence about chronology. I need the procedure to tell me when I'm doing the bad version of these moves versus the fine version, not just to ban anything that sounds teleological. Mark which tests are basically mechanical (hunt for these phrases, count these things) and which are judgement calls in disguise, and be honest about that. Also tell me where the procedure has to stop and punt the question to an actual historian, because I don't have one on retainer.

Then run it on two passages from stuff I've actually written. First, my lede from a piece on Lamarck: I framed him as a laughingstock who was "vindicated at last" by epigenetic inheritance research. Second, a passage about Alfred Wegener as a lone genius who saw the truth about continental drift while the hidebound geology establishment sneered, until the evidence finally forced them to admit he was right. For each one, walk the procedure through step by step and show what survives and what gets cut. And include at least one case — from these two or one you construct — where the procedure forces a rewrite of a passage the writer clearly loves, and say what the procedure fundamentally can't fix, e.g. if the problem is the whole framing of my column rather than any particular sentence.
