Last night I spent the better part of four hours in the pub arguing with an old friend about whether 'better than' is transitive, and I came away thoroughly dissatisfied with my own performance. I want to rehearse the debate properly this time, so I would like you to play her.

Her position, which you are to defend, is a serious one, so please do not strawman it. She holds that 'better than' is transitive, full stop, and that the so-called spectrum arguments — Parfit's continuum of populations leading to the Repugnant Conclusion, and Temkin-style sequences in which an enormous number of barely noticeable discomforts is weighed against a single life of agony — cannot be evaded by denying transitivity. On her view these are not sorites fallacies in disguise, because at every step the improvement is genuine and perceptible, not a borderline case: each successive state of affairs really is better than its predecessor. Anyone who blocks the conclusion by abandoning transitivity, she says, has been bullied by an argument into giving up the most basic structural feature of value, and owes us a principled account of where exactly in the sequence the chain breaks and why it breaks there rather than anywhere else.

The rules of the exercise. You are sharp but fair: you will concede minor points when they are genuinely lost, but never the transitivity claim itself. If I appeal to cyclical preferences in ordinary life, you will treat these as cases of shifting criteria or under-described options rather than genuine intransitivity of value. If I invoke vague boundaries, you will press the difference between vagueness about where a boundary lies and the claim that no boundary exists. Keep each reply to a single paragraph, so that this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture, and stay in character until I say 'enough'.

Here is my opening move, which is where I stumbled last night. You insist that every step in the spectrum is a genuine improvement, but it seems to me that what makes each step look better is a different consideration at different points in the sequence: near the top we are moved by the intensity of suffering relieved, further down by its duration, and towards the bottom by the sheer number of people affected. If the relation being chained together is not one relation but a succession of different ones, then transitivity has been smuggled in by equivocation, and the argument never gets off the ground. What is your reply?
