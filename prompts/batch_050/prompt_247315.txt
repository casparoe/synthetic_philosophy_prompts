For the past year, the journal club at the AI safety organization where I work has been split between two camps, and every session ends in the same stalemate. The first camp holds that the only alignment assurance worth the name is a worst-case one: some argument, ideally approaching a guarantee, that a sufficiently capable system will not behave catastrophically even in conditions its designers did not anticipate — under distribution shift, under novel capabilities that appear late in training, and under the possibility that the system is actively modeling and outmaneuvering its own evaluation. On their view, empirical evidence of good behavior is nearly worthless precisely where it matters most, because a system that fails only in rare or adversarially-selected conditions will look exactly like a system that never fails, right up until it does. The second camp holds that safety is an empirical science like bridge engineering or drug approval: you test systems across the distribution of deployments you actually expect, you red-team, you iterate on observed failures, and you accept residual risk as the price of doing anything at all. On their view, the first camp is demanding a kind of guarantee that no learned system could ever provide, that no other engineering discipline provides either, and that the demand itself is doing the damage — freezing useful work and treating the absence of a proof as the presence of a disaster. Each side thinks the other's standard is incoherent: one thinks "low expected harm" papers over the tail where the catastrophe lives, the other thinks "no catastrophic failure under any conditions" is an unfalsifiable demand dressed up as rigor.

I want you to draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. The document should have three parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: what both sides accept about what current evaluation and red-teaming methods do and do not establish, about whether a worst-case guarantee for a learned system is attainable in principle (as opposed to merely unattained), about whether the two standards are even rivals or are answers to different questions, and about what each kind of assurance would cost. Second, a statement of the remaining disagreements, each formulated in wording the side it describes would accept as accurate — not the unflattering paraphrase its opponents use at the whiteboard. Third, a cruxes table: for each crux, the observation, experiment, or future development that both sides agree bears on it, and each side's prediction about that observation recorded in advance. I suspect the cruxes include whether a system that has never once shown deception under extensive probing thereby gives strong evidence it is not deceptive; whether capabilities and alignment-relevant properties generalize together or can come apart sharply at capability thresholds; and whether there exists any possible empirical result that would satisfy the worst-case camp, or any possible argument that would satisfy the average-case camp that such results are insufficient.

If, in the drafting, you find that part of the dispute dissolves — that the two camps are using "safe" or "aligned" to mean different things, or that one side's guarantee and the other's empirical track record are not competitors but components a serious safety case needs both of — say so explicitly, identify which part, and explain why the parties might nonetheless experience it as a substantive disagreement. Conversely, if some crux turns out to be one that no feasible observation could settle, mark it as such rather than forcing a test onto it, and say what that implies about how the two camps should allocate their effort in the meantime.
