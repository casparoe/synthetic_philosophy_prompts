I am taking a final-year course on gender, psychology and public policy, and last week we covered the so-called gender-equality paradox: countries that score highest on standard indices of gender equality — the Nordic states especially — tend to produce a smaller proportion of women among STEM graduates than many markedly less egalitarian countries. The candidate explanations we discussed ranged from genuinely different average interests, to women's comparative advantage in verbal domains, to the thought that STEM careers are chosen as a route to financial security precisely where the welfare state is thin; our lecturer also mentioned a live methodological dispute about how the underlying indices are constructed.

For my assessed essay I want to argue about what, if anything, this finding shows. Please help me think it through. Consider three debates in which the paradox is routinely invoked: the nature-versus-culture question about gendered preferences; the justice question of whether a gendered occupational distribution is itself a defect that policy ought to correct; and the practical question of whether STEM-recruitment programmes aimed at girls are well conceived. For each, say whether the finding actually bears on it at all, what further empirical or normative premises the inference would require, and which popular conclusions drawn from the paradox — in either political direction — fail to survive scrutiny. Then tell me which of the three debates the finding should most reshape, defending your ranking by appeal to how much each debate genuinely turns on empirical versus normative commitments, and state what further evidence would reorder it. Please answer in British English.
