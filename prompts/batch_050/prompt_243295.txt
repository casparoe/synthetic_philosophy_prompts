A friend of mine audited a philosophy of language seminar last term and has been summarizing what he took away from the unit on fictional discourse. Over coffee he gave me roughly this: "The whole problem of fictional names is overblown. Sherlock Holmes doesn't exist, so the name 'Sherlock Holmes' refers to nothing, and a sentence containing a name that refers to nothing cannot be true. So 'Sherlock Holmes is a detective' is false, or at best meaningless, while 'Sherlock Holmes does not exist' is unproblematically true, since it merely denies that anything answers to the name. Some philosophers try to dodge this by claiming fictional characters exist as abstract objects, but that's just Platonist mysticism: if Holmes already existed as an abstract object, then Conan Doyle discovered rather than created him, which is absurd, since Doyle obviously made him up by writing the stories. The only sensible position is that talk about fiction is a kind of pretending or make-believe, so strictly speaking nothing we say about Holmes is ever really true."

Something about this bothers me, though I can't articulate what. My friend is generally careful, and I believe he's reporting the seminar in good faith, but I notice he slides between "false" and "meaningless" as if those were the same verdict, and his way of dismissing the abstract-object view seems to assume the very thing the view is trying to explain (namely, what an author does when inventing a character). I'm also puzzled by the tension between his first and last moves: if fictional talk is all pretense such that nothing about Holmes is really true, what status does the sentence "Holmes does not exist" itself have, and on what basis is that one spared the pretense treatment? And if a name's failure to refer really made every sentence containing it untrue, it seems the negative existential should come out untrue too, which would be a strange result.

Could you audit his summary for me? I'd like to know which parts reflect standard, defensible positions in the literature on empty names and truth in fiction (I believe names like Russell, Meinong, and Walton came up in his seminar), which parts conflate distinct views or overstate a contested claim as settled, and whether his bottom-line conclusion — that the problem is overblown and nothing said about fiction is ever really true — actually follows once the summary is corrected. In particular, I'd appreciate an explanation of why "Sherlock Holmes does not exist" is considered philosophically trickier than his presentation suggests, and whether the "Doyle created Holmes, so he can't be an abstract object" objection is as decisive as my friend thinks.
