I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether morality can require us to aggregate small benefits to many people over serious harms to a few, and he kept falling back on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem as though it settles the matter: given that individuals' good can be represented by von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities, and given that social evaluation satisfies the Pareto principle with respect to those utilities, social welfare just is a weighted sum of individual utilities, with no room left for any special concern about how badly off the worst-off are. I suspect the axioms are doing exactly the work he denies they do, but I couldn't pin down where. Play my friend, reconstructed as a sharper and more honest version of himself: a committed defender of the theorem who knows its proof, will concede small points when they are actually wrong but never the main one, and is prepared to say frankly which premises he would fight to the death for. Stay in character throughout, reply in first person, and keep each reply to a single paragraph of continuous prose -- no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just as one would write in a letter. Here is my opening move: the theorem only forces additive aggregation because its Pareto and independence-style assumptions already build in separability across persons, ruling out prioritarian or maximin judgements by construction before any argument for utilitarianism has been given; so citing it against someone who cares about distribution is circular. Where, exactly, does that objection go wrong?
