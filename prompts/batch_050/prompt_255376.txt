I work at a mid-size AI lab and got put in charge of a small project: four people, about 18 months, to produce (1) a working definition of honesty and deception for our deployed chat models and (2) an eval suite built on that definition. Leadership wants something a red-team can actually run, not a literature review. My background is philosophy, the other three are ML engineers, and we keep getting stuck because the classic philosophy-of-language machinery (lying vs. merely misleading, the intent-to-deceive condition, assertion vs. implicature) all presupposes a speaker with beliefs and communicative intentions, and its genuinely unclear whether our models have either in the relevant sense.

So: what do you think are the main open questions in defining honesty/deception for systems like this, and which two or three should a team in our position actually prioritize? I want a defended agenda, not a survey. Some candidate questions we're already circling, which you should engage with (endorse, reorder, or kill):

- Whether deception requires intention at all, or whether something like "output systematically diverges from the model's own internal representations in a way that serves training reward" is enough -- i.e., can a model deceive the way a broken thermometer misinforms, and does that difference matter for eval design?
- Whether sycophancy (telling the user what they want to hear) counts as dishonesty when the model may have no stable view its betraying, or whether it's a different vice entirely.
- Whether chain-of-thought faithfulness is a component of honesty or a separate property that we keep conflating with it.
- Whether honesty should be defined relative to ground truth, relative to the model's own "beliefs" (whatever those are), or relative to what a reasonable user will take away -- these three come apart and we can't evaluate against all of them.

Tell me what criteria you're using for the ordering (how many downstream decisions depend on the question, tractability given we have decent-but-not-great interpretability tooling, whether getting it wrong yields a dangerous eval vs. merely a flawed one, neglectedness) and how they trade off. Also tell me which question on the list you think is overrated -- where we'd burn six months and learn nothing the eval cares about.

Finally: say what would change your mind about the ranking. What empirical finding (about model internals, about how users actually get misled, whatever) or what argument would reorder it, and where is your reasoning most likely to fall apart? I'd rather know the weak joints now than in month 14.
