ok so quick context: I'm a novelist, three drafts into a book, and my friend Mara (philosophy lecturer, huge Arendt head, will not shut up about Eichmann in Jerusalem) has been my unofficial consultant on my villain. She has very strong opinions about which characters, real or fictional, count as "banal" evil in Arendt's sense versus some other kind of evil, and I've honestly lost track of what her actual rule is.

Pretend you're both an expert on Arendt and moral psychology AND a halfway decent fiction editor. Think carefully, and maybe brainstorm the candidate rules out loud before commiting to one.

Her verdicts so far, as best as I can reconstruct from our pub conversations:

1. Eichmann as Arendt describes him — yes, obviously, the paradigm case.
2. A fanatical SS ideologue who sincerely swallowed the propaganda and killed out of conviction — no, not banal, even though his victims are just as dead.
3. A railway clerk who schedules deportation trains, never asks where they go, and is mainly worried about his pension — yes, banal.
4. A guard who genuinely enjoys cruelty and volunteers for the worst duties — no, that's sadism, different category entirely.
5. A middle manager at a chemical company who signs off on dumping waste, tells himself the reports are above his pay grade, and never meets anyone downstream — yes, banal.
6. A whistleblower-adjacent character who saw everything clearly, stayed quiet for three years out of fear, then finally spoke — no, fear-driven silence isn't banality because she WAS thinking about it the whole time, that was the problem.
7. A corrupt cop who takes bribes to look the other way — no, ordinary calculated self-interest, "not enough thoughtlessness to be interesting" (her words, roughly).

Now the actual problem. My villain is Dorothea, a municipal housing official in a near-future city. She personally processes the exclusion orders that keep certain families out of the districts with clean air. Unlike the clerk and the manager, she is NOT remote from her victims: the families appeal in person, she looks them in the eye, she's polite, even genuinely sympathetic, and then she signs anyway. But she's no ideologue either. She privately suspects the policy is unjust, she just finds that thought exhausting and has gotten very very good at scheduling it away ("I'll think about it after the quarter closes"). She doesn't enjoy any of it. She profits modestly — her housing assignment, her kid's school placement.

So: predict Mara's verdict. Does Dorothea count as banal evil in the Arendt sense or not? More importantly, tell me what rule Mara has actually been running all this time. I can see at least two candidates that fit all seven verdicts — roughly (a) banality is about absence of ideological motive plus a failure/refusal to think, versus (b) banality is about structural remoteness, routinized paperwork evil where you never see the people you harm — and they seem to diverge on Dorothea, since she's face-to-face with the families but also kind of half-thinking about what she does and batting the thought away. Which rule fits her track record best, what does each rule predict about Dorothea, what's your best guess and why, and what further test case (I can invent any character you want) would settle which rule Mara actually holds?

And selfishly, one more thing: if you were my editor rather than Mara's interpreter, which verdict makes Dorothea a better character? Keep it casual, no citations or reading lists please, I can't check them anyway.
