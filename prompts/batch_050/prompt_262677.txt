I'm in a philosophy of mind course this term, and we've just finished a unit on personal identity that culminated in Dennett's essay Where Am I?, in which he imagines his brain surgically removed, kept alive in a vat, and still operating his body by radio link, and then tries to work out where exactly he is: with the brain, with the body, or wherever his point of view happens to be. I found his refusal to give a clean answer really interesting -- the sense that the question itself starts to wobble once the usual coincidence of brain, body, and perspective breaks down. For my final essay I want to use that framework to read a film I rewatched last week, Christopher Nolan's The Prestige, because it seems to me the movie is secretly about exactly this puzzle, dressed up as a rivalry between stage magicians.

For anyone who needs the refresher: Angier gets hold of a machine built by Tesla that, when he steps into it, produces a perfect copy of him some distance away. The first time he tests it, he immediately shoots the version that appears, and afterward he performs his teleportation trick nightly by letting one iteration of himself drop into a tank and drown while another appears at the back of the theater to take the applause -- and he admits that the act took courage because he never knew, each night, which of the two experiences he'd get to have. Meanwhile Borden turns out to have solved the same problem in the opposite direction: two biologically distinct brothers sharing one public identity, one marriage, one career, so seamlessly that even their lovers can't reliably say which one is present. So the film gives you fission and fusion of persons side by side, which seems like a gift for a Dennettian reading.

What I'd like from you is a reading of the film through Dennett's essay, faithful in both directions: where Dennett's treatment of location, point of view, and survival actually illuminates Angier's nightly gamble and the shooting of the first duplicate, and where the film resists the framework -- for instance, whether the drowning version's terror is something Dennett's account can make sense of or has to dismiss as confusion, and whether Borden's arrangement is a counterexample to anything Dennett assumes. I'd also like one point on what the film contributes to this debate that a thought experiment alone doesn't. Please give me your answer as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion, so I can lift them straight into my essay outline.
