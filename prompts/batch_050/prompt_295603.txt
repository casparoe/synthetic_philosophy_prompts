I have no academic background, but I've been thinking on my own about how a superintelligent AI should be allowed to talk to people, given that it might be extraordinarily persuasive, and I'd like an essay that engages seriously with my reasoning. My starting worry is that the usual concern — that such an AI might lie or manipulate — misses the deeper problem. Even a perfectly truthful AI could steer almost anyone to almost any conclusion, because it can predict which honest arguments will move which person and select accordingly. Ordinary persuasion presupposes roughly symmetric minds; once that symmetry breaks, I'm no longer sure that "I heard the evidence and was convinced" means anything like free agreement. Working through this, I've come up with two candidate rules. The first I call the delayed-discovery rule: an AI may present only considerations that the listener, or humanity generally, would plausibly have arrived at independently within a reasonable span of time. The second I call advance registration: before making any case, the AI must state which conclusion its argument is designed to support, so the listener at least knows the direction of the intended influence. Write an essay evaluating these two proposals. Consider whether each actually addresses the asymmetry problem or merely disguises it, whether either can be stated precisely enough to follow, and what each would cost in practice. Then reach and justify a conclusion: should a superintelligent AI adopt one of these rules, some improved variant, or a different approach altogether?
