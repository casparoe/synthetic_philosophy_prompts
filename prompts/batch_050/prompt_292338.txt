I co-teach a philosophy unit at a community college, about 18 students, and I have one 75-minute session on population ethics. Design a game or staged activity that makes the nonidentity problem land as a real problem and not just a cute puzzle. The setup I have in mind: students' choices should determine which other students "get to exist" in the game and at what quality of life (tokens or points as welfare), so that when the group picks the selfishly attractive policy, the "victims" who want to complain turn out to be people who only exist because that policy was picked. I want them to feel the weirdness of a complaint that seems obviously legitimate and then collapses when they try to state who's been wronged. Include the actual rules, how to assign roles, what the choices and payoffs look like, and make sure there's a round where the policy harms *identifiable* existing people so they can feel the contrast with the nonidentity round. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the philosophical moves (person-affecting intuitions, the "no one is worse off" reply, attempts to recover the wrong via impersonal betterness), plus what will predictably go wrong when I run it -- e.g., students conflating "a life barely worth living" with a life not worth living, or trying to rescue the complaint by pretending the people would have existed anyway. Also tell me honestly which part of the actual nonidentity problem the game fudges, since I suspect having real students stand in for possible people distorts something important. Don't name Parfit or use the term "nonidentity" until the debrief; the activity has to work cold.
