At a workshop on bounded rationality, a game theorist cut off my suggestion that players might cooperate for several rounds of a centipede game with the line: "Common knowledge of rationality unravels the centipede — rational players take the money at the first node." I want to know what precise claims that slogan could be making. Please lay out, as a list, the distinct readings: a claim about the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium of the standard game; a claim about what follows epistemically from common knowledge of rationality; a prediction about how actual people play; and a normative claim about what a rational player should do. For each reading, state the assumptions it needs — the structure of payoffs, mutual versus common knowledge, and how a player is supposed to interpret an unexpected cooperative move at a node the reasoning says should never be reached — and give a verdict: platitude, substantive but defensible, or false. Finally, tell me which readings would actually justify my colleague's dismissal, and whether any of them survive once each player assigns even a small probability to the other being a habitual cooperator.
