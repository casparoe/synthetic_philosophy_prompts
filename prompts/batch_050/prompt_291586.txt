I am a PhD student in ancient history, currently TAing a historiography seminar on late antiquity, and I keep running into the same wall. The debate over the end of the Western Roman Empire has, by my reading, been stalled for decades between two camps that both command the same evidence. On one side are the transformationists, descending from Peter Brown's "world of late antiquity," who stress continuity: Roman law, Christian institutions, Latin literacy, and elite culture mutate rather than vanish, and the successor kingdoms present themselves as heirs, not destroyers. On the other side are the catastrophists, Bryan Ward-Perkins most prominently, who point to the material record -- the collapse in the volume and distribution of pottery, coinage, roof tiles, even the shrinking average stature of skeletons -- and insist that for ordinary people something real and terrible ended in the fifth century. Both camps cite the same archaeology, the same texts, and largely the same chronology; nobody is being accused of getting facts wrong; and yet the two literatures talk past each other with a persistence that feels structural rather than accidental.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is covertly definitional: that "the fall of Rome" names at least three different events -- the end of a political unit, a decline in material complexity, and a rupture in elite culture -- and that the camps are each right about their own event while treating the others' as somehow not the real question, with the choice among them smuggling in a value judgment about what a civilization essentially is. But I can also imagine the diagnosis going differently: perhaps it is a genuine empirical disagreement about the pace and distribution of change (a catastrophe for Gaul can be a transformation for the eastern Mediterranean), or a disagreement about standards -- about whether continuity of institutions or welfare of populations is the right meter for civilizational "ends." What I would like is a diagnosis, not a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this, in your analysis: verbal, factual, normative, or several layered on top of one another? Does the long shadow of Gibbon -- and the polemical uses each side makes of the other ("nostalgic reactionaries" versus "apologists for decline") -- indicate that the debate is partly about what periodization is for, rather than about the fifth century at all?

Given your diagnosis, please also tell me what would count as progress. If the camps are answering different questions under one heading, what would a disciplined disambiguation of the question actually look like, and would it dissolve the debate or merely relabel it? If a hidden empirical question is doing the work, is there a class of evidence -- settlement survey data, ancient DNA, ice-core lead pollution as a proxy for economic output -- that could in principle settle it, or do the two sides weigh even that evidence against different background standards? I am not asking for a bibliography; I want the structure of the impasse mapped, with reasons, and an honest statement of whether the debate is the kind that can be ended or only the kind that can be outgrown.
