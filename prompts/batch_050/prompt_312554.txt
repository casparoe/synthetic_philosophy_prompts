I work in operations at a logistics firm and have no formal training in philosophy, but for the past year I've been part of a nonfiction reading group, and we recently read Harry Frankfurt's essay On Bullshit. It got under my skin, because I think it explains something everyone around me is getting wrong about AI chatbots. My reading of Frankfurt, in my own words: the liar knows the truth and hides it, the honest person knows it and tells it, but the bullshitter simply doesn't care whether what they say is true, only whether it serves their purpose. My thesis, which I've agreed to present at a local tech-and-society meetup, is that so-called hallucinations by chatbots are neither lies nor ordinary errors but bullshit in exactly this sense: the system is built to produce text that sounds right, not text that is right, and so its outputs stand in the same relation to truth as the bullshitter's -- indifference, not deception and not honest mistake. I want to be careful here: I am not claiming the machine has feelings of indifference. My claim is that the category fits what the system does, not what it feels, and that getting the label right matters because calling these outputs errors invites patience and better engineering, while calling them bullshit invites a different attitude entirely -- distrust of the genre, not just correction of the instance.

The audience is about thirty people, and I know roughly who will be there: several machine-learning engineers from local companies who I suspect will say the whole framing is confused because a next-token predictor has no attitudes whatsoever and cannot be indifferent to anything; two or three people with philosophy degrees who will press me on whether Frankfurt's account, written about human speakers, can even apply to a system without intentions, and whether I am really talking about the machine or about the companies that deploy it; and a number of non-technical regulars who will want to know what follows practically -- whether my label lets the manufacturers off the hook or, conversely, puts more blame on them. The engineers care about precision and dislike anthropomorphic language; the philosophy people care about whether I have distorted Frankfurt; the regulars care about who is responsible when the chatbot confidently invents a fact. Each group will object in its own vocabulary, and I get maybe twenty minutes of questions.

Please prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections I am most likely to face, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they do if I fumble them, each phrased the way that particular audience member would actually phrase it. For each one, give me a reply I can deliver in under a minute, note where that reply is weakest, and tell me honestly whether it is an objection I should concede, partially concede, or fight. Tell me what I should concede up front in my opening remarks to take the sting out of the obvious attacks -- I suspect the no-attitudes point has to be granted in some form, but I do not want to concede so much that my thesis evaporates. Finally, tell me the one objection I am quietly hoping nobody raises, the one where my position is thinnest, so I can decide in advance whether to address it preemptively or pray.
