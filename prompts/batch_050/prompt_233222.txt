I am an economist with formal training (general equilibrium theory, mechanism design, some computational economics) about to commit to a multi-year research program, and I keep returning to the socialist calculation debate as unfinished business. My reading of it is this. Mises, in his 1920 essay on economic calculation in the socialist commonwealth, argued roughly that where the means of production are not privately owned and exchanged, there are no money prices for capital goods, and without such prices no one can compare the costs of alternative production plans, so rational allocation in a complex economy is impossible in principle. Hayek later reframed the difficulty as less about arithmetic and more about knowledge: the relevant information is dispersed, contextual, and partly inarticulable, and prices aggregate it in a way no central board can replicate by collecting statistics. Lange's reply was that a central planning board could mimic the market by announcing prices, instructing state firm managers to follow marginal-cost rules, and adjusting prices in a trial-and-error process until markets clear.

What I want is not a verdict on who won -- the historiography is stale and mostly tribal -- but a defended research agenda. Suppose I can devote about five years to questions that descend from this debate. Which open questions should I prioritize, and in what order? To make this concrete, here are candidates I can see; tell me which deserve the years, which are already effectively settled, and which are traps:

1. Whether Lange's trial-and-error scheme fails on informational grounds (the board cannot know the right excess-demand signals), on computational grounds (solving the system is intractable at scale), or on incentive grounds (managers and boards will not play by the rules), and whether these three objections can actually be disentangled or secretly presuppose one another.
2. Whether modern computation -- vast data collection, fast optimization, machine learning -- changes the answer Mises and Hayek gave, or merely relocates the bottleneck (for instance, to incentive-compatibility of reported information, or to the absence of genuine entrepreneurship).
3. Whether the tacit-knowledge argument is empirically testable at all, and what would count as evidence for or against it, given that its defenders often state it in a way that seems immune to refutation.
4. What the actual performance of planned economies does and does not show about the theoretical dispute, given that real planning systems were never Langean and real markets never textbook-Walrasian.

For your ordering, state your criteria explicitly -- e.g., how many downstream questions each answer unlocks, tractability for someone with my toolkit, and how much the answer would matter for live institutional questions today (platform-mediated allocation, industrial policy, AI-assisted resource planning inside large firms, which are in some ways mini planned economies). I care less about comprehensiveness than about the reasoning: why is your top question upstream of the others, what would you deprioritize and why, and what finding over the next few years would make you reorder the list? If you think the framing of one of my four questions is confused -- for example, if you think question 1 rests on conflating distinct objections that Mises and Hayek kept separate -- say so and reframe it rather than answering it as posed.
