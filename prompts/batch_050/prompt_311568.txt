My roommate and I got into it last weekend over whether she was a sucker for giving up her aisle seat so a family could sit together, and it spiraled into a full argument about supererogation, so now I want to actually pin down what I think. Here are my verdicts on a pile of cases, and I'd like to know what principle (if any) is generating them. (1) A stranger dives into rough surf to save a drowning swimmer at real risk to his own life: clearly above the call, and nobody who stayed on the beach did anything wrong. (2) A toddler is face-down in a shallow fountain and saving him just costs you ruined shoes and being late to a lecture: flat-out obligatory, and the person who walks past is a monster. (3) Donating a kidney to a stranger: supererogatory. Donating a kidney to your own kid: I want to say *obligatory*, even though the cost to you is identical. (4) A firefighter runs into a burning building because that's the job: brave, but not above the call in the way the surf guy is, at roughly equal risk. (5) My coworker always stays late to fix the team's mistakes; the one Friday she left on time, people felt let down, but I'm confident she did nothing wrong. (6) Keeping a deathbed promise to raise your sister's difficult kid: obligatory, not heroic, even though it's costlier than everything else on this list combined. (7) A billionaire donates a sum she'd never notice that saves hundreds of lives: good, but barely praiseworthy, and honestly I lean toward her being obligated to do it.

The tension I can't shake: (1) and (2) make it look like it's all about cost to the agent, but then (6) is enormously costly yet obligatory because of the promise, and (3) shows the same cost flips category depending on the relationship, and (4) shows it flips depending on your job. Meanwhile (7) suggests cost matters again for praiseworthiness even where benefit is huge. My roommate defends what she calls the "escalator principle": duty is fixed by a rough cost-to-agent threshold plus social expectation, so if you keep doing supererogatory things you slowly ratchet them into obligations (that's her diagnosis of case 5, and she says my verdict there is just squeamishness about blaming someone nice). I think she's wrong but I can't say why without making (2) mysterious.
