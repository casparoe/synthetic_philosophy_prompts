I'm a philosophy prof — ancient philosophy mostly, with a side interest in epistemology — but a few years ago I did a sabbatical embedded in a CS department and I've honestly never fully recovered. These days I think in terms of estimators, calibration, and expected value of information more comfortably than half my colleagues think in terms of justified true belief. So here's what I want help with. Take the debate about why knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief: it kicks off in Plato's Meno, where Socrates points out that a true opinion about the road to Larissa guides you there just as well as knowledge does, and his only answer is roughly that true beliefs are like the statues of Daedalus — fine as long as they stick around, but they run away unless tied down with an account of the reasons. The modern version of the puzzle is the swamping problem for reliabilism, nicely captured by Zagzebski's espresso machine analogy: if all you care about is the good cup of coffee, then once the cup is in hand it doesn't matter whether it came from a reliable machine or a lucky defective one — reliability gets "swamped" by the truth it produced. Restate this whole debate, one position at a time — process reliabilism, virtue/credit accounts, safety-based views, Plato's own tying-down move, whatever else you think belongs — in the vocabulary of machine learning and statistical decision theory: calibration, bias and variance, expected value of information, model uncertainty, regret, that sort of thing.

For each position, I want a genuine translation report: what carries over cleanly, what you have to bend or distort to force it into the framework (flag the distortion explicitly — don't just redefine terms until everything fits), and what simply has no expression in that vocabulary at all. My hunch is that the first-personal dimension of knowing, and maybe the whole notion of credit belonging to the agent rather than the process, will end up in the residue pile, but I could be wrong. Then the part I actually care about: does whatever fails to translate show that the ML framework is too thin to capture what the debate was really about, or does it suggest the epistemologists were chasing something the framework exposes as confused or redundant? I'm drafting a paper on this and my CS friends keep telling me the swamping problem is "just" a point about ex ante versus ex post value, which feels too quick, but I want to stress-test whether they're right before I definately commit to a position in print.
