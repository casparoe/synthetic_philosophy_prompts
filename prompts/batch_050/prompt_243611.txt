You are a designer of educational games with deep expertise in AI governance and in the strategic dynamics of advanced AI development, and you have run classroom simulations many times. I want you to design an in-person activity, and I want you to think carefully and methodically rather than reaching for the first game that comes to mind.

The setting: a graduate seminar on AI policy, 24 students, a single 90-minute session. The goal is for participants to feel, from the inside, the force of two related ideas before either is named: the competitiveness constraint (a developer that spends more on safety than its rivals loses ground and may not survive, even if its safety work is genuinely valuable) and the alignment tax (safety effort costs real resources that would otherwise go toward capability and speed). The crucial thing I want participants to experience viscerally is that a room full of reasonable people, every one of whom would privately prefer a world where everyone develops AI safely, can still race into a corner-cutting equilibrium — and that this outcome is no one's single blameworthy decision. Do not let me lecture beforehand; the concepts should only be named in the debrief.

Hard requirements:

1. A multi-round game for 4–6 teams. Each round, teams allocate a fixed budget of effort between capability work and safety work. Capability determines market share and survival (teams can be eliminated); aggregate corner-cutting raises a shared catastrophe probability that, if it fires, wipes out everyone including the current leader. Give concrete numbers: the budget, the payoff function, the catastrophe schedule, and what a typical playthrough looks like.
2. The alignment tax must be real in the mechanics, not nominal — safety spending must visibly cost teams things they want — and it must be *asymmetric*: at least one team's safety work must be cheaper or more effective than the others', so participants can discover what differential tax does to the race.
3. A mid-game "policy window" in which teams may negotiate a binding safety standard. Specify the monitoring and enforcement mechanism, including imperfect detection of cheating, so that the negotiation round can fail for structural reasons rather than only from stubbornness.
4. A variant in which one team is state-backed and cannot be eliminated. Say what this changes and what new feeling it produces.
5. A payoff scheme without real money, plus a complete materials list, such that a facilitator could run the session from your answer alone.
6. A debrief guide that maps specific moments of the game (the first round a team cuts safety; the failed or succeeded negotiation; the moment a cautious team is eliminated) onto the two concepts, and discussion questions that distinguish this dynamic from a generic one-shot prisoner's dilemma — in particular the difference between continuous erosion and binary defection, and between a shared catastrophe risk and a private cost.
7. A frank list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (for example: teams collude instantly and nothing is felt; the catastrophe never fires and the constraint stays abstract; a team treats elimination as unfair and checks out), and how to handle each.
8. An honest account of what the game cannot reproduce — for instance, that real safety research may be positive-sum across rivals, that reputational and regulatory pressures exist outside the game, and that the size of the actual alignment tax is an empirical question — and what the facilitator must therefore supply in words.

Before committing, briefly sketch three candidate game architectures (one paragraph each), note the failure mode of each, and then give the full design for whichever you judge best. Rules first, then the debrief, then the caveats.
