Consider the following case, which I have designed to press on the standard debate from a slightly unusual angle. A potter takes a quantity of clay and shapes it into a vase at noon on Monday; call the lump of clay Lump and the vase Vase. Vase sits intact on a shelf until noon on Friday, when it is crushed back into a shapeless mass. The very same clay is then stored, and at noon the following Monday it is re-formed into a vase perceptually indistinguishable from the first; call this second vase Vase-2. Nothing else relevant happens: no parts are added or lost, and the potter stipulates no artistic intentions beyond what is stated.

I would like your credences on two propositions, each with its reasoning decomposed rather than defended globally.

First: during the first week, Vase is numerically identical to Lump — there is exactly one object on the shelf, variously describable. Second: Vase-2 is numerically identical to Vase, so that the original vase has an intermittent existence rather than a numerically distinct successor.

For each credence, please decompose the question into the sub-questions your number actually depends on, assigning a rough probability to each and noting how they combine. Candidates include, but are not limited to: whether ordinary artifacts have the sortal-relative persistence conditions our talk attributes to them (a lump survives crushing, a vase arguably does not), and how confident you are that this is a fact about the world rather than about our concepts; whether Leibniz's Law applies unrestrictedly to modal and temporal properties, so that a difference in persistence conditions entails numerical distinctness; how much evidential weight the counting intuition carries — the felt oddity of saying there are two exactly coinciding objects on the shelf; and the probability that some background metaphysics (four-dimensionalism, stage theory, relative identity, or a deflationary pluralism on which the question has no determinate answer) dissolves or relocates the puzzle entirely.

Two further requests. First, identify which sub-question each of your two credences is most sensitive to — the one where a modest revision would move the final number the most. Second, note that the two propositions are often treated as independent, but arguably are not: someone who denies the first on the strength of persistence conditions may face pressure on the second, since Vase-2 shares Vase's shape, matter, and spatiotemporal neighborhood. Tell me whether your own two credences are constrained by each other, and if so, state the constraint and what it would take to break it.
