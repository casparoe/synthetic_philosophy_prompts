i'm building a session on theories of biological function for a phil bio seminar and i need a thought experiment constructed to order. here are the specs.

Build me one scenario in which the etiological (selected-effects) account and the causal-role account (Cummins-style functional analysis) deliver the *same* function ascription for some trait, but the forward-looking propensity account (Bigelow-and-Pargetter flavoured) dissents -- either ascribing a different function to the trait or denying it has one at all. The split has to be generated by the details of the case, not stipulated.

Constraints:
- real biology only. use an actual trait with an actual selection history -- and if the selection history you're relying on is genuinely contested in the literature, flag it and either hedge or switch to a cleaner trait. no swamp creatures, no instant origins, no hypothetical species with magic organs
- the causal-role verdict has to be principled: say explicitly which containing system and which capacity of that system the functional analysis is targeted at, and it has to be a capacity an actual working biologist would want explained. if the agreement with the etiological verdict only goes through on a gerrymandered choice of system, the case fails
- one scenario, short enough to fit on a slide, and the divergence should be visible from the slide without a wall of text

Then the checks:

1. walk through each of the three theories' verdicts and show the reasoning, including what each says about a perfectly intact token of the trait operating in the *changed* circumstances your scenario sets up
2. malfunction test: introduce one damaged or abnormal token of the trait whose damage happens to leave its bearer no worse off, or even better off, in the new circumstances. is it malfunctioning, on each theory? i particularly want to see what the causal-role theorist gets forced to swallow here, since malfunction is supposed to be their weak point, and whether the propensity theorist actually does any better or just trades one awkward verdict for another
3. identify the single structural feature of the case that makes the propensity account peel off. if you remove it (say, restore the ancestral environment), do all three theories collapse back into agreement, or does some residue of the split survive?
4. steelman the propensity theorist's best countermove -- e.g. relativising the fitness-conferring propensity to the trait's natural or ancestral habitat rather than the current one. does the case survive that move intact, does it dissolve, or does it just get relocated?

the pedagogical point is to show students that "what was it selected for?" and "what is it doing now that keeps it around?" can come apart with zero exotic metaphysics -- so if your case secretly depends on reading one of the three accounts uncharitably to get the split, say so at the end rather than letting me find out in front of the class.
