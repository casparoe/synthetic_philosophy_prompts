I teach a second-year undergraduate course in practical rationality, and I want a 75-minute in-class activity for roughly 18 students that makes them feel the force of Parfit's hitchhiker case and the surrounding idea of rational irrationality -- that it can be rational to make oneself the kind of person who will later act against what is then rational, and that transparently rational agents can do worse than agents with credibly non-maximizing dispositions. The students will not have read Parfit; I don't want the case described or named up front. They should discover the structure by playing.

Please design a staged game with the following features, and write it up so I can run it directly:

- A setup that reproduces the hitchhiker's structure without anyone hearing the story first: one party can confer a large benefit at no real cost to themselves, but only if they trust the beneficiary to make a costly payment afterwards, at a point where paying is strictly against the beneficiary's interest. Use points rather than money, with concrete payoff numbers you have checked actually generate the intended incentives (show the arithmetic).
- Some mechanism by which dispositions are at least partly transparent -- track records, declarations, reputation, or roles -- so that students experience both sides: the frustration of the transparent maximizer who is passed over, and the strange advantage enjoyed by the player who is genuinely disposed to pay even when paying is pointless.
- At least one complication introduced mid-game (for instance, a one-time binding commitment device, or a round with anonymity) so that the group can feel the difference between character-based trust and mechanical commitment, and notice which one the original problem is really about.
- A timeline with rough minutes per phase, any materials I'd need, and facilitator moves for keeping the game on track without tipping the lesson.
- A debrief plan that maps specific moments in the game onto the philosophical material: the hitchhiker case itself, the distinction between it being rational to have a disposition and it being rational to exercise it, and the way this pressures purely act-oriented theories of rationality. Only here should Parfit be named.
- A candid note on what will predictably go wrong the first time (students colluding, treating it as an iterated game when it isn't meant to be, reputation effects swamping the intended structure, etc.) and how to handle each.
- An honest account of which feature of the original case your design cannot reproduce in a classroom -- presumably something about the driver's genuinely reliable reading of character, or the one-shot stakes -- and what I must supply verbally to compensate.

Since this is a designed artifact rather than an argument, end with a brief self-assessment: how confident you are that the incentives will produce the intended experience, and the single design element you are least sure of and why.
