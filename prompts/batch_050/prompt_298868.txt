I sit on the academic integrity panel of a small university. Our code requires that closed-book examinations be completed "from the student's own knowledge, without external materials or assistance," and no ruling in our history has ever defined the word "own." Our record so far: (1) a student who concealed formulas inside the label of a water bottle — violation; (2) a student with near-eidetic recall of the assigned readings — no violation; (3) a student who had spent months drilling mnemonic techniques in order to memorize the course material — no violation; (4) a student who used a calculator model the syllabus had expressly authorized — no violation. Nothing in this record has yet forced us to say what makes a resource part of the student rather than an aid to her.

That has now changed. A third-year student with a documented neurological condition has severely impaired episodic memory. In the years since her diagnosis she has kept a single, meticulously organized notebook: it accompanies her everywhere, she consults it fluently and without apparent effort, and she endorses its entries as automatically as anyone endorses a remembered fact. She used it during a closed-book examination in political theory. Her instructor reported her; she responds that the notebook was not external material or assistance but the functional site of her memory, and that barring it amounts to barring her from thinking. The disability office has declined to classify the request, noting that no existing accommodation category obviously covers it.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before we vote. It should recommend a concrete ruling; identify the principles that could each claim to unify our four prior rulings — at minimum, a rule drawing the line at the boundary of the body, a rule keyed to how deeply a resource is integrated into the student's cognitive life, and a rule keyed to whether the resource performs the very competency the examination is meant to test — and explain why our record underdetermines the choice among them; state which principle the panel ought to adopt and apply it to the case at hand, giving an honest account of the strongest argument available to the losing side; and close by describing one hypothetical future case whose resolution would reveal which principle the panel had in fact committed itself to.
