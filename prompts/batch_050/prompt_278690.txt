So I've been teaching a history and philosophy of mathematics seminar for a few years now, and every spring we do Proofs and Refutations and the students love it but I always struggle with the final session. This year I want to close with a counterfactual: Lakatos died in 1974, which is tantalizingly just before the four-color theorem was proved by Appel and Haken in 1976 with that famous computer-assisted case check, and long before things like the Coq formalization of the Feit-Thompson odd order theorem, the Lean/mathlib project, AlphaGeometry, and now LLMs that can spit out plausible-looking proof sketches on demand. I want my students to reconstruct what Lakatos would have made of all this, and I'd like you to give me a worked-out version of the answer first so I can push back on them intelligently.

The specific things I'd like you to take a stand on: (1) Is a machine-checked proof a proof in Lakatos's sense at all? His whole framework treats proofs as thought-experiments embedded in a dialectic of conjecture, counterexample, monster-barring, lemma incorporation, and proof-generated concepts -- so does a Lean formalization count as a participant in that dialectic, or is it a retreat back into the Euclidean/formalist style he spent the book attacking, just with better notation? (2) Where would his machinery actually show up in the practice of formalization? E.g., when a formalization effort forces you to make explicit lemmas that informal proofs gloss over, is that lemma incorporation, or something else he didn't have a category for? (3) There's an old worry (Tymoczko pressed a version of it about the four-color theorem) that unsurveyable computer proofs introduce a new kind of empirical element into mathematics. Lakatos obviously never addressed this. Would he welcome it as confirmation that mathematics is quasi-empirical all the way down, or would he insist the quasi-empirical character he cared about is located in the *conceptual development*, not in the fallibility of the checking? And (4) what would he say about proofs or conjectures generated by a system nobody would describe as understanding them -- can there be a dialectic of proofs and refutations when one side of the dialogue can't, even in principle, respond to a counterexample?

Please argue for definite answers rather than surveying possibilities; I want a position I can defend and then let the students tear apart. If part of the counterfactual strikes you as unanswerable because Lakatos's framework just doesn't have the resources, that's fine, but say *why* it breaks rather than hedging.
