Here is the situation. I am applying for a two-year postdoctoral fellowship and need a research proposal of roughly 1,200 words. My half-formed idea concerns the so-called scale argument for taking insect welfare seriously: roughly, the thought that even if the probability that insects are sentient is low, their numbers are so vast that the expected moral importance of insect suffering may swamp that of all farmed vertebrates combined. My hunch is that the standard way of running this argument contains a hidden structural flaw. The argument is usually presented as a multiplication of three independent quantities: the probability that insects are sentient, the number of insects affected, and the moral weight of an insect's suffering conditional on sentience. I suspect the first and third quantities are not independent. The theories of consciousness that would raise our credence in insect sentience tend to be undemanding theories — ones on which valenced experience is cheap and widespread — and those same theories typically imply that any given insect's experiences are shallow, simple, and low-intensity. Conversely, the demanding theories that would make insect suffering matter enormously per individual are precisely the ones that make insect sentience improbable. If this coupling is real, then multiplying the three quantities as if they were independent systematically distorts the conclusion — though I am genuinely unsure in which direction, and that uncertainty is part of what I want to investigate.
