I'm drafting the "position statement" section of my dissertation prospectus in formal epistemology, and before I circulate it to my committee I want a stress test of the core commitments. My working set is the following five claims:

(1) Rational credences, when precise, obey the probability axioms, and the right justification for this is accuracy-dominance: any non-probabilistic precise credence function is strictly dominated with respect to closeness-to-the-truth by a probabilistic one.

(2) Accuracy is the sole fundamental epistemic good. Every other epistemic norm I accept — conditionalization, reflection-like constraints, norms of evidence-gathering — has to be vindicated in accuracy terms or else demoted to mere pragmatic advice.

(3) Imprecise credences are sometimes rationally permissible, and occasionally rationally required: when the evidence is sparse, ambiguous, or conflicting (as in Ellsberg-style cases, or when one has barely any evidence about a proposition at all), a rational agent may or must suspend judgment across an interval rather than commit to a point value.

(4) Refusing free, decision-relevant evidence is always irrational. If information costs nothing and bears on what I have credences about, I should gather it before updating or acting.

(5) A rational agent should take her own anticipated future irrationality into account: if I know in advance that I will update badly on some piece of evidence — that I'll overreact to it, or misremember it, or draw the wrong conclusion under pressure — it can be rational for me now to decline that evidence, or to adopt a credence or policy I currently judge less accurate, as a hedge against my future self.

Here is what I'd like from you. First, go through the set and identify which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. I care about the discrimination: I don't want a list where everything is flagged as conflicting with everything else, so for each apparent tension that dissolves on inspection, say explicitly why it dissolves. Second, rank the genuine tensions by severity — meaning roughly how much of the surrounding framework has to be rebuilt to resolve each one — rather than treating them as equally bad. Third, for each genuine tension, state the cheapest revision that restores consistency and what that revision costs me: which motivation I have to give up, or which case I can no longer get the verdict I want on. Fourth, if there is a single revision that resolves more than one tension at once, identify it and say what I'd be left with.

Two constraints on the analysis. I have not told you which commitment I'd be willing to drop, and I'd like you to keep it that way: don't assume (3) or (5) is the obvious casualty just because it's the least standard. And please engage the commitments at the level of their justifications, not just their surface formulations — for instance, if two claims are logically consistent but the standard argument for one undercuts the standard motivation for the other, I want that counted as a tension and ranked accordingly. Please write your answer in American English.
