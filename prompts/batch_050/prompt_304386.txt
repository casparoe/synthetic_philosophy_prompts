For the final session of a philosophy-of-science seminar on Goodman's new riddle of induction, I want a poem that does not merely mention the riddle but performs it. Here is the background the poem needs to get right. Define 'grue' as applying to a thing just in case it is examined before some fixed future time t and is green, or is not examined before t and is blue. Every emerald observed to date has been examined before t, so the accumulated evidence supports 'all emeralds are grue' exactly as well as it supports 'all emeralds are green' — yet the two hypotheses contradict each other about any emerald first examined after t. Goodman's further point is that the oddity cannot be diagnosed merely by pointing at the time-indexed definition, because from within a language that takes 'grue' and 'bleen' (blue if examined before t, green otherwise) as its primitives, it is 'green' that ends up with the gerrymandered, disjunctive, time-indexed definition.

Write a poem of sixteen lines in rhymed couplets, with this structure. The first six couplets are dated observation reports, each recording the examination of a particular emerald before time t, and each consistent with both hypotheses. The seventh couplet marks the crossing of t itself — the calendar arriving at the threshold. The eighth and final couplet must be given twice: once as the continuation licensed by the 'green' induction, and once as the continuation licensed by the 'grue' induction. The two endings must genuinely diverge in what they claim about the next emerald while being equally licensed by everything in the preceding fourteen lines, and the poem should print them side by side and refuse to choose, so the reader experiences the underdetermination rather than being told about it.

One further constraint on the second ending: voice it in the mouth of a speaker for whom 'grue' and 'bleen' are the natural, unremarkable color words, so that it is our induction — the emerald will be green — that comes out sounding bizarre and disjunctive in their idiom. Goodman's symmetry between the two languages should be enacted by the poem's voices, never explained in prose.

The couplets should scan loosely iambic, and the observation reports should accumulate concrete, mundane detail (settings, instruments, handlers, weather) so that the evidence genuinely feels like evidence. After the poem, add one last line beginning 'Summary:' that states in a single sentence which feature of the new riddle the poem's structure enacts.
