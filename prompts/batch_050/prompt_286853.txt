A colleague who teaches bioethics summarized the procreation asymmetry for a class handout as follows: "The procreation asymmetry is the view, associated with anti-natalism, that bringing a miserable child into existence is wrong, while bringing a happy child into existence is not good -- or at least not something anyone is obligated to do. Its defenders argue for this by pointing out that life contains more suffering than pleasure, and that even ordinary lives involve pain, frustration, and eventual death, so that coming into existence is on balance a harm. From this they conclude that procreation is always morally wrong, and that anyone who accepts the asymmetry but has children is simply being inconsistent." I have the sense that this runs together several claims that the literature keeps apart, and that the slide from an axiological claim about the value of creating lives to an empirical claim about how actual lives go, and then to a blanket verdict on procreation, involves some shortcuts. Which parts of this summary would a careful philosopher working on population ethics accept as accurate, which parts conflate distinct positions (for instance, a structural asymmetry in the value of creating lives versus a judgment that existence is always a net harm, and either of these versus a moral prohibition on procreation), and does the final accusation of inconsistency follow once those distinctions are restored?
