So I run the adult ed night class at our church (long story, it's basically a philosophy seminar with worse coffee), about 14 regulars, mix of college kids and retirees, and we just spent three weeks on Fear and Trembling. It came up for me because earlier this year I had to make a call -- pulling my daughter out of her school mid-year and moving us two states away for reasons I genuinely cannot explain to anyone, not my wife's family, not my closest frends, without sounding either crazy or like I'm hiding something worse. Everyone wanted the reasons, and there was nothing I could say that would add up for them. Someone at group said "that's very Abraham of you" as a joke and it has been eating at me ever since.

Here's the passage that got me, from the Hollander translation on wikisource. It's not one of the famous bits, it's Johannes de silentio talking about the man obsessed with the story:

"His wish was, to have been with Abraham on the three days' journey, when he rode with sorrow before him and with Isaac at his side."

And then in the retelling: "Silently they rode for three days."

Three days. That's the part nobody stages. Everybody talks about the knife moment on Moriah, but the knife moment is a second. The three days are walking next to your kid, making small talk or not making it, knowing what you know, and being completely unable to say it, because the second you try to justify it you've either lied or become a murderer explaining himself. That's what I want the group to actually feel next week, because right now they're treating the teleological suspension of the ethical like a vocab term.

So: design me a 90-minute activity (we have the church basement, tables, index cards, nothing fancy) that puts them inside those three days. I don't want a roleplay of the akedah itself, that turns into bad theater in thirty seconds. I want something where participants have to actually make and then *carry* a decision they cannot defend in the group's shared moral language -- where the structure of the situation does the work, not me lecturing. Some things I think I want, push back where I'm wrong:

- real small stakes of some kind (snacks, points, who has to clean up, whatever) so refusing or complying actually costs something
- a rule that blocks the normal escape hatch of "let me just explain my reasons to everyone"
- at least one moment where a participant looks like a monster to the rest of the room and we all sit in that
- they should NOT know this is about Kierkegaard until the debrief. No lecture up front.

Then give me the debrief: map what happened onto the ethical as the universal, the single individual, why Abraham *can't* speak (not just won't), and what the teleological suspension would have to be for the passage's wish -- wanting to be there on the road -- to make any sense. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (14 people, half of whom have known each other for 20 years, is a weird dynamic for this), and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce -- my guess is it can fake the silence but not the part where Abraham is actually *right*, the faith part, and I don't know how to handle that gap in the debrief without either cheapening it or making it sound like I'm endorsing anyone who claims God told them something.
