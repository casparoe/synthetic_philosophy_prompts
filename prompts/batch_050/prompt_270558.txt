The topic is idealized-preference accounts of value — the family that runs from classic "what you'd want if fully informed and rational" theories through to coherent extrapolated volition for AI alignment. My hunch: all of these treat idealization as basically a function. Feed in my actual messy preferences, crank up information, reflection time, coherence, whatever, and out pops a determinate improved set of preferences. But I think there's a problem nobody takes seriously enough, which I want to call the menu problem: there are many defensible idealization procedues (in what order do I get the information? do my emotional dispositions get amplified, preserved, or flattened? is the extrapolation allowed to carry me across changes I'd currently regard as identity-breaking?), they plausibly converge on different outputs, and choosing between them seems to require exactly the kind of substantive normative judgment the procedure was supposed to produce for us. So the research question is roughly: is underdetermination among idealization procedures a fatal objection to these accounts, an engineering problem to be solved by aggregation (take the intersection of outputs, weight procedures somehow, just pick one and be honest about it), or not a problem at all because value was never that determinate in the first place?

Write the proposal for me: state the question precisely enough that three months could actually yield an answer, explain why the standard defenses of idealization (it's just an extension of ordinary deliberation, approximate convergence is good enough, etc.) don't already settle it, lay out what I'd concretely do month by month, and say what a result would look like — including what would count as my hunch failing. Include one short paragraph on why someone designing AI systems around extrapolated-volition-style ideas should care about the answer. Don't bother with citations, I'll add those myself. Keep the whole thing under 700 words.
