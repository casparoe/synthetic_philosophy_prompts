I'm a philosophy professor putting together a grad seminar on probability and induction, and I keep circling around an argument a former colleague of mine used to wheel out whenever the reference class problem came up. He'd present it as a kind of reductio, but honestly it always felt to me like it was proving too much, and I could never put my finger on exactly where. Here's my reconstruction, with some extra steps he never said out loud but clearly needed:

(1) A single-case probability claim (this patient has a 30% chance of surviving five years, this coin has a 1/2 chance of landing heads) is justified only if we can identify the *correct* reference class for the case and read the probability off the frequency in that class.
(2) Every individual case belongs to indefinitely many reference classes, and these classes have wildly different frequencies. The patient is a 60-year-old, a 60-year-old smoker, a 60-year-old smoker with this tumor type, a 60-year-old smoker with this tumor type born on a Tuesday, and so on down to classes containing only him.
(3) There is no objective, non-circular rule for selecting the right class: "the narrowest class" is a singleton with frequency 0 or 1, "the narrowest class with reliable statistics" already requires a judgment about which statistics are reliable, and any rule we write down seems to smuggle in the very judgment it was supposed to replace.
(4) Therefore no single-case probability claim is ever justified over its rivals; at best we have many rival assignments and no way to adjudicate.
(5) But medicine, insurance underwriting, forensic science, and half of public policy essentially traffic in single-case probability claims.
(6) So all of these practices rest on arbitrary choices, and their pronouncements have no more rational standing than astrology.
(7) That's absurd, so instead my colleague concluded probability just *is* subjective -- degrees of belief all the way down -- and the frequentist's dream of objective single-case chances is dead.

Now, (7) strikes me as a wild overreaction, and I suspect something slipped between (3) and (4) -- the move from "no unique rule" to "no justified choice" feels fast -- but I've also had smart students defend (1) as the real culprit, and I worry that giving up (1) costs more than people admit, because if you don't tie single-case probabilities to frequencies *somehow*, what are we even talking about when we say the coin is fair?

Tell me which premise or step you reject, why it looked plausible in the first place, and what rejecting it actually costs. I'd also like your sense of whether my colleague's inference to full-blown subjectivism in (7) follows even if everything up to (6) is granted, because I suspect there's a second sleight of hand hiding there. Feel free to mention the standard figures in this literature (Reichenbach, Salmon, Hajek, etc.) if their positions help you price the alternatives, but I'm less interested in who said what than in which premise is cheapest to give up. Defend your answer the way you'd defend it in a seminar where someone keeps pushing back.
