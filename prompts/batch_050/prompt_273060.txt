Having retired last year after four decades as an experimental chemist, I have spent my newfound leisure reading philosophy, and I find myself in the odd position of needing it professionally. I sit on the board of a regional arts fund that distributes roughly £400,000 a year in small grants to artists. Applications are currently assessed by a panel of five expert judges — critics, curators, and practising artists — who score each submission on originality, technical command, and coherence of vision.

The system is now in crisis, for three reasons. First, our administrator reviewed the last three funding rounds and found that the judges' scores correlate strongly for craft-centred categories (printmaking, figurative painting, classical composition) but correlate almost not at all for experimental categories (sound installation, conceptual and performance work): in the latter, one judge's top choice is routinely another's bottom quartile. Secondly, a rejected applicant has published a widely shared letter arguing that the panel simply rewards conformity to the tastes of a self-perpetuating circle, and that its air of objectivity is a disguise. Thirdly, this has split the board. One faction proposes abandoning expert judgement altogether: screen applications against a minimal competence threshold, then allocate grants by lottery, since — they say — the disagreement data shows that beyond basic competence there is no fact of the matter about artistic merit, only taste, and taste is subjective. The opposing faction replies that disagreement among our particular judges shows only that we have chosen them badly, not that no standard exists. In my reading I have encountered Hume's essay Of the Standard of Taste, which seems to support the second faction: he argues that although beauty is not a property of objects in the way mass or charge is, the verdicts of judges who possess delicacy of discrimination, extensive practice, wide comparison, and freedom from prejudice carry genuine authority, and that time vindicates them. The lottery faction counters that Hume's criteria merely describe the tastes of a particular educated class dressed up as neutral expertise, and that our own data — convergence on the traditional, divergence on the new — is exactly what one would expect if "trained judgement" were trained conformity.

The board meets in six weeks and must settle on one of four options: (A) retain the expert panel unchanged; (B) adopt the threshold-plus-lottery system; (C) retain expert panels but reform them — blind review, explicit selection of judges against Humean criteria, rotating membership, and mixed panels including lay members; (D) replace the panel with a public vote among the region's residents. I have been asked to draft the decision memo. Please write it: a clear recommendation of one option, the reasoning behind it, an honest and strong statement of the best argument for the option you reject most reluctantly, and practical criteria the board can actually apply when implementing your recommendation (for instance, what would count as evidence that a reformed panel was tracking something more than shared background). Bear in mind that the memo will be read by people on both factions and must persuade rather than merely assert.

Please conclude with a final line in exactly the form \answer{X}, where X is the single letter of the option the memo recommends.
