I've been thinking about Aumann's agreement theorem and the broader claim that honest, rational people with the same information cannot persistently disagree -- it came up in an argument with a friend who runs a forecasting group, where I found myself insisting that some disagreements are perfectly healthy while conceding that others seem like they shouldn't be able to survive open discussion. I've tried to get clear on my own intuitions by collecting verdicts on cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, unifies them. Here are my verdicts. First: two meteorologists with identical training, given identical satellite data, who announce different probabilities of rain tomorrow -- I think at least one of them has made a mistake, full stop; if they talked it through and still disagreed, something has gone wrong. Second: two brilliant, honest economists who have argued with each other for twenty years, fully understand each other's models and evidence, and still disagree about whether a minimum-wage hike reduces employment -- I find myself thinking this disagreement can rationally persist indefinitely, even though it looks structurally just like the meteorologist case. Third: two chess engines evaluating the same position, one calling it clearly winning for white and the other calling it a draw -- neither seems irrational to me; one is simply wrong, and the matter gets settled by playing it out. Fourth: a doctor who tells a patient a treatment succeeds 70% of the time, and a patient who doubts it because a friend's cousin had a bad outcome -- the patient's disagreement seems rational given what she has to go on, even though she's wrong. Fifth: two traders with access to the same public information who keep taking opposite sides of the same bet, week after week, each knowing the other is doing so -- I have the strong sense they can't both be rational, that the other one's continued willingness to bet is itself evidence that should move each of them. Sixth: two divorcing spouses, each sincerely convinced they did the majority of the housework -- I think both are being honest, but I don't want to call both fully rational either, since self-serving bias seems to be doing the work, and I'm unsure whether that means the case falls outside the scope of whatever principle governs the others.

What I'm after is a precisely stated principle -- or a small set of candidate principles -- that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, saying exactly when persistent disagreement is rationally permissible and when it isn't, and flagging the role played by shared evidence, differing starting points, common knowledge of each other's positions, and the parties' confidence in each other's reasoning. If no single principle accommodates all six verdicts, tell me which one has to go and why; my suspicion is that the economist case and the meteorologist case cannot both stand as I've judged them, but I'd like to see that demonstrated rather than assumed. Please show which clause of your principle each case motivates, and then apply the finished principle to two cases I haven't listed: two people who can only exchange their numerical credences back and forth, with no opportunity to share their underlying evidence, and two people disagreeing about a matter of taste -- say, whether a film they both just watched and discussed at length is any good.
