I teach a one-semester decision theory module to master's students in public policy, most of whom are comfortable with elementary probability and expected utility but have no formal background in statistics beyond that. Next week I have ninety minutes, a whiteboard, and one aim: to teach Good's theorem on the expected value of information — the result that, for a Bayesian expected-utility maximiser facing a single-person decision, the ex ante expected value of costless information is never negative, since one could always ignore it. I want a plan for the session, not the lecture itself.

Set out the order in which the ideas must be introduced and defend that order by what depends on what: in particular, where the distinction between the ex ante perspective (averaging over messages one might receive) and the ex post perspective (what one learns after a particular message) has to be established, and whether the theorem's conclusion should be stated before or after the first worked case. Specify the single numerical example that should carry the whole session — I had thought of a diagnostic test informing a treatment decision — and say exactly what its numbers must make true and must avoid (it must be possible to compute the value of the test in a few lines, and it must not be a case where the test is worthless).

Then give me the misconception map: the predictable errors this audience brings or forms mid-session — that the theorem shows more information is always better for anyone in any setting (it says nothing about non-Bayesian updaters, costly studies, or strategic situations), that a favourable test result guarantees a better outcome in hindsight, that the theorem licenses funding any proposed research because evidence can never hurt, and that the value of information is positive whenever there is any chance it would change the decision — and indicate at what point in the sequence each should be headed off rather than corrected afterwards. Propose a check for understanding that would show the theorem has genuinely landed (my instinct is to ask them to construct a case where receiving free information leaves someone worse off and to diagnose which premise failed). Finally, say what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — Blackwell's more general ordering, value-destroying information in games, or whatever you judge — and how to flag the omission without inviting confusion.
