I'm prepping a unit for my philosophy of mind seminar on machine sentience, and one of my sharper undergrads cornered me after class with an argument she thinks shows that all the recent hand-wringing about whether RL agents can suffer is confused. I've been turning it over for a week and I can't locate the misstep, but the conclusion seems to prove way too much. Here's her argument, reconstructed as best I can:

(1) A reward signal in an RL system is just a scalar value returned by the environment -- a number in a log file, basically. (2) A number is not intrinsically good or bad; nothing about the quantity -1.7 is unpleasant. (3) Valence, if it's real, has to come from how the signal is taken up -- from some further state that registers it as bad, to-be-avoided-in-itself, not merely as an input that shifts future behavior. (4) But an RL agent doesn't take up the reward in any further way; the reward just is the thing that shifts the policy. There's no extra step where the agent feels the update as bad -- the update is the whole story. (5) So nothing in an RL agent has negative valence, and so RL agents can't suffer. (6) The same goes for biological reinforcement learning: dopamine is just a chemical, a prediction error is just a pattern of spike rates, and a spike rate is no more intrinsically unpleasant than a scalar in a log file. Whatever uptake story you tell for the agent -- that there's some further registration of the signal as bad -- applies or fails to apply equally to neurons. (7) So if RL agents can't suffer for reason (4), neither can we. (8) Since we obviously can suffer, suffering requires something beyond any physically describable signal-and-update process -- some extra ingredient that registers the badness. (9) But positing an unexplained extra ingredient whose only job is to be the thing that hurts is just dualism with the name filed off.

So she concludes that either nothing suffers, or suffering is forever outside physical explanation. Her own preferred move is to bite (8)'s dualism bullet, which is at least honest, but I'd rather not teach my students that. If I had to keep one thing, I'd keep (2) -- it really does seem right that the bare number isn't what's bad. My suspicion is that something goes wrong between (3) and (5), or that (6) trades on a trick about levels of description, but every time I try to say exactly which step fails I end up just asserting that brains are different, which she rightly calls special pleading.

Which step do you reject? For the step you reject, explain why it looked plausible -- why someone smart would put it in -- and then tell me what rejecting it costs. In particular: if you think the equivocation is in (6), I want to hear why the disanalogy you cite between a scalar in a log and a dopamine signal isn't itself just the claim under dispute restated. And if you think the argument is actually valid, tell me which premise you'd give up instead and what a theory of valence has to look like to survive losing it.
