I work as a software engineer and have been reading philosophy of mind on my own for about a year. I've written up my current position below — partly to clarify it for myself, partly because I want it stress-tested before I inflict it on my reading group. What I want is an audit: which of my commitments are actually in tension with each other, which only look like they are, how severe each tension is, and what the cheapest fix would be in each case. If one revision would clean up more than the others, identify it. Please rank rather than treating everything as equally problematic.

Here is where I've landed:

1. Causal closure of the physical. Every physical event that has a cause at all has a fully sufficient physical cause. Nothing non-physical ever pushes a neuron.

2. Anti-illusionism about experience. Conscious experience is real and not reducible to anything functional or physical. No description of information processing, however complete, captures what it's like to see red or to have a headache. I'm not denying experience exists; I think the "it's all just brain states" move leaves out the one thing that was supposed to be explained.

3. Epiphenomenalism. Experiences are generated by brain processes but have no downstream causal effects of their own. My best analogy: a read-only monitoring dashboard attached to a production system — it faithfully displays the system's state but nothing in the system depends on what the dashboard shows. A functional duplicate of me with no experience at all would behave identically, including everything it says about its inner life.

4. Introspective authority. I know what my current experience is like, and my introspective judgments are generally reliable — that the red of a tomato looks closer to orange than to purple, that my headache is throbbing rather than stabbing. This is the one place where I take myself to have something close to direct, non-inferential knowledge.

5. A sensitivity condition on knowledge. A belief counts as knowledge only if it's formed in a way that's appropriately responsive to the truth of what's believed — roughly, if things had been otherwise, the belief would have been otherwise too. Beliefs that would be the same whether or not they're true are lucky guesses, not knowledge. I hold this across the board; I don't want a patchwork epistemology with ad hoc exceptions.

6. A judgment about AI. When a large language model produces fluent first-person reports of experience, those reports are no evidence that it has any experience — the reports come from the machinery, not from any inner life, and would be produced regardless. I take this to follow straightforwardly from (3).

A few ground rules for the audit. First, don't just tell me epiphenomenalism is unpopular or out of fashion — I want to know whether my specific package is internally coherent, not whether it's mainstream. Second, if you think two commitments are in tension, state exactly what the argument from one to the negation of the other would be, not just that they sit uneasily together. Third, please separate what is genuinely settled in this area from what is still contested among people who work on it: if some tension has a well-known resolution that most specialists accept, say so, and if the "fix" I'm likely to reach for is itself a minority or contested position, flag that rather than presenting it as the consensus solution. I have no formal philosophy training, so if I'm using a term in a non-standard way or missing a distinction everyone in the field takes for granted, I'd rather be corrected than accommodated.
