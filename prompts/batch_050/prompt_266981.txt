So my partner and I have been going back and forth for months about whether to have a kid, and somewhere along the way I fell down the rabbit hole of L.A. Paul's work on transformative experience, which felt uncomfortably on the nose. I now have to give a short presentation in my practical rationality seminar laying out my own position, and before I get up there I want someone to stress-test whether my commitments actually cohere, because I've assembled them piecemeal from different places and I have a sneaking suspicion two of them are pulling against each other. Don't tell me whether to have a kid -- just audit the view.

Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Paul's diagnosis is basically right for the kid case. Having your first child is both epistemically and personally transformative: I can't imagine my way to what it's like from the inside, and the experience may rewrite my core preferences, so the standard expected-utility calculation can't even get off the ground because I don't know what utilities to assign the outcomes.

(2) Still, the decision can be rational rather than a coin flip. The way to make it rational, I think, is Paul's own move: you decide on the basis of whether you value the revelation itself -- whether discovering that kind of life first-hand is something you want to opt into.

(3) None of this touches ordinary big decisions. Switching careers, moving to another country, going back to school -- those are decididable the normal way, by weighing pros and cons and projecting yourself into the outcomes. Paul's problem is specific to cases like the kid one, not a general collapse of cost-benefit reasoning about your own future.

(4) Endorsements from people who already have kids are nearly worthless as evidence for me. Their values were rewritten by the very transformation in question, so when a friend says they can't imagine life without their daughter, that tells me about their current evaluative standpoint, not about whether pre-kid me would be glad I did it.

(5) That said, one consideration that genuinely pulls me toward doing it: I have never met a parent who regrets having kids, and I strongly expect future-me wouldn't regret it either.

(6) I should add that I'm not exactly going in blind. I've spent years around my sister's toddlers -- the sleep deprivation, the tantrums, the logistics, the weird tenderness of it -- so I have a decent working knowledge of what having a small child involves.

Which pairs of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and how bad is each one? For the real ones, tell me what I'd have to give up to fix it and how much that costs, and rank them by severity. If one of them dissolves once you're careful about a distinction, say so rather than inflating my crisis count -- I'd rather find out my view is boring than incoherent.
