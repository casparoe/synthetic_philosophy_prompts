In my political philosophy elective we recently read parts of Locke's Second Treatise, including the idea that you come to own unowned things by mixing your labour with them, subject to leaving enough and as good for others. Around the same time I started paying attention to the unwritten rules at my own school, and now I can't stop seeing Locke everywhere, which is honestly annoying. Our school library has a strict first-come, first-served norm for the good desks by the windows: whoever arrives first gets the desk, and dropping your backpack on the chair counts as claiming it, even if you then leave for an hour to have lunch. Meanwhile the gym works completely differently -- you cannot reserve equipment at all, and leaving your towel on a bench while you go to the water fountain is considered rude and the towel gets moved. A friend of mine also told me that at the hotel where her family vacations, guests run down at six in the morning to lay towels on the pool loungers and then go back to sleep, and the hotel tacitly enforces the resulting claims all day.

So here is what I would like you to do: reconstruct what the library's first-come, first-served practice presupposes about acquisition and ownership. Does it really presuppose a labour-mixing view, or would several rival theories of property license the same rule, in which case the practice is underdetermined? What has to be true for a backpack left on a chair to count as "occupying" the desk rather than merely littering it, and does the lounger case show that our intuitions about this break down somewhere? Does the practice quietly commit to something like Locke's proviso, and if so, what happens to that commitment on exam weeks when the library is full and latecomers get nothing -- is the rule then self-undermining, or does it only ever have applied to a world of abundance? Finally, why do you think the gym and the library can coexist under one institution if they presuppose conflicting theories: does one of them presuppose nothing at all and rest on pure convention, and what would concretely have to change in the library if we replaced the labour-based reading with, say, a view on which resources belong to whoever needs them most at each moment?

Please answer in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables. I would rather read one well-developed line of reasoning than a list of fragments.
