I cover science and policy for a magazine and I'm trying to write a section that treats the spring 2020 school closure fights as a live philosophical case, not just a policy story. Facts I'll stipulate so we're on the same page: officials had to weigh lives (mostly of older, vulnerable people) against kids' education, social development, and mental health; the evidence on both sides was shaky at the time; and nobody could honestly convert one side into units of the other.

Analyze this through the literature on incommensurable values and hard choices — Raz-style incomparability, Chang's idea of parity and the small-improvement argument, whatever else fits. I want it run in both directions. First: on each view, what was the officials' actual situation — mere ignorance of facts that would settle it, genuine incomparability, or options "on a par"? And what, if anything, does the theory say they should have done? Does the small-improvement argument actually get traction here (an extra two weeks of closure as a sweetener to one side), or does it misfire when one side of the ledger is deaths? Second: what does the episode say back at the theory? Governments mostly tried to force everything onto one scale anyway (QALY-style estimates, cost-benefit talk), then made gut calls at the end. Is that a confirmation that the choices were hard in the philosophers' sense, evidence that incommensurability collapses into commensuration under institutional pressure, or something else?

Where the theory just doesn't speak to some feature of the case, say so rather than stretching it. And tell me what would change your verdict — what fact about how these decisions actually went down, or what argument, would push you to a different reading.
