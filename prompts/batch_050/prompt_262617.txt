Here's something I've been chewing on for a philosophy reading group I'm in. The two big strategies for answering the problem of evil — the free will defense (evil is the price God pays for significant human freedom) and the soul-making theodicy (suffering is justified because it's needed for moral and spiritual growth) — usually get discussed as if they cover the same territory. I suspect they actually come apart, and I want a test case for it.

Construct a single realistic scenario of suffering where the two strategies deliver opposite verdicts: a Plantinga-style free will defense counts the evil as justified (or at least as the kind of thing God couldn't prevent without forfeiting genuinely free human choice), while a Hick-style soul-making theodicy counts it as gratuitous — meaning it doesn't build anyone's soul, so on that view God should have prevented it.

Some constraints so nobody takes the easy route:

- Keep the scenario to one paragraph, ordinary life, no natural disasters or freak illnesses — the suffering has to trace back to human choices, otherwise the free will verdict gets murky.
- The victim must be a competent adult. No infants, no animals, that's too easy.
- No afterlife patching. Assess the soul-making verdict on what happens in this life; don't let "it all gets sorted out postmortem" do the work.
- Don't stipulate the verdicts inside the story (no narrator hinting that God approved or disapproved). Just describe the events and let the verdicts fall out of the two theories.

After the scenario, do the bookkeeping:

1. Walk through why the free will defense covers this case.
2. Walk through why soul-making classifies it as gratuitous — be explicit about what soul-making actually requires (real opportunities for growth, a victim still in a position to grow, etc.) and which requirement your case violates.
3. Name the single feature of the case that makes the two theories diverge, and say what happens if you remove it — does the case collapse back into a standard moral-evil example both theories handle identically?
4. The obvious counterattack from the soul-making side is second-order growth: the victim's suffering builds the souls of people around them (caregivers, witnesses, maybe even the perpetrator's conscience later on). Either close that loophole inside the scenario itself or explain why the reply fails on soul-making's own terms, e.g. because it treats the victim purely as a means.

Please write your answer in American English.
