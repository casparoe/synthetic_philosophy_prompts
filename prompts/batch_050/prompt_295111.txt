A friend and I spent last night arguing about a policy at the community hardship fund where I volunteer, and we reached no resolution. The fund makes small grants to local residents facing financial emergencies. Two recent applications drove the dispute. The first comes from a man who cancelled his flood insurance to free up money for a business venture; the venture failed, and a flood then destroyed his uninsured home. The second comes from a woman whose home was destroyed in the same flood; she had wanted insurance but could never have afforded the premiums on her income. My friend insists that need is need — both applicants lost everything, and interrogating people about their past choices is intrusive and degrading. I concede that the interrogation is ugly, yet I cannot shake the thought that the fund's scarce money should go first to those whose misfortune was no fault of their own. A third application has since unsettled me further: a man who smoked for forty years and can no longer work because of lung disease. By my own principle, his misfortune traces to his choices — yet denying him feels monstrous.

Please tell me who is right. Set out the strongest version of each position, diagnose where my instinct goes wrong if it does, and say what policy the fund should actually adopt in light of the fact that people's choices are themselves shaped by their circumstances. Conclude with a table classifying all three applicants plus a fourth hypothetical (an applicant who held insurance, but whose insurer went bankrupt before paying out), with these columns: Applicant; Choice, circumstance, or mixed; Should the fund weigh this in its decision, and how; Strongest objection to your verdict.
