Philosophy prof here — though I should say up front my area is early modern, and my knowledge of social epistemology basically begins and ends with having read Fricker's book a few years back. So forgive me if this is well-trodden ground.

Here's a pattern I've noticed for years in faculty meetings, and I keep seeing it in seminars too, and I don't know whether it has a name. A junior colleague — fairly often a woman, though not always — makes a suggestion or raises an objection. It isn't rejected. Nobody argues against it, nobody says it's wrong. The conversation just flows around it, as if she'd said something politely neutral. Then twenty minutes later a senior colleague makes essentially the same point, sometimes in nearly the same words, and now it's a good point. People engage, it goes in the minutes, and by the next meeting it's "David's proposal." The part that gets me: the first speaker was never disbelieved. If you'd polled the room right after she spoke — is that true? — everyone would have said sure, yes, probably. The utterance failed not by being doubted but by not registering as a move in the conversation at all, and then it registers once re-voiced by the right person, with the credit quietly attaching to the re-voicer.

Same thing in my grad seminar last term: a quiet student offered a reading of the text, it landed with a thud, and fifteen minutes later the star student "rephrased" it to general acclaim.

So: is there an established concept for this exact configuration? It doesn't seem to be Fricker's testimonial injustice as I understand it, because there's no deflated credibility judgement — nobody thinks she's unreliable, and indeed the very same content is accepted later. It's not hermeneutical injustice either; no concept is missing, everyone in the room fully understands the point when David makes it. And it's not what I've seen called epistemic exploitation, since nobody is demanding explanatory labour from her.

If there's no name, coin one and define it — but I don't just want a label, I want the carving. In particular: does the wrong require the credit-transfer, or is the initial non-uptake already the thing going wrong? Whose failure is it — the audience's, the re-voicer's (who may honestly have forgotten where he first heard the point), or nobody's in particular, just the structure of the room? And what distinguishes it from the innocent cases: where the second speaker genuinely improved the idea, or where a point simply lands badly the first time through no one's fault? That last boundary is the one I really care about, since without it the concept risks covering every half-heard remark ever made.
