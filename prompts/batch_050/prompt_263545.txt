Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend from my master's program about Capgras delusion, and we talked ourselves into a corner I now can't get out of. She took the common-sense line that delusions are obviously beliefs -- patients say their spouse has been replaced by an impostor, they seem sincere, some even act on it -- and she thinks the philosophical worry about whether delusions "really" qualify as beliefs is just definitional squeamishness. My reply was that the leading empirical account of how delusions form, the two-factor theory, seems to sit badly with her confidence. On that account, a delusion requires both an anomalous experience or neuropsychological deficit (factor one, which explains the content) and a failure of normal belief evaluation and revision (factor two, which explains why the implausible hypothesis is adopted and retained). But if the second factor is precisely a breakdown in the mechanisms that make something a belief rather than a passing thought, it is at least not obvious that the resulting state is a belief in the full sense -- and conversely, if it turns out that delusions are best understood as some other kind of attitude (an imagining treated as real, say), then maybe the "second factor" isn't a reasoning deficit at all but something else. Neither of us could find a knock-down move, and it struck me afterward that the two literatures -- the two-factor theory in cognitive neuropsychiatry and the doxasticism debate in philosophy -- seem to proceed almost independently of each other, even though each constrains the other.

I'd like to turn this into a one-year master's thesis proposal, which I need to submit to my department's funding committee in about a month. Please write it as a proposal of roughly two pages. State the central question precisely -- something like: does the two-factor theory's account of how delusions are maintained presuppose, support, or undermine the claim that delusions are beliefs, and what would the theory have to look like if they are not? Explain why the existing positions leave this open: the two-factor theory was developed to explain monothematic delusions and is largely silent on the attitude question, while the doxasticism debate tends to argue from surface features of delusions (conviction, behavioral inertness, circumscription, resistance to counterevidence) without engaging the etiological model. Lay out the method concretely -- I envision a conceptual analysis of what the "second factor" is actually claimed to be, followed by case-by-case examination of a few well-studied monothematic delusions (Capgras, Cotard, mirrored-self misidentification) asking what each side of the attitude debate would predict about them -- and give a rough month-by-month breakdown. Say what a successful outcome looks like (at minimum, a map of which combinations of two-factor variants and attitude theories are coherent, and ideally a defense of one combination) and what would count as the project failing. Also include an honest paragraph on scope: what I should explicitly set aside (e.g., schizophrenia, delusion-like phenomena in hypnosis and healthy subjects, clinical implications) to keep this feasible in a year.
