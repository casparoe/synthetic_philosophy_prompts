Some years ago I came across a description of an experiment that has bothered me ever since: a red dot is flashed briefly at one point on a screen, then a green dot is flashed at a point some distance away, and observers report seeing a single dot move across the screen and change color partway along its path. The puzzle, as I understand it, is that the observer seems to experience the color change before the green dot has appeared -- yet the experience of the change could not have contained the color green unless the brain already "knew" what was coming. I have no formal training in philosophy or psychology, but I have thought about this on my own and read what I could find in books aimed at general readers.

I have settled, provisionally, on the view that there is simply no fact of the matter about which of two rival stories is correct. The first story says the experience itself is staged after the fact: the brain holds everything back, assembles a finished film that already includes the mid-path color change, and only then does consciousness "roll." The second story says the experience happens promptly -- the dot is experienced as red all the way to the far position -- but the record of what was experienced is instantly rewritten, so that by the time the observer remembers or reports anything, the memory is of a mid-path change even though the original experience contained none. My position is that the difference between these two stories is not a difference any evidence could ever detect, and so there is nothing for one of them to be right about. Consciousness is not a place where content arrives at a determinate moment; it is more like a set of competing drafts, and asking when the "final version" played is asking a question with no answer.

What would count against this position? I want you to take it seriously as a target rather than as the settled wisdom I have taken it to be. Specifically:

First, suppose neuroscience one day identifies a definite mechanism -- a threshold of global broadcast, say, or a particular recurrent loop -- such that content becomes conscious exactly when it crosses that threshold. Would that settle the question between the two stories, or only push it elsewhere? I want to know whether my position depends on the brain turning out to be messy in a particular way, and if so, how exposed that makes it.

Second, are there possible experiments, even only in principle, where the staged-in-advance story and the rewritten-record story would make different predictions -- about what a subject can act on, respond to, or be primed by before they can report? If the two stories can be pried apart by any probe whatsoever, does my position collapse, or can it absorb that?

Third, what does the position do with first-person authority? It seems committed to telling subjects that there is no truth about whether they actually saw the change or only misremember it. Is that commitment a defeater in itself, or a consequence to be accepted? Would a committed defender of my view concede that this costs the view something?

Finally, which objections would the defenders of this position themselves treat as dangerous, as opposed to the ones they have standard replies to? I would like the defeaters arranged from fatal down to merely awkward, and an honest verdict on whether my position is well insulated -- and if it is, whether that is because it is true or because it has been defined so that nothing can touch it.
