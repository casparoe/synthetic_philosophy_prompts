Two colleagues of mine, a logician and a philosopher of mind, argued over dinner about whether Gödel-style arguments in the Lucas–Penrose family show that human mathematical reasoning outruns any Turing machine, and they asked me to adjudicate. (1) The logician opened with the standard move: for any consistent formal system F, its Gödel sentence G(F) is true but unprovable in F, yet we can see that G(F) is true; so no such F captures our own reasoning. (2) The philosopher replied that the step from "G(F) is unprovable in F" to "we see that G(F) is true" requires assuming F's consistency, since G(F) is provable once you add Con(F) as an axiom; and by the second incompleteness theorem, F itself cannot establish that assumption — so a machine handed the same consistency assumption gets the same result, and humans have no privileged access here. (3) The logician answered that we can recognize the consistency of the systems we actually use, such as Peano arithmetic, by grasping their intended interpretation — a semantic insight, not a formal derivation — whereas a machine's "assumption" is merely an appended axiom without that understanding. (4) The philosopher countered that this equivocates on the idealization involved: an idealized reasoner who never errs and has unbounded memory and time is itself a kind of machine, while actual humans are fallible, so the machine that would truly simulate us may be an inconsistent one — for which the Gödel asymmetry simply does not arise. He added that a machine can in any case iterate Gödelization through transfinite progressions, matching us step for step. (5) The logician's last word: at every stage of any such progression there is a further Gödel sentence, and we can always take one more step, so no single machine exhausts what we can in principle come to see. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — which steps are sound, which conceal an illicit assumption, and which side's central commitment (semantic grasp of consistency versus fallibilist naturalism) does more work than its owner admits. Then give an overall verdict, and say what both parties are missing.
