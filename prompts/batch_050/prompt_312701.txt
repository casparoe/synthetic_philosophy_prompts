A colleague of mine in the philosophy of science has distilled the replication crisis into the following argument, and I can't decide whether it proves far too much or simply forces an uncomfortable choice on us. I'd like a diagnosis.

(1) You are justified in believing the output of a belief-forming procedure only if, in the conditions in which you actually rely on it, the procedure rarely delivers falsehoods. Reliability in the circumstances of use is what separates warranted confidence from a hunch.

(2) In large parts of psychology and biomedicine, the actual conditions of reliance are these: most hypotheses that get tested are false, samples are small, and analysts enjoy wide discretion over which comparisons, transformations, and exclusions to report.

(3) Under those conditions, the proportion of statistically significant findings that are false can exceed half. A result at p < .05 constrains only the probability of data at least that extreme given the null hypothesis; once you combine low pre-study odds of the hypothesis being true with modest statistical power, most results that clear the bar are flukes even so.

(4) Therefore, by (1), researchers and readers in these fields are not justified in believing most of what their flagship inferential procedure certifies.

(5) Now consider the ordinary faculties. Perception operates under poor lighting, haste, and prior expectation; memory reconstructs emotionally charged events; testimony arrives from interested parties with incentives to shade. In these conditions — which are not a marginal annex of everyday life but a large share of it — those faculties deliver falsehoods at rates no careful person would call rare.

(6) Therefore, by (1), we are not justified in believing most of what perception, memory, and testimony deliver in those conditions either.

(7) But that is a route to pervasive everyday skepticism, and such skepticism is absurd: it cannot be lived, and the very evidence for premises (2) and (5) was gathered by the faculties that (6) impugns, so the argument saws off the branch its own premises sit on.

(8) Hence (1) must be false — and with (1) falls the only principle that ever made the replication crisis a crisis of justification rather than a statistical curiosity. If reliability in the conditions of use is not the standard, my distrust of unreplicated significant findings was never principled to begin with.

Here is where I stand. I want to keep both the critique in (4) and the anti-skeptical verdict in (7), and the argument tells me I cannot have both without giving up (1), which still strikes me as close to a platitude. So: is some step in this chain invalid? If so, name it, and explain why it looked valid — I suspect something shifts between (1) and (5), or between (5) and (6), but I cannot pin it down. If instead you take the chain to be valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender and exactly what that surrender costs. And whichever way you go, address this directly: is there a principled difference between the statistical case and the case of the ordinary faculties that the argument fails to honor — some difference in what it is to rely on a procedure, or in the conditions under which its outputs get accepted — or is the difference only one of degree, in which case I want to know what follows from admitting that?
