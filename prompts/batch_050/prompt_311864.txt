In the first chapter of Book IV of The Social Contract, in G. D. H. Cole's 1913 translation, Rousseau describes the citizen who casts a vote from private interest rather than from a view of the common good:

Even in selling his vote for money, he does not extinguish in himself the general will, but only eludes it. The fault he commits is that of changing the state of the question, and answering something different from what he is asked. Instead of saying, by his vote, "It is to the advantage of the State," he says, "It is of advantage to this or that man or party that this or that view should prevail." Thus the law of public order in assemblies is not so much to maintain in them the general will as to secure that the question be always put to it, and the answer always given by it.

A few pages later, at the opening of the next chapter, he adds:

The more concert reigns in the assemblies, that is, the nearer opinion approaches unanimity, the greater is the dominance of the general will. On the other hand, long debates, dissensions and tumult proclaim the ascendancy of particular interests and the decline of the State.

I sit on the rules committee of a standing citizens' panel: about sixty residents, recruited by sortition, who advise our local authority on planning and licensing matters. We hear evidence, deliberate in small groups and then in plenary, and settle our recommendations by secret ballot under a simple majority. I would like to know whether these passages should change anything we do, so please set out what you take to be their most important implications for the design and conduct of a body like ours.

Please work through at least the following. First, the framing of questions: Rousseau seems to hold that a voter's corruption consists in answering a different question from the one put, and that the institution's job is to keep the right question in front of him. Does it follow that the wording of our motions should ask not which option members prefer but which option best serves some stated common end, and would that rewording do any real work, or does the passage overestimate how far people answer the question they are asked rather than the one they wished had been asked? Secondly, the sequence of business: if the vice the passage identifies is the intrusion of particular interest, what follows for the order in which evidence, small-group discussion and voting should occur, and for whether we should tolerate caucusing, log-rolling or the circulation of members' personal stakes before a ballot? Thirdly, the aftermath of a vote: if each ballot is a judgement about a common matter rather than the expression of a taste, what posture does that imply for members who find themselves in the minority, and is that posture one we could honestly recommend to people whose interests genuinely conflict with the majority's? Fourthly, the second passage sits oddly with our own working assumption that vigorous debate is a sign of health: should we read our longer, more fractious sessions as Rousseau would, as a symptom of decline, or is his diagnosis the one at fault? Finally, say where the passages' presupposition — that there is a common advantage for the question to be about — gives out entirely, and what the design advice collapses into for decisions where it does.

Throughout, keep two things distinct: what follows strictly from the quoted text, and what requires further premises that Rousseau supplies elsewhere and that a modern panel might reasonably deny. And one question about the first passage itself, since my reading of everything else turns on it: when Rousseau says that even the man selling his vote still wills the common good and merely answers the wrong question, is that an empirical claim about voters, which could be false, or a stipulation about what "the question" in an assembly is, which cannot be — and does the practical advice differ depending on which it is?
