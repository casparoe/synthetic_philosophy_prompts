I'm in a reading group on critical theory and last week one of the members, who'd read a popular introduction to the Frankfurt School, gave us this summary of Habermas and it kind of became the thing we argued about all evening. Her version went roughly like this: "Habermas says there's a special kind of rationality built into language itself. Whenever we talk to each other, we're aiming at agreement, and every statement we make comes with three promises: that it's true, that it's morally right, and that the speaker means it sincerely. If the other person doubts any of these, we have to prove it with arguments. Now in real life, communication is always distorted by power -- money, status, coercion -- so real conversations never actually live up to this. But Habermas thinks we can work toward what he calls the ideal speech situation, a society where everyone gets to speak freely, nobody is excluded, and the only force at work is the forceless force of the better argument. Once we get there, whatever everyone agrees on is the truth, since truth just is ideal consensus. So his whole project is basically a utopian one: fix the power imbalances, and rational debate will settle our political disagreements for us."

Now, I only know Habermas second-hand, mostly from a couple of lectures, but a few things here made me suspicious. The "three promises" part rang a bell but I had the impression comprehensibility is somehow also in the picture, and I distinctly remember our lecturer insisting Habermas is not a consensus theorist of truth, or at least backed away from that -- whereas the summary makes it sound like that's the centerpiece. Also the whole framing of the ideal speech situation as a future society we should "work toward" felt off to me, like it's turning some kind of presupposition or counterfactual into a political program, but I couldn't articulate why.

So: how fair is this summary? Can you go through it and tell me which parts a sympathetic reader of Habermas would accept as basically right, which parts are common simplifications that a specialist would wince at, and which parts misrepresent him? I'm especially curious whether the last line follows -- if the accurate parts of the summary are right, does it actually commit Habermas to the utopian picture where removing power imbalances lets debate settle politics, or is that the reading group's conclusion rather than his?
