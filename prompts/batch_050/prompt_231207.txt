In three weeks I am presenting a paper at a two-day workshop on the epistemology of AI-assisted reasoning, and I need a preparation sheet for the question period. My thesis is that factored cognition fails as an idealization of deliberation — not as an engineering proposal, but as a model of what good thinking is. By factored cognition I mean the picture, familiar from work on iterated amplification and from the factored cognition experiments run at Ought, in which a reasoner of fixed, limited capacity is idealized as a tree of question-and-answer calls to copies of itself: a hard question is decomposed into sub-questions, each answered by a fresh copy with no memory of the others, and the answers are aggregated back up the tree, so that arbitrarily sophisticated deliberation is approximated by recursion over parts small enough for a weak agent to handle.

My argument against this as an idealization of deliberation has three steps. First, decomposition assumes that sub-questions can be answered in relative isolation, but in actual deliberation the correct interpretation of a sub-question depends on the framing of the parent question — on what the whole inquiry has made salient — and a copy answering in isolation inherits only whatever framing the decomposition happened to transmit. Second, aggregation presumes that judgments compose: that the verdict on the whole is recoverable from verdicts on the parts. I argue this fails systematically, not just occasionally, because revising one part of a view typically shifts the weight given to other parts, so the tree's leaves are evaluated under background commitments the tree structure cannot represent. Third, I conclude that the factored picture quietly builds in a context-independence assumption — that what a sub-question means and what its answer is worth do not depend on the rest of the inquiry — which is precisely the assumption a theory of deliberation should be arguing for rather than stipulating. My positive claim is modest: I do not offer a replacement idealization, only the diagnosis that any adequate one must treat salience and framing as load-bearing rather than as noise.

The audience is split. Roughly half are AI alignment researchers who know this literature technically, including at least two who have worked on scalable oversight and regard iterated amplification as the most promising alignment strategy we have; they will hear my paper as an attack on that program even though I explicitly disavow that target. The other half are philosophers: several formal epistemologists comfortable with idealized models of agents, a couple of philosophers of mind sympathetic to enactive or anti-computational views who may try to claim me as an ally, and at least one person whose first move at every talk is to ask what the speaker's positive account is.
