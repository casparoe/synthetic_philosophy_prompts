At my company we've been building out an AI control stack, and the design docs keep using two incompatible pictures of the system we're controlling, sometimes in the same paragraph. Concretely, the measures are things like: running the model in a sandbox with no direct production access; having a weaker but trusted model review the stronger model's code and actions before they execute; inserting deliberate "honeypot" opportunities where a misbehaving model could defect and get caught; random auditing of outputs; and capability restrictions, like giving the model read-only access it doesn't strictly need escalated. The underlying assumption, stated explicitly in the docs, is that we should operate as if the model may be misaligned -- that alignment techniques might fail -- and design so that even a misaligned system can't do much damage.

Here's what puzzles me. These practices seem to presuppose that the system has stable goals that persist across contexts, that it can tell the difference between a test and the real thing (otherwise the honeypots and audits are pointless), and that its behavior under observation is systematically unrepresentative of its behavior unobserved. That is, the whole stack only makes sense if the model is something like a strategic agent with concealed intentions. But we deploy the exact same model as a tool: it writes code, we treat its outputs as having no standing to complain about anything, and the threat model elsewhere in the org assumes it's a stochastic artifact, not an adversary.

So: what view of the controlled system does a control regime actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory -- about agency, goals, deception, and what behavior is evidence of -- and tell me whether the regime strictly needs that theory or is compatible with several (e.g., a picture on which the model has no goals at all but is predictably unreliable). Also say whether the two pictures my org uses can be made consistent, and what would have to change in the control stack if we dropped the strategic-agent assumption entirely.
