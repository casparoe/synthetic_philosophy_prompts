I'm a journalist covering the science-and-ideas beat, and I'm working on a review of a pop-psych book that leans hard on one move: nations have personalities the same way individuals do. The author's argument, roughly, is that personality psychologists measure people on traits like conscientiousness or openness and use those scores to predict how they'll behave at work, in relationships, under stress -- and we can do the exact same thing with countries. Average the survey responses of a million Germans or Brazilians, get the nation's "profile," and then use it to predict how that country negotiates trade deals, responds to crises, shows up to meetings on time. He uses this to defend generalizations of the "don't expect deadline flexibility in Zurich, do expect it in Naples" variety, and to say the squeamishness about national character is just politeness getting in the way of data.

I ran this by a social psychologist I sometimes call for fact-checks and she basically said the whole thing is the ecological fallacy wearing a lab coat -- that a mean computed over seventy million people isn't a personality, doesn't predict anything the way an individual score does, and mostly measures who got sampled and what they thought the "right" answer sounded like. But she couldn't fully convince me either, since surely it's not pure noise that these surveys come out differently across countries.

Can you actually audit the analogy for me? What maps onto what -- the individual trait, the trait score, test-retest stability, the predictive validity for behavior -- and what's the national-level thing each of those is supposed to correspond to? Where does the mapping quietly import features that individuals have and aggregates don't (a single brain doing the behaving, for one)? And once the disanalogies are on the table, which conclusions survive: is there anything left of "national character" that a writer could responsibly generalize about, and if so, what kind of statement is it -- statistical, historical, institutional -- rather than a personality profile? If there's a better analogy for whatever cross-country differences genuinely exist, I'd take that too.
