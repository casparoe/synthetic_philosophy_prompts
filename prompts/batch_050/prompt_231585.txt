Exam question (25 points total):
(a) A hotel with infinitely many rooms, numbered 1, 2, 3, ..., is completely full. Explain how the manager can accommodate (i) one additional guest and (ii) infinitely many additional guests arriving at once, without anyone sharing a room and without evicting anyone. What does this show about infinite sets? (8 points)
(b) State what Cantor's diagonal argument establishes, and sketch the argument. (9 points)
(c) A finitist denies that completed actual infinities are legitimate objects of mathematics. Briefly characterize the finitist position, name a thinker associated with it, and say whether the scenario in (a) counts as evidence for or against the finitist. (8 points)

Student answer:

(a) For one new guest, the manager moves each current guest from room n to room n+1, freeing room 1. For infinitely many new guests, this trick won't work, since shifting by one only frees a single room. Instead, the manager moves each current guest from room n to room 2n. This frees all the even-numbered rooms, of which there are infinitely many, so the new guests can be accommodated there. This shows that an infinite set can be put into one-to-one correspondence with a proper subset of itself, which cannot happen for finite sets; indeed this is often taken as the definition of an infinite set.

(b) Cantor's diagonal argument establishes that the real numbers are uncountable: there is no one-to-one correspondence between the natural numbers and the real numbers, so the reals form a strictly larger infinity than the naturals. The proof proceeds by contradiction. Suppose the reals between 0 and 1 could be listed, with each real written as an infinite decimal expansion. Construct a new number whose nth decimal digit differs from the nth decimal digit of the nth number on the list. This constructed number differs from each number on the list in every decimal place, so it cannot appear anywhere on the list, contradicting the assumption that the list was complete. Hence the reals are uncountable. This was Cantor's first proof of the uncountability of the reals, published in 1874.

(c) Finitism holds that mathematics may only legitimately deal with finite objects and finite procedures; talk of completed infinite totalities is meaningless, though potential infinity (a process that can always be continued) is acceptable. The classic statement of this view is due to Leopold Kronecker, who rejected the use of non-constructive infinitary objects, and the position was later taken up in David Hilbert's program, which is why Hilbert insisted that mathematics be secured on finitistic foundations and treated Cantor's transfinite cardinals with suspicion. The hotel scenario counts as evidence against the finitist: it shows that once one accepts actual infinity, paradoxical-sounding conclusions follow quite rigorously, which demonstrates that actual infinity is a coherent and fruitful concept. But the finitist would reply that the scenario is a thought experiment about a physically impossible object, so it begs the question by assuming the very completed infinity that is in dispute.
