A handbook of Chinese philosophy describes Wang Yangming’s doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action as follows. In moral matters, there is ultimately no gap between knowing what one ought to do and doing it: someone who claims to know filial piety while behaving unfilially possesses only verbal information, not genuine knowledge. Wang therefore rejects the Cheng–Zhu approach of first investigating principles in external things and only afterward cultivating conduct. Since knowledge is completed only in action, ethical instruction cannot really consist in learning doctrines, maxims, or examples; books and teachers can at most prompt the learner to recover liangzhi, the innate moral knowledge already fully present in the mind. The handbook then infers that Wang regards any study which does not immediately alter outward behavior as worthless, and that he treats moral failure as strictly self-contradictory, like affirming and denying the same proposition.

I am unsure how fair this characterization is. It seems plausible that Wang would deny that moral knowledge can be entirely inactive, but the handbook’s claims about books, study, and contradiction feel too strong. Please assess the summary point by point: which parts accurately capture Wang Yangming’s position, which distort the relation between knowledge and action, and which conflate his critique of a purely theoretical approach to ethics with a rejection of learning or textual study altogether? In particular, explain whether failure to act must mean that genuine knowledge was absent, whether action here means only outward behavior, and whether the handbook’s final comparison with logical self-contradiction follows from Wang’s view.
