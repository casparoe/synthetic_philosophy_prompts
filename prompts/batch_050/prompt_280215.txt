I'm a high school senior, and in two weeks I'm giving a twenty-minute talk to my school's science and philosophy society defending the following thesis: the famous lesson that "Tit-for-Tat won, so be nice, retaliatory, forgiving, and clear" badly oversells what the tournament results actually show. My argument is that Axelrod-style round-robin tournaments without errors reward strategies that never defect first, but once you add even a small chance of mistakes -- a misread move, a trembling hand -- plain Tit-for-Tat collapses into endless mutual retaliation, and the strategies that actually thrive are ones like Generous Tit-for-Tat and Win-Stay, Lose-Shift (Pavlov), which tolerate occasional defections. I'll further argue that in evolving populations you don't get a stable reign of any single "best" strategy at all, but cycles: cooperation spreads, drift lets unconditional cooperators accumulate, defectors invade and feast, and cooperation only re-establishes from clusters. So the real lesson, I'll claim, is about forgiveness and population dynamics, not about a four-point recipe for individual behavior.

My audience will be: my AP Biology teacher, who accepts kin selection and reciprocal altruism and will judge me on whether the model says anything about actual animals; a math teacher who knows classical game theory well (Nash equilibria, backward induction in finitely repeated games) but, I suspect, not evolutionary game theory; and about twenty classmates whose entire exposure is the one-shot prisoner's dilemma and who think defecting is obviously rational, full stop.

Prepare a Q&A prep sheet for me. Give me the eight to ten objections I'm actually likely to get from this audience -- not the objections a conference of game theorists would raise -- ordered roughly by how much damage they'd do to my talk if I fumble them. Phrase each objection the way that specific audience member would put it (the math teacher will talk about backward induction unravelling cooperation in finitely repeated games; the biology teacher will ask about vampire bats and whether any animal really conditions behavior like this; a classmate will just ask why anyone would ever cooperate against a stranger they'll never meet again). For each objection, give me a two-minute reply, note explicitly what I should concede up front versus what I should hold firm on, and flag where the reply is soft. Finally, tell me which question I'm hoping nobody asks -- the one that hits the weakest joint in my thesis (I suspect it's something about whether Win-Stay, Lose-Shift is actually robust once unconditional cooperators can drift in, but tell me if there's a worse one I'm not seeing) -- and how I should answer it if it comes.
