I am a high school student trying to understand the two-envelope paradox beyond the usual claim that the calculation somehow ignores symmetry. Please explain the conceptual issue carefully.

Suppose one envelope contains twice as much money as the other. I choose one at random, open it, and find $100. The familiar argument says that the other envelope contains either $50 or $200, so its expected value is $125 and I should switch. Yet before opening either envelope, switching seems pointless because the envelopes are symmetric.

Please identify exactly which step in the switching argument fails. In particular, distinguish among:

1. choosing and switching before observing anything;
2. choosing, observing a particular amount, and then deciding whether to switch;
3. treating “the amount I observed” as a fixed number rather than as a random variable;
4. assuming that the two possibilities for the other envelope are equally likely when no probability distribution over the amounts has been specified; and
5. assuming an unbounded uniform distribution over all possible base amounts.

Do not merely say that expected value does not apply. Explain what valid probability model, if any, would justify the 50–50 assignment after I see $100, and why the symmetry between the envelopes does not by itself supply that model.

Then analyze this finite version: the host chooses an integer k uniformly from 1 through 100, places $k in one envelope and $2k in the other, and randomly determines which envelope receives which amount. I select an envelope at random and observe $128. Should I switch? What if I observe $200? Please give the relevant conditional probabilities and explain how the finite upper bound affects the conclusion.

Finally, state the general lesson in intuitive terms: when is information that appears purely symmetric nevertheless decision-relevant, and what exactly must be known about the process generating the two envelopes before a switching strategy can have a well-defined expected value?
