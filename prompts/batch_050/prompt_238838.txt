I am a novelist by profession, and I serve as the external lay member of a small university committee charged with reviewing the names attached to our buildings, prizes and endowed lectures. We now face our most difficult case to date. A student campaign, backed by several members of the philosophy faculty, has petitioned us to strip Gottlob Frege's name from the annual memorial lecture his admirers endowed some seventy years ago. The petition rests on the political diary Frege kept privately in the last years of his life, which surfaced in print only decades after his death. It contains, amongst other things, antisemitic passages, contempt for parliamentary democracy and socialists, and sympathy for an authoritarian nationalist politics. Nobody on the committee disputes the diary's contents; what we dispute is what follows from them.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before our next meeting, recommending that we either retain the name, rename the lecture, or retain it subject to conditions, with a timeline and practical terms (including what, if anything, we say to the descendants of the endower, whose gift was made on condition that Frege be commemorated). The opinion should do the following. First, state and answer the petitioners' strongest argument rather than a weak version of it. Second, take a position on whether a thinker's private, unpublished diaries should weigh differently from his public conduct or published work when we decide whom to honour. Third, engage the question I keep pressing in our meetings as the lone non-philosopher: whether there is any tension worth dwelling on between the politics of the diary and the universalist ambition of Frege's logic, which purports to lay bare the structure of thought for any reasoner whatever, or whether that is merely an irony of biography with no bearing on the philosophy. Fourth, since colleagues keep telling me that a novelist above all should believe a work can be prised apart from its maker, address whether the literary analogy actually holds for formal and technical work, or breaks down somewhere. Finally, set out general criteria we could apply to future naming cases, so that whatever we decide does not stand as an unprincipled one-off. The committee includes historians, scientists and one canon lawyer, so the opinion must be rigorous without presuming training in logic.
