I would like to stage a philosophical role-play, and I want the first reply to be your response in character rather than any request for clarification.

You will play George Berkeley — not a commentator on Berkeley, but the man himself, speaking in the first person, at some point after the publication of the Principles of Human Knowledge. I will play your persistent critic. The ground rules are as follows:

1. Remain in character throughout. You hold, sincerely and without embarrassment, that the being of sensible things consists in their being perceived; that matter, understood as an inert mind-independent substratum, is an incoherent notion; and that the continued existence of objects when unobserved by us is secured by the perception of God.

2. Confine yourself to the resources of your own system. You may not cite, name, or borrow from any philosopher or development after your own lifetime — no later theories of reference, intentionality, or modality. You may lean on your attack on abstract ideas, the distinction between ideas and notions, the passivity of perception, the argument from the impossibility of likeness between an idea and anything but an idea, and your treatment of our knowledge of spirits.

3. Take my objections seriously. Answer the strongest version of each objection, not a weakened one. You need not concede unless you are genuinely cornered; but if you are cornered, admit it honestly rather than changing the subject.

4. Write in a manner befitting an eighteenth-century philosopher addressing an able opponent: courteous, precise, unhurried, occasionally dry — but do not lapse into theatrical pastiche, and do not lecture me about your life or historical context.

My opening move is this. Your so-called master argument — the challenge to conceive of a thing existing unconceived — appears to me to equivocate. When I conceive of a tree existing unperceived, my conceiving is of course an act of mine; but the object of that act, the tree, is not thereby made an idea or rendered dependent on my mind. You have shown, at most, that I cannot conceive of a tree without conceiving; you have not shown that what I thereby conceive cannot exist unconceived. Worse, the argument seems to prove too much: if the act of thinking of a thing draws that thing into the mind as an idea, then no one could so much as think of anything beyond their own ideas — which would make nonsense of your own claims to know of God and of other spirits, whom you certainly do not perceive as you perceive trees. So either the argument equivocates between the act of conception and its object, or it convicts your own system of the very incoherence you charge against matter. What say you?
