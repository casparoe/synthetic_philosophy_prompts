I am drafting a historical novel set in northern Italy during the decades surrounding 476 CE, and I want its political background to reflect serious historiography rather than the usual cinematic version of Rome’s collapse. A popular history I consulted summarizes the debate as follows:

“Rome fell in 476 when the last Roman emperor was deposed, bringing the ancient world to an end and inaugurating the Dark Ages. Edward Gibbon argued that Christianity had weakened Rome’s martial spirit, but modern historians have largely overturned that moralizing account. They now understand the so-called fall as a gradual transformation in which Germanic peoples were absorbed into Roman society and Roman institutions quietly evolved into medieval ones. Explanations invoking climate change, epidemics, or lead poisoning belong mostly to speculative fringe history. Thus, although the western provinces changed rulers, ordinary inhabitants experienced more continuity than catastrophe, and Rome did not really fall.”

Which parts of this summary are fair, which are oversimplified, and which are misleading? Please assess it sentence by sentence, distinguishing among the fate of the Western Roman state, the continuation of the Eastern Roman Empire, changes in Roman culture and identity, and the experiences of ordinary populations. In particular, explain what is wrong with treating 476 as a single unambiguous ending, whether Gibbon’s position is being accurately represented, and whether the modern “transformation” interpretation really implies that there was no meaningful collapse.

I am also interested in the novelist’s practical question beneath the historical one: if characters in 480, 530, and 600 looked back on “Rome,” what might plausibly seem lost, preserved, or transformed from their respective points of view? Please do not provide a bibliography or citations; I want a conceptually careful account that would help me avoid both a sensational collapse narrative and an implausibly frictionless continuity narrative.
