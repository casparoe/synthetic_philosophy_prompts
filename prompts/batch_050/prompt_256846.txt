I'm writing a grant proposal where I have to commit to a position I keep flip-flopping on, so I want your actual credence with the working shown, not a survey of the literature.

The setup: within a decade a lot of negotiation will happen between AI agents acting on behalf of humans or firms — splitting the surplus from a trade, allocating compute, dividing the gains from some joint project. Cooperative bargaining theory has two canonical solutions:

- The Nash bargaining solution: pick the outcome that maximises the product of each agent's gain over the disagreement point. It satisfies independence of irrelevant alternatives (deleting feasible options nobody would have picked doesn't change the outcome), and it needs no comparison of utilities across agents — each agent's utility function is only pinned down up to affine transformation.
- The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution: find each agent's ideal outcome (the most they could get while the other still gets at least their disagreement payoff), then take the maximal point on the line from the disagreement point to this 'utopia point', so each agent recieves the same fraction of their best-case gain. It drops IIA in favour of individual monotonicity (if the feasible set expands in a way that only helps you, you shouldn't end up worse off), but it implicitly treats fractions of maximal gain as comparable across agents.

The proposition: for AI agents negotiating on behalf of principals, Kalai–Smorodinsky is the better default fairness rule than Nash. Give me your credence in that, decomposed into the sub-questions it actually rides on. Candidates: whether 'same fraction of your best case' is genuinely fairer than product-of-gains, or just feels that way; whether dropping IIA is a bug or a feature in this setting (KS outcomes shift if you add options nobody would take, wich smells manipulable — an agent could pad its feasible set with phantom alternatives to pull the solution toward itself); whether the utopia point is even robustly definable when agents can misreport their utility functions; whether comparing fractions of best case between two systems with no shared utility scale is coherent at all; plus whatever terms you think I'm missing. A number and one line of justification for each, then the combined figure.
