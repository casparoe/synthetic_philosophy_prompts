I am a retired research physicist who has lately been reading philosophy of science, and I should like my present position audited rather than admired. My starting point is a passage from Henri Poincaré's essay 'Mathematical Creation' (in *Science and Method*), which struck me because it does not treat the mathematician's aesthetic response as a mere pleasure ancillary to the work but as something with a discernible role in it:

"It may be surprising to see emotional sensibility invoked à propos of mathematical demonstrations which, it would seem, can interest only the intellect. This would be to forget the feeling of mathematical beauty, of the harmony of numbers and forms, of geometric elegance. This is a true esthetic feeling that all real mathematicians know, and surely it belongs to emotional sensibility."

I take Poincaré to be saying that this feeling is a genuine faculty that does real work in mathematical and scientific thinking, not an ornament upon it. Here, then, is roughly where I have landed, after forty years in the laboratory and two in the library:

(1) Cases such as Maxwell's completion of his equations and Dirac's prediction of the positron show that a theory's beauty can be genuine evidence for it — not merely a motive for working on it, but a reason, however defeasible, for believing it.
(2) The history of physics is equally a graveyard of beautiful theories — the steady state, Kelvin's vortex atoms, the proton decay predicted by the elegant grand unified theories of my own working lifetime — so beauty is a systematically unreliable guide.
(3) What physicists call beauty in fact decomposes, on inspection, into simplicity, symmetry, and unifying scope, each of which earns its epistemic keep independently; there is no further, irreducibly aesthetic ingredient doing evidential work.
(4) My own sense of elegance was trained on the theories that happened to succeed, so it is downstream of the evidence rather than an independent source of it.
(5) None the less, when I must choose between two empirically equivalent hypotheses, I follow my aesthetic sense, and I maintain it is rational to do so.

What I want to know is whether this set hangs together — with each other, and with my reading of the Poincaré passage. In particular: which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, and how severe is each; which pairs look tense but dissolve under scrutiny; for each real tension, what is the cheapest revision that restores consistency, and what does it cost; and which single amendment would clean up the most? I should also like to know whether commitment (3) can survive my reading of Poincaré, or whether the passage, so interpreted, already commits me to an aesthetic faculty that (3) denies.

Please begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — the audit itself, with no preamble and no restating of my question. For each tension you diagnose, indicate how confident you are that it is genuine, and end by singling out the one point in your own analysis about which you are least sure. Kindly write in British English.
