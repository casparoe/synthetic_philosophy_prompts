I've been chewing on this question of who's actually "allowed" to call somebody out for something, and I want to figure out what rule my gut is following. Verdicts from my own life:

- My mom smoked for 25 years, quit about five years ago. When she gets on my case about vaping it annoys me but it seems totally fair. She did her time, and she knows the struggle from the inside.
- A guy at work fudges his expense reports, everybody knows it, its kind of an open secret. Last week he called me out in front of coworkers for taking home printer paper, and it felt completely out of line, like he has no right to open his mouth about it.
- My aunt had an affair years ago that blew up the family. When my cousin started cheating on his wife, she was the one who sat him down about it, and honestly I think she was maybe the best person to do it.
- My brother-in-law is drowning in gambling debt, still gambling, and he lectures me about my credit card spending. He's not even wrong, but the lecture makes my skin crawl.
- Now the one that breaks my pattern: a close friend is a recovering alcoholic, still goes to meetings, relapsed last month. When she tells me I drink too much it doesn't bother me at all, it actually lands harder than it would from anybody else. But she's still in it, same as my brother-in-law. So why does one get a pass and the other doesn't?

It can't just be "did the thing at some point" because my mom and aunt did and they're fine. And it can't just be "is doing it right now" because the last two cases split. Is there one principle that covers all five, or do I have to drop one of these verdicts? Two more things: when someone without the right calls you out, is their point still true and just worth less coming from them, or should it carry no weight with me at all? And what would the principle say about the case I can't settle -- our priest, who I'm pretty sure (small town, people talk) is having an affair, giving sermons about marriage.
