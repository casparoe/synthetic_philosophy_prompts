Exam question: A new medication is tested at two clinics. At Clinic A, 90 of 100 patients given the medication recover, compared with 160 of 200 patients given a placebo. At Clinic B, 200 of 400 patients given the medication recover, compared with 10 of 50 patients given a placebo. (a) Compute the recovery rates for medication and placebo at each clinic separately, and pooled across both clinics. (b) Explain how the pooled rates can favor the placebo even though the medication does better at each clinic. (c) A doctor asks whether she should prescribe the medication. Based only on these data, which comparison should she rely on, and why?

Student's answer: (a) Clinic A: medication 90%, placebo 80%. Clinic B: medication 50%, placebo 20%. Pooled: medication 290/500 = 58%, placebo 170/250 = 68%. (b) This is Simpson's paradox. It occurs because the medication group is concentrated at Clinic B, where recovery rates are low for everyone, while the placebo group is concentrated at Clinic A, where most patients recover regardless. Pooling ignores clinic, so the medication looks worse overall even though it helps within each clinic. This kind of reversal only arises when sample sizes are small or uneven; with sufficiently large samples, the pooled comparison would have to agree with the within-clinic comparisons. (c) She should rely on the per-clinic comparisons. Whenever a third variable such as clinic or illness severity also affects the outcome, controlling for it always reveals the treatment's true effect, so the pooled figure is simply misleading. The medication is therefore genuinely better, and she should prescribe it.
