I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm writing a long feature on the extended mind thesis for a general but philosophically literate readership. In the course of my research I've gathered what I take to be the standard objections to Clark and Chalmers' view, and I don't need any of them restated: (1) the coupling–constitution fallacy — Otto being causally coupled to his notebook doesn't show the notebook is a constituent of his mind; (2) cognitive bloat — if the notebook counts, so do libraries, the internet, and other people, and the mind becomes unboundedly large; (3) the mark-of-the-cognitive objection — genuine mental states have intrinsic, non-derived content or play certain fine-grained functional roles that notebook entries don't; (4) the epistemic asymmetry — Inga's belief is immediately available in a way Otto's isn't, so Otto only knows the address after consulting the notebook, not before; (5) the trust-and-glue conditions are ad hoc, reverse-engineered to let the notebook in while keeping everything else out.

My worry is that my piece will just rehash the familiar dialectic. Is there an objection to the extended mind thesis that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or combination of items on it? It should also not be a merely rhetorical point (e.g., that the view sounds bizarre) but something with argumentative bite against either the parity principle or the dynamical-systems route. Please state it as crisply as you can, explain how it differs from the objections above, and then tell me honestly whether you think it survives the replies a committed proponent of the extended mind would give.
