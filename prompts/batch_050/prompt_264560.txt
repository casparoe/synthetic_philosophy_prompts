I am a literary novelist with no philosophical training, and the craft I know intimately is making a reader understand a character through dialogue alone — through diction, evasion, silence, and contradiction — with no narrator on hand to explain. I have been reading about Donald Davidson's radical interpretation, and the principle of charity as he deploys it, and I would like the position restated in the vocabulary I actually think in: subtext, point of view, showing versus telling, the reader's inference of what a character believes from what she says, the writer's control over what evidence the reader is given, the unreliable narrator as the calculated withdrawal of a reader's default trust. Restate the core of the view as precisely as that vocabulary allows: that an interpreter with no shared language must fix a speaker's meanings and beliefs together, from the same evidence, by assuming the speaker is largely coherent and largely right, and that this assumption is not a mere heuristic but partly constitutive of meaning anything at all. Then give me the residue report: what translates cleanly, what you had to distort, and what has no counterpart in fiction at all — I suspect the constitutive claim and the appeal to triangulation. For each piece of residue, say whether it marks a limit of the novelist's frame or reveals that Davidson's claim depends on something, such as the difference between interpreting a real speaker and encountering a constructed one, that my craft simply has no use for. Where scholars disagree about how to read Davidson, mark the disagreement as such rather than presenting a contested interpretation as settled.
