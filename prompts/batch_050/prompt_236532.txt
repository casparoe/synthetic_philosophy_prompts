I'm a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, where Aumann's agreement theorem gets invoked constantly — usually to argue that persistent disagreement between honest, thoughtful people means someone is being irrational. Last week I listened to two friends, both of whom also read this material, argue about whether that invocation is legitimate. Neither convinced the other, and I'd like you to adjudicate.

Mara opened by stating the theorem roughly as follows: if two rational agents share a prior over events and their posteriors about some proposition are common knowledge between them, the posteriors must be equal. Her conclusion: since common knowledge of disagreement is impossible among such agents, whenever two people openly disagree and each knows the other's considered view, at least one of them is failing rationality — or else privately doesn't really believe the other is honest and competent.

Devon replied that the conclusion doesn't follow. The theorem's antecedent — a shared prior plus genuinely common knowledge of each other's posteriors — is never satisfied by actual humans, so a result about idealized agents says nothing about whether real disagreement is irrational. Invoking it in everyday disputes, he said, is like citing the physics of frictionless planes at the scene of a highway crash.

Mara conceded the idealization but said the informal moral survives: you cannot simultaneously regard someone as your epistemic peer, fully trust their honesty, know their settled view, and retain your own without flinching. The discomfort the theorem's name is famous for provoking — the sense that agreeing to disagree is somehow cheating — points at a real norm, even if the formal result covers only idealized agents.

Devon answered that the informal moral misreads what the theorem actually delivers. Even in the idealized case, it says the two posteriors coincide once they are common knowledge; it says nothing about averaging credences, meeting in the middle, or deferring to peers. And in the real case, persistent disagreement after discussion is compatible with both parties being reasonable, because conversation transmits conclusions rather than the entire body of private evidence and experience that produced them — so two people can rationally hold different posteriors indefinitely.

Mara's final move: if differing priors are doing all the remaining work, then priors shouldn't be treated as beyond scrutiny. If she and Devon have no independent reason to think their priors differ in reliability, symmetry demands they adjust toward each other, and refusing to do so is just privileging whatever one happened to start with.

Devon's final move: symmetry arguments prove too much. Taken seriously, they would oblige her to split the difference with everyone she disagrees with, including people she regards as confused — and if she restricts the obligation to those she judges reliable, then her own judgment of reliability is doing all the work, not the theorem.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible with caveats, and give the reason in a sentence or two. Then tell me what, if anything, both parties are missing. I have my own view but would rather not bias the adjudication by stating it. Keep your whole answer under 500 words, in American English.
