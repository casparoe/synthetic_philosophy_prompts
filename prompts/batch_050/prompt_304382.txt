so i'm in this philosophy of social science seminar and we got onto whether "national character" is even a coherent idea. someone defended it with an analogy i also hear all the time from football: club teams genuinely have a *style* that survives a complete turnover of players -- Ajax keeps playing like Ajax even when literally every player from ten years ago is gone -- so why couldn't a nation have a character that survives a turnover of citizens? on a team the style isn't inside any one player, it's carried by the institution: the academy, the coaching, what gets celebrated in the locker room, who they buy in the transfer market. same move for nations: maybe "german punctuality" or whatever isn't a claim about any individual german, let alone about genetics, but about institutions and transmitted norms, and generalizing about peoples is fine as long as you aim it at that level.

please act as a professional philosopher of social science who's also a stats-literate football nerd. think carefully, brainstorm correspondences first before judging. then audit the analogy properly:

1. the correspondence table -- what maps onto what (players, coach, academy, transfer policy, locker room culture, pundits who describe the style)?
2. where it breaks -- esp. these two things that bug me: clubs *select* and can expell members while nations mostly get their members by birth, and clubs are like 25 people who train together daily while nations are millions of strangers. do these kill the analogy or just weaken it?
3. what it actually licenses -- even if the analogy works, does it support claims about *peoples* or only about *states and institutions*? because those seem different.
4. final verdict: does the "it's about the institution, not the blood" conclusion survive, and is there a better analogy for whatever part of it does survive?
