I TA for a second-year applied ethics course and I'm writing this semester's midterm. I want one essay question on the ethics of eating meat that students can't answer with the standard factory-farming-is-cruel essay they've all basically pre-written.

Here's the angle I want. Suppose a student, Maya, decides to stop eating beef but keep eating chicken, and her reasoning is: cows are big, smart, and live for years, chickens are small and not very bright, so cutting out beef is the most impactful dietary change. The question should force students to actually evaluate this reasoning rather than just applaud it, and in particular it should make them confront three things:

1. The deaths-per-calorie problem. A cow yields an enormous amount of meat, a chicken very little, so eating chicken instead of beef might mean vastly more animals killed and vastly more days of (probably quite bad) farmed life per unit of food, even granting that each cow matters more than each chicken. Students should have to do at least rough moral arithmetic, and say which estimates the conclusion is sensitive to.

2. The land-use problem. Beef, especially grass-fed, uses hugely more land than chicken, and converting land to pasture displaces wild animals. But whether that's a cost or a benefit depends on whether wild animal lives are on average worth living at all, which is genuinely contested. I want students to notice that this cuts the opposite way from point 1, and that you can't resolve the comparison without taking a stance (or at least flagging uncertainty) on both the quality of wild animal lives and how much weight to give insects/rodents versus charismatic megafauna.

3. The meta-level problem. Given deep empirical and axiological uncertainty, what should Maya actually do? Students should have to say something about decision-making under moral uncertainty instead of just picking a side.

So: write me the exam question itself (students get 60 minutes, closed book), a model answer of around 600 words, and a marking scheme. For the marking scheme, give me the five things a top answer absolutely must do, the marks each is worth out of 20, and for each one a sentence on the way weaker students typically botch it (e.g. assuming habitat loss is obviously bad without noticing the net-negative-wild-lives wrinkle, or doing the deaths-per-calorie math but silently assuming all animal suffering counts equally regardless of capacity for suffering). Also tell me what seperates a pass from a fail on this question.
