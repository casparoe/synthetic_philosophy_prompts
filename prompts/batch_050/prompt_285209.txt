I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar called "Stoicism Then and Now," which reads the ancient texts alongside the modern revival — Stoic Week, the therapeutic appropriations, and the running argument about whether Stoic ethics can stand once Stoic physics and theology have been quietly set aside. I need help with a task that has landed on me outside of coursework, and I'd like it treated as seriously as a paper.

Dr. Eleanor Voss has taught ancient philosophy at my university for twenty-eight years. This spring the university announced that the ancient philosophy programme is being discontinued as a cost-saving measure, and her retirement next month is, in effect, a forced one. She has been publicly sharp about the decision, calling it institutional vandalism, and nobody who knows her expects her to go quietly. I have been asked to give the main speech — eight to nine minutes — at the farewell evening the student philosophy society is hosting for her. The audience is awkwardly mixed: her current and former students, her colleagues, her family (including grandchildren), and, unavoidably, the dean who signed off on the closure and will be sitting in the front row.

Every student who has passed through her first-year course knows the dichotomy of control, and in our seminar she once said that the sentence of Epictetus she would keep if she had to give up the rest comes from the short chapter of the Enchiridion — chapter VIII in most editions — where, rather than telling us to fight for what is up to us, he tells us not to wish for events to turn out as we want, but to want them to turn out as they do, promising that life will then flow along smoothly. It is the less-quoted half of his teaching, and the harder half. I want the speech organised around that chapter as a load-bearing structure, not a decoration: the passage should determine what the speech is permitted to say about the closure, about her anger at it, and about what this university owed her and failed to pay.

Two constraints she would insist on. First, the dichotomy must not be used to imply that the closure doesn't matter, or that her anger is a philosophical mistake — she would hear that as both a betrayal and a misreading of Epictetus, since what is not up to us can still be called by its right name. Second, the speech has to land for her grandchildren and for the scholars in the room at the same time. It may quote the chapter itself at most once, and only near the end.

Please respond with these sections, in this order:

The Speech — roughly 700–850 words, calibrated to be spoken aloud in eight to nine minutes.

Load-Bearing Points — about 200 words identifying three specific moments where the chapter determined what the speech could or could not say, and what it ruled out.

The Physics Problem — about 150 words. In seminar I have argued that the modern revival wants Epictetus's consolation without the Stoic cosmology that originally underwrote the wish that things happen as they do. Does this speech covertly rely on that older framework, or does it manage without it? Say which, and whether it is a defect.

The Sentence I Refused — about 100 words giving the one sentence a purely orthodox Stoic speech would have contained, which the speech deliberately does not, and why leaving it out was the honest choice.
