I am preparing a position paper for a reading group on the ethics of deception, loosely organized around Augustine's two treatises on lying, and before I circulate it I want to know whether the positions I have staked out can actually be held together. The paper defends the following commitments, all of which I currently endorse:

(a) Lying is wrong in itself, not in virtue of its consequences. To lie is to use speech against its natural purpose, which is to make known what the speaker takes to be true. This strikes me as the best element of Augustine's account, and it is the commitment I am least inclined to abandon.

(b) Not all lies are equally grave. Something like Augustine's grading of lies into more and less serious kinds is right: a lie told to shield an innocent person from grave harm is a far lesser wrong than one told for profit or malice.

(c) Grading is not licensing. Even the least grave lie remains impermissible. In the classic case of the murderer at the door asking where his intended victim is hiding, the morally available moves are silence, refusal to answer, or evasion short of falsehood — never the lie itself, however well-intentioned.

(d) Speaking what is literally true while intending and expecting the hearer to draw a false conclusion — the artfully misleading true statement — is sometimes permissible, including against the murderer at the door.

(e) Duties of disclosure track entitlements. What one is obliged to reveal depends on what the hearer has a right to know, and someone who seeks information in order to do wrong is owed no disclosure at all.

(f) Consent and convention transform the moral facts. Bluffing in poker, a feint in fencing, feigned ignorance before a surprise party, and polite fictions like answering "fine, thanks" when one is miserable are unproblematic or are not lies at all, because the conventions governing those exchanges do not engage truth-claiming in the way ordinary assertion does.

Here is what I am asking for. First, identify which pairs or triples among (a)–(f) are in genuine tension and which only appear to be; I suspect at least one apparent tension dissolves on inspection, and I want you to say explicitly why it dissolves rather than flagging everything indiscriminately. Second, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each one state precisely what would have to give — which commitment, revised how — and what that revision costs the overall view. Third, tell me which single revision would clean up the most.

Two constraints on the form of your response. Treat my commitments, not Augustine's texts, as the object of analysis: I am not asking for a survey of Augustinian scholarship, though you may note where my position departs from what Augustine himself held if that departure is itself a source of instability. And pay particular attention to whether the rationale I give in (a) can actually support the permissions I claim in (d) and (f), since I suspect — but am not sure — that the trouble, if there is any, lives there.
