In the philosophy of mind seminar I help teach, we have spent three weeks on the badness of pain, and the discussion has reached an impasse I keep turning over. The standard set-up is pain asymbolia and kindred conditions (following certain lesions, cingulotomy, or in some reports morphine administration): patients say they feel pain, identify it as pain, can often localize it and even describe its quality, yet report being entirely unbothered -- no distress, no avoidance, no impulse to make it stop. One faction in the seminar takes this as near-decisive evidence that the badness of pain is not in the phenomenal character itself but in some further attitude -- aversion, displeasure, caring -- that pain normally but contingently triggers; on their view the asymbolic patient's state is genuinely painful but not bad for them. The other faction insists the lesion merely removes the behavioral and motivational expression of pain, so something bad is still happening to these patients even though they are strangely incapable of minding it; alternatively, some of them argue the patients no longer feel pain proper at all, and what remains is pure nociceptive sensation being misdescribed as pain.

What I cannot figure out is what kind of disagreement this actually is, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is it a verbal dispute about which component of the normal pain experience gets to be called "the pain" -- the sensory dimension or the affective one -- with both sides agreeing on everything about the patient's condition and differing only over labeling? Is it a substantive first-order disagreement about where disvalue resides -- in experience itself or in our orientation toward it -- in which case asymbolia does real evidential work? Or is it a disagreement about which subjects count as reliable data: whether the asymbolic patient's calm testimony should be taken at face value or treated as compromised by the very condition under study, versus the authority of ordinary sufferers? My working hypothesis is that the debate is stalled because each side gets to define the phenomenon it purports to explain: phenomenalists take hurtfulness as primitive and relegate asymbolia to marginal or noisy data, while attitude theorists take the dissociation at face value and treat ordinary pain as a compound -- so no case can serve as common ground. Tell me whether that is right, and given your diagnosis, what would concretely count as progress: a conceptual distinction (say, between the sensory and affective dimensions and an account of which one carries the badness), new empirical work on such patients (and what would it even have to show?), or an argument that the question as posed is ill-formed and should be replaced. If your diagnosis implies that no resolution is to be expected, argue for that instead.
