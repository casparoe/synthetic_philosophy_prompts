I'm taking an undergraduate seminar in normative ethics, and after last week's unit on supererogation — acts that are good but supposedly go beyond what morality requires — I got into an argument with a classmate that we couldn't resolve. I defended the common-sense idea that there is a real gap between duty and moral excellence: donating a kidney to a stranger is admirable precisely because no one could demand it of you. My classmate argued that supererogation is incoherent: if an act is the morally best thing available to you, and nothing morally weighs against it, then the only honest thing to say is that you ought to do it, and calling it "optional" is just a way of letting ourselves off the hook. Neither of us managed to move the other, and I want to see the dispute carried out more carefully than we managed.

Acting as an expert in moral philosophy, write a dialogue of roughly 900–1200 words between these two positions. Before writing, briefly brainstorm for yourself which moves each side has available, then produce the dialogue itself. Make both speakers equally competent: the defender of supererogation should not merely assert intuitions, and the skeptic should not merely repeat that morality is demanding. They must respond to each other's actual arguments, and each must concede at least one genuine point to the other. Have them work through at least three concrete cases where the views plausibly come apart — for example, a person who gives 10% of their income to effective charities but could give 30%, a bystander who risks serious harm to rescue a stranger, and a soldier who falls on a grenade — and notice that the cases may not all cut the same way.

The dialogue must also engage two specific pressure points rather than skirting them. First, the defender of supererogation should press the objection that if all morally best acts are required, then morality leaves no room for a personal life, and the skeptic should give a serious answer rather than accept the charge. Second, the skeptic should press the puzzle that if an act truly is optional, it becomes mysterious why we admire and praise it so strongly, since we do not praise people for mere permissible choices like ordering tea instead of coffee. The dialogue should not end with agreement; instead, have the two speakers identify, in the final few exchanges, exactly where they think the disagreement bottoms out — whether in competing intuitions about the cases, in different conceptions of what morality is for, or in a merely verbal dispute about the word "ought" — and have them say whether they think that bottom-level difference could in principle be argued about further.
