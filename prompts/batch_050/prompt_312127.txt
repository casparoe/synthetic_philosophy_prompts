So I've been chewing on the phrase "Western values" for like a year now, mostly because I keep hearing it used in totally contradictory ways. One politician means free speech and gay rights, another means Christianity and traditional families, a third means "the Enlightenment" (whatever exactly that cashes out to), and meanwhile plenty of non-Western countries do democracy and plenty of Western countries clearly don't always. I can't tell if there's anything real underneath the phrase or if its just a team jersey people put on.

Here's my half-baked taxonomy, which you can push back on if its wrong. Seems like "Western values" could mean: (a) values that were historically practiced more in Western countries, regardless of origin, (b) values that originated in the West but are now genuinely global, in which case calling them "Western" is kind of a vanity label, or (c) whatever the speaker's coalition happens to like this decade, rebranded as civilizational heritage. And I have a pet hypothesis that the phrase does most of its work in arguments as a boundary marker -- deciding who's "in" and who's "out" -- rather than as an actual description of anything. Sort of like how "common sense" is usually code for "the stuff my side believes."

Here's what I actually want help with. I've got maybe six months of spare evenings and a blog almost nobody reads, and I want to write a series of essays that get to the bottom of this. So: which open questions about the coherence and use of "Western values" are most worth my limited time, and in what order? Don't just list them -- defend the ordering. Some things I'm wondering whether to include as candidates: whether the category can be made coherent at all or whether it's incoherent-by-design (and if the latter, whether that's actually a problem, since lots of useful political terms are fuzzy); whether the interesting question is empirical (what values are actually statistically distinctive of the West, if any) or rhetorical (what work the phrase does in argument); whether debating the history (was tolerance really invented in Europe, etc.) is load-bearing or a distraction; and whether my boundary-marker hypothesis, if true, means everyone should just retire the phrase or whether boundary markers are a legitimate thing for a society to have.

For each question you rank, tell me what makes it upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is for a smart non-academic with no greek or german, and what would change your ranking. And flag which of the debates I'm tempted by are overrated -- i.e., where I'd burn three months and end up with an essay that doesn't actually settle anything. The deliverable is a defended agenda for the series, not a survey of what people have said about the phrase.
