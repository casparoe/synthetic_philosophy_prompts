I'm putting together a syllabus for a political philosophy seminar and I want to pair Aldous Huxley's Brave New World with Fukuyama's end of history thesis. Specifically this bit from the Introduction to The End of History and the Last Man, which I think is the crux and not the part everyone quotes:

"Both Hegel and Marx believed that the evolution of human societies was not open-ended, but would end when mankind had achieved a form of society that satisfied its deepest and most fundamental longings. Both thinkers thus posited an 'end of history': for Hegel this was the liberal state, while for Marx it was a communist society."

Here's what's bugging me. Huxley's World State seems to hit Fukuyama's end-of-history criterion almost perfectly: all the really big questions settled, no ideology left to fight over, desires either satisfied on demand or engineered away before they arise. But it gets there with a eugenic caste system, conditioning, and soma -- not liberal democracy, not even close. So which way does the novel cut? Option one: it's a counterexample that guts the thesis, because it shows you can close history by satisfying (or deleting) the fundamental longings without anything like universal recognition, meaning the engine Fukuyama thinks drives history forward can simply be switched off by technology. Option two: it actually vindicates him, because look at what happens to the characters who still want more than comfort -- Bernard's vanity, Helmholtz's itch for real expression, John's whole catastrophic arc, and Mustapha Mond basically admitting that religion, Shakespeare, and dangerous science all had to be sacrificed to keep the thing stable. Maybe the reservation and the islands are doing the same work that "internal contradictions" do in Fukuyama's scheme, evidence the system hasn't satisfied our deepest longings after all, just anesthetized most people into not noticing.

I'd like a reading that commits to one of these (or tells me why both are wrong) and is honest about where the novel resists being recruited into the debate at all -- e.g. whether Huxley is even worried about history ending, or about something else entirely, like happiness being the wrong target. Also: does the Savage's ending tell us anything a philosohy paper about thymos couldn't? Point me to the specific scenes you'd actually teach.
