So I've been reading around the idea of standing to blame: roughly, the thought that a hypocrit who condemns someone for doing the very thing they themselves do has lost the standing to issue that condemnation. The criticism might be entirely accurate, but the critic isn't properly positioned to deliver it — there's something defective about the blaming itself, not just awkward about it.

Here's what's been bugging me. If that's right, it looks like it opens a really convenient loophole. Suppose I work somewhere where fiddling expenses is completely endemic — literally everyone does it, me included. Then none of my colleagues have standing to blame me for my dodgy claims, since they're all at it too. And as for outsiders with clean hands, well, they don't understand the context, they don't know what the norms are round here, and honestly I'd just write them off as self-righteous. So by making sure I'm exactly as bad as my environment, I've effectively insulated myself from all legitimate blame. Being worse turns out to be protective. The more corrupt the whole milieu, the safer everyone in it is from criticism.

Obviously that conclusion is mad, so the standing-to-blame idea must break down somewhere — or my application of it does. Where exactly does the reasoning go off the rails? Is the mistake about what standing actually is, or about who's supposed to have it, or about what losing it costs the blamer? And when a hypocrite's blame misfires, is the thing that's lost something about the blame itself, or only about the blaming — and does that distinction actually do any work here, or is it just philosophers being precious?
