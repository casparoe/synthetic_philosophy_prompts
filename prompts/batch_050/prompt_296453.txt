I am a novelist working on a literary novel set roughly thirty years from now, in a society where nearly everyone leases cognition from a subscription service: an always-on assistant that answers questions, drafts correspondence, settles disagreements, recommends opinions, and remembers birthdays. The service is reliable — genuinely, almost boringly accurate — and that is precisely the condition my novel keeps circling: its users hold more true beliefs than any generation before them and understand almost none of what they believe.

For a chapter set at a university graduation, I need the full text of the commencement address, delivered by the service's founder and chief executive, an invented figure of your devising. The university is the last in the country to have retained a philosophy department, a fact the speaker regards as a compliment he is graciously returning. Write roughly 700–800 words in the register of a real commencement address — warm, sweeping, self-congratulatory, studded with personal anecdote and advice to the graduates — and keep it entirely deadpan. The speaker must never suspect he is saying anything damning; the satire should live wholly in the gap between what he means and what he says, legible to the reader without a single wink from the author.

The humor must arise from the epistemology rather than from slapstick or technological malfunction. Without using jargon or naming any literature, work in at least the following: the gap between knowing that something is the case and understanding why it is (this class holds more true beliefs than any in history and cannot reconstruct a single argument for any of them); the circularity of vouching for the service's trustworthiness by consulting the service; the question of where the speaker's mind ends and his own earpiece begins, which he should illustrate unself-consciously by pausing mid-sentence for his next thought and resuming unbothered; and the silent disappearance of the capacities the service made redundant, mourned by no one because no one remembers having had them. Include one sustained set piece: the conferral of the annual thesis prize upon a student whose honored thesis neither she nor anyone in attendance can state the conclusion of unaided, played by the speaker as the crowning triumph of the afternoon.

Constraints: no real companies, products, institutions, or public figures — the university and all persons are invented. No narration or stage business explaining the joke; at most the sparse bracketed beats of a published transcript ([applause], [pause]). A reader versed in the philosophy of testimony, cognitive offloading, and the extended mind should judge the satire fair rather than strawmanned — the service really is accurate, and the speaker's best arguments for it should be genuinely good ones — while a reader innocent of that literature should still laugh and afterward feel faintly queasy. The speaker must not drift into veiled self-criticism as he closes; he means every word. If there is a sting, let it land in the final line without his noticing.
