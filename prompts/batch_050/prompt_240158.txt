Write a five-paragraph prose poem about a version of Kavka’s toxin puzzle for an audience of philosophically literate readers. In this version, an eccentric benefactor offers ten million dollars to anyone who can sincerely intend, at midnight, to swallow a vial at noon the following day. The vial would cause a day of severe illness but no permanent damage. The prize depends entirely on the verified midnight intention: the agent need not actually drink, and both agent and benefactor know this from the start.

The poem should be narrated by the agent between the offer and the appointed noon. Let each paragraph mark a distinct stage in the difficulty: first, the apparent ease of intending now what one need not do later; second, the discovery that the future self will have no forward-looking reason to drink; third, the failure of trying to sustain the intention through mere resolution, ritual, or self-manipulation; fourth, the strange state of mind after the prize has been secured; and fifth, the agent standing beside the unopened vial at noon, unsure whether refusing it is a rational abandonment, a broken commitment, or the successful completion of the bargain.

Use the sentence I intend to drink it at noon as a refrain exactly once in each paragraph. Its meaning should change through context and syntax without the sentence itself changing: it should function successively as plan, assurance, incantation, remnant, and open question. The form should thereby enact the puzzle’s central instability rather than merely describe it. Do not name Kavka in the poem, explain decision theory directly, or end with a tidy resolution; someone familiar with the puzzle should recognize that the poem understands why intending and later acting come apart.

Write in American English. Do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or explanatory commentary. Present the poem as five continuous paragraphs of polished prose.
