I run a small foundation that funds independent "shoot-down" tests of disputed scientific claims. Basically: you bring us a claim, and if it passes screening we pay a lab to try to break it -- not to confirm it, to break it. We get maybe 60 applications a year, two staff with undergrad science degrees do the screening, and decisions have to be defensible because rejected applicants appeal, and some of them have followings.

Our current rule is embarrassingly simple: we ask the claimant "what result would make you give up?" and if they can name one, we consider it falsifiable and move to budgeting. This stopped working. People have learned to name a defeat condition in the application and then explain away the defeat when it arrives. Also I reread my old Popper notes and realized our rule treats falsifiability as something the *claimant* performs (saying the magic words) when it's supposed to be something about the claim and how its defenders actually behave under pressure. But when I try to write down the real criterion I immediately hit the wall that apparently has a name, the Duhem thing: every test assumes auxiliaries (instruments work, controls are clean, the dose was absorbed), so strictly speaking *nothing* is ever conclusively falsified, and if that's right our whole foundation is funding theater.

So: write me an actual screening procedure. Ordered steps, what counts as a passing answer at each step, where the screener should stop with a no. It has to handle, at minimum:

- Probabilistic claims ("this dowsing method beats chance"), where no single failure refutes anything
- Rescue moves: how do we distinguish a legitimate refinement of a theory after a failed test from a patch that just plugs the hole? I want a rule a screener can apply, not a vibe
- Defenders who refuse to name any defeating observation, but whose claim still *feels* testable
- Claims that are only falsifiable with equipment that doesn't exist or budgets we don't have -- unfalsifiable in principle vs unfalsifiable at our price point
- Whatever we should do with the Duhem problem so it doesn't dissolve the whole exercise, but also doesn't let us pretend single experiments settle things

Then run it on three applications from last cycle (paraphrased):

1. A breathing technique claimed to cure chronic fatigue by raising blood oxygen. The applicants note that if the trial fails it would only show participants weren't performing the technique correctly.
2. An econometric model that predicts recessions within 18 months of an indicator crossing a threshold. It failed twice; both times the team added a lag parameter and resubmitted. The newest version hasn't failed yet.
3. A group claiming heavy screen use harms adolescent mental health, with a detailed mechanism. Three well-run studies found nothing; the group says the studies measured the wrong platforms and the wrong age windows. I personally suspect they're right, which is exactly why I want to know what the procedure says.

Also tell me honestly what the procedure can't do: where it has to hand off to a human, what parts of the actual philosophy I'm butchering by compressing them into a checklist, and whether "unfalsifiable" is even the right verdict for the cases where the *claim* is fine but its current defenders will absorb any result. And if one of the three applications is the kind of case where the right answer is "fund it but it will never be decided," say so plainly.
