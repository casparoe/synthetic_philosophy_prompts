I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, and I got into an argument at a reading group (don't ask why a bunch of devs are reading Bernard Williams, long story) about the famous saving-your-wife case and the one thought too many objection. The rough idea as I understood it: a man can only save one of two drowning people, his wife or a stranger, and if he saves his wife while thinking something like "in situations of this kind it is morally permissible to save one's wife," that deliberation is itself a moral defect — the fact that she's his wife should have been enough. Fine. But I tried to write out the principle underneath the objection in a general form and it seems to me to prove way too much, and now I'm stuck. Here's my version:

(1) The defect in the drowning case is a deliberative detour: the agent routes his reason to act through an impersonal principle (what is permissible for anyone in this situation) when a personal, particular reason (this is my wife) was available and should have sufficed on its own.
(2) So the general principle: whenever an agent could act directly from a personal or particular ground, consulting or relying on an impersonal rule instead is the same kind of defect — one thought too many.
(3) But that detour happens constantly and we actively encourage it. A pilot runs the pre-flight checklist instead of trusting her feel for the plane. A surgeon who adores her patient still verifies the dosage against the chart rather than going on instinct. A firefighter follows triage protocol instead of rushing first to the victim he recognizes. In each case a personal ground was available and the agent checked an impersonal rule instead or in addition.
(4) So by (2), all of them have one thought too many, and checklist culture in safety-critical fields is just institutionalized moral defectiveness.
(5) That's absurd — the checklist and the triage protocol are exactly where we *want* the detour. So (2) is false, and since (2) is just the generalized form of Williams's objection, the objection is either wrong or it proves too much.

My reading group split on this. Half said the objection really is just sentimentalism that collapses once you generalize it. The other half said my (2) isn't actually what Williams meant, but nobody could articulate the difference in a way that didn't sound like special pleading for spouses. So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If there's an equivocation hiding in there — say, between a rule *justifying* the action and a rule *serving* a motive that's already in place, or between the thought occurring to you and the thought doing the load-bearing work — spell it out explicitly, because "it's just different" is what my friends kept saying and it isn't an argument. I'd most like to keep (5), and honestly I'd like to keep the drowning-case intuition too, so if there's a way to have both I'd love it, but not at the price of a distinction that only works for that one case.

Also: tell me what would change your verdict.
