I have been teaching the Death in Damascus case in my undergraduate decision theory seminar, presenting it alongside Newcomb's problem as a second illustration of how an accurate predictor exposes the inadequacy of causal decision theory. The parallel seemed natural to me: in Newcomb's problem the predictor foretells your choice and the two-boxer's reasoning leaves money on the table; in the Damascus case Death foretells where you will be, and so, whether you stay or flee to Aleppo, you learn afterward that she was waiting for you. Both cases, I told my students, show deliberation defeating itself under accurate prediction.

What puzzles me is the standard escape my colleagues in the department keep offering. They say the rational Damascus agent should simply randomize — flip a fair coin to decide whether to stay or go — on the grounds that this is the unique equilibrium of the situation, and that the case therefore poses no special difficulty. But this seems to me to miss the point entirely. If Death is a genuinely perfect predictor, she foresaw the coin toss as well; one cannot evade an infallible forecaster by delegating one's choice to a device whose outcomes she knows in advance. Randomization merely relocates the puzzle one step back. So the case appears to establish something stronger than my colleagues admit: under perfect prediction there is no rational policy in Damascus at all — deliberation is strictly impossible there.

And this leads me to my real question. Since Newcomb's problem rests on the very same supposition — a predictor whose accuracy is supposed to survive whatever the agent does — shouldn't we conclude that both problems are built on an incoherent premise, rather than treating either as a datum against causal decision theory? That is, if perfect foreknowledge of an agent's choices cannot coherently be combined with those choices being up to the agent, then the interesting question is not what the rational agent should do in Damascus or in front of the boxes, but why philosophers ever took such cases to be well-posed tests of a decision theory. Am I right about this, and if not, where exactly does the argument go wrong?
