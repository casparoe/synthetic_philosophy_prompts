I am presenting a paper at a two-day decision theory workshop next month, and I would like help preparing for the question period. The paper defends an extension of Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility theory into distributive ethics. The core claim is that the same apparatus Buchak uses to make sense of ordinary risk-averse preferences over individual gambles -- weighting outcomes not by probability alone but by probability filtered through a risk function that gives extra weight to worse prospects -- can be repurposed to justify priority for the worst-off in social choice, so that prioritarianism falls out of a theory of rational evaluation rather than needing to be stipulated as a separate moral intuition. My further, more contentious move is to argue that this unification is an argument for the risk-weighted approach over orthodox expected utility theory: expected utility theorists, I claim, must treat aversion to personal risk and concern for the badly-off as two unrelated phenomena, whereas my framework derives both from one formal principle, and unity of explanation is a genuine theoretical virtue here rather than an aesthetic bonus.

The audience is roughly forty people. About half are formal decision theorists, several of them committed expected utility orthodox who have published on why probability weighting is a confusion of risk attitude with belief; there will be at least two people who know Buchak's own work very well and will spot immediately if I misstate what her axioms do and do not permit; there are three or four experimental economists who will want to talk about elicitation and identifiability of the risk function; and there is a contingent of political philosophers sympathetic to prioritarianism who may nonetheless object to being handed a rationale for their view that comes dressed as prudential rationality. I have thirty minutes of questions. What I most fear is being forced either to defend diachronic inconsistency or to retreat to a purely permissive reading of the theory, since my paper needs the stronger claim that risk-weighting is rationally required in the distributive case.

Please prepare a prep sheet in British English, structured in the following sections, in this order: first, "Reading the room" (about 150 words), a sketch of which factions will press hardest and what each is really trying to protect; second, "The objections in the order they will come" (the bulk of the sheet, 600-800 words), five or six objections phrased as the specific audience members would actually phrase them, each followed by what I should concede, what I should hold, and a two-sentence reply I could give aloud; third, "Where the reply is weakest" (about 150 words), an honest note on which of my replies would not survive a follow-up; fourth, "The question I am hoping nobody asks" (about 100 words), the single most damaging objection and the least-bad way to handle it if it comes; fifth, "The ninety-second version" (about 100 words), a compact spoken answer to the objection I am most likely to face, suitable for delivering when the chair signals that time is short.
