Pascal has that line of thought in the Pensées where he basically says justice on its own is weak and force on its own is tyranny, so the whole trick of politics is making the strong thing also be the just thing — and he's skeptical about how well that ever goes, since what people call justice tends to shift with borders and custom. Take the Nuremberg trials as the episode. Run Pascal's lens over them: you've got victors punishing the defeated, crimes defined after the fact, and a real question of whether the legal framing was genuine justice finally given teeth or just force dressing itself up as justice, which is exactly the fusion Pascal wanted but also exactly the thing he distrusted. Be careful with the mapping — who holds the force, who defines the justice, what the tu quoque objection looks like in Pascalian terms. And then go the other direction: does Nuremberg vindicate Pascal's suspicion, or does it show his framework is too cynical, i.e. that a tribunal can actually create justice rather than merely ratify power? Where does Pascal's account just run out and have nothing to say?
