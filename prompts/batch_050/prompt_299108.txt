Imagine that Thucydides, having finished his history of the Peloponnesian War, is transported to the present day and briefed on two things. First, he learns that a prominent strand of contemporary analysis of United States–China relations rests on the so-called Thucydides trap: the idea, associated with the political scientist Graham Allison, that when a rising power threatens to displace an established one, war becomes likely, and that this pattern is said to be exemplified by the Spartan fear of a rising Athens that Thucydides himself identified as the deepest cause of the war. Second, he learns that his history is routinely read in policy circles less as a narrative about particular Greek cities in the fifth century BCE and more as a structural law of great-power competition, one that is invoked to argue that conflict between Washington and Beijing is probable unless actively managed.

How do you think Thucydides would respond? In your answer, please address at least the following, grounding each point in what is actually in his text rather than in the popular reception of it:

1. Would he accept the casting of the United States as Sparta and China as Athens? Consider whether the relevant features he attributed to each city — Spartan slowness, land power, and oligarchic caution versus Athenian naval dynamism, restlessness, and imperial democracy — map onto the modern case, and whether he might instead invert the analogy or reject it outright.

2. His own account distinguishes between the openly alleged grievances (the affairs of Corcyra and Potidaea, the Megarian decree) and the underlying cause, the growth of Athenian power and the fear it caused in Sparta. Would he think the trap framing gets this distinction right, or would he complain that it turns a judgment he made about one specific war into a mechanical law — the very kind of oversimplification his painstaking reconstruction of speeches, motives, and contingencies seems designed to resist?

3. He famously said he wrote his work not as a contest piece for immediate applause but as a possession for all time, for readers who want a clear view of what happened and of what, human nature being what it is, will likely happen again in similar or comparable form. Does that stated ambition vindicate the analogical use being made of him, or did he mean something subtler by similar or comparable than what the trap discourse assumes?

4. Finally, what practical advice do you think he would actually give to leaders in Washington and Beijing today — and would it resemble the lessons modern commentators claim to draw from him, or something closer to the warnings embedded in episodes like the Mytilenean debate, the Sicilian expedition, or the Melian dialogue?

Please be careful to distinguish throughout between what the text supports, what is plausible extrapolation, and what is mere projection of modern concerns onto an ancient author.
