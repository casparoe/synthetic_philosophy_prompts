I'm in a reading group on paradoxes of rational choice and last week we got completely stuck on Quinn's self-torturer puzzle -- the guy with the device that delivers imperceptably increasing pain, where each tiny click up the dial comes with a big pile of cash, so step by step he walks himself into agony. Everyone in the group agreed the puzzle shows something weird about rationality, but we split on what. My friend Mara thinks the whole problem is just that the self-torturer can't precommit and if he could lock the dial he'd be fine. I suspect the deeper issue is that his preferences are intransitive, and I'm not even sure that's a defect.

So here's what I want you to build for me: a realistic, non-sci-fi scenario (no dials, no torture machines -- think medicine, consumer tech, housing, career decisions, whatever works) in which an agent faces pairwise choices and rationally prefers A over B, B over C, and C over A. A genuine cycle, not just a spectrum the agent could stop in the middle of. Each pairwise preference has to be compelling on reasons the agent herself actually endorses, not brute stipulation -- I don't want "she just prefers it," I want to feel the pull of each comparison. And the case has to block the standard money-pump objection: the agent faces each choice at most once and keeps whatever she ends up with, so nobody can siphon money off her by shuttling her around the loop repeatedly.

After the scenario, walk through each of the three pairwise preferences and explain why it looks rational from the inside, then state exactly which structural feature of your case stops the money pump from getting traction. Then give me the strongest objection from someone who thinks transitivity is a genuine requirement of rationality and can't just be engineered around, and tell me honestly whether your case survives it or whether that defender can force the intransitivity back into being a mistake. I'd like the whole thing as continuous prose, like an email -- no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
