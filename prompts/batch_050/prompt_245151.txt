A friend who has been reading about social choice theory explained the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem to me over dinner, and before I rely on his account I would like to know how much of it to trust. Some context: our department's committee is about to choose a rule for electing its chair from among four candidates, and my friend argues we are wasting our time agonising over the procedure. His summary ran as follows:

"The theorem is one of the bleakest results in the social sciences. It shows that every reasonable voting rule — first-past-the-post, instant-runoff, Borda count, approval voting, you name it — is manipulable: in any election conducted under it, at least one voter could secure a better outcome by misrepresenting their preferences rather than voting sincerely. The only rules that escape are dictatorships, where the result simply tracks one person's ballot. Tactical voting, in other words, is not a remediable defect of particular systems but a mathematical certainty built into collective decision-making as such, so reformers who promise a system immune to it are selling snake oil, and a committee fretting over which rule to adopt is rearranging deckchairs."

I am inclined to believe him, since the result is famous and he is usually careful, but something about the phrase "in any election" nags at me. Please audit his summary: separate what a social choice theorist would accept as accurate from what is subtly wrong, paying particular attention to the theorem's exact scope (which decision procedures it actually covers, and under what assumptions about voters' preferences and the number of alternatives) and to its logical strength (does it say manipulation is available in every election, or merely in some?). Then tell me which of the errors, if any, matter for practice, and whether his conclusion — that our committee may as well pick a rule at random, since all are equally gameable — follows from the corrected version.
