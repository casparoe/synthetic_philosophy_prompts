In the 1690s England faced a monetary crisis: the hand-struck silver coinage then in circulation had been so heavily clipped that much of it passed at well under its legal weight, while full-weight coins were melted down and exported because silver bullion was worth more abroad than the coin's face value. William Lowndes, Secretary to the Treasury, proposed recoining the currency at a raised nominal value — in effect debasing the standard by roughly a fifth, so that creditors would be repaid in lighter coins. John Locke argued against him that money's value resided in its silver content, that raising the nominal value was a disguised expropriation of creditors, and that the only honest remedy was a full recoinage at the old weight and fineness. Parliament followed Locke, and the Great Recoinage of 1696 restored the old standard at considerable public expense and with deflationary side effects; within a generation, moreover, commerce was increasingly carried on bank notes and other paper credit rather than silver.

Analyse this episode through the lens of George Berkeley's theory of money as developed in The Querist (1735), where — writing in a different context, namely Ireland's shortage of specie — he treated money not as a commodity whose value lies in its metal but as a sign, ticket or counter that records and transfers the power to command the labour of others, with the consequence that a well-managed paper currency could serve the monetary function as well as gold or silver.
