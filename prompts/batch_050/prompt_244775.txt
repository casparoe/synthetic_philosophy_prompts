I am a retired physicist, and late in life I have developed an interest in metaphysics. Next month I have been invited to give a colloquium talk in a university philosophy department, defending two connected claims: first, that a universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres, of exactly the same composition, temperature, and internal constitution, separated by some fixed distance in an otherwise empty symmetric space, is genuinely metaphysically possible, so that the Principle of Identity of Indiscernibles is at best contingently true; second, the stronger claim that modern physics gives us reason to think the principle fails in the actual world, since the standard treatment of quantum particles of the same kind appears not to distinguish one electron from another in any qualitative respect, and yet we count them as two. I am prepared for the reply that the spheres, or the particles, are weakly discernible -- that standing in the relation of being two units apart from something qualitatively identical to oneself, or of being entangled, individuates them -- but only half-prepared, because I suspect philosophers deploy this move in more than one way and I may not know all of them.

The audience will be mostly analytic metaphysicians: I gather there is at least one committed bundle theorist who denies bare particulars, one philosopher who defends some version of the principle, several philosophers of physics who will know more quantum field theory than I do at this point, and a room of graduate students inclined to be unimpressed by a scientist straying into their territory.

Please prepare my question-period briefing: the objections I should actually expect from this audience, ordered roughly by how damaging each would be to my two claims, each phrased the way such a philosopher would actually put it, with a two-minute reply to each and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Distinguish the objections that target my modal premise from those that concede the spheres' possibility but resist the quantum argument. Where a reply requires me to take sides on a dispute among the philosophers present (say, haecceitism versus the bundle theory), tell me which commitment I am being forced into and whether I can stay neutral. Finally, include the question I should hope nobody asks, and what to do if it comes anyway.
