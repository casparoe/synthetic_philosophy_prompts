Hey, so I'm a philosophy PhD student (I work on agency/responsibility stuff) and I'm TAing an undergrad seminar on free will this semester, about 14 students, one 75-minute meeting. We just finished the Frankfurt literature -- the original 1969 setup with Black and Jones, plus the standard pushback they tend to find compelling (the flicker-of-freedom move, the dilemma defense à la Widerker, that whole cluster).

Here's my problem: my students read Frankfurt cases and just nod along, or they reject them out of hand because the mind-reading device feels like cheating. Nobody has actually *felt* what it's like to be Jones, or more importantly to be Black -- the intervener who never has to do anything. I want an activity where they run into the structure themselves before I name it.

So: design me a runnable in-class game or role-play that gets them to viscerally grasp the claim that you can be morally responsible for something even when you couldn't have done otherwise. Ideally it should work in two phases: first, something where one student is secretly assigned the Black role (monitoring, ready to intervene, but the rules make intervention nearly costless to avoid if the "agent" behaves), so the class discovers after the fact that most agents never had alternatives and yet we still want to blame/praise them. Then second, a phase that lets them feel the force of the *objection* too -- like, they should notice on their own that there was always some tiny sign or prior inclination the intervener was reading, and start arguing about whether that's the "flicker" doing all the work. I don't want to hand them the flicker objection in the debrief, I want them to trip over it.

Practical constraints: no tech beyond a whiteboard and index cards, it has to run in about 50 minutes so we have 25 for debrief, and some of my students are pretty shy so it can't depend on everyone being comfortable performing in front of the class.

Give me: the full ruleset (roles, what info is hidden from whom, what the "decisions" actually are, any stakes/points), a rough timeline, how to run the reveal, and a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the game onto the actual dialectic (PAP, the counterfactual intervener, the flicker move, and the prior-sign worry). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong -- like students colluding, or the Black player intervening too eagerly and ruining the point -- and how to handle it. And be honest about which feature of the real debate the activity *can't* reproduce and I'll just have to supply in lecture; I suspect the actual vs. counterfactual sequence distinction is going to be the casualty here but maybe you see a way to keep it.
