Sidgwick ends The Methods of Ethics in an impasse he calls the dualism of practical reason: the axioms of egoism and of universal benevolence both appear self-evident to reflection, yet they can prescribe incompatible courses of action, and he despairs of any theoretical reconciliation short of postulating a divinely ordered universe in which duty and interest coincide. Please argue that this impasse is not a genuine standoff between two coordinate intuitions but an artifact of Sidgwick's own framing -- specifically, of his granting the egoistic principle the same axiomatic status as the principles of impartial benevolence and prudence. Your argument should do at least three things: first, explain why the self-evidence criteria Sidgwick himself lays down (clarity, consistency with other beliefs, and consensus among competent judges) arguably count against the egoistic axiom in a way they do not count against the axioms of benevolence and prudence; second, address the strongest reason for thinking egoism is nonetheless rationally compulsory -- that a person's own good presents itself to her with a practical authority no merely impartial standpoint can simply override; and third, say what follows for Sidgwick's pessimistic conclusion that, absent a religious postulate harmonizing duty and interest, practical reason is left incoherent with itself.
