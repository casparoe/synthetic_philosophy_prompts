Ok so I teach an undergrad comparative ethics seminar and I'm completly sick of the baby at the well doing all the heavy lifting in my Mencius unit. Every year it's the baby, every year the students nod, and every year half of them walk out thinking Mencius and Xunzi are basically saying the same thing in two different vocabularies. I want a thought experiment built to order that makes that reading impossible, and I want you to prove the case actually meets the spec rather than just asserting that it does.

Here's the spec. Construct a scenario, present-day and fully realistic (a workplace, a school, a court diversion program, something like that — no sage kings, no ritual masters, no appeals to how things worked in the Warring States), in which Mencius's and Xunzi's pictures of human nature agree about what's going wrong with the person in the story but prescribe two concrete, incompatible remedies. Roughly what I have in mind: on the Mencian reading the fix looks like removing whatever is crushing or starving the sprouts so an existing tendency can grow on its own, while on the Xunzian reading the fix looks like imposed training, habituation, and deliberate artifice that builds dispositions the person doesn't already have. Your job is to engineer the case so that which remedy is actually right turns on ONE identifiable feature of the situation, and everything else is held fixed.

Constraints on the scenario itself: one or two paragraphs max; the person in it must be an ordinary adult or teenager (a shift supervisor, a kid in a diversion program, a new hire), the stakes must be ordinary (not life and death), and both remedies must be things a real decision-maker in the story could plausibly choose between this week. It has to work for second-years who've had exactly one lecture on each thinker, so nothing that depends on fine-grained textual scholarship.

After the scenario, give me: (1) each theory's verdict — the diagnosis, the prescribed remedy, and the reasoning that gets there, so I can see the case generates the verdicts rather than stipulating them; (2) the single load-bearing feature and what happens to the case if you remove it (does it collapse into both remedies working, or into a coin flip?); (3) the strongest attack from whichever side your case treats worse — e.g., if the case favors Mencius, how does a Xunzian argue the environmental fix only looks like it works, and vice versa; (4) an honest note on whether you cheated anywhere by baking a verdict into the setup.

Then, after all the prose, give me a summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "scenario_one_line" (the setup in a single sentence), "mencius" (an object with "diagnosis", "remedy", "predicted_outcome"), "xunzi" (same three subfields), "load_bearing_feature", "case_without_feature" (what happens when it's removed), "strongest_objection", and "stipulation_check" (one sentence on whether the verdicts were generated or assumed).
