I'm a PhD student in value theory and I'm stuck on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism — i.e. what, if anything, all pleasures have in common (a hot bath, finally getting a joke, someone else's misfortune, whatever). For a chapter draft I need a thought experiment built to order, not another rehash of the experience machine.

Build me one realistic scenario where a felt-quality theory of pleasure (pleasures are unified by a shared phenomenal character — there's something pleasantness feels like) and an attitudinal theory (a sensation counts as a pleasure iff the subject has the right intrinsic pro-attitude toward having it, whatever it feels like) give different verdicts about whether the subject is experiencing pleasure at a specific moment, while the phenomenal character of the underlying sensation is held fixed across the whole scenario. Constraints:

- no neurosci-fi, no wireheading, no stipulative drugs — the shift has to be psychologically ordinary (satiety, duration, repetition, context change, that kind of thing)
- it's one continuous sensation, and the subject can honestly attest that the raw feel didn't change
- don't cheat by just stipulating that the sensation "stops being pleasant" — whether that's even a coherent description is exactly what's at issue
- the scenario itself in one paragraph, under 250 words

Then the demonstration part: state each theory's verdict at the moment the subject's attitude flips, why the theory is committed to that verdict, and which bullet each one has to bite. Say which feature of the case is doing the work and what happens if you remove it — does it collapse back into both theories agreeing? Finally, a paragraph on what the case actually shows about heterogeneity: does it support the line that pleasures have nothing in common except our stance toward them, and does the felt-quality theorist have a viable reply (e.g. claiming the phenomenology does change, just too subtly for the subject to self-report — is that move even available, and what does it cost in terms of making the theory empirically unanswerable)?

Close with \answer{felt-quality} or \answer{attitudinal} for whichever theory the case embarrasses more.
