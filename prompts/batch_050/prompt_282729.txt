I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of history and historical explanation, and I am setting this year's take-home final. Students have covered the Great Divergence debate and the theory of contrastive explanation — the idea that a request to explain P is always shorthand for explaining why P rather than some foil Q, so that a why-question is well posed only relative to a chosen contrast. I want one essay question, answerable in two hours, on the question "Why did sustained industrial growth begin in Britain rather than in China?" — the modern heir of the Needham question about why modern science and industry developed in Europe and not in China despite China's earlier technological lead.

The question must be unanswerable by reciting the usual inventory of candidate causes (coal endowments, colonial resources, high wages, institutions, scientific culture). Specifically, it should force students to examine what happens to each candidate explanation as the foil varies: explaining Britain against Qing China taken as a whole is a different explanatory task from explaining Britain against the Yangtze Delta region alone, or against Song China, or against a stipulated counterfactual China that never built the Grand Canal. A strong answer should have to address which choice of foil is historically and explanatorily defensible, what it even means for "why Britain and not China?" to be well posed, and whether the Needham-style framing — why did China fail to industrialize? — covertly assumes a default expectation that industrialization was the natural course of things, so that Europe's deviation is what truly demands explanation.

Please write three things. First, the exam question itself, phrased exactly as it would appear on the paper. Second, a model answer of roughly 600 words. Third, a marking scheme out of 20, built around five must-hit points with the marks each carries, and for each point a one-line note on how weaker candidates typically fumble it. The scheme should make explicit what distinguishes a first-class answer from a merely competent one — for instance, whether the candidate recognizes that the choice of contrast determines which evidence is even relevant to the question, rather than treating the foil as mere rhetorical framing, and whether they can distinguish the claim that a question is ill posed from the claim that it is simply hard.
