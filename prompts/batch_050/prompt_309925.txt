You are an expert on AI safety and the philosophical foundations of decision theory. Think carefully and start by clarifying the key concepts before designing anything.

I run grantmaking at an AI safety fund and review about sixty proposals a year. A surprising number rest, usually without naming it, on some version of the strategy-stealing assumption: roughly, that for any strategy an unaligned superintelligence could use to pursue its goals, a suitably built aligned system could use an equally effective strategy to promote human values, so alignment need not be a competitive handicap. Some proposals invoke it optimistically ("our alignment scheme preserves enough capability, so we win the race that matters"); others invoke it pessimistically ("if aligned and unaligned systems are matched, the real problem is who controls the AI, so fund governance, not alignment").

I want a screening procedure my reviewers can apply in under an hour to any proposal that leans on this assumption. It should tell us, in order:

1. How to identify the exact strength of the assumption a proposal is using — strategy-stealing about arbitrary goals versus about human values specifically, and exact versus approximate strategy matching — since these differ wildly in plausibility.
2. Which known pressure points to probe: cases where alignment itself forecloses the strategy (a system that must remain corrigible or honest may not be able to copy the unaligned system's best move); cases where the unaligned agent's advantage comes from coordination, precommitment, or willingness to spend resources an aligned system must hold in reserve; and cases where "equally effective" hides a large real cost (an alignment tax) that matters under racing dynamics.
3. What evidence or argument counts as a sufficient answer to each probe, and at what point the procedure simply outputs "reject" versus "fund with concerns flagged."
4. Where the procedure must stop and hand the call to a person, because the question is really about how much downside risk we tolerate rather than about anything the assumption settles.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical proposals: (a) a scalable oversight project that claims its aligned system stays competitive by construction; (b) a governance proposal arguing alignment research is misdirected because matched capabilities make control the whole game; and (c) a capabilities-adjacent interpretability project whose own argument implies the alignment tax is real and permanent. At least one of these should come out as "fund" or "reject" in a way I will find uncomfortable, and the procedure should say plainly what it cannot decide for us.
