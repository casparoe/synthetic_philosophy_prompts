I sit on the board of a grant-making foundation that funds animal welfare work, and we are deadlocked over how to allocate this year's final £2 million. Our standing policy ranks applications by 'neurological complexity', treating neuron count as a rough proxy for a species' capacity to suffer: the more neurons, the higher the priority. Two proposals have exposed a fault line in that policy. The first would fund a sanctuary and lifetime veterinary care for a few dozen chimpanzees retired from research — large-brained animals that score very high on the policy's per-individual metric. The second would fund research into whether standard slaughter methods in prawn farming cause suffering, potentially changing conditions for hundreds of billions of animals a year, each with a nervous system orders of magnitude smaller than a chimpanzee's. Half the board says the policy already answers the question: welfare attaches to individuals, complexity measures the depth of what an individual can experience, and chimpanzees therefore come first. The other half replies that the policy measures the wrong thing — that if neuron counts are our proxy at all, they function better as evidence about the probability that a creature can suffer than as a measure of how much it can, and that aggregating even modest probabilities across hundreds of billions of prawns swamps the chimpanzees; to rank by per-animal brain size, they say, is simply to prefer animals that resemble us.

Draft the memo I will circulate before the board votes, recommending which proposal to fund — or whether to divide the money, and on what grounds. The memo should have an actionable bottom line the board can adopt or reject, should engage seriously with both readings of what makes brain size morally relevant (depth of an individual's experience versus probability that there is any experience at all), and should give the strongest honest statement of the losing side's case rather than a caricature. Structure it as exactly five numbered sections — the recommendation; the case for it; the strongest objection; the reply to that objection; and any conditions or revisions to the ranking policy proposed — each a short paragraph of three to five sentences, with no preamble or sign-off. Write in British English.
