So I got into a dumb argument with a friend about the weather app on my phone and now I cant let it go.

Here's the setup. Modern weather forecasting runs on models of the atmosphere that are, as far as I understand, completely deterministic. Feed the simulation the same starting state twice and you get the same result every time, no dice involved. And yet the output the whole practice delivers to the public is probabilistic: 30% chance of rain tomorrow, 70% chance the hurricane turns east. The way forecasters actually generate these numbers is ensemble forecasting -- they nudge the initial conditions by tiny amounts, rerun the model a bunch of times, and count how many runs produce rain. So the probability comes from the spread of the ensemble, not from anything random in the physics.

Meanwhile Lorenz, the guy who basically founded this field, summed the whole thing up as: "Chaos: When the present determines the future but the approximate present does not approximately determine the future."

Take that seriously for a second. If the present determines the future, then tomorrow's weather is already fixed, and there is no 30% out there in the world -- it will rain or it won't. So what exactly does the practice of probabilistic forecasting presuppose about what probability IS? Is the 30% a confession about the limits of our measurements (we only know the approximate present, and the ensemble spread is a map of our ignorance)? Is it a disguised claim about frequencies -- on days like this it rained 30% of the time? Or is it something about the model itself rather than the atmosphere?

Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of the practice: what view of chance makes "30% chance of rain" a coherent, honest thing to say given deterministic physics, and which views of probability (objective chance in the world, degrees of belief, long-run frequencies) does the practice quietly rule out or sit badly with? Also: could a Laplacean demon, who knows the exact present, coherently issue a probabilistic forecast, and if not, does that mean our probabilities are really about us and not about the weather? And tell me what would have to change in the practice if you swapped in a different answer -- e.g., if probability is purely about ignorance, is there any reason to prefer the ensemble method over just stating one best guess plus an honest "I don't know"?

Don't give me a balanced menu of options. Pick a definite position on what the practice actually presupposes and whether it's coherent, and argue for it. If part of the practice is genuinely compatible with several views and you're just guessing, say which part and why, but commit on the main question.
