I’m putting together a handout for a reading group on uncertain evidence, and I keep loosing track of which updating rules are genuinely different rather than just redescribing one another. Please make a 10–12 question diagnostic quiz called Which updating rule do your judgements actually fit? Each question should be a short concrete case with three or four possible verdicts, phrased in terms of what the agent should now believe rather than with technical labels. The cases must include: glimpsing a ball in bad light; a partly reliable weather app; an unsure memory about locking a door; two successive reports where the order seems irrelevant; evidence that fixes an expectation but not probabilities across a neat partition; and evidence that supplies only a range of acceptable probabilities.

First give a compact decision tree, then the quiz and a scoring key. The leaves should include ordinary conditionalisation, conditionalisation on a richer proposition describing the experience itself, Jeffrey conditionalisation, minimum-relative-entropy updating, a no-revision/suspension response, and a custom “bounded Jeffrey” rule: the experience imposes an interval for each cell of a coarse partition, and probabilities inside each cell are preserved where possible. If some of these cannot be separated by the cases, say so openly rather than pretending the quiz is more discriminating than it is.

Build the tree from the underlying choices — certainty versus probability shift, fixed versus interval constraints, preservation versus revision of within-cell odds, whether order matters, and whether uncertainty about the source blocks updating — rather than simply listing famous rules. Mark any unoccupied answer combinations as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The scoring key should say which answers are decisive and which only provide evidence for a classification. Begin directly with the decision tree, with no prefatory explanation, and use British English.
