I’m drafting a philosophical novel about a declining seaside town, and I’ve got a council-meeting scene where the characters argue about whether they owe more to their own neighbours than to distant strangers. Build me a thought experiment I can adapt for the scene. The council can fund only one of two realistic flood-protection schemes: one protecting part of its own town, the other protecting a poorer town abroad where the same money would save more people. Engineer the case so that three familiar positions come apart: first, that compatriots have special claims because they share institutions and a common civic life; second, that special claims arise from participation in a cooperative scheme, such as paying taxes or contributing labour; and third, that everyone’s welfare counts equally regardless of nationality. Include at least one local resident who benefits from the town’s institutions without really contributing to them, one foreigner who has materially contributed to the town despite lacking membership in it, and one resident whose membership is secure but whose reciprocal contribution is genuinely ambiguous. No sci-fi, no ticking bomb, no obviously corrupt officials, and no making one project useless just to force the answer.

After giving the scenario, show me that it does what I need. State precisely what each of the three positions says the council may or ought to prioritise, and make sure at least one version of nationalism converges with cosmopolitanism for a different reason while the reciprocity-based view dissents. Identify the feature of the case doing most of the philosophical work, say what happens if that feature is removed, and don’t let the scenario simply stipulate its own verdicts. Finish with the strongest objection available to whichever position comes out looking worst, and say whether that objection reveals a defect in the thought experiment or an uncomfortable consequence of the position itself. Please answer in British English.
