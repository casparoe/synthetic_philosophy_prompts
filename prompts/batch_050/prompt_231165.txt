my uncle has this line he wheels out at family gatherings: looking at a photograph of someone is literally *seeing* them, same as seeing a star through a telescope. the star's light left years ago, the star might be dead by now, but nobody says you don't see the star. so when i look at a 1978 photo of my grandmother, i'm seeing her, and anyone who says i'm "just looking at a piece of paper" has to deny telescopes too. he uses it to argue photography goes on the side of windows, mirrors, and periscopes -- prosthetics for the eye -- not on the side of paintings, which only show you what somebody made. (i later found out philosophers do actually fight about roughly this, under the label of whether photographs are "transparent," so it's not just him.)

audit the analogy for me. i suspect it proves too much -- is a voicemail *hearing* her? is a fossil *seeing* the dinosaur? is a death mask *seeing* the face? -- but i can't pin down where it goes wrong, if it does.

give me:

1. a correspondence table with exactly these columns: "element in the telescope/window case" | "alleged photographic counterpart" | "holds / strains / breaks" | "why". rows should cover at least: the light path from object to eye, the time delay, the medium between scene and viewer, what happens to the image if the scene changes while you watch, the role of human intention in producing the image, and how the viewer can check what they're seeing (leaning sideways, waiting for something to move).

2. a verdict: does "we literally see people in photographs" survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion? and sort the edge cases -- a live CCTV feed, a broadcast with a ten-second delay, a photgraph developed and printed years later, a heavily retouched portrait -- saying which side of the line each lands on and which column of the table does the sorting.

3. if the telescope analogy fails, what's the better analogy for what a photograph actually does, and does it leave my uncle anything he wanted?
