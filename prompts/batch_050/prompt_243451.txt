Philosophy hobbyist question. I've been reading around the literature on dispositions — fragility, solubility, that whole corner — and I know the two standard troublemakers: finks, where the object loses the disposition the moment the stimulus arrives (the wire hooked up to a device that makes it non-conductive the instant it's touched), and masks, where the disposition stays but something else blocks the showing (the glass wrapped in bubble wrap). Fine. But I keep running into a third pattern in actual cases and I can't find anyone giving it its own name.

The pattern: the stimulus shows up, and the object responds to it — but the response is to grow or deploy its own blocker, so the manifestation never happens. Examples:

1. Some metals are highly reactive, but the instant air or water touches them they form a thin skin of oxide that seals the surface, and then nothing more happens. Reactivity was there, trigger arrived, no visible reaction — but the reason is the metals own doing.
2. I knew someone who was, in the ordinary sense, completely outspoken — she'd tell anyone exactly what she thought — except that the moment a real confrontation started she would freeze up and go polite. The very thing that should trigger the outspokenness is what switches on the freezing.
3. Lots of drugs work like this: you take a blood-pressure medication and the body promptly releases counteracting hormones, so the pressure barely drops. The drug works perfectly well on tissue in a dish.

It's not a fink, because nothing is lost — the metal is still reactive, the woman is still outspoken, the drug still works on the tissue. And it's not a mask in the usual sense, because no third party is doing the blocking; the blocker is part of how the object itself operates, and crucially it's switched on by the stimulus rather than standing by beforehand. In the bubble-wrap case you can peel the wrap off and keep the glass; here you can't remove the blocker without changing what the thing is.

So: does this configuration have an established name? If it counts as a special case of masking, what marks these cases off within that category, and do the standard analyses of dispositions handle them the same way as ordinary masks or differently? And if it has no name, coin one, define it, and tell me where the edges are — e.g. does it matter that the blocker is triggered by the stimulus rather than merely present, and does case 2 really belong with 1 and 3 or is it secretly a fink after all?

One more thing: please be explicit about which parts of your answer are settled or widely accepted among people who work on this, and which parts are contested or just your own proposal. I'd rather get "nobody has named this, here's my suggestion" than a guess dressed up as textbook.
