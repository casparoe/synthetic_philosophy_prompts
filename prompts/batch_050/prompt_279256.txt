A colleague in my department has produced the following argument. I cannot accept its conclusion, but I cannot cleanly locate the misstep either, and I would like a diagnosis.

Background: Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles says, roughly, that numerically distinct things must differ in at least one property. Max Black's well-known thought experiment imagines a universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres floating one meter apart in otherwise empty space, and takes the intelligibility of that scenario to refute the principle. My colleague believes he can run Black's machinery in reverse:

(1) For any objects x and y, if x and y share every property, then x is numerically identical to y.

(2) Every property of a physical object is either an intrinsic qualitative property (mass, shape, composition, and so on) or is grounded without remainder in the intrinsic qualitative properties of that object and whatever else exists in its world; there are no irreducible relations, and no "thisnesses" over and above qualitative profile.

(3) There is a conceivable — and therefore genuinely possible — world containing exactly two uniform iron spheres, one meter apart, perfectly alike in every intrinsic qualitative respect, in an otherwise empty and symmetric space.

(4) In that world, every intrinsic qualitative property of either sphere is a property of the other.

(5) Every remaining property of either sphere is, by (2), grounded in the intrinsic qualitative properties of the world's contents; since the spheres agree on all of those and the world is symmetric, every such grounded property possessed by one sphere is possessed by the other as well.

(6) Hence the two spheres share every property; by (1) they are numerically identical; so the world described in (3) contains exactly one sphere, contradicting its own description.

(7) Therefore (3) describes no genuine possibility: the apparent conceivability of two indiscernible spheres is an illusion. And since nothing in the argument turned on the spheres being spheres, the same verdict applies to any alleged pair of perfectly indiscernible objects — two electrons in identical states, mirror-image twins in mirror-image rooms, an entire universe duplicated wholesale. There cannot exist numerically distinct objects that differ in no respect whatsoever.

The conclusion seems to me to prove far too much. Physics routinely traffics in enormous populations of qualitatively identical particles, and the leap from a worry about one contrived thought experiment to the outright impossibility of qualitative duplicates anywhere is enormous. I would most like to retain (1) in some respectable form, and I would also like to retain the thought that the world in (3) is at least intelligible. But I suspect I cannot keep everything.

Please do the following: (a) identify the premise or inferential step at which the argument goes wrong; (b) explain why that step looks innocent on a first reading, including which true principle it trades on or might be confused with; (c) price each of the main rescue strategies — restricting (1) to a weaker reading and letting (3) refute only that weaker version; denying (2) by admitting irreducible spatiotemporal or haecceitistic facts; attacking the inference from conceivability to possibility in (3); and rejecting (1) outright in favor of bare particulars or brute bundling; and (d) state which rescue you find cheapest and why.

End your answer with a final line of exactly the form \answer{<premise or step label>: <gloss of at most eight words>}, naming the point of failure you take to be primary.
