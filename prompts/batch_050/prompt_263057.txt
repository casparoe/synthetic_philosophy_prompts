I am a novelist in the early stages of a historical novel set in a provincial European town in the decades before a revolution, and I am applying to a small arts-and-ideas fund for a nine-month grant. The grant requires a research component, not just the novel, so I need a two-page proposal for a project on the following half-formed thought.

Historical scholarship has long been warned against reading the past as a march toward the present, but I suspect the problem takes a distinctive and underexamined form in historical fiction. The historian may distort the past by selection and emphasis; the novelist, who must know how everything turns out and must give characters a coherent inner life, seems to face a structural dilemma: either my characters reason as plausible inhabitants of their own time, which makes the narrative feel to modern readers like a costume drama full of fools and villains, or they reason with a foreknowledge borrowed from their author's hindsight, which makes them feel like contemporary people in period dress. My suspicion is that this dilemma is not forced -- that there are craft techniques and narrative structures that generate the felt contingency of living forward rather than the felt inevitability of being looked back upon -- but that the existing discussions of Whig history, aimed at historians, give the novelist almost no usable guidance.

Turn this into a proposal. State the question precisely enough that it has an answer; explain why discussions of teleology in historiography do not already settle it for fiction; describe what I would actually do over nine months, including what reading, what comparative study of existing historical novels, and what drafting experiments the project would involve; say what a deliverable looks like (I am imagining an essay alongside the manuscript, but tell me if that is wrong); and specify what would count as the project having failed. I will add citations myself, so describe positions by their content rather than by reference.
