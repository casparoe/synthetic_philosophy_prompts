I'm a statistician by training — my working vocabulary is parameters and realizations, estimands and estimators, conditional expectations given covariates, identifiability, and the ways small samples betray you. I recently joined a reading group with some philosophers of biology, and we spent an evening on a passage from Chapter III of the first edition of Darwin's On the Origin of Species: "Owing to this struggle for life, any variation, however slight and from whatever cause proceeding, if it be in any degree profitable to an individual of any species, in its infinitely complex relations to other organic beings and to external nature, will tend to the preservation of that individual, and will generally be inherited by its offspring. The offspring, also, will thus have a better chance of surviving, for, of the many individuals of any species which are periodically born, but a small number can survive." What struck me is Darwin's modal, probabilistic phrasing — "will tend to the preservation," "a better chance of surviving" — which to my ear sounds like talk of an underlying parameter or expectation, not of a realized count of offspring.

The debate I then got walked into is this. The old tautology objection says that if the "fittest" are simply defined as those who in fact survive and reproduce, then "survival of the fittest" is empty — survival of the survivors. The standard reply is the propensity interpretation of fitness: an organism's fitness is not its actual reproductive output but a probabilistic disposition, roughly its expected reproductive contribution given its traits and circumstances, of which realized offspring numbers are only noisy, fallible manifestations. Critics of that reply press further: propensities seem to belong as much to the environment as to the organism; it's obscure what a propensity amounts to in a single, unrepeated case; and some argue that fitness so construed can't bear the causal-explanatory weight evolutionary theory places on it. I followed all of this only loosely, and I kept wanting to reach for my own tools.

Do the translation honestly, without quietly importing the assumption that the statistical framing is the correct one. Then give me a residue report: which parts of the debate translate cleanly (my hunch is that the tautology objection becomes a point about defining an estimand in terms of its own noisy estimate, but tell me if that's wrong or only half-right), which parts you had to distort to fit, and which parts have no expression in statistical vocabulary at all. For each piece of residue, say whether it marks a genuine gap in my framework or a place where the philosophers may be mistaking modeling choices for facts about nature. Finally, come back to the Darwin passage: on your translation, is his "will tend" and "better chance" language already committed to the propensity reading, is he describing realized outcomes, or is the passage genuinely ambiguous in a way that matters for this dispute?
