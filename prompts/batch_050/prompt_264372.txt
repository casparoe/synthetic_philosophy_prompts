Construct a thought experiment that isolates, as cleanly as the literature on fakes and originals permits, the question whether an original artwork's value over a perceptually indistinguishable copy rests on anything beyond (a) market or investment value, (b) the displeasure of being deceived, and (c) social status. I want the case built to the following specifications.

1. The scenario. A museum holds an original painting and a duplicate that is perceptually indistinguishable from it by any means available to viewers, including expert examination under laboratory conditions (you choose the provenance of the duplicate -- conservator-made with the artist's cooperation, later molecular fabrication, or something else -- and must justify why that choice best serves the design). The museum can preserve only one of the two, and the decision-maker knows all the relevant facts and will never be found out, so that neither deception nor reputation can enter her reasoning. Stipulate away monetary value (the works are contractually unsalable, or the stipulation of your choice). The load-bearing twist: the duplicate realizes the artist's settled intentions better than the original does -- the original's pigments shifted after completion in ways the artist publicly lamented, or the final state of the original reflects a printer's or foundry's error the artist tried and failed to reverse, while the duplicate corrects exactly this. Keep the whole scenario to two paragraphs and make it physically mundane: no perfect duplicators unless you can show the case needs one.

2. The demonstration. Show that the case generates divergent verdicts from three positions, each stated in your own words rather than by label alone: the view that all aesthetic value supervenes on perceptible properties of the object; the view that an artwork's identity and value are fixed partly by its history of production; and the view that the artwork is whatever best embodies the artist's intentions. The first position must come out indifferent or favoring the duplicate; the second must favor the original; the third must be genuinely torn, and you should say exactly where it is torn. If any verdict has to be stipulated rather than derived, the case has failed and you should say so.

3. The design notes. Identify the single feature of the case that does the work in separating the first two positions, and show that removing it collapses the case into an ordinary deception scenario in which the copy's inferiority is just the inferiority of being lied to. Then give the strongest available response for the supervenience view: that the decision-maker's pull toward the original is a collector's or pilgrim's attitude, continuous with preferring the napkin the artist once used, and therefore not an aesthetic preference at all. Adjudicate this exchange rather than merely reporting it.

4. The honest coda. State whether your case, if it works, establishes that the original's surplus value is aesthetic value or only that it is some kind of non-monetary value (historical, relational, memorial), and explain what hangs on that distinction for the broader debate. In particular: may your analysis invoke the notion that the original has a special presence or aura? Only if you cash the notion out in terms of a specifiable property or relation; otherwise leave it out and say what, if anything, is lost by leaving it out.
