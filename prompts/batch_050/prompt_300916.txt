The question as set was the following: In his writings on analogy, from Gödel, Escher, Bach onward, Douglas Hofstadter defends the view that creativity is not the conjuring of ideas out of nothing but the artful variation of existing themes. On this model, every concept carries adjustable "knobs" — features that can be twiddled while the underlying conceptual skeleton remains intact — and the creative act consists in sensing which knobs may slip, and how far, in a given situation. (i) Explain this knob-twiddling model and contrast it with the romantic picture of creativity as ex nihilo invention. (ii) Illustrate the model with one recognized creative achievement, identifying the theme, the knobs, and the twiddling. (iii) Critics object that the model accounts only for incremental novelty and cannot explain genuinely revolutionary creativity — radical breaks, paradigm shifts, jumping out of the system. Does the model have the resources to answer this objection? Defend your answer.

Here is the student's answer in full. (i) On Hofstadter's model, concepts are not rigid definitions but flexible structures equipped with knobs, parameters that can be adjusted while the concept remains recognizable. Creativity is the perception of slippability: the creative person is the one who sees which features of a concept are load-bearing and which can be changed without collapsing it. This is opposed to the romantic picture of the genius who invents from nothing, unbound by anything that already exists. On Hofstadter's view that picture is a myth. Even the most original work is a variation on a theme, and it merely looks like creation from nothing because we cannot trace the theme being varied. (ii) Take Einstein's special relativity. The theme is classical mechanics. Einstein twiddled the one knob everyone else treated as welded in place, the absoluteness of simultaneity, while holding fixed what his predecessors had been prepared to adjust. What appears to be a conceptual revolution is therefore, on this model, an unusually deep adjustment of a parameter within an inherited framework, and the depth of the knob distinguishes the revolutionary from the tinkerer. (iii) The objection, however, succeeds in part. Hofstadter himself acknowledges a second mode of creativity, jumping out of the system, in which the creator twiddles no knob within the theme but abandons the theme altogether and begins a new one, as when atonal composers simply discarded tonality rather than varying it. So the model explains incremental variation but must be supplemented by this distinct mechanism to cover revolutionary cases. The model is further supported by the success of evolutionary algorithms and genetic programming, which generate random variations and select the fittest results, demonstrating that variation plus selection is sufficient to produce genuinely creative outcomes. Creativity, then, is mostly knob-twiddling, occasionally system-jumping, and always fundamentally a matter of generating variants.
