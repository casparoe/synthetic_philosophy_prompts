After forty years in experimental physics, I have retired and begun reading political philosophy properly rather than in airport-book form. I am sympathetic to the thought that a government should be judged by what it reliably delivers: safety, sanitation, schooling, stable prices, low corruption, protection from arbitrary violence. I am also suspicious of my own sympathy, because in the laboratory a clean readout can conceal a badly posed question.

Consider an ideal-type constitutional device, assembled from familiar debates around Singapore and contemporary China but not intended as an empirical test about either. A state is governed by a Covenant of Measurable Order. Every seven years an insulated Audit Court publishes a ledger: homicide and serious assault, road deaths, median real household income after housing costs, particulate pollution, waiting times for essential surgery, lower-secondary completion, and confidence in the courts measured by an independent survey. If at least five of seven indicators meet thresholds fixed in advance, restrictions on assembly, an internal security service, and the executive’s power to rule by decree are renewed automatically. If three or more indicators fail, a competitive election must be called within ninety days. Citizens may challenge the measurements before the Audit Court, may propose better indicators for the next cycle, and may sue ministers for personal corruption; but they may not vote directly on renewal except through the trigger of failure, and during a successful cycle they may not campaign to abolish the Covenant itself.

My question is not whether such an arrangement would be wise, nor which real country resembles it. I want to know what it presupposes. What must legitimacy be, if authorisation can be renewed by outputs whilst the people are forbidden to reject the whole framework during a successful run? Does the Covenant treat citizens primarily as beneficiaries whose welfare is to be secured, as patients under competent care, as wards, or as agents who retain a standing power to say no even when things are going well? Which rival views does it quietly rule out: consent renewed at each generation, rights as side-constraints rather than policy outcomes, republican freedom as absence of domination, or the idea that some procedures are valuable even when inefficient?

Please separate what the device strictly presupposes from what merely fits it comfortably. In particular, examine the circularity at its centre: authority is justified by the ledger, yet the thresholds, weightings, inspectors, and the very distinction between order and oppression are fixed by that same authority. The Audit Court is meant to break the circle, but its independence is justified by trust rather than by its own performance; can a performance theory account for that trust, or must it smuggle in a non-performance standard? Also ask how the Covenant distributes responsibility: if median income rises because of global conditions, or falls because a previous generation consumed the seed corn, whom does the ledger credit or blame, and what theory of collective agency is required to make that sensible rather than merely administratively convenient?

Finally, say what would have to change if the presupposed theory were replaced. If legitimacy were grounded in recurring consent, in capabilities rather than aggregate outcomes, or in non-domination rather than order, which parts of the Covenant would become incoherent, which could survive as mere policy, and which would reveal that prosperity and order were never justifications for rule but only preconditions for asking who may rule? No bibliography or citations, please; reason from the design itself.
