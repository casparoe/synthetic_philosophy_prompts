So my two friends Marta and Devon got into this huge argument last weekend and it's still bugging me, so I want an outside verdict. Some background: a mutual friend of ours, Priya, opened a little bakery about a year ago. Everyone knows most small businesses fail, and the neighborhood already had two bakeries, but Priya was completely convinced from day one that hers would make it, and honestly it sort of is making it -- she's still open and just hired someone. Marta said Priya was still *wrong* to be that confident at the start, because she had no good evidence, just vibes. She brought up some essay she'd read a summary of, about a shipowner who talked himself into believing his old ship was seaworthy without really inspecting it, and the point was supposedly that believing stuff on weak evidence is morally wrong even if nothing bad happens, because of what it does to you as a person. Devon pushed back with a different essay he half-remembers, by William James I think, defending that sometimes you're *allowed* -- maybe even supposed -- to believe beyond the evidence when the choice is unavoidable and the belief itself helps make the thing true. His point was: if Priya had "been rational" and just gone off the base rates, she'd never have opened the bakery at all, so her confidence wasn't a mistake, it was basically load-bearing.

Then it went back and forth for like an hour. Marta said the James thing only applies where evidence genuinely can't settle the question, but here the statistics were right there and Priya just ignored them, so it's exactly the shipowner case. Devon said that's too fast, because the base rates describe *other people's* bakeries, and Priya's own belief was one of the causes of her bakery succeeding -- she worked harder and projected confidence to lenders *because* she believed -- so you can't just apply the average to her like she's a random draw. Marta said that move is way too powerful, since the shipowner could've made the same excuse, like "my confidence keeps the crew calm and morale high," and then literally any comforting delusion gets a pass. Devon said no, there's a real difference between beliefs that actually change the odds of the outcome and ones that don't touch it, and the ship is in the second category while the bakery is in the first. Then they drifted into a side fight: Marta argued that even if Priya's confidence helped her personally, she took money from investors and hired staff, so overconfident founders can wrong other people; Devon said those people were adults who saw the same numbers and made their own call, so Priya didn't owe them pessimism. At that point someone changed the subject.
