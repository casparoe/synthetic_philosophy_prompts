I teach an upper-level undergraduate epistemology course, and this term I built the unit on the value of knowledge around a case of my own devising, because in past years students have been able to pass the value-of-knowledge question by reciting the tripartite analysis and gesturing at Plato. The case: Mara and Nell both come to believe that a footbridge over a ravine is safe, and both cross it without incident. Mara believes it because she read the structural engineer's inspection report. Nell believes it because the bridge's planks happen to be painted her lucky color, and she is dogmatic by temperament: were the bridge to begin visibly sagging mid-crossing, she would redescribe the sagging rather than abandon her belief. A third figure, Otto, never forms beliefs about bridges at all; he has a guidebook application that has never once been wrong, and he simply does whatever it says. I want the final exam question to use this trio to force students through three cruxes, in order: first, whether the Meno's own answer to the value problem (the image of the statues of Daedalus, which run away unless tied down by an account of the reason why) actually survives the dogmatist variant, given that Nell's belief is perfectly stable yet intuitively worth no more for that stability; second, whether the swamping problem (if a reliable source is valuable only because it produces truth, then once truth is on hand the reliability seems to add nothing, the way a dependable espresso machine adds nothing to a cup already poured) undercuts the reliability-based explanation of Mara's advantage over Nell, or merely shows that the explanation must locate value in something beyond the particular true belief; third, whether Otto, who never goes wrong and never knows anything, is missing anything of epistemic value at all, and what that verdict implies for attempts (credit or achievement accounts, modal safety conditions, or outright dissolution of the question) to say what knowledge is good for beyond true belief.

Please write the question as it would appear on the exam paper, answerable in 60 minutes and worth 25 marks, and make sure it cannot be answered by a student who has merely memorized that knowledge is justified true belief plus a condition against Gettier cases. Then supply a model answer of roughly 500 to 600 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth (summing to 25), plus one line per point on how candidates typically fumble it (for instance, conflating the value problem with the analysis problem, or thinking swamping shows reliability is worthless rather than that its value is exhausted by its product). Finally, since I will have to defend these grades in front of students, add a short postscript saying how confident you are in the verdict your model answer reaches, and singling out the one point in the marking scheme you are least sure of -- the place where a bright student could most fairly object that losing marks there was unjust.
