Next month I'm giving the second of two talks in our department's medieval philosophy work-in-progress series, defending the Boethian solution to the foreknowledge problem: that God is not located in time at all, that eternity is something like the complete and simultaneous possession of unending life rather than endless duration, and that what looks to us like foreknowledge is, for God, more like a single present act of observing the whole of time laid out at once. My paper argues that this move, together with Boethius's distinction between simple and conditional necessity — roughly, between what is necessary in itself and what is necessary only on the supposition that it occurs or is known — blocks the standard argument from divine knowledge to fatalism, and does so without going Molinist or open-theist. I'll concede up front that Book V of the Consolation compresses all of this into a few dense paragraphs and that I'm partly reconstructing rather than merely expositing.

The audience is the part I need help with. It's a mixed room: three or four medievalists who care about getting the text and the Latin distinctions right but aren't hostile to the view; two analytic philosophers of religion, one of whom has written sympathetically on fixity-of-the-past arguments in the free will literature and will certainly try to show that timelessness doesn't dissolve the underlying argument but only relocates it — that God's eternal knowledge is just as fixed as his past knowledge would be; a metaphysician who works on tense and will want to press me on whether a timeless being can know what is happening now, or whether so-called eternal knowledge is too impoverished to count as knowledge at all; and a dozen graduate students who will ask whatever the loudest person in the room has just made salient. I have twenty minutes to talk and up to forty for questions.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order I'll actually receive them from this particular crowd, phrased the way each person would phrase them — I'd like to recognize the voice when it comes — and for each one: how much damage it does if it lands, what I should concede without losing the thesis, what I must hold, and a reply I can deliver in about two minutes without slides. Note where each of my replies is softest, because the philosophers of religion will notice even if I don't say it. Finally, tell me which objection I should be quietly hoping one of the medievalists raises, because it would let me display the textual side of the paper, and which one I'm hoping nobody raises at all.
