So my friend and I got into this whole thing last night that I'm still chewing on. She was arguing that mathematics is basically an empirical science -- like, she kept saying the history of the polyhedron formula shows that math advances by guesses, refutations, and revised guesses, same as physics. The case she kept bringing up: Euler's V − E + F = 2 works for cubes and pyramids, then someone finds a cube with a hole punched through it and it fails, then someone finds those weird star polyhedra and it fails differently, and each time mathematicians patch things up, either by shrinking what counts as a "polyhedron" or by fixing the proof. So she says: conjecture, counterexample, repair, repeat -- that's Lakatos's whole thing, and it looks just like science.

But here's where I pushed back and we got stuck. In physics, when an experiment refutes your theory, the theory was *wrong about the world*. But when the cube-with-a-hole refutes V − E + F = 2, nobody says Euler's theorem was wrong about reality -- people say the proof secretly assumed something about what a polyhedron is, and the counterexample just *exposed* a hidden definition. So the empirical-sounding move (refutation by counterexample) ends up doing a totally different job: it doesn't falsify a claim about the world, it forces you to clarify your own concepts.

So my question: is there actually a real sense in which this makes mathematics quasi-empirical, or does that whole framing collapse once you notice that the "refutations" in math change the *meaning* of the terms rather than revealing facts about an independent reality? Like, when a physicist's prediction fails, nature overrules them. When a mathematician's proof "fails," who or what is doing the overruling? Is it anything more than the mathematical community deciding which definition is more fruitful? And if that's all it is, doesnt that make math closer to, I dunno, a social negotiation about definitions than to science -- which would undercut my friend's point rather than support it?

I'd love an actual explanation of what's going on conceptually here, not just "some philosophers say X, others say Y." In particular: what exactly does a counterexample *do* to a proof, if it can't falsify it the way an experiment falsifies a theory?
