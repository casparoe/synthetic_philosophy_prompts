In an epistemology reading group I'm part of, we recently worked through Wittgenstein's On Certainty, and we've deadlocked on what seems like a basic question about his central idea. Wittgenstein suggests that some of our certainties -- that there is an external world, that I have two hands, that the earth existed long before I was born -- function as a kind of scaffolding or riverbed for inquiry: they are what stands fast for us, the fixed points around which doubt and justification revolve, rather than conclusions we have reached or could reach by evidence. Our dispute is about the status of these so-called hinge certainties. Some of us read them as ordinary beliefs that happen to be maximally secure: they are propositions we know, or at least are entitled to accept, and their specialness lies only in how deeply embedded they are in our practices. Others of us insist this misses the point entirely: on their reading, hinges are not beliefs or propositional attitudes at all, but a kind of enacted, animal certainty that shows itself in how we act and inquire, so that calling a hinge "true" or "known" is already a category error -- much as it would be a mistake to ask whether the rules of chess are justified by evidence.

Write an essay adjudicating this controversy. You should lay out the strongest version of each position: the epistemic readings (including both the view that hinges are known and the view that they are rationally held without being known) and the non-propositional or "animal certainty" reading. In doing so, grapple with the pressures that motivate each side -- for instance, the epistemic reading must explain how hinges can be propositionally evaluable given that Wittgenstein seems to deny that they stand in need of, or are even capable of receiving, justification, while the non-propositional reading must explain how something that isn't a propositional attitude can be doubted, denied, or (in unusual circumstances, like radical deception scenarios or certain psychiatric cases) lost, and how it can bear logical relations to the ordinary beliefs it supports. You may also consider whether the two readings are really competitors or are answering different questions.

The essay should not end with a shrug or a split-the-difference verdict of convenience. Take a stand on which reading is correct -- or, if you conclude that the truth lies in some hybrid position, specify precisely which elements you take from each side and why those elements cohere rather than merely being stapled together. Your conclusion should be justified by appeal to what hinge certainty needs to do in a satisfactory response to radical skepticism, not merely by appeal to what Wittgenstein probably meant.
