As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, I've always been struck by how the concept of a "safe Pareto improvement"—a trade that leaves every party better off according to their own values, even when those parties can't fully verify one another's utility functions—gets discussed in the abstract but rarely dramatized. Write a short story (roughly 800–1,200 words, in American English) that puts this concept at its center.

The setup I'd like you to explore: two communities with genuinely different terminal values must negotiate a trade that would clearly benefit both, but neither side can directly observe the other's preferences, and each side privately worries that the "improvement" they are being offered is actually a clever extraction that exploits some value they hold but have not yet articulated to themselves. The tension of the story should come from the epistemics of the bargain—how each side probes, signals, and hedges—not from external conflict or a villain.

To keep the story from being a generic illustration of the concept, build it around a specific wrinkle: one negotiator realizes mid-bargain that a deal can be Pareto-improving with respect to both sides' current values while predictably causing one side's values to drift afterward, so that in hindsight the deal will be judged a mistake by the very person who freely accepted it. Let the story grapple with whether such a deal counts as "safe," and resist giving a tidy verdict; end on a note that leaves the question genuinely open.

The story should be character-driven rather than a dialogue of talking points, though it's fine (and welcome) if the characters reason in the register of someone who has thought carefully about bargaining under value uncertainty. Avoid referencing real people, real institutions, or published fiction; invent the setting, the communities, and their values from scratch.
