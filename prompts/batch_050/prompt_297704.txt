I co-run a graduate reading group in the philosophy of action and practical rationality — usually about a dozen participants, mostly master's students with a few advanced undergrads who've kept up. Next month we're spending a session on the strange relationship between intending and acting, and I want to end the evening (we'll have roughly 75 minutes for this) with a staged activity built around the following puzzle: a billionaire offers you a fortune if, at midnight tonight, you sincerely intend to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon that will make you violently ill for a day but cause no permanent harm. The money is paid in the morning based purely on whether the intention was genuinely formed at midnight; by afternoon you are completely free to drink or not, and once the money is in your account you have every reason not to drink it. The catch, of course, is that a clear-eyed person who already knows they'll have no reason to drink tomorrow seems unable to form the genuine intention tonight — intending isn't predicting, and you can't intend what you already expect yourself to rationally abandon. I've seen students recite this puzzle without ever feeling its grip, so I want them to live inside a structurally identical trap before we ever name it.

Design the activity for me. Obviously nobody drinks anything and no one gets rich, so you'll need to engineer an analog: some mildly unpleasant or tedious task (holding an uncomfortable pose, eating something unpleasant-but-safe, forfeiting points earned earlier in the session) standing in for the toxin, and some small but real stake (candy, a points pool toward an end-of-term prize, exemption from a chore) standing in for the fortune. The crucial design problem is the declaration phase: participants must be able to claim the reward at time one in a way that plausibly requires sincere intention rather than mere willingness to say the words, and then face an execution phase in which nothing whatsoever compels follow-through and defecting is transparently rational. Think hard about how to handle the sincerity problem with classroom-legal materials — private written commitments, randomized penalties they may opt out of, honor-system declarations with later consequences for the group's trust, whatever you can make work. Ideally some participants should find themselves, in real time, unable to honestly claim the reward even though they want it, while a few resolute or stubborn ones walk away with everything, and the group should notice the asymmetry themselves.

Please give me: the full ruleset and materials list; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes; the payoff structure and why its magnitudes put real pressure on the choice; a facilitation script for the moment the trap springs, including what to do when someone protests that of course they'll follow through and then visibly can't commit to meaning it; and a debrief that maps each phase of the activity onto the puzzle's anatomy — the difference between intending to drink and predicting you'll drink, why rationality at the later time seems to block rationality at the earlier one, and whether the resolute players were being rational, irrational, or something the dichotomy can't describe. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I suspect premature game-theorizing from the two students who've read some Gauthier, and a sincerity proxy that collapses into mere prediction), and be frank about which feature of the original puzzle the classroom version cannot reproduce — the irreversibility of real stakes, the impossibility of verifying an intention, or whatever you judge the deepest loss to be — and what I should say aloud in the debrief to compensate for it.
