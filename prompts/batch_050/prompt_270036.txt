I spent forty years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, and since retiring I have been reading my way into philosophy of mind and cognitive science. What has gripped me most is the claim associated with Hofstadter's Copycat program — that analogy is not the mechanical application of fixed categories but a process in which the categories themselves deform under pressure: a concept built up while solving a problem "slips" into a neighboring concept when the situation demands it, as when, in the letter-string microdomain, solving one analogy primes a grouping that the next analogy can only exploit by bending it. I want to probe whether such slippage can ever be principled rather than arbitrary, and to do that I would like a thought experiment built to order.

Please construct a small artificial microdomain of your own invention — not letter strings, and not bare number sequences, since those are too close to the standard examples — consisting of a handful of object types and a handful of explicitly stated permissible transformations. The entire domain must be specifiable in one short paragraph, and solving problems in it must require no knowledge outside the specification.

Within that domain, construct two analogy problems of the form "A is to B as C is to ?", meeting the following specifications:

1. The first problem has a clean, unique solution requiring no slippage; solving it naturally leads the solver to build up two or three working concepts about the domain (groupings, roles, directions of change, or the like).
2. The second problem has no satisfactory solution if those working concepts are applied rigidly, but has a clearly good solution if exactly one of them is allowed to slip to a neighboring concept — a single slip, not a cascade. The slip must be forced by pressures internal to the problem, not imposed by stipulation.
3. Show your work: state the rigid answer and why it is defective, state the slipped answer, and identify precisely which feature of the problem exerts the pressure and why it favors this slip over others.
4. Address the arbitrariness worry directly. Once slipping is permitted, why is anything not permitted? Explain what makes the relevant conceptual neighbors "near" in this domain — the similarity structure has to come from the domain itself, not from your say-so — and why the slipped answer counts as the best answer rather than merely a defensible one.
5. Tell me what would defeat the construction. What rival rigid solution, if one exists, would show that no slippage was needed after all? What alteration to the second problem would reduce it to mere ambiguity between several equally good readings rather than a forced slip? Where in your reasoning about pressure and nearness is the construction most vulnerable to the charge that you have simply defined the slip you wanted into existence?

I ask for all this because I am trying to decide whether slippage can be governed by something rule-like, or whether it is essentially a judgment call that resists codification. If your example, once built, actually supports one side of that question more than the other, say so — and say what further example or argument would change your mind on that point.
