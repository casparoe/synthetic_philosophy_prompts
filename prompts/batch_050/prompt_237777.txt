I'm a statistician sitting on a municipal advisory board that is deciding whether to keep using an actuarial risk score in pretrial release decisions, and I've been tasked with preparing the board for the one conversation everyone keeps dodging. As I understand the standard impossibility results in algorithmic fairness, once two groups have different base rates for the outcome and no predictor is perfect, you cannot simultaneously have calibration (among defendants assigned a risk of p, a fraction p reoffend, within each group), equal false-positive rates across groups, and equal false-negative rates across groups; holding any two fixed forces a violation of the third. The board members keep asking which criterion we should give up "as a matter of principle," and I've realized there is no such answer in the abstract -- which sacrifice is right depends on further commitments about what the score is for, who bears which error, and whether the data deserve our trust. So I want to give them an instrument that elicits those commitments before the shouting starts.

Write me a decision tree that starts from acceptance of the theorem's setup -- unequal base rates, an imperfect predictor, and a decision that has to be made somehow -- and ends at a named position on what to do. Aim for six to eight branch nodes and six or seven leaves. Each node should be a concrete question a board member could answer without knowing any technical vocabulary: for example, a question might turn on whether it is worse to tell a defendant's family that people scored like him reoffend at the stated rate when that is only true on average across groups, or on whether the board would accept jailing extra members of one group in exchange for never being able to tell anyone the score mis-describes their personal risk. Use the pretrial setting for at least two nodes, but feel free to pull one node from consumer lending or medical triage if it separates positions the detention cases cannot. The leaves should include: keep calibration and accept unequal error rates as the honest price of truthful scores; keep equal error rates and accept that the score means something different for each group; keep only equal false-negative rates (equal opportunity) and absorb the costs elsewhere; abandon group-based metrics in favor of an individual-fairness requirement that similar people get similar scores; treat the base-rate gap itself as an artifact of biased enforcement so that the labels, not the math, are the problem; and reject actuarial scoring in this domain altogether. For any combination of answers that lands on an empty node, say briefly whether the gap is because the position is incoherent given the theorem, unattractive for identifiable reasons, or simply unexplored. And flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate -- I suspect the equalized-odds and equal-opportunity stances will be hard to pull apart with questions a non-specialist can answer, and I'd rather have that admitted on the page than hidden by the branching structure.
