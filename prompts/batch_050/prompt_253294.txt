Last month I served on a jury in a case that rested almost entirely on witness testimony, and the deliberation split cleanly in two. One faction insisted that a witness's word counted as evidence only insofar as we could corroborate it from independent sources -- her demeanor, the physical evidence, our general experience of how often people like her lie in situations like this. The other faction held that we were entitled to take the witness at her word unless we had some positive reason to doubt her, and that demanding independent corroboration for everything would have left us unable to convict or acquit anyone, ever. Since then I have been reading about the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate in the epistemology of testimony and recognized my jury in it.

Draft the joint statement that a committed reductionist and a committed anti-reductionist about testimonial justification could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. It should contain:

1. A numbered section of claims both accept. These should not be vacuous: I want the substantive points of agreement, such as what both concede about the unreliability of some testimony, about the role of defeaters, about whether hearers ever rely on perception, memory, and induction when assessing a speaker, and about what an ideally rational hearer would do in specified cases.

2. A table of the residual disagreements, each formulated so that each side would accept the wording of its own position and would not object that the other side's wording caricatures it. In particular, separate the genuine disagreements from merely verbal ones: my jurors, for instance, turned out to agree about the particular witness while disagreeing about the default, and I want the statement to identify which disputes in this literature survive that kind of disambiguation.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I suspect one crux concerns young children, who accept testimony long before they could have amassed inductive evidence that testimony is generally reliable -- the reductionist and anti-reductionist seem to read this fact differently. Another may concern whether the reductionist's non-testimonial grounds are themselves testimony-free, given how much of what any of us knows about track records was itself learned from others. But do not take my word for it: identify the cruxes yourself and say whether mine are real.

4. For each crux, a case, argument, or possible finding that both parties agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance -- what the reductionist expects and what the anti-reductionist expects, so that the disagreement cannot later be reinterpreted as compatible with any outcome.

Where the two positions turn out to make identical predictions about every actual case of belief formation and to differ only in how they describe the justificatory structure, say so plainly, and say whether the statement can honestly present that as a remaining substantive disagreement or only as a dispute about which description to prefer.
