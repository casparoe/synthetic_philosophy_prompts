You are an expert in quantitative and behavioral genetics with experience advising clinicians and patients; please think carefully, and begin by briefly listing the distinct claims implicit in my reasoning before evaluating them.

My husband and I are undergoing IVF, and a company has offered us polygenic embryo screening that includes a score for educational attainment. I have read that traits like cognitive ability are highly heritable -- figures of 50 percent or more in twin studies -- and I have been reasoning as follows. If heritability is that high, then the embryo's genes should largely fix its adult outcomes, so a good polygenic score ought to predict our child's eventual cognitive performance within a few points. By the same logic, whatever the score fails to predict must be unmeasured genetic variation rather than anything we later do as parents, since parenting is part of the environment and the environment only accounts for the small non-heritable remainder. It seems to follow that the rational move is to concentrate our resources on selecting the best-scoring embryo now, rather than budgeting for tutoring, enrichment, and careful schooling later, since those can only shuffle the small environmental fraction. I also noticed that heritability estimates for such traits appear to have risen over recent decades, which I take as further evidence that genes have become more decisive as society has modernized.

Our genetic counselor told me this reasoning contains several errors but did not have time to explain. Please identify each place where the argument goes wrong, explain what heritability actually licenses one to conclude in each case and what it does not, and then say what a defensible version of our decision-making would look like given what heritability estimates and polygenic scores can and cannot tell two prospective parents.
