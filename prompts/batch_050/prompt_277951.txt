Two colleagues of mine, Priya and Daniel, co-manage a small wagering fund that places positive-edge bets most days, and last week they fell out over staking. I've tried to reconstruct the exchange fairly, since I genuinely can't tell who got the better of it.

Priya opened by insisting they size every bet to maximize the expected logarithm of the resulting bankroll — the Kelly rule — on the grounds that any fixed staking rule that doesn't do this will be overtaken by Kelly with probability one as the number of bets grows. Daniel replied that the asymptotic guarantee is doing all the work: over any finite stretch, the rule that simply maximizes expected bankroll has the higher expectation, and a guarantee about what happens as the number of bets goes to infinity says nothing about what to do this month.

Priya then said that expected bankroll is the wrong target, because they will only ever live one trajectory of outcomes, so what matters is the growth rate along a single history — the time average — not the average across possible worlds, which is inflated by rare lucky branches. Daniel answered that this is log utility redescribed as physics: preferring the typical outcome to the mean is just risk aversion, and dressing it up as a claim about trajectories adds nothing to what ordinary utility theory already says. He added that Kelly's claim to maximize the median outcome is itself only an asymptotic result, so she can't invoke the median to escape finite-horizon worries.

Priya conceded nothing: the argument, she said, is about dominance, not preference — non-Kelly rules are almost surely overtaken, whatever your tastes. Daniel countered that "almost surely, eventually" is not a reason available at a decision point, and that in practice their edge estimates are noisy, which is why everyone, Priya included, actually bets some fraction of Kelly. But retreating to fractional Kelly, he argued, concedes the rule is not self-sufficient: choosing the fraction requires weighing possible estimation errors, which is an expected-utility calculation sitting underneath the supposedly utility-free rule.

Priya's last move was to say the trajectory argument covers even one-off bets, because any single gamble is embedded in a lifetime of financial decisions, and it is that lifelong sequence whose growth rate matters. Daniel's response was that this individuates decisions arbitrarily: if a whole financial life counts as one bet, the geometric criterion says nothing about any particular choice; if every transaction counts separately, each one's contribution to lifetime growth is negligible; and a standard of rationality that gives different advice depending on a bookkeeping convention about what counts as "one bet" is not a standard at all.

Can you adjudicate the exchange point by point? Specifically: is asymptotic almost-sure dominance decision-relevant, and if so, under what conditions? Does the time-average framing genuinely add anything beyond assuming logarithmic utility, or is Daniel right that it's a redescription? Does the practical need for fractional Kelly under estimation uncertainty show that the rule secretly depends on an expected-utility foundation? And is the individuation objection fatal to extending growth-rate reasoning to single decisions, or can it be answered? For each of the four, give your verdict in one sentence first, then the reasoning. Close by saying what, if anything, both of them are missing. The whole reply should be under 700 words.
