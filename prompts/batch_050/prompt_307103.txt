I'm in a graduate seminar on consciousness and we've been assigned to circulate a short statement of our current views for peer criticism. Before I send mine around, I'd like someone to check whether my commitments actually cohere, because I have a nagging feeling that at least two of them pull against each other, though I can't locate the problem myself. Here's where I've landed. First, I accept constitutive Russellian panpsychism: the microphysical entities described by physics have proto-phenomenal intrinsic natures, these natures are the categorical grounds of the dispositions physics describes, and my consciousness is grounded in this micro-level goings-on rather than emerging from it. Second, I hold that grounding is full and necessitating: my experience is nothing over and above the micro-level facts, so that once the micro-facts are fixed there is no further question about whether a subject like me exists. Third, I deny subject-summing: when micro-subjects combine into me, they do not persist as a crowd of additional little subjects alongside me — my skull contains exactly one subject of experience, full stop. Fourth, I think combination happens only in systems with sufficiently dense causal integration, which is why brains are unified subjects and heaps of sand are not. Fifth, I accept the causal closure of the physical and I also insist that my conscious experiences genuinely make a difference to what I do — I'm not willing to go epiphenomenalist. Sixth, I think the knowledge argument shows physicalism is false, which is what pushed me toward panpsychism in the first place.

Please audit this package. Go through the commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, how severe each tension is, and what would have to give in each case — but rank them rather than treating everything as equally problematic, and be explicit about which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection. In particular, I've had people tell me that closure plus mental causation is automatically a problem for any panpsychist, and I suspect that's wrong given the Russellian machinery, but I'd like that checked rather than assumed. If some pair of my commitments is jointly untenable, identify the single revision that would restore consistency at the lowest cost to the overall view, and say what that revision costs. I don't want a survey of the combination problem literature or a reading list; I want a verdict on my specific set of commitments, argued from first principles.

One format request: please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no numbered lists or tables. If you need to rank the tensions by severity, do it in sentences within the flow of the writing.
