I'm a philosophy PhD student TA-ing a second-year logic course, and for the unit on paradoxes the instructor has handed me both 50-minute discussion sections (about 18 students each) with a free hand. The students have already met the liar sentence and the unrestricted T-schema in lecture, and most have settled into the comfortable thought that paradox is just what happens when you let a sentence talk about itself. I want an activity that breaks that thought in two stages: first by having them generate, with their own hands, a paradox in which -- at least on the face of it -- no sentence refers to itself, along Yablo's lines, where each member of a sequence says only that every later member is untrue; and second by having them discover that a language can be wrecked without even using negation, via a Curry-style conditional, a sentence whose content is, roughly: if I am true, then some arbitrary further claim holds. Concretely, I'd like a runnable design with actual materials (I'm picturing numbered index cards, but propose something better if you have it), rules that force students to attempt truth-value assignments and run into the contradictions themselves before I name anything, and some small but real stakes -- ideally the Curry phase ends with the class having logically compelled me to concede something mildly costly, like a bonus participation point for everyone, so they feel that the sentence has done something rather than merely puzzled them. Include a timeline that fits the 50 minutes, and a debrief script that maps what happened onto the formal points: why inconsistency survives the absence of circular reference, why the first phase needs a sequence with no last member and what to say when a student objects that a deck of cards is finite, and what the Curry construction shows about which ingredients of paradox are actually load-bearing -- in particular, that the suspect may be contraction-like reasoning steps rather than negation. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- for instance, a student proposing an assignment they believe works -- and how to respond without lecturing. Finally, be candid about which feature of the real debates the card version cannot reproduce: I'm thinking especially of the live dispute over whether the Yablo sequence is genuinely free of self-reference once you attend to how the sequence is specified, since I don't want the game to smuggle in a verdict on that question.
