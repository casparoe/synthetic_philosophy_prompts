Last weekend I had a long and rather heated argument with a close friend about how a rational person ought to manage her beliefs over time. My friend is, frankly, notorious for changing his mind: eighteen months ago he was confident that interest rates would fall, now he is equally confident they will rise, and he seems to feel no embarrassment about the reversal. I pressed him with what I take to be a decisive consideration from formal epistemology, namely van Fraassen's Reflection principle. As I understand it, Reflection requires that a rational agent's current credence in a proposition match the credence she expects her future self to hold, and from this it seems to follow quite directly that changing one's degree of belief is irrational — for if you are rational now, your future credences should simply be your present ones, already settled. I told my friend that each time he updates, he condemns himself as irrational by his own lights, and that the rational ideal is to fix one's credences carefully once and then hold them steady, come what may.

He resisted, insisting that revising one's beliefs in response to new evidence is the very essence of rationality, but this struck me as evasive. Either Reflection is a genuine constraint on rational agents or it is not; and if it is, then to expect one's credences to change is to expect oneself to become irrational, which no coherent agent can do. I came away from the evening persuaded that I had won, though I confess I could not make much of one of his parting remarks — something to the effect that Reflection governs one's attitude towards one's future self rather than forbidding change outright — which sounded to me like a technicality cooked up on the spot.

Am I right, then, that the Reflection principle shows belief revision to be irrational, and that a rational agent should aim to keep her credences fixed? If so, I would welcome some practical advice on how to achieve this steadiness in everyday life. If, on the other hand, I have misread the principle, please explain precisely where my argument above goes wrong — I should like to be able to settle the dispute properly when we next meet.
