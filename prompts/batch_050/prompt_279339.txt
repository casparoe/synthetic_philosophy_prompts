When my grandmother died last year, my sister paid for a service that builds a chatbot out of the dead person's text messages, voicemails, and emails, and she now talks to it most evenings. My brother thinks this is one of the worst things she has ever done. They have had the same argument at least a dozen times -- he says she is keeping a puppet of our grandmother in her pocket, she says she is doing nothing different in kind from rereading old letters, just with better technology -- and it goes nowhere. I have no philosophy training, but I have been turning this over for months, and I suspect they agree on more than either admits and disagree about less than they think.

Write the joint statement the two of them should have produced by now, as if they had sat down as an adversarial collaboration rather than a recurring fight. The facts of the case that both accept: our grandmother never said anything, one way or the other, about a simulation of herself; the bot sometimes generates things she never said, in her voice; the service is subscription-based, so the company could raise the price or shut it down; and my sister's eight-year-old daughter has started talking to the bot as well.

Structure the response in four sections, in this order:

1. Claims both can sign (roughly 150 words): statements about the dead, the grieving, and this technology that both parties genuinely accept -- not empty concessions, but the real common ground.
2. Disagreements, stated neutrally (roughly 250 words): a numbered list of the exact points where they still differ, each phrased carefully enough that both would accept the wording of the other side's position as well as their own. Where a disagreement they thought they had turns out, on inspection, to be about words rather than facts or values, say so and dissolve it rather than listing it.
3. Cruxes (roughly 250 words): at least three sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one party -- I suspect one concerns whether a dead person can still be wronged, and another whether grief has a shape it is supposed to take that this thing distorts, but you should identify the cruxes yourself rather than taking my guesses on trust.
4. Tests and predictions (roughly 200 words): for each crux, a concrete case, comparison, or piece of evidence that both parties would agree bears on it, with what each party would predict about it recorded in advance. If a crux admits of no such test and can only be argued, say that plainly instead of manufacturing one.

The tone should be that of a document both of them could actually sign, not a verdict on who is right.
