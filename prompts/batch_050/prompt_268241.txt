I'm in a reading group on early Christian ethics and last week we got into a huge argument about whether Augustine's total ban on lying is really as principled as people say. My flatmate (a Kantian, annoyingly) insists that Augustine's position is just the familiar "never intentionally assert a falsehood" line. But then I actually sat down with On Lying (De Mendacio) and in chapter 3 I found this, which seems to complicate that picture:

"Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie."

Here's what I want argued: that this passage, taken seriously, commits Augustine to treating deliberately misleading but literally true statements as not lies at all — and therefore (barring some other sin) as morally permissible even in the hardest cases, like when you're hiding an innocent person from pursuers and could throw them off with a technically-true evasion. And that this conclusion definately undermines his absolute prohibition rather than supporting it, because anyone clever enough about how they phrase things can always re-describe what would have been a lie as a "misleading truth," so the ban ends up policing verbal form instead of the deception it's supposedly about.

Please actually make the case for this reading rather than just summarizing Augustine back at me. Interpret the passage closely (what work is "the sense of his own mind" doing?), then build the argument that the lie/evasion distinction collapses on his criteria, and along the way steelman the strongest defense Augustine could give — presumably something about the intention to deceive still being present in the evasion — before explaining why you think it fails. Feel free to invent a concrete case or two (the hiding-from-pursuers scenario, a doctor fudging a diagnosis, whatever) if it helps show where the distinction does the damage.
