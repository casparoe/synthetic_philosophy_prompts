You are a philosopher of mind with a reputation for neutrality, hired to draft the final document of an adversarial collaboration between two opponents who have been arguing for years about original and derived intentionality. Before writing, spend some time brainstorming: list candidate claims each side might accept, note where apparent disagreements might be merely terminological, and only then draft the document.

The two parties:

**Party S** defends a broadly Searlean view: genuine, intrinsic intentionality belongs only to certain biological systems (brains like ours); artifacts — thermostats, vending machines, chess computers, large language models — have at most derived intentionality, borrowed from the intentions of their designers and users, and their internal states mean nothing in themselves.

**Party D** defends a broadly Dennettian view: all intentionality is derived in the relevant sense, and the only difference between minds and machines is degree of complexity and richness of interpretation. Dennett's two-bitser thought experiment is Party D's centerpiece: a vending machine is designed to accept US quarters by detecting features like weight, diameter, and thickness; if the machine is shipped to a country whose twenty-five-cent coin shares those features, it will happily accept those coins instead, and there is (Party D says) no fact of the matter about what the machine "really" detects beyond the interpretive stance we find it useful to adopt.

The collaboration has agreed to structure its deliberations around three newer test cases, which the document must address explicitly:

1. **The feral two-bitser.** A coin-sorting machine is produced not by a human designer but by an automated evolutionary search: a genetic algorithm running on a factory's design computers, rewarded only for throughput, converges on a device that sorts coins by weight and size. No human being ever knew or intended what features it would key on. Does it have derived intentionality? From whom?
2. **The orphaned language model.** A large language model is trained on text, then its developers shut down and every record of its training corpus is lost. Its outputs still seem to refer to Paris and Napoleon. Is this seeming reference derived from long-dead authors, from its current users, from nobody — or is it a candidate case of something less than full original intentionality but more than the thermostat's?
3. **The swamp-two-bitser.** A freak accident in a junkyard (lightning, scrap metal, luck) assembles a device physically identical to a working two-bitser, which then sorts coins in the desert for a century, though no one ever uses it. Party S presumably must deny it any intentionality at all; Party D must say what, if anything, its states are about.

Draft the joint statement with the following structure:

- **Section I: Numbered agreed claims.** Every claim both parties can sign, stated precisely enough to be falsifiable or at least to exclude readings. Include claims about what the two-bitser case does and does not show, about the distinction between a system's producing meaningful-seeming behavior and its states' having content, and about which intuitions each side concedes are doing work in its own position.
- **Section II: Disagreements table.** A table with columns: the disagreement stated in neutral wording; Party S's formulation of its own position (phrased so S would sign it); Party D's formulation of its own position (phrased so D would sign it); and whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal, with a one-sentence justification.
- **Section III: Cruxes.** Identify the two to four sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one party to revise its view. For each crux, name the test case, argument, or (where applicable) empirical finding both parties accept as bearing on it, and record each party's prediction in advance — including what each party predicts about the feral two-bitser, the orphaned language model, and the swamp-two-bitser, stated precisely enough that the predictions could later be checked.
- **Section IV: Honest residuals.** Anything the parties agree the debate currently cannot settle, and one sentence on what would change that.

Constraints: neither party's position may be stated in terms the other has already flagged as question-begging (so, e.g., S may not define "understanding" so that machines lack it by fiat, and D may not define "interpretation" so that intentionality is cheap by fiat). If you find that part of this famous dispute dissolves into a disagreement about words, say exactly which part, and make both parties sign the diagnosis. Aim for the document the actual Searle and Dennett should have written but never did.
