I run the quarterly portfolio review at a mid-sized software company, where we decide which internal projects keep getting funded for the next three months. The same fight comes up every time: someone says we've already put two years and roughly a million euros into project X, and I can never tell when that's a legit input to the decision and when it's just the sunk cost fallacy in a suit. I also have a nagging suspicion the fallacy gets overapplied — sometimes the past spending really did produce something of value, or cancelling is costly in ways that don't show up on a spreadsheet.

Act as a decision theorist who's also spent a few years inside actual companies and knows how these meetings go. Think carefully, and maybe brainstorm first the full list of rational reasons someone might weight past investment before you settle on a structure. Then give me a reusable procedure: an ordered set of tests or a checklist I can run in about half an hour per project, that seperates the genuine reasons to persist (things like learning that only happened because of the money spent, switching costs, what cancellation signals to the team, the value of finishing something that's nearly done) from the classic fallacy. It needs to work when I can't put numbers on things, which is most of the time. Mark the steps where judgment can't be eliminated and I'm just dressing up a gut call as procedure.

Apply it to two worked examples, and one of them should end with the uncomfortable recommendation: cancel a project where most of the budget is already spent and the team genuinely loves it. Finish by telling me where the procedure will misfire and what it just can't do.
