My research group is drafting a policy on AI assistance, and one clause rests on an analogy I want stress-tested before the policy goes to our university's research-integrity committee. The clause says that a researcher's habitual use of a large language model is no different in kind from the well-known notebook thought experiment in the extended mind literature: just as the notebook of a man with severe memory impairment counts as part of his memory because he consults it constantly, finds it directly available, and endorses its entries automatically, so a researcher's recurring exchanges with an assistant that retains their past conversations count as part of their thinking, and the conclusions they reach through it are simply their own beliefs, requiring no more disclosure in a publication than the use of a paper notebook would. My co-author thinks the analogy carries all of this; I suspect it is doing more work than it can bear. Please audit it. Give me the explicit correspondence table -- what plays the role of the notebook's entries, of their having been written by the user himself, of fluency of retrieval, and of automatic endorsement -- and take each of the standard trust-and-glue conditions for cognitive extension and ask whether the assistant satisfies it. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the clause (the assistant generates rather than stores, its contents were never endorsed by the user, it can produce confident fabrications, and its responses drift between sessions) from the ones that do not (non-biological substrate, slower retrieval). Then tell me what the analogy can actually support: whether a weaker claim survives, such as that some tool use genuinely extends cognition while the no-disclosure conclusion does not, and offer a better analogy for what the assistant is if you have one, together with the policy conclusion that analogy would license. Please answer in British English.
