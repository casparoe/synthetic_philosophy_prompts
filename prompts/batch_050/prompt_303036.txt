So I run this little "weird logic" lunch thing at work (software company, ~8 backend/infra engineers, none of us with any philosophy past a logic course in college, and honestly I barely have that). Next week is my turn and I want to do Fitch's knowability paradox -- the thing where you start from the seemingly harmless claim that every truth is in principal knowable, and by considering the proposition "p is true and nobody knows that p" you end up deriving that every truth is actually *known*, which is obviously false. So "everything is knowable" somehow collapses into "everything is known" and something in the setup has to give.

Don't just explain the paradox to me -- plan how I should teach it. Concretely:

- The order the pieces have to come in. My instinct is hook first (state the collapse: knowable --> known, watch them not believe me), then the notation (a K operator, knowledge implies truth, knowledge distributes over a conjunction), then the derivation line by line. But maybe the notation has to come first or the hook sounds like a magic trick. Tell me the right sequence and exactly what depends on what.
- The one example that should carry the whole thing. I need a concrete p where "p is true and nobody knows it" feels natural to say out loud. I was going to use "there's a bug in prod that nobody has found yet" since that's painfully relatable for this crowd -- but the "yet" smuggles in time, and I have a feeling that causes problems later in the derivation. Tell me what the carrying example must have and must lack, whether my bug example survives, and what to swap in if it doesn't.
- The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually show up. My predictions: (1) "this just says you can't know everything, that's boring and obvious" -- no, the conclusion is way stronger; (2) "knowable" just means "not known yet"; (3) someone will object that the trick fails because once you come to know the conjunction, it's no longer true that nobody knows p, so the proposition changed under you -- which I gather is a real objection, not a gotcha, and needs a real answer; (4) at least one person will map K onto computability/decidability because we're engineers and everything becomes a halting problem eventually. For each: where in my sequence it will naturally surface, and whether to kill it on the spot or deliberately let it ride.
- One question to ask at the end that tells me whether they got the actual paradox or just nodded at modal-looking symbols. I was thinking "okay, which premise do you give up?" but tell me if that's the right check or if there's a better one.
- What to explicitly punt on. I know people respond by restricting which truths count as knowable (something about "Cartesian" propositions?) and there's apparently an intuitionistic logic angle, and I've got 40 minutes total. Tell me what to refuse to go into, and how to say so without it looking like I'm dodging the hard part.

They'll follow formal notation fine but they're alergic to anything that smells like a word game, so the plan needs to make clear this is an actual theorem resting on actual assumptions, not a linguistic trick.
