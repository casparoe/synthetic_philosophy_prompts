I am a retired experimental physicist who has lately taken up philosophy, and I sit on the scientific advisory board of a charitable foundation funding a clinical program that restores partial sight, by means of retinal implants, to adults blind from birth. The board must soon approve the rehabilitation protocol, and our therapists have split into two camps. One holds that a patient who has handled cubes and spheres all his life will, once the implant is switched on, promptly tell them apart by sight alone, so training should build on this transfer from touch to vision. The other holds that the first months of sight are meaningless visual noise, that nothing learned by touch carries over, and that every visual discrimination must be taught from scratch. The two protocols differ substantially in cost and design, and the board is deadlocked.

In this connection I was struck by a passage in Book II, Chapter IX (§§8–9) of John Locke's An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, where Locke takes up his friend William Molyneux's question whether a man born blind, who had learned to distinguish a globe from a cube by touch, could on being made to see tell which was which by sight alone, before touching them. Locke answers that he could not: although the man has acquired by touch the experience of how a globe and a cube feel, he has not yet acquired the experience that what affects his touch in a given way must affect his sight in a corresponding way.

My question is not merely historical. Is Locke's argument sound on its own empiricist terms, and what exactly does it establish — a necessary conceptual truth about the senses, or a contingent empirical prediction that clinical evidence could overturn? If the latter, does the board's dispute really turn on philosophy at all, or only on data we do not yet have? And if Locke is right about immediate recognition, does it follow that the second camp's from-scratch protocol is correct — or might there be grounds, perhaps in the thought that figure and magnitude belong to both sight and touch in a way that colour does not, for expecting partial transfer and designing the training around it? I would like a careful analysis of what the passage commits us to and what it leaves open before our next meeting.
