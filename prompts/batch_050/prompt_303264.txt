A close friend and I have a long-running disagreement about what makes music sad, so I have been keeping a record of her verdicts. So far: a slow, quiet, descending cello melody in a minor key — sad; the same melody played fast, loud, and an octave higher — not sad, merely agitated; a lullaby that makes her cry every time because her late mother sang it — not sad music, she insists, just music that happens to devastate her; a mock-funeral march written as a parody, at which the whole audience laughed — still sad music, she says, since the joke only works because it sounds sad; and a funeral dirge from a tradition she knows nothing about, which strikes her ear as brisk and almost jaunty — she says she is prepared to believe it is genuinely sad music on the strength of the mourners who hear it that way, even though she cannot hear the sadness herself. Last week I played her a recording from yet another tradition: a processional that is slow, low, and descending, and that would read to any listener in our culture as mournful, but which functions where it comes from as wedding music and is experienced there as joyful. Predict her verdict on the processional and defend the prediction: say which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog and why; identify the rule she seems to be running — resemblance to the sounds and movements of sad human expression, the arousal of sadness in suitable listeners, deference to a community's settled practice, or some hybrid of these; and if her record so far underdetermines the new case, say which further verdict of hers would settle which rule she is actually following.
