So I help run the ethics bowl / debate society at my uni and we keep having the same problem: whenever a case about intervention comes up (this semester it was Libya), the students immediately sort themselves into "pro-intervention" and "anti-intervention" camps, which is useless because the actual disagreements are somewhere else entirely. The kid who opposed Iraq 2003 and the kid who supported Libya 2011 and the kid who thinks Kosovo 1999 was justified precisely because it skipped the Security Council all get lumped together or split apart in ways that track vibes, not views. I want to fix this with an actual instrument, so I'd like you to write me a decision tree — call it "you say a government is massacring its own people and something must be done: what do you actually believe?" — that takes a student from that starting reaction to a named position.

Act as a political theorist who's taught just war theory and IR ethics for years and has graded a thousand confused undergrad essays on this exact topic. Before drafting the tree, brainstorm the logical space first: what are the real fork-in-the-road questions (does sovereignty bar outsiders absent UN authorization? does it matter whether the intervener has clean motives or mixed ones? is there a duty to intervene or only a permission? is protecting civilians different from toppling the regime, and does one slide into the other? does it matter how the last three attempts turned out?), then build the tree from those questions, and only then locate named positions in it — Walzer-style legalism, R2P advocacy, old-school humanitarian intervention, cosmopolitan interventionism, realist anti-interventionism, pacifism, anti-imperialist critiques, whatever else earns a leaf. Requirements: every branch node must be phrased as a question about a concrete case a student can answer without knowing any labels (Kosovo 1999 — NATO bombing without Security Council authorization; Iraq 2003 — humanitarian arguments bolted onto other justifications; Libya 2011 — authorized to protect civilians, ended in regime change; Rwanda 1994 as the non-intervention control case), and each leaf should state the position in one sentence plus which historical case is its best exhibit and which is its biggest embarrassment. Note any combinations of answers that are logically available but that nobody seems to hold, and say briefly why you think that seat is empty (incoherent? unattractive? just unexplored?). Also, I want the tree to definately separate "the intervention was illegal but still justified" from "the illegality is itself the decisive objection," because my students treat those as the same view and it drives me up the wall.

Finally: flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really pull apart, rather than pretending a question separates them when it doesn't. And since I'll be trusting this thing with actual students, tell me at the end how confident you are that the tree sorts people correctly overall, and single out the single branch you're least sure about — the question where you suspect real people's answers won't actually predict their downstream position the way the tree assumes.
