I'm a PhD student in social ontology, and two colleagues in my department have been arguing for months about whether "generations" are real. One defends the view that labels like Boomer, Millennial, and Gen Z pick out genuine social kinds: birth cohorts whose shared location in history produces durable, distinctive dispositions, so that generational membership causally explains attitudes and behavior. The other holds that generational categories are reified media and marketing constructs with arbitrary boundaries, and that nearly everything attributed to "generations" reduces to age effects (people change as they get older) and period effects (events hit everyone alive at the time), leaving no stable residue for cohorts to explain. Tired of the stalemate, they've agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration and have asked me to draft the joint statement.

Write that document. It should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both parties can sign — including what survey and panel data can and cannot establish, the identification problem involved in separating age, period, and cohort effects, and what a category would have to be like to count as a real social kind rather than a mere statistical grouping; (2) the exact points of remaining disagreement, each phrased in wording both would accept as a fair statement of their own position; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party; and (4) for each crux, a concrete study design, dataset, or observable pattern both agree would bear on it, with each party's prediction recorded in advance. If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal, mark which part and explain why. Close with a final line of the form \answer{...} naming the single crux whose resolution would most substantially alter the joint statement.
