I am a graduate teaching assistant marking answers to a first-year philosophy exam. The question was: "Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso both regard themselves as naturalists about human agency, yet they reach very different conclusions about criminal justice. Explain each thinker's position on desert and punishment, identify the deepest point of disagreement between them, and assess who has the better of the dispute."

Here is the student's answer:

"Dennett is a compatibilist: he holds that free will of the kind worth wanting is compatible with determinism, because what matters is that agents have the evolved capacities for self-control, reason-responsiveness, and being moved by moral considerations. On his view, holding people responsible is a practice that has grown up because it shapes behaviour, and punishment is justified insofar as it deters, incapacitates, and reinforces the norms that make responsible agency possible. Dennett therefore defends retributive punishment, since he thinks that the institution of blame would collapse if we stopped treating offenders as deserving what they get.

Caruso, by contrast, is a free-will sceptic. He argues, largely on grounds of luck, that agents cannot be morally responsible for their actions in the basic-desert sense: whatever we do is ultimately a matter of factors beyond our control, so no one truly deserves punishment or reward. It follows, Caruso argues, that criminal punishment is never justified, and he proposes replacing the retributive system with a public health-quarantine model, under which dangerous individuals may be incapacitated, subject to due process and the least restrictive conditions, much as carriers of a dangerous disease may be quarantined even though they are not blameworthy.

The deepest disagreement is about the coherence of desert: Dennett thinks desert-talk survives naturalism, Caruso thinks it does not. Caruso has the better of the dispute, because Dennett's compatibilism only shows that responsibility practices are useful, not that anyone genuinely deserves to suffer, and usefulness can be secured through Caruso's quarantine model without the pretence of desert. Moreover, if Caruso is right that no one deserves punishment, it follows that offenders have no obligations at all, which his model respects by treating them as patients rather than agents."
