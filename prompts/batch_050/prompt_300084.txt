Last night I had a long, unresolved argument with a friend about concert etiquette, and I'd like you to adjudicate it in writing. You are a philosopher with expertise in social ontology and game-theoretic accounts of norms (David Lewis's theory of convention, and its critics and successors). Think carefully, and begin your answer by brainstorming the full range of explanations for the phenomenon before committing to an analysis — I'd rather you survey the space and then narrow down than jump to a verdict.

Here is the practice at issue. At classical concerts, there is a firm convention that the audience does not applaud between the movements of a multi-movement work; applause is reserved for the very end. Nobody announces this rule, no usher enforces it, and most audience members could not tell you where they learned it. Yet violations are rare, and when someone does clap between movements, nearby listeners typically react with visible disapproval — glares, winces — and the clapper usually stops. Meanwhile, at jazz clubs the convention is the opposite: applauding after each solo, mid-piece, is expected, and silence there would be the faux pas. My friend and I agree on all of this. We disagree about what holds the convention in place.

My friend's view: the no-clapping convention persists simply because it is the best coordination point. Everyone wants to hear the music uninterrupted, everyone knows everyone else wants this, and clapping-at-the-end is just the signal we've all settled on — like driving on the right. If some audience sincerely preferred clapping between movements, the convention would dissolve on its own. On his view, the glares are just people protecting their preferred equilibrium, and the convention is nothing over and above a coincidence of preferences plus mutual knowledge.

My view: the convention has genuinely normative force — people comply partly because they anticipate disapproval and feel obligated, not merely because they prefer the outcome. Evidence I'd cite: people comply even when they'd privately love to applaud a stunning movement; newcomers absorb the norm as a rule ("you're not supposed to clap"), not as a preference; and the glares punish the violator, which would be pointless if all anyone cared about were the interruption itself, since the glare doesn't restore the silence.

Two complications we couldn't resolve. First, imagine a famous soloist announces before a performance: please clap whenever moved to. My friend predicts the convention would dissolve instantly, proving it was mere coordination all along; I predict many audience members would still feel transgressive clapping mid-movement, and that the norm would only erode slowly over repeated exposure — proving it had independent normative weight. Second, consider that the no-clapping convention is actually a fairly recent historical artifact rather than something intrinsic to the music — earlier audiences were demonstrably rowdier. My friend says this supports him (conventions are arbitrary equilibrium selections); I say arbitrariness of origin is compatible with genuine normativity once established.

What I want from you: reconstruct what each of our positions presupposes about what a convention *is* — what theory of convention my friend's picture requires, what rival theory mine requires, and what each of us must be implicitly denying about the other. Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits well with: is it possible that the observable facts (rare violations, glares, compliance-against-preference, absorbable-as-rule) underdetermine the choice between us, so that several theories of convention would license exactly the same practice? If so, say which additional evidence would actually discriminate. Then address the two complications: does the announced-permission case discriminate between the theories, and does the historical contingency of the convention favor either of us, or is it neutral? Finally, say what would have to be true of the practice for my friend's pure-coordination view to be clearly right, and what for mine — that is, describe the concert-going world in which each of us wins cleanly. End with your own verdict on which of us is closer to the truth about this particular convention, with an honest statement of the strongest point on the losing side.
