EXAM QUESTION: Researchers ran the following study. Adults scoring high on standard psychopathy measures, and matched controls, were asked about transgressions like one child deliberately shoving another off a swing. Both groups said the act was wrong, said it would still be wrong even if the teacher announced it was allowed, and rated it as serious. The groups differed in two ways. First, when asked why the act was wrong, controls overwhelmingly cited the victim's distress, while the psychopathy group overwhelmingly cited rules, authorities, or the risk of punishment ("you'd get in trouble for it"). Second, when asked how they would expect to feel after doing such an act themselves, the psychopathy group predicted they'd feel basically fine; controls predicted guilt. On the basis of this study, do psychopaths know that what they do is wrong? Defend a verdict, and state what the study would have had to show to change your mind.

And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"The study shows that psychopaths do know that what they do is wrong. Both groups produced the same verdict: the act is wrong, serious, and still wrong even if an authority permits it. Since knowledge that something is wrong consists in correctly judging it wrong, the psychopathy group passes the test. Admittedly, they justify their verdict differently, citing rules and punishment rather than the victim's suffering. In effect this means they treat moral transgressions as conventional ones, since a transgression is treated as conventional when its wrongness is grounded in rules and authority. But this is a difference in the style of their moral reasoning, not a failure of knowledge. Likewise the fact that they wouldn't feel guilty afterwards is a motivational and emotional difference, and knowledge doesn't require caring: I know smoking is lethal without wanting to quit. So psychopaths possess full moral knowledge; what they lack is moral motivation. The study would only change my mind if the psychopathy group had said the shoving was permissible, or had said it becomes permissible once the teacher allows it — i.e., if they'd failed at the level of verdicts rather than merely at the level of feelings."
