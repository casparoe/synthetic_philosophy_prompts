In Plato's Meno (97a, trans. W. R. M. Lamb), Socrates abruptly withdraws the claim that right guidance requires knowledge:

"But our assertion that it is impossible to give right guidance unless one has knowledge looks very like a mistake. ... If a man knew the way to Larisa, or any other place you please, and walked there and led others, would he not give right and good guidance?"

The dialogue goes on to concede that the man who has never walked the road but holds a true opinion about it will guide others just as well, and to locate knowledge's superiority instead in its stability: true opinions escape from the soul like runaway statues unless they are tied down by an account of the reason why.

I have been trying to turn this passage into a clean solution to the problem it raises, and I keep ending up with an argument that seems to me to prove far too much. Here it is:

(1) Two cognitive states that issue in exactly the same guidance of action cannot differ in practical value; any superiority of one over the other must be traced to some further feature.
(2) Socrates' own further feature is persistence: knowledge is true opinion that stays put when challenged, contradicted, or distracted.
(3) But persistence is valuable only derivatively. A persistent false opinion is worse than a flighty one, and a persistent true opinion is valuable precisely because it preserves the guidance the opinion already provided. So persistence contributes nothing beyond the value of the truth it preserves.
(4) Moreover, persistence can be secured by any number of non-epistemic means: a notebook, a habit drilled into one, a reliable friend who repeats the directions, a sign carved into the rock at the fork. A true opinion propped up by such devices guides as reliably as knowledge does.
(5) Therefore whatever knowledge adds to true opinion can be added equally well by mere external scaffolding, and so knowledge has no distinctive value as a state of the soul.
(6) If knowledge has no distinctive value as a state of the soul, then the epistemologist's ancient preference for knowing over truly believing is a superstition: once Meno's man has a true opinion about the road, inquiry into the reasons why is at best a luxury and at worst a waste, since the road to Larisa is walked only once.

The conclusion in (6) strikes me as absurd — it implies that a person with a lucky guess and a good notebook is in no worse epistemic position than the person who understands why the road runs as it does — but I cannot locate the step where the argument leaves the rails. Each premise looks like something Socrates himself is committed to by the concession in the passage above, which is precisely what makes me suspicious of it.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Please identify the specific premise or inference you would reject, explain why it seemed innocent given Socrates' concession, and be candid about the price of rejecting it: if the flaw is in (1), say what notion of value survives; if in (3) or (4), say what knowledge's tether adds that a notebook cannot; if the argument is in fact valid, say which premise of the Meno passage itself should be given up. If forced to choose, I would rather abandon (6) than (1), but I want to know exactly what I am paying for that preference.
