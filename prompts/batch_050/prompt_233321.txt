I'm designing a unit on the epistemology of financial markets for an upper-level political economy course, and I want to screen Adam McKay's The Big Short (2015) as its centerpiece. Write a critical reading of the film — roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words — through the cluster of results usually called no-trade theorems and their close relatives: Milgrom and Stokey's theorem that agents with common priors and common knowledge of rationality will not trade on private information alone, Aumann's result that rational agents cannot agree to disagree, and the Grossman–Stiglitz paradox that perfectly informationally efficient prices leave no one with an incentive to gather information in the first place. The film looks, on its face, like a feature-length dramatization of the conditions under which these results break down; I want to know how good a dramatization it actually is.

Please structure the reading around these questions:

First, Michael Burry's edge consists in actually reading the mortgage-bond prospectuses that everyone else in the market treats as boilerplate. Does the film understand his advantage as costly information acquisition — the Grossman–Stiglitz point that someone has to be compensated for doing the reading, and that prices are informative only because markets are a little bit inefficient — or does it present him as simply more rational than everyone else, which would collapse back into the common-knowledge-of-rationality assumption the no-trade theorem needs? These are very different epistemologies, and the film may not have chosen between them.

Second, the no-trade results require that trades happen only for informational reasons. Identify the scenes where trade is driven by something else — banks desperate to offload risk, ratings agencies whose incentives corrupt the signals everyone else prices off, Burry's own investors threatening to pull their money and forcing him to restrict withdrawals. For each, say whether the film is showing an assumption of the theorem failing or showing rational actors responding to a broken incentive structure, and whether the distinction matters.

Third, press on the film's recurring suggestion that the information was sitting in plain sight and the market simply didn't look. Does the film appreciate the tension here: if the relevant facts were genuinely public, the no-trade logic suggests the trade shouldn't have existed, whereas if they were private-but-acquirable, the story becomes one about the price of attention rather than the blindness of the crowd? Does the film conflate these, and if so, is the conflation philosophically innocent?

Fourth, Mark Baum knows the collapse is coming, profits from it, and is morally sickened by both facts. What does his position show about the difference between having a true belief and having knowledge — about confidence, testimony, and what it's like to hold a view that every institution around you prices as false? And does taking the short position change what he can know, given that his trade is itself a small piece of the market he is judging?

Finally, be honest about where the film resists the reading: its celebrity-explainer interludes are didactic in a way that quietly assumes the audience can now see what Wall Street couldn't, and its narrative of a handful of clear-sighted outsiders flirts with survivorship — many people shorted the housing market too early and were destroyed for it. Say what the film, at its best, adds to the philosophy that the theorems alone don't supply, and where a philosopher of economics should warn students that the movie is flatter than the theory.
