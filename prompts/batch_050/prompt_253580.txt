Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been working my way through the literature on rational choice, and Warren Quinn's self-torturer puzzle has stuck with me — the device with a thousand settings, each step up imperceptibly more painful than the last, each step handsomely rewarded, until the agent arrives at agony he would pay to escape. The standard diagnosis is that the agent's preferences must go intransitive somewhere along the sequence. I want to know whether the puzzle can survive without that.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. Build a self-torturer-style case in which the agent's preferences remain transitive throughout — at no point does he prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A — yet he still walks himself, step by rational step, into an end state he would reject outright if offered it as a single choice. Constraints: no implanted devices, no futuristic technology, and no cash payments sweetening each step; the scenario should be one that could occur in ordinary life or in a clinic, with each step offering a genuine, perceptible benefit while the accumulating cost stays below the threshold of discrimination. The scenario itself should run to a paragraph at most, and the agent must know the whole sequence in advance.

After the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specification: state the agent's preferences at a representative step, show why transitivity is preserved across the sequence, and show why the bad endpoint is nonetheless reached. Then identify which structural feature of the case is doing the work — I suspect it is the pairing of a perceptible dimension of benefit with a sub-threshold dimension of harm, but tell me if I am wrong — and say what happens if that feature is removed. Finally, explain how a defender of the intransitivity diagnosis would attack the case, and whether that attack succeeds. The point of the exercise is to establish whether the puzzle fundamentally requires cyclical preferences or only a limit on what agents can discriminate.
