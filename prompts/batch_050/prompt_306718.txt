I direct the historical-verification desk at a nonprofit that produces reference materials for secondary schools. We receive claims about events more than 1,500 years old and must decide how confidently each can be presented. Our reviewers are capable generalists, not ancient historians, and usually have about ninety minutes per claim. They have access to a source dossier prepared by researchers, but they cannot consult specialists during adjudication.

Develop a reusable source-critical procedure for them. It should translate the broad historiographical practices associated with Herodotean inquiry, Thucydidean cross-examination and appeal to likelihood, and later source, transmission, and material criticism into an ordered process that a non-specialist can actually follow. Do not provide citations or a bibliography.

The procedure must, at minimum:

1. Rewrite each submitted claim into its smallest assessable components, distinguishing who acted, what occurred, where and when it occurred, the scale of the event, the actor’s motive, and the historian’s causal interpretation. Explain which components may legitimately receive different confidence ratings.
2. Construct a source map recording each source’s date, genre, authorial position, intended audience, stated or inferable access to the event, likely sources of information, and reasons for selection, omission, exaggeration, or silence.
3. Test apparently independent attestations for dependence. Include practical indicators of literary dependence, such as shared errors, unusual common details, parallel sequence, common terminology, and incompatible claims to eyewitness authority. The procedure must distinguish three situations: two sources independently preserving the same event; one source copying another; and two sources independently drawing on a lost common account. Explain how the third situation should affect confidence.
4. Audit the chain of transmission. Address missing links, later quotation of earlier material, translation, editorial normalization, possible interpolation, and the difference between a text’s surviving manuscript date and the date at which its information was composed. Specify when uncertainty about wording undermines the entire claim and when it affects only a detail.
5. Distinguish authorial reliability by domain rather than treating a source as simply reliable or unreliable. A source may be strong on administrative routine but weak on enemy motives, strong on public events but weak on numbers, or strong on local geography but weak on speeches delivered decades earlier.
6. Establish an ordered set of tests covering chronological and geographical feasibility, anachronism, consistency with material evidence, genre conventions, incentives to distort, specificity of detail, adverse testimony, silence, and explanatory economy. Say which tests can defeat a claim by themselves and which can only lower confidence.
7. Define how to use likelihood without allowing modern intuition to become decisive. Include a rule for culturally unfamiliar practices, socially routine events, unusually precise numbers, retrospective speeches, and reports that benefit the narrator’s community.
8. Assign one of six labels—Established, Probable, Plausible, Weakly Attested, Unsupported, or Contradicted—and give operational thresholds for each. The thresholds must specify how much corroboration is required and how contradictions are resolved. Include a separate category for claims that are historically meaningful but not adjudicable because they concern intentions, private speech, or causes.
9. Identify the exact points where judgment cannot be eliminated, what the reviewer must record at those points, and which disagreements must be escalated to a historian rather than resolved internally.
10. State at least three predictable failure modes of the procedure itself, including a case in which excessive skepticism could erase a well-attested event that looks implausible to modern readers.

After presenting the procedure, run it fully on the following three dossiers. For each dossier, rate every component of the claim separately, identify the decisive evidence, and show where a reviewer might be tempted to reach the wrong result.

Case 1. A regional tradition says that Queen Mavra personally pardoned the leaders of a failed grain revolt after hearing a farmer appeal during a public procession. Three surviving accounts agree on the procession, the appeal, the queen’s words, and the pardon. Researchers have shown, however, that all three reproduce the same improbable sequence of seven minor details and the same mistaken location for a gate that was renamed generations later. One account calls itself an eyewitness report but was composed two centuries after the event. A genuine tax register from Mavra’s reign records a remission of grain debts in the relevant district shortly after the alleged procession, though it gives no reason.

Case 2. A single surviving inventory from a border fortress lists 412 winter cloaks, 203 pairs of boots, and a daily ration issue for eleven weeks. The inventory is a routine administrative tablet with no narrative purpose, and its measurements match storage rooms excavated at the site. The submitted claim is that the fortress housed approximately four hundred soldiers that winter. The tablet does not say who used the cloaks, whether every soldier received one, whether replacements or visitors were included, or whether the listed items were all distributed.

Case 3. Four authors separated by geography and language report that a city’s harbor turned red for three days, after which thousands of fish died and residents fled. Two accounts attribute the event to divine punishment, one to poison dumped by an invading army, and one to a volcanic disturbance. Their narratives differ in sequence, vocabulary, and political sympathies, but all place the event in the same autumn and identify the same two neighborhoods as emptying first. Marine scientists say that a particular algal bloom known in that region can produce a red surface, oxygen depletion, mass fish death, and a sulfur-like odor. None of the authors mentions an odor. The submitted claim is that an invading army poisoned the harbor.

Conclude by revising the procedure in light of any weaknesses exposed by the three cases. The final result should be usable as staff instructions rather than as an essay about historiography.
