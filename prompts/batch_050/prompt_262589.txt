I'm a philosophy professor -- ethics mostly, some action theory -- and I keep ending up in conversations with the CS folks here about AI alignment where I suspect we're talking past eachother but I can't locate exactly where. So I want the translation done properly, by you.

Here's the debate as I understand it from their side. Intent alignment, in the narrow sense some of them use: a system is intent-aligned with an operator if it is trying to do what the operator wants. The success condition concerns the system's aims or motivations tracking the operator's intentions, not whether what it does is actually good. Value alignment, by contrast: the system's behavior or objectives should accord with human values, where the values in question are supposed to be defensible ones, not merely whatever someone happens to want. The narrow definition of "the alignment problem" takes it to be the first of these -- getting a powerful system to reliably try to do what its designers intend. Some researchers argue value alignment is a different problem entirely, a philosophical distraction, or downstream of intent alignment plus good governance. Others reply that intent alignment alone is nearly worthless: an operator can intend terrible things, and "what the operator wants" may not even be well-defined at the scales that matter.

Now restate this whole dispute in my vocabulary: moral worth, acting from duty versus merely in accordance with it, reasons-responsiveness, the good will, virtue versus continence, rule-following and what it is to have an end at all -- whatever from moral psychology and normative ethics genuinely fits. Take the main claims one by one (what intent alignment demands, what value alignment demands, the claim that intent alignment is the real problem, the claim that it's empty without value alignment) and for each say what translates cleanly, what you had to distort to make it fit, and what has no expression at all in the moral-philosophical vocabulary. Then the residue report: does what fails to translate point to something the alignment debate is missing, or to something moral philosophy simply can't say about artifacts? I have a hunch that intent alignment is basically acting in accordance with duty while the debate secretly needs acting from duty, but I've also been told the Kantian machinery presupposes a kind of freedom nobody attributes to an artifact -- in which case my hunch might itself be the mistranslation.
