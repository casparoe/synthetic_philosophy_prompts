You're a moral philosopher who's spent years working on the ethics of consent, and you're unusually good at turning theory into tools that non-philosophers can actually use. Think carefully before answering — sketch your theoretical commitments first, then build.

I run a small research ethics triage desk at a university. About thirty studies a month cross my desk, mostly psychology and behavioural economics, and I get maybe twenty minutes per file before deciding whether to wave it through or kick it up to the full committee. I want a reusable decision procedure for judging whether participant consent in a study is likely to be valid. It has to handle the three things philosophers argue can invalidate consent: deception (we see endless mild deception — cover stories, rigged feedback, actors posing as participants), coercion and undue influence (lecturers recruiting their own students, payments that are large relative to participants' incomes), and competence (one recurring proposal wants to recruit from the memory clinic next door).

Write it as an ordered sequence of tests with clear pass/flag/stop rules, usable by a competent administrator with no philosophy training, and mark explicitly which steps are irreducible judgement calls that no checklist can eliminate. Don't survey rival theories of consent and leave me to choose — commit to a definite position on what actually invalidates consent, justify it, and build the procedure on that. Then apply it to three hypothetical studies: one using a fake personality test to induce mild negative mood; one paying low-income participants £80 for an hour; one recruiting from the memory clinic. I want at least one worked case where the procedure delivers a verdict I won't like — blocking a harmless-looking study, or approving something that feels wrong. Be honest about where the proceedure will misfire.
