I am a novelist in the early stages of a book about a composer, and I need a companion piece — a short story of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words — that I will use both as a tonal exercise and as a way of pressure-testing the ideas the novel will rest on. I'd like you to write it.

The premise, which I want you to take as given: Elias Voss, a recently widowed composer in his sixties, becomes convinced that the sadness listeners hear in music is not really a property of sound at all but a kind of borrowing — that melodies move us only because their shapes echo things we already know: the falling inflection of a grieving voice, the slowing gait of someone exhausted, the catch in speech before tears. To prove this to himself, he builds what he privately calls the grief engine: a short piece for solo cello constructed entirely from the recorded pitch-contours and rhythms of his late wife's voice in her final months — her sighs, the cadence of her good nights, one long sentence she never finished. When he plays it for others, it fails. Listeners find it strange, angular, even agitated, but no one hears grief in it. He has transcribed her sadness perfectly, and it is not sad.

The story should follow what happens next. I have a direction in mind but I want you to own it: perhaps he discovers that the piece becomes devastating when played at half tempo, or when heard by someone who knew her, or perhaps it never works and the discovery is of a different kind. What matters is that the story genuinely wrestles, through its events rather than through exposition, with the question his experiment raises: if the sadness of music were merely resemblance to sad human behavior, his engine should have worked; since it doesn't, what is missing — convention, expectation, the listener's own contribution, something in performance, or something in the sound that his theory never touched? I do not want the story to settle this question, but I want a reader to feel that every scene is pulling on it.

One further thread I require: at some point, an audience must willingly gather to hear sad music and be glad they came. The old puzzle — why anyone seeks out art that makes them feel something like grief — should be present in the room, dramatized, never named.

Craft constraints: third person, close to Elias; no lectures, no characters who speak like philosophy essays; the music itself must be rendered concretely enough that a reader can almost hear the difference between the first performance and whatever the piece becomes; and the ending should resolve Elias's situation without resolving his question. Write with restraint — this is a story about a man and a cello that happens to contain an argument, not an argument wearing a costume.
