I convene a philosophy reading group that meets monthly — eight members or so, all analytically trained, fluent in possible-worlds talk and at home with decision theory, but whose physics bottoms out at one undergraduate course in special relativity plus a popular book or two on Einstein. This semester we are working through the metaphysics of spacetime, and I have drawn the short straw: next session, ninety minutes, I present the hole argument. The group has read nothing technical in preparation, and I have resolved to keep every tensor off the whiteboard. What I need from you is not the explanation itself but a plan for the explanation.

Tell me the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what each depends on. My own instinct is that I cannot open with Einstein's 1913 predicament, because the group will not yet see why the underdetermination of the metric inside a matter-free region should count as a failure of determinism rather than as a trivial observation about descriptions; tell me whether that instinct is right and what has to be in place before the dilemma actually bites. Choose the single concrete construction the session should be built around — presumably some version of taking a model of spacetime, marking off a region, and smoothly rearranging the fields within it while leaving everything outside untouched — and say what this carrying case must include and must exclude: I suspect it has to work without ever writing down a field equation, and that it must be built to resist being misheard as a point about coordinates, but you may push back on either count. Then give me the misconception map: the readings this particular audience will predictably reach for, in the order they will reach for them, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. I expect at least these — that the argument is merely the banality that coordinate systems are conventional; that its conclusion is that spacetime points do not exist; that the two rearranged models are simply two genuinely distinct possible worlds (my group will leap to this one, possible worlds being their native tongue); and that whatever the trouble is, quantum mechanics already broke determinism, so nothing of value is lost. Note that some of these are best defused before the argument is even stated, while at least one might better be allowed to form so that the standard reply has something to work against — tell me which is which. Finally, give me one question to pose at the end whose satisfactory answer would show the argument has landed, something that distinguishes having understood it from merely having been impressed by it, and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level: I assume the literature on whether more sophisticated forms of substantivalism escape the argument, and anything touching quantum gravity, belongs on that list, but correct me if not.
