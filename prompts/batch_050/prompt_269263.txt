For my final-year high school course on literature and ethics, I'm writing an essay on Bernhard Schlink's The Reader, and I want to read it through the fourfold distinction Karl Jaspers draws in The Question of German Guilt between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt. Hanna is plainly criminally guilty in Jaspers's sense, but the novel seems much more interested in Michael, who belongs to the postwar generation and committed no crime himself: does his entanglement with Hanna — and his later inability either to condemn her cleanly or to forgive her — dramatize something like Jaspers's moral or metaphysical guilt, or is it a different phenomenon altogether that Jaspers's scheme doesn't capture? I'd like a reading that is careful in both directions: show where the novel genuinely illuminates the distinctions (for instance, whether Michael's disclosure dilemma at the trial turns on conflating criminal guilt with something else), but also where it resists the framework, since Michael's burden seems tied to intimacy and inheritance rather than to the solidarity Jaspers describes. Finally, does the novel's use of Hanna's illiteracy as the thing she hides even at the cost of her own defense sharpen the question of individual responsibility, or does it risk softening her culpability in a way a Jaspersian reading should reject? No need for quotations from either text.
