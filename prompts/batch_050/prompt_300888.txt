For a grad seminar I'm TAing on Scanlonian contractualism I want to make a decision-tree handout that walks students from "what makes an act wrong is whether someone could reasonably reject the principle permitting it" to an actual position on when the numbers get to count. Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: each branch node should be one concrete question on which contractualists genuinely split, phrased as a rescue or triage case or a general claim that a student could answer yes/no without already knowing which camp the answer lands them in. I'm thinking questions along these lines (you decide the order and which ones make the cut): whether a complaint only gets to compete against another complaint if it's of the same kind or gravity (the "relevant grounds" idea); whether smaller burdens on many people can ever be pooled into something that outweighs a bigger burden on one; whether, when complaints are exactly tied, you may break the tie by saving the greater number or whether you have to give everyone an equal chance (flip a coin); whether it matters that a benefit can only be given to one group as a block rather than divided up.

The leaves should land on at least: Scanlon's own restrictive view, Taurek's coin-flip position, the "relevant claims" approach associated with Voorhoeve, Hirose's more aggregation-friendly contractualism, and a full-blown maximizing view that just adds everything up (as the boundary case contractualism is supposedly contrasted with). If two named positions end up at the same leaf, say so and explain what further question would be needed to seperate them rather than fudging it. If some combination of answers describes a position nobody actually holds, mark the leaf as unoccupied and say in a sentence whether that's because it's incoherent, just unattractive, or plausibly unexplored.

Use the classic cases where they help: one person stranded vs five stranded with resources for only one rescue, the person trapped in the transmitter whose rescue would ruin a broadcast millions are enjoying, and one person's death or paralysis vs a vast number of people's mild headaches. Seven to nine branch questions total. End the handout with a short worked example: trace how a student who answers "yes, tied complaints justify saving more" but "no, headaches never aggregate against death" moves through the tree and where they end up.
