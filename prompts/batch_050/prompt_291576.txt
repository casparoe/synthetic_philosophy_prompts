I'm a retired chemist (40 years in the lab, mostly spectroscopy) and lately I've gotten hooked on philosophy, especially this business about when you're allowed to just flat-out *say* something. Last week two friends of mine got into a real argument about it at my place and I've been chewing on it since. Here's the exchange as best I remember it.

Anna had criticized Ben for telling our neighbor, flat out, that the bridge into town was closed. He'd seen it on a traffic app that morning, one source, and it turned out the app was out of date -- the neighbor drove over fine. Round by round:

(1) Anna: "You shouldn't have said it like that. You didn't *know* the bridge was closed, so you weren't entitled to just assert it. At most you could have said the app says it's closed."

(2) Ben: "That's setting the bar absurdly high. I had perfectly good evidence. If you need to know something before you can say it, nobody could assert anything about traffic, the weather, or the news -- we'd spend our whole lives saying 'I read somewhere that...' Half of what a working scientist reports wouldn't survive that standard either."

(3) Anna: "Hedging costs you two words. The problem isn't that you believed it, it's that you presented it as plain fact. You passed off someone else's claim as your own knowledge, and the neighbor made plans based on that. If you'd been right it would've been luck, like winning a bet you had no busines making."

(4) Ben: "So on your view I can do everything right -- check a reliable source, no reason for doubt -- and still be criticisable forever just because the world didn't cooperate? A rule that punishes you for bad luck alone is a bad rule. And you assert things from memory all the time, Anna, and memory fails way more often than that app does."

(5) Anna: "From memory I'd still hedge if it mattered. The point isn't certainty, it's that the flat form of an assertion tells the listener 'you can take this to the bank,' and he couldn't."

I stayed out of it because honestly I couldn't decide -- both sounded right at different moments. Please adjudicate. Don't sit on the fence: I want you to commit to who's right about which specific point, even where it's uncomfortable, and say who's more right overall and why. If you think both are talking past each other because they're assuming different norms of assertion, fine, but then commit to which norm we should actually adopt.

Format-wise, could you structure it like this:
- Bottom line (2-3 sentences, your verdict stated plainly)
- Anna's moves (roughly 200 words: rate each of (1), (3), (5) as clearly right / partly right / clearly wrong, with brief reasons)
- Ben's moves (roughly 200 words: same treatment for (2) and (4))
- What both are missing (roughly 150 words)
- The norm we should actually live by (roughly 100 words, and here really commit, no "it depends")

Keep it readable for a scientist who's new to the jargon -- if you use terms like "knowledge norm," explain them in a clause or two.
