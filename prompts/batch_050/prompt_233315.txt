I've come to accept a broadly Strawsonian account of moral responsibility: to be responsible just is to be a fitting target of reactive attitudes like resentment, gratitude, and indignation, and questions about when these attitudes are appropriate are to be settled from within our participatory practices rather than by appealing to some independent metaphysical fact about desert or ultimate control. Tell me what would count against this view. As a test case, imagine empirical research establishing that indignation toward strangers is a culturally specific installation -- robustly present in some communities, entirely absent in others whose members nonetheless feel guilt, gratitude, and personal resentment. Would the absence of one attitude in an otherwise intact web of reactive attitudes refute the constitutive claim, or would a Strawsonian comfortably absorb it as variation within the shared human framework? More generally: which cases, intuitions, or results in adjacent areas (about blame's fairness, about psychopaths and their apparent exemption, about whether fittingness itself needs grounding) would be decisive defeaters as opposed to merely awkward ones, and which of these would committed defenders themselves concede as damaging? Finally, assess whether the view's habit of redescribing objections as questions about when attitudes are fitting insulates it against refutation -- and if so, whether that is a strength or a symptom that nothing is really at stake.
