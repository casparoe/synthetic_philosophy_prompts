I direct a small grantmaking program that funds legal-reform projects in three middle-income countries, and our board has asked me to produce a strategy memo on what "strengthening the rule of law" should actually mean for us over the next five years. The classic formulations I keep returning to are Fuller's eight desiderata for a legal system (generality, publicity, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, practicability, stability, and congruence between official action and the declared rules), Raz's thinner, instrumental conception on which the rule of law is essentially the capacity of law to guide conduct, and Waldron's thicker procedural account on which the heart of the ideal lies in courts, hearings, argumentation, and the dignity of being answerable to reasons rather than merely to outcomes. The complication that prompted this memo is that the administrations we work with are rapidly adopting automated systems for licensing, benefits, tax auditing, and even sentencing recommendations -- systems that are, by design, perfectly consistent, non-retroactive, and stable, and that arguably satisfy much of Fuller's list better than any human bureaucracy ever has, while making Waldron's side of the ideal nearly impossible: there is no one to argue with, no reasons given that a citizen could contest, and in some cases no human being who fully understands why a given decision issued.

So here is what I need from you. Treat the three conceptions as rival agendas competing for scarce reform resources, and tell me which components of the rule-of-law ideal deserve priority when the principal threat to legality is no longer the erratic, bribable official but the opaque, hyper-consistent automated administrator. Which elements of the classic accounts turn out to be doing the real normative work in this setting, which look overrated or merely instrumental toward goods that are now secured by other means, and does the automated case reveal that the traditional lists were missing something -- some desideratum (contestability? answerability? reason-giving?) that human legal systems happened to supply without anyone having to list it? I am less interested in a balanced survey of the three theorists than in a defended ordering: for each priority you identify, explain what substantive good it protects, why that good is genuinely at risk (or genuinely safe) under automated administration, and what evidence or development over the next few years would move it up or down your ranking.

Please conclude with your final ordering stated compactly at the very end of your response as \answer{...}, listing your top priorities from first to last (for example, \answer{1. contestability of decisions, 2. ...}).
