I'm taking a course on the ethics of artificial intelligence this semester, and a discussion with my classmates last week left me genuinely puzzled about something we all use daily. As I understand it, the large language models built by major labs are aligned through a roughly two-stage practice: first, paid contractors rank model outputs according to written guidelines about helpfulness and harmlessness, and a reward model is trained to reproduce the aggregate of those rankings; second, the labs publish usage policies and system-level instructions that make the assistant refuse certain requests outright, regardless of what the user wants. At the same time, the same labs publicly say their assistants should not take sides on contested moral and political questions and should be tools that serve the user. What struck my group is that the people whose judgments actually shape the model's values are a small, unrepresentative pool of annotators and policy staff: their rankings are aggregated by something like majority rule, no weight is given to how intensely anyone cares, users and people affected by the model's outputs have no direct input, and the refusals encode substantive moral views that nobody voted on. One classmate thought this is just how any mass-market product works and there's nothing philosophical here; I suspect there's a great deal.

So here is my question, which I'd like to write a paper on. What theory of whose values an aligned AI should serve does this arrangement actually presuppose? I can see several candidates it might be running, and I'd like help separating them: a majoritarian view on which the right values are whatever the aggregate of surveyed judgments supports; a trustee or delegate model on which annotators and lab staff legitimately decide on behalf of users and the public, the way representatives or civil servants do; a liberal-neutrality view on which the assistant should be procedurally neutral between controversial conceptions of the good, which the rhetoric suggests but the refusal policies seem to violate; or a plain market view on which the values embedded are simply the lab's, as its property, and users take it or leave it. Which of these does the practice strictly require to make sense, which does it merely happen to fit, and which rival views does it quietly rule out — for instance, user sovereignty (the assistant serves each user's values within the law), weighting by affected parties (people harmed by outputs get a say even if they never use the product), or moral expertise (alignment should track the views of trained ethicists)?

Finally, is the practice even internally consistent? It seems to presuppose neutrality in its public-facing justifications, aggregation in its training stage, and something closer to paternalism in its refusal policies, and I'm unsure whether one coherent theory of legitimate value-authority could underwrite all three, or whether the practice is simply philosophically underdetermined at some joints and genuinely committed at others. If you think the inconsistency is real, tell me which stage would have to change to make the whole thing coherent, and what the practice would concretely look like under two replacements: one built on user sovereignty, and one built on a deliberative or democratic procedure that includes affected non-users. I'm less interested in whether the current approach is good or bad overall than in making its implicit theory explicit and seeing whether it can survive being made explicit.
