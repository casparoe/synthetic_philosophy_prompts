I'm putting together the take-home final for a graduate seminar on the philosophical foundations of AI safety, cross-listed between philosophy and computer science. I need one essay question on whether capability-preserving decomposition can preserve alignment, and I want it engineered so that students who have merely absorbed the standard iterated-amplification talking points cannot pass, while students who actually understand the mechanism can show it.

Please build the question around this setup, which should be stated in full on the exam so that no outside reading is assumed. An oversight protocol answers a hard query by having an overseer decompose it into subqueries, each handed to a fresh AI instance that may decompose further, until every leaf query is simple enough for the overseer to check directly; the leaf answers are then propagated back up and assembled. A lightweight model is then trained to imitate the assembled answers and deployed on its own, without the decomposition tree. The exam question should force candidates to adjudicate a specific tension: decomposition preserves alignment only if the overseer can verify each node, but genuinely competent solutions to hard problems may route through intermediate steps that are individually inscrutable, and the deployed model imitates only final answers, not the tree's internal checks.

Deliverables: the exam question itself, phrased for a three-hour take-home essay of roughly 1,500 words; a model answer of about 700 words that takes a definite position rather than surveying both sides; and a marking scheme out of 30 points identifying six must-hit moves, the points each carries, and a one-line note on the characteristic way weaker candidates bungle each. The scheme should also state, in one or two sentences, what distinguishes a first-class answer from a merely competent one. I want at least one must-hit point to concern whether decomposition can correct errors as well as compound them, and at least one to concern what is lost when a distilled system inherits outputs without the procedure that produced them.
