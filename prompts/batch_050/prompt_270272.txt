I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a longread about the "other" John Stuart Mill -- the Mill of the posthumously published Chapters on Socialism (written in the late 1860s, came out in 1879), where he takes the socialist critique of capitalism dead seriously instead of treating it as a crank position. My fact-checker and I have got stuck on one of his moves, and it's turned into a genuine philosophical headache.

The relevant bit, paraphrased: Mill considers the standard objection to socialism, that if nobody's reward depended on their own effort, everyone would sink into idleness and the whole economy would stall. His reply is that this objection proves far less than its users think, because the great majority of workers under the present system are day-labourers on fixed wages with no personal stake in the product of their work at all. The supposed motive that only private property can supply is one most workers already don't have. So the incentive objection, if it worked, would condemn the status quo as much as the socialist alternative.

My colleague has turned this into an argument he says is a machine for dissolving objections, and I think the machine runs away with itself. His version:

(1) If an objection to a proposed system applies with at least equal force to the system we already have, the objection is a wash -- it cannot count against the proposal.
(2) The incentive objection applies with at least equal force to existing wage labour, since the wage labourer already works without any direct personal stake in the product.
(3) So the incentive objection cannot count against socialism (Mill's conclusion, and it looks right).
(4) But the same test generalises: "under socialism, unaccountable officials would allocate jobs and goods" -- we already have unaccountable bosses; "under socialism, dissidents would be frozen out of work" -- employers can already blackball people; and so on for any defect that exists in any form under the status quo.
(5) Therefore no objection to any proposed reform can succeed if the defect complained of already exists somewhere in the present system.
(6) But that is absurd: it would make every harebrained scheme immune to criticism, since pretty much any defect you can name exists somewhere or other right now.
(7) So either Mill's reply fails, or something between (3) and (5) slipped.

I'd really like to keep (3) -- the piece leans on it, and honestly it seems like the most interesting thing Mill says in the chapter. And (2) looks like plain historical fact about nineteenth-century wage labour. My colleague says (1) is the culprit but won't say what's wrong with it beyond "it's too strong," which feels like name-calling rather than diagnosis. I suspect there's something fishy in how "applies with equal force" gets used at (2) versus what (1) actually needs, but I can't pin it down, and I definately can't articulate why the move from (3) to (5) might be illegitimate if (1) is granted.

Which step do I reject? For whichever one you pick, explain why it looked perfectly fine on first reading, and then price the alternatives: if instead I keep (1) and deny (2), what does that cost the Mill reading? If I block the generalisation at (4), on what principled grounds -- what feature would an objection need to have for Mill's move to be legitimate? And does any of this force me to soften the claim in the piece that Mill refuted the incentive objection, or is there a way to keep the strong version?

Answer in British English please, and no bibliography or reading list -- I just want the reasoning.
