Last week a friend and I argued deep into the night about the Hart–Devlin debate, and I talked myself into a position I now have to defend in public. Next month I present a paper to our jurisprudence seminar arguing that Devlin's social-disintegration thesis survives Hart's critique, but only if it is recast as a claim about what constitutes a political community rather than as an empirical prediction about what befalls a society whose shared morality decays. On my reading, Hart's demand for evidence of disintegration answers a hypothesis Devlin was never really advancing; the cost, as my friend kept insisting, is that the recast Devlin may no longer be entitled to the coercive enforcement of morals that was the whole point of his position.

The seminar is twelve people: mostly criminal-law theorists formed by Hart and Feinberg, two legal historians, and one Dworkinian who regards the entire debate as superseded. Prepare me for the question period. Give me the seven or eight objections this audience will actually raise — not the ones a conservative public-morality audience would raise — ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way these people would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front, and identify the question I am hoping nobody asks.
