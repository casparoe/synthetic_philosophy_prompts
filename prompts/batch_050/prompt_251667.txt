In "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (Daedalus, 1980; later reprinted in his book The Whale and the Reactor), Langdon Winner argues that technologies can embody political properties in two distinct ways: some technical arrangements become methods of settling the affairs of a community, as when a design is adopted in order to exclude or disadvantage a group, and some systems are inherently political in that they appear to require, or to be strongly compatible with, particular kinds of political relationships. His celebrated example of the first way is Robert Moses's Long Island parkway bridges, which, on the story Winner relays (following Robert Caro's biography of Moses), were deliberately built too low for buses, so that poor and Black New Yorkers who relied on public transit would be kept off the parkways and away from Jones Beach.

Here is the complication I want you to work through. The bridge story's factual basis has since been seriously challenged: historians have noted that Moses's parkways predated mass bus ownership, that commercial vehicles were banned from the parkways anyway by regulation rather than by concrete, that Jones Beach was reachable by public transit, and that some of the allegedly low bridges postdate the relevant planning decisions. Bernward Joerges pressed this critique directly against Winner in the late 1990s.

Treat the Moses bridges episode as a test case for Winner's theory, and run the analysis in both directions. First, supposing Caro's story were true, give the best reconstruction of exactly which of Winner's two categories the bridges fall into, and what the episode is supposed to show about the limits of the view that technologies are neutral tools whose politics come only from their use. Second, supposing the story is substantially false, determine what happens to the theory. Does the critique merely remove a colorful illustration while leaving the argument intact — in which case, identify which of Winner's other examples (the mechanical tomato harvester in California, McCormick's pneumatic molding machines) can carry the argumentative weight — or does it expose a deeper problem: that claims about a design being built "in order to" exclude depend on intentions and counterfactuals that are systematically hard to establish, so that the first category of political artifacts may be empirically fragile in general? Finally, give your verdict: if Winner's most memorable case dissolves under historical scrutiny, is his thesis about artifacts having politics confirmed by its other cases, weakened but standing, or revealed to trade on stories that are rhetorically powerful precisely because they are hard to verify? Where you think the historical record is genuinely unsettled, say so rather than resolving it by fiat.
