Okay so I write novels for a living (literary fiction, one modest hit a decade ago, since then midlist), and through a series of bad decisions -- mostly involving an op-ed I wrote for the local paper after two glasses of wine -- I have become the public face of the campaign to save the wall in Hallowell Park. Quick context: there's a low stone wall that runs diagonally across the park for no reason anyone can determine, it doesn't enclose anything, doesn't mark a boundary, and the city wants to demolish it as part of a big redesign with a new playground and a splash pad. My position is basically Chesterton's fence plus some Burkean stuff I absorbed secondhand: if nobody can tell you what the wall is doing there, that's a reason to go slow, not a reason to grab a sledgehammer, because places accumulate functions nobody wrote down. I've also got a novelists riff about how the wall is load-bearing in the neighborhood's story about itself -- kids learn to balance-walk on it, the marathon route turns at it, three separate couples got engaged at the bend in it -- and how "we can't see the point of it" and "it has no point" are not the same observation.

Anyway, I'm speaking at the city council hearing next Tuesday and I need a Q&A prep sheet. The audience: five council members, two of whom are very data-brained "we ran the numbers" types, one historic preservationist who frankly worries me because she'll overclaim and drag me down with her, the landscape architect who designed the redesign and will be sitting right there, and a public-comment crowd heavy on young urbanist folks who think this is boomer nostalgia standing between their kids and a playground. There's also a local reporter who would love nothing more than a gaffe.

What I want: the eight to ten objections I'm actually going to get, phrased the way each of these specific people would phrase them (the landscape architect will not sound like the urbanist kids), roughly ordered by how likely times how damaging, each with a reply I can deliver in under two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front -- I know someone will say the fence argument proves too much and would have defended every terrible ordinance in history, and "well, this is different" is not going to cut it. And give me the question I'm praying nobody asks. One constraint: I refuse to argue the wall is beautiful or historically significant, it isn't either, and the preservationist has that lane covered anyway. My whole case is epistemic humility about demolition, so the prep has to work within that.
