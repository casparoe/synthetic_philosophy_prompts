I run a reading group on AI alignment for philosophy grad students and a couple of curious CS people, about 10-12 participants, and we have a 2-hour session. We're doing iterated amplification and distillation (the Christiano-style proposal) and I want them to actually feel the central worry rather than just nod along to it: namely, whether you can break a hard task into easier subtasks, get answers to those, and reassemble them -- over and over, with each generation of the system trained to imitate the previous one's amplified output -- without the thing quietly drifting away from what you wanted or losing competence at the edges.

Design me an activity built around a human analogue of this loop. The rough idea I have in mind is that the group has to collectively perform some task that no single person can reliably do alone (something like evaluating a complicated argument, judging a tricky text, or making a fine-grained classification), where one person plays the "overseer" who can only decompose and delegate, other people play the "subagents" who answer pieces without seeing the whole, and then a "distillation" step where a fresh participant who wasn't in the room has to learn to mimic the whole procedure from the amplified outputs alone -- and then the group runs a second generation where the distilled person becomes the new overseer. But I'm not attached to this exact structure if you have a better one; the key thing is that decomposition, amplification, and imitation all have to be things participants *do*, under rules, not things I describe to them.

The activity should be set up so that drift or degradation happens on its own -- ideally participants are surprised by how much, or by *where* it happens (e.g. it might not be random noise, it might be systematic simplification, or the overseer's implicit criteria leaking into subagent answers). Don't tell participants in advance what phenomenon we're hunting for; they should only hear the names "amplification" and "distillation" afterwards.

Please give me: the concrete task (with actual materials -- e.g. the specific text or set of cases, or at least a precise recipe for generating them, since I can't assume participants share background knowledge), the roles and exactly what each role is and isn't allowed to see or say, a timeline for the two hours, how to measure the drift (some way of scoring generation-2 performance against generation-1 or against the "ground truth"), and a debrief that maps each thing that went wrong in the room onto the corresponding worry about the actual proposal (e.g. does our drift correspond to capability loss, to objective corruption, or to something else?).

Also: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (people peeking, the task being too easy so no drift shows up, the distillation step collapsing into just copying, etc.) and how to head it off. And be honest about the disanalogies -- human subagents share a huge amount of background with the overseer in a way that model copies supposedly don't, or maybe do; I want a frank note on which features of the real proposal the game structurally can't capture and that I'll have to just assert in discussion.

One more thing: when you map the activity onto the literature, keep clearly separated what is a broadly shared technical observation about these schemes from what is a live disagreement (e.g. whether decomposition preserves corrigibility, whether amplification is supposed to be a training scheme or an alignment target, whether distillation even claims to preserve anything exactly). Don't present a contested reading as the standard one -- flag it as contested and say who'd push back, roughly.
