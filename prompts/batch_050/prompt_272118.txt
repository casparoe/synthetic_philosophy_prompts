I'm in the first year of a philosophy PhD and my supervisor has told me to carve out a dissertation-sized problem from Lockean property theory by Christmas -- roughly a year of full-time work, just me and a library, no empirical projects. I came into this wanting to write about the labour theory of acquisition in the Second Treatise, but the more I read the more I suspect the interesting action is all in the proviso and in cases Locke couldn't have imagined. My flatmate, an economist, keeps telling me the whole framework is a museum piece and I should just do public economics instead, wich is part of why I want to get the prioritisation right before committing.

Here are the candidate problems I'm weighing up, though you're welcome to tell me I've misdrawn the list. First: whether the proviso should be read as a sufficient-resources condition (leave enough for others) or a no-worsening condition (acquisition must not leave anyone worse off than the unowned baseline), and how much of the rest of the theory hangs on that choice. Second: a thought I had in a seminar and haven't seen treated anywhere -- instead of reading the proviso as a side-constraint that voids acquisitions when violated, read it as generating something like a standing ledger of compensable debts, where appropriators owe ongoing compensation indexed to the scarcity they create. Does that collapse into the standard no-worsening-plus-compensation reading, or is it a genuinely distinct position with different implications for, say, carbon budgets and the atmosphere? Third: whether labour-mixing ever gets going at all for non-rival goods like data, designs, and gene sequences, where the proviso seems trivially satisfied by anyone's appropriation -- does Lockean theory licence unlimited enclosure of the digital commons, or does it show labour-mixing was always a metaphor for transformation of rivalrous stuff and simply doesn't apply? Fourth: the old chestnut about whether mixing labour extends the labourer into the object or just wastes the labour, but specifically whether anything downstream actually depends on settling it.

What I want is a defended agenda, not a survey. Rank these (or replacements you think are stronger) for a year of one person's effort, and be explicit about the criteria: how many other questions in the area are downstream of each one, tractability with armchair methods alone, and how neglected each is relative to its importance. If you think one question is upstream of the others -- such that answering it changes what the others even ask -- say so and let that drive the ranking. I'd also like you to flag which question you'd steer me away from despite its surface appeal, and why. Please say what would change your mind about the top pick -- which argument or consideration, if it landed, would demote it -- and where you think your own ranking is most likely to be wrong.
