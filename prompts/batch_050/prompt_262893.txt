I teach a third-year philosophy of biology seminar, and every year the units of selection unit ends the same way: half the room leaves convinced that the gene's-eye view and group selection are just two ways of describing the same mathematics, so the whole controversy was a mistake; the other half insists that multi-level selection theorists have shown something real about, say, the evolution of altruism or major transitions, and that the equivalencers are missing the point. The frustrating part is that both sides seem to have been saying exactly this since at least the early 1990s, through the sociobiology wars and their aftermath, without much visible movement. My own hypothesis, which I hold loosely, is that the debate stalled because it quietly runs together several different questions that participants never disentangled: a formal question about whether the competing mathematical frameworks (kin selection, multi-level selection, the Price equation decompositions) can represent the same processes; an empirical question about which causal structure actually obtains in particular cases; and an interpretive question about which description is most perspicuous or causally accurate. If that's right, then 'equivalence' results, even when correct, settle less than either side assumes.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a resolution of it and not a survey of the positions. Take seriously the possibility that at least some participants were talking past each other: when a gene-centrist says group selection reduces to inclusive fitness, and a multi-level selection theorist replies that this misses the causal structure of, for example, trait-group or species-level processes, is that a factual dispute, a dispute about what counts as causal structure, or a dispute about which level of description earns the title 'the real unit of selection'? I'd also like you to consider a subtler possibility: that the disagreement is substantive but not empirical in the usual way, being instead about modelling conventions — about which features of a model count as representing the world and which count as bookkeeping — and that this kind of disagreement cannot be closed by data alone, only made explicit. If you think I'm wrong and there is a crisp factual core to the dispute, say so and locate it.

Finally, given your diagnosis, tell me what progress would actually look like. Would it be a formal result (say, a theorem about when the decompositions come apart), a canonical empirical case that forces one description, a piece of terminological legislation, or simply an agreement to split the question into several and stop using the old vocabulary? Or is this one of those debates where no further progress should be expected because the remaining disagreement is purely one of emphasis? Please give reasons rather than hedged summaries, and write your answer in British English.
