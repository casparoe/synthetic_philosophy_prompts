So last night my friend Jess and I got into it for like three hours over Arendt and Eichmann and it's still bugging me. Her position: the banality of evil is basically a psychological diagnosis — Eichmann was a thoughtless bureaucrat, a cog, and the scary lesson is that ordinary careerists can do monstrous things without hatred. My position, the hill I've decided to die on: that's exactly the pop misreading people accuse Arendt of, and the real thesis is about a failure of judgment — specifically the inability to think from the standpoint of other people, which lets someone keep functioning "morally" in a world where the normal moral coordinates have been swapped out. The point isn't that Eichmann had no ideology, it's that no depth of ideology explains what happened at the trial itself, where the categories were the problem, not the man.

Anyway, in a moment of weakness I agreed to defend this reading at our department's political theory reading group next week, and I'm starting to regret it. The crowd: mostly Arendt-sympathetic political theorists who treat the Eichmann book as close to scripture; two historians who think the archival record shows Eichmann was a convinced, self-driving antisemitic ideologue and that Arendt got played by his courtroom performance of ordinariness; and one moral psychologist who keeps wanting to steer everything toward moral disengagement and situationism and thinks the whole interpretive fight is armchair anthropology.

Give me a prep sheet, not a defense: the objections I'll actually get from this specific crowd (not generic "but wasn't he a monster?" stuff), roughly in the order they'll come and sorted by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that person would actually phrase it, with what I should concede, what I should hold, a two-minute reply, and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I'm praying nobody asks. My guess is it's something about whether my failure-of-judgment reading secretly drains Eichmann of responsibility, which is the opposite of what Arendt wanted, but you tell me — maybe there's a worse one I'm not seeing.

One thing though: if you think my reading is itself the confused one — like if the thoughtlessness/judgment distinction doesn't actually hold up across her work, or I'm the one flattening her by reading the Eichmann book backwards through the later stuff on thinking — say that up front instead of politely prepping me for a talk I shouldn't give. I'd rather lose to you here than to the historians on Tuesday.
