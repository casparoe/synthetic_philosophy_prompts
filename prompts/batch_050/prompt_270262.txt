Okay so here's a question I've been chewing on. Say Adam Smith gets dropped into the present day and given a thorough briefing on the Nordic countries: Denmark, Sweden, Norway, the lot. So he learns about the high marginal tax rates, the universal welfare state, the flexicurity thing where its easy to fire people but the state catches them, the strong unions negotiating sector-wide deals, and also the surveys showing these countries have basically the highest levels of generalized social trust ever measured, most people there say strangers can be trusted. Assume he takes the empirical picture at face value.

Here's what I want to know. In the Theory of Moral Sentiments he seems to think society can hold together on mere justice and mutual self-interest, beneficence is nice but not load-bearing, and then in the Wealth of Nations he gives the famous point about the butcher and baker not feeding us out of benevolence. But the Nordic model looks like it runs on something more than that: it apparently depends on people trusting strangers enough to tolerate paying half their income to be redistributed to people theyve never met, and trusting that those people arent freeriding. So: would Smith see generalized trust as just his notion of sympathy scaled up through institutions, or is it a genuinely different social glue that his framework can't account for? Would he classify the welfare state as justice (enforceable, required for society to function) or as compulsory beneficence (which I think he'd be more suspicious of)? And given his worries about how institutions can corrupt or cultivate the sentiments, would he think Nordic trust is the cause of the model working or an effect of the institutions themselves, which matters a lot for the export question, i.e. whether a low-trust country like, say, Brazil or the US could adopt the policy package and grow the trust, or whether that just collapses without the pre-existing trust.

Give me your best reconstruction of his answers to each of these, flagging honestly where the texts genuinely underdetermine what he'd say versus where you think there's a clear Smithian verdict.
