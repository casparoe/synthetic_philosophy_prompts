I am a healthy 34-year-old engineer with a comfortable income and no dependents. Several colleagues of mine have signed contracts with a cryonics organization: in exchange for annual membership dues and a life-insurance policy that pays out roughly $80,000 upon death, the organization will vitrify my body (or just my brain) at legal death and store it indefinitely, on the stated hope that future medicine will be able to repair the damage, reverse the preservation process, and restore me to life.

My colleagues argue that I am being irrational not to join. They concede that the probability of eventual revival is low — they will grant numbers like one in a thousand, perhaps lower. But the payoff conditional on success, they say, is staggeringly large: potentially centuries of additional healthy life, which by any reasonable valuation dwarfs the modest financial cost. A tiny probability multiplied by a sufficiently enormous payoff yields a positive expected value, and that, they claim, settles the matter. One of them added that I already accept this style of reasoning every time I buy ordinary insurance: I pay real money to guard against outcomes I consider unlikely.

I remain unpersuaded, but I cannot articulate exactly why, and I would like to know whether my hesitations are cogent or merely squeamishness dressed up as argument. Three things nag at me. First, the logic seems to prove too much: there is a long queue of speculative, low-probability, high-payoff ventures competing for the same money — asteroid-defense charities, life-extension research, for that matter religions promising everlasting life to the faithful — and the expected-value recipe my colleagues invoke would appear to recommend nearly all of them, which is impossible. Second, I doubt that the probability in this case is the kind of thing one can even estimate; actuarial tables exist for house fires, but where would a one-in-a-thousand figure for future revival come from, and does expected-value reasoning retain any grip when the probability is little more than a made-up number? Third, the promised payoff, however vast, is presumably finite, and I have a vague recollection that wagers of this form are at their most compelling only when the payoff is strictly infinite, and become ordinary gambles — no longer argument-proof — the moment it is not.

Please identify the general structure of my colleagues' argument, evaluate whether each of my three worries genuinely damages it or can be answered, and render a decision-relevant verdict: should I sign the contract, and what single piece of additional information would most change your answer?
