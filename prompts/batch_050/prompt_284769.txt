After a long pub arguement with a mate, I still can’t work out what people mean when they say an artefact itself can have politics. He thinks that’s obviously a category error: people have politics; speed bumps, delivery robots and cashless ticket gates are just stuff. I suspect both of us are sliding between different claims.

Design a 75-minute activity for 18 second-year undergraduates, run before any lecture, that makes them feel the force of this debate rather than just state positions. Give each team a small design problem involving a campus delivery robot, with competing goals, limited resources and several concrete decisions about routes, sensors, defaults, priority rules, failures and who can override the system. The rules or payoffs should lead them to build in political or distributive consequences while sincerely trying to remain neutral. Then have them use one another’s designs from assigned social positions, without asking students to disclose personal vulnerabilities.

Please provide setup, materials, roles, scoring, timeline and debrief. The debrief should distinguish political intent, political consequences, technological determinism, and the claim that artefacts are morally neutral instruments. Say what will predictably go wrong, and be honest about what the game cannot capture: especially long-run feedback between institutions and technology.
