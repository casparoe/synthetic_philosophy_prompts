So I'm giving a 25-minute paper at a history-of-ethics workshop in about three weeks, and I want to be ready for the Q&A. The paper is on the dualism of practical reason in Henry Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics, and my thesis is a bit contrarian: I argue that the dualism -- Sidgwick's admission, at the end of the book, that rational egoism and universal benevolence are both self-evident and yet irreconcilable -- is not the famous failure it's usually read as, but the intended and honest payoff of the method he announces right at the start. Here's the passage I'm leaning on, from the Preface to the first edition (1874):

"I have thought that the predominance in the minds of moralists of a desire to edify has impeded the real progress of ethical science: and that this would be benefited by an application to it of the same disinterested curiosity to which we chiefly owe the great discoveries of physics."

My claim is that this anti-edification pledge explains the shape of the whole book: Sidgwick can't force a harmony between the two principles precisely because he's committed to reporting what disinterested examination actually delivers, so the closing chapter should be read as a finding, not a confession of defeat. The edifying move would have been to fudge it; he refuses to.

The audience: a dozen or so people. Two senior Sidgwick scholars who know the editions and the correspondence and will definitely catch any sloppiness, a couple of utilitarian moral philosophers who mostly care about whether the dualism can be dissolved rather than endured, one grumpy Kantian who thinks the whole utilitarian project was doomed anyway, and some grad students. Nobody there is a decision theorist.
