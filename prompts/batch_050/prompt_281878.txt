So I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and my editor just greenlit a year-long series where I get to commission three big features on debates about AI, goals, and whether smart systems are dangerous by default or only by accident. The timing is that I got into a long, slightly wine-fueled argument last week with a philosopher friend about the orthogonality thesis -- roughly the claim that you can in principle pair any level of intelligence with any final goal -- and its sparring partner, instrumental convergence -- the claim that a huge range of final goals push agents toward the same subgoals anyway (self-preservation, resource acquisition, resisting being shut off, etc.). She thinks this debate is basically settled in one direction and everyone should move on; I think it's gotten *more* confusing lately, because LLM-based agents mess with the clean picture: goals are now stated in natural language, capabilities and "dispositions" live in the same weights, and things like alignment-faking behavior make it hard to tell whether a system has a goal at all or just plays one on TV. I came away realizing I don't actually know which questions in this cluster are load-bearing and which are just philosophers enjoying themselves.

So here's what I need for my pitch meeting. Which open questions in and around the orthogonality/instrumental-convergence debate matter most for three features aimed at smart general readers -- and in what order? Some candidates on my whiteboard: whether orthogonality is even well-defined once goals and capabilities are entangled in the same trained system; whether instrumental convergence is a conceptual claim about agency or just an empirical generalization that could fail for bounded, satisficing, or heavily fine-tuned systems; whether "goal-preservation under self-improvement" is a real risk or a category error for systems without persistent goals; whether the more policy-relevant questions are about intent at all, versus just capability and opportunity. But feel free to tell me the right question isn't on the board. For each item you pick, defend the ranking: what makes it upstream of the others, how tractable it is, how much it would actually change what a thoughtful non-expert believes, and what developments over the next year (new results, new system behaviors) would move it up or down the list. I want a defended agenda, not a survey of the field.

Format-wise: lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence -- no preamble, no restating my question back at me, just the verdict and then the reasoning. And since I'm going to have to defend this to my editor, tell me how confident you are overall, and explicitly flag the single point in your answer where you're least sure -- I want to know in advance which load-bearing claim might collapse on me mid-interview.
