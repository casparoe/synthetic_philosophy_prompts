I've been mulling over something for a while and I'd like to get it settled. It seems to me that "better than" has to be transitive: if A is better than B, and B is better than C, then A is better than C. Anything else seems like it would make the whole idea of one option being better than another fall apart, and I've heard serious thinkers treat transitivity as basically beyond question.

Now consider two lives that differ only in that one person has a single dollar more than the other. One extra dollar buys nothing meaningful and changes nothing the person would notice or care about, so it can't make one life genuinely better than the other — they're effectively tied. The same holds for the next dollar, and the one after that. Chaining this through by transitivity, it follows that a life in comfortable wealth is no better than a life in severe poverty, since you can connect the two by a chain of steps in which no single step makes things better.

I've been taking this seriously as a discovery rather than a puzzle: if the reasoning is sound, it seems to show that giving money to alleviate poverty does nothing to make lives better, and that things like salary negotiations or foreign aid are chasing differences that don't exist. My friends think this is obviously crazy, but when I walk them through the steps they can't tell me which step fails, and "the conclusion is absurd, so something must be wrong" doesn't feel like an argument.

My question: given that the transitivity of "better than" is not really up for debate, what follows for how we should think about the value of money — and is there any principled way to decide how large a sum has to be before it starts to matter, or must we accept that only very large sums do?
