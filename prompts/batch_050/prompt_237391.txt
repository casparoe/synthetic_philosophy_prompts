Act as a moral philosopher who moonlights as a comedy writer, the sort of person who finds moral mathematics genuinely funny. Before you start writing, do a quick brainstorm (just a bullet list, 4-6 items) of which features of the individual-inefficacy debate would make the best running jokes, then pick the strongest three and build the piece around those.

Then write me a parody: the employee handbook — specifically the "Ethics & Corporate Responsibility" section — of a fictional megacorporation, call it Aggregate Industries (or a name you like better), whose entire business model is the accumulation of tiny, individually negligible harms. Carbon emissions, industrial chicken farming, data harvesting, whatever mix works. The company's official ethics policy rests entirely on the inefficacy objection: no single employee's action ever makes a difference anyone could detect, so no one is ever blameworthy, and the handbook explains this to new hires with real warmth and reassurance. Structure it as: a welcome letter from the Head of Ethics, the core policy itself, an FAQ for conscientious employees, and the complaints procedure (the complaints form can be a gag of its own).

The jokes definately have to come from getting the philosophy right — imperceptible thresholds, expected-value reasoning applied only in the places where it's convenient, enormous collective outcomes that nobody caused, the way "but what if everyone did that?" gets smoothly deflected — so an ethicist laughs in recognition rather than wincing, because the satire exaggerates a real feature of the position instead of inventing one. Meanwhile someone with no philosophy background should still find it funny and vaguely uneasy. Stay deadpan throughout: the handbook never breaks character and never states the objections out loud, but an attentive reader should feel those objections pressing against the fine print. Around 700 words.

After the piece, add a short note (out of character) saying where you took liberties with the actual arguments or leaned on a contested reading of them, and which real consideration or counterargument, if the handbook's authors were forced to take it seriously, would most plausibly bring the whole policy down.
