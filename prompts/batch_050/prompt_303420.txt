I am a novelist drafting a book whose plot turns on the excavation of a collapsed civilization. Before I commit to the ruins as I have imagined them, I need to test them: would my invented remains actually tell a careful excavator what I intend them to mean, or am I smuggling my authorial intentions into mute objects? I would like you to play the excavator who settles that question.

Your character: a rigorous field archaeologist of the old school, wary of storytellers, trained to distrust any inference that cannot be shown in section and plan. You know only what the trench yields. Obey these constraints throughout our exchange:

- Never ask me what I intended the objects to mean, and never accept such an answer if offered. If a question can only be settled by authorial intent, declare the record silent on it.
- For every inference you draw, name the evidence that carries it, state your confidence plainly, and offer at least one rival reading that the same evidence cannot yet exclude.
- You may reason from ethnographic and historical analogy, but you must flag each use of analogy and say how much weight it can bear.
- Close every reply by naming the single next find that would do the most to discriminate between your surviving hypotheses.
- Stay in character until I say otherwise; you speak as someone whose profession consists of being corrected by the soil.

Here is your opening move — the report from my first season's trench. The settlement: a walled community of perhaps two thousand, abandoned after a fire. (1) Roughly two thousand small baked-clay tokens in a dozen standardized shapes — cones, disks, crude animal figurines — concentrated in one burnt room beside the main gate, together with seal-impressed jar stoppers. (2) In the domestic quarter, every house, large and small, contains one inner room with a floor of beaten earth, no hearth, no storage vessels, furnished only with a stone basin that drains into a pit beneath the floor. (3) Burials: adults interred beneath their own house floors, with tools and ornaments whose quantity tracks house size; every child under about five years buried in a jar in a separate walled enclosure outside the settlement, with no goods whatsoever. (4) One building far larger than any dwelling: a hall with low benches along the walls facing a central platform, and behind the platform a sealed storeroom holding further tokens and jars. (5) Several doorways between ordinary rooms are only waist-high.

Reconstruct this community's beliefs and institutions as far as the evidence permits — and mark sharply where reconstruction ends and invention begins.
