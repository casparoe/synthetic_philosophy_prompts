I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been turning over the decision-theory puzzle known as counterfactual mugging. As I understand it, a flawless predictor (usually called Omega) tells you that it flipped a fair coin yesterday. Had the coin landed heads, it would have offered you the following deal: pay it £100 now and receive £10,000. The coin in fact landed tails, so Omega now simply asks you for the £100, with no reward attached. Some people argue you should pay, because a person disposed to pay would, in advance of the flip, have been better off under Omega's arrangement; others reply that the coin has already landed, nothing you do now can change the past, and paying is just handing over money.

I want to pressure-test a variant of my own devising, and I would like you to write a dialogue about it. In my variant, Omega does not flip a coin. Instead, before approaching you, it publicly commits to a secret seed by publishing a cryptographic hash of it — the sort of commitment scheme I work with professionally — and the "outcome" is whatever a publicly specified pseudo-random number generator produces when run on that seed. The result was therefore fixed, and in principle knowable, from the moment the hash was published; it is unknown to you only because you cannot invert the hash. As before, the generator resolves to tails, and Omega asks for the £100.

Write a dialogue of roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words between two equally capable interlocutors — you may make them engineers like me, but neither should be a mouthpiece or a fool. Priya holds that you should pay; Marcus holds that you should not. The dialogue must meet the following conditions:

- The exchange must consist of genuine back-and-forth: each speaker should engage with the other speaker's actual arguments, concede where a point lands, and not simply restate an opening position.
- The interlocutors must take the committed-seed variant seriously as a variant. They should address whether replacing a genuinely chancy flip with an outcome that is fixed but epistemically inaccessible changes the case, and whether the rational thing to do can depend on the agent's computational limitations — on the fact that you cannot invert the hash but a more powerful agent could.
- At some point they should compare the case with this one: you are stranded in the desert, a driver offers to rescue you if you promise to pay her £100 on arriving home, and once you are safely home you must decide whether to keep the promise. They should argue about whether the comparison illuminates or misleads.
- Priya should be pressed on whether "be the kind of agent who pays" is a genuine reason or a rationalisation, and Marcus should be pressed on the fact that a policy of refusing is precisely the disposition that predictors stop offering profitable arrangements to, so that his refusal has, in a sense, already cost him.
- One of them should introduce a structurally similar case and the other should respond to it properly: a perfect predictor offers, for £50, to tell you whether your house has termites, and it sends you its report if and only if the report is bad news. Paying means buying bad news exactly when there is bad news to buy.
- The dialogue should end with genuine disagreement, or at most a narrow and honestly earned point of agreement. Do not force a reconciliation, and do not have one side simply win.

After the dialogue, give me a structured summary as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "key_moves": an array of objects, each with the fields "speaker", "argument" (one sentence stating the move), "strongest_objection" (one sentence stating the best reply it provoked) and "outcome" (one sentence on whether the move survived);
- "seed_variant_verdict": a string stating, in two or three sentences, whether the dialogue concluded that the committed-seed variant makes a difference, and why;
- "points_of_agreement": an array of strings;
- "unresolved_questions": an array of strings.

Please keep everything self-contained: no citations, footnotes or references to the academic literature, and explain any technical terms as you go, since my background is in engineering rather than philosophy.
