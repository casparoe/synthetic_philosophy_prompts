A colleague of mine who moonlights as an algebraist sent me an argument whose conclusion I can't accept, but I can't locate the misstep either. It purports to show that every nontrivial group has a maximal proper subgroup — a proper subgroup contained in no other proper subgroup. His reasoning:

(1) Let G be a nontrivial group and consider the set P of all proper subgroups of G, partially ordered by inclusion. P is nonempty, since it contains the trivial subgroup.

(2) Every chain in P has an upper bound in P: the union of a chain of proper subgroups is itself a subgroup of G (a standard fact about chains of subgroups), and it is proper because every member of the chain is proper — no member of the chain contains every element of G, so their union cannot either.

(3) Since every chain in P has an upper bound in P, Zorn's lemma applies, and P has a maximal element.

(4) A maximal element of P is, by definition, a maximal proper subgroup of G. Hence every nontrivial group has one.

This seems to prove too much. Applied to the additive group of the rational numbers, it yields a maximal proper subgroup of (Q, +), and I have a strong suspicion that no such subgroup exists — though I admit I can't prove that either. I'd like to keep Zorn's lemma itself, the standard machinery of chains and unions, and ideally the conclusion for the cases I actually care about (finitely generated groups, say). Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine in the first place, and what rejecting it costs — in particular, whether this is a local problem with step (2) or a sign that arguments of this general shape need a hypothesis my colleague omitted.
