As a high school senior, I am writing a 2,500-word capstone essay on what modern democracies should learn from classical Athens's three great failures: the Assembly's decision on Mytilene in 427 BC and its overnight reversal, the Sicilian Expedition of 415–413 BC, and the trial of Socrates in 399 BC. Given the word limit, I can examine only one episode in real depth.

Act as a scholar of both ancient history and contemporary democratic theory. Think carefully, and briefly brainstorm the candidate lessons of each episode before committing to an answer. Then tell me which episode should anchor my essay and, within it, which two or three open questions deserve most of my attention. I am less interested in the standard textbook morals (the mob is fickle; majorities tyrannize) than in a defended agenda: state the criteria you are using — for example, how far a lesson generalizes beyond direct democracy to modern representative institutions, how contested the standard reading of the episode is, and how much hangs on historical details that are genuinely uncertain — explain the trade-offs among those criteria, and say what would change your ranking.

One tension I want addressed: my textbook treats all three episodes as proof that popular rule is irrational, but the Mytilene reversal might equally show that democracies can correct themselves, and Sicily might have been an information failure rather than a deliberation failure — problems no political system is immune to. If either reframing is right, the standard lesson collapses. Does that make these episodes more worth prioritizing, or less?
