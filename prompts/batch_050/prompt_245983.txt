So I run this informal "nerd night" thing at my company (we're a mid-size SaaS shop, I do backend stuff, zero philosophy background) where once a month a mix of engineers, PMs, and a couple designers meet after work and do some kind of structured discussion or game. Usually around 12-14 people, we've got a conference room for about 75 minutes including pizza time. Last month someone mentioned the "handicap principle" from biology -- the idea that peacock tails and stotting gazelles work as signals precisely because they're wasteful and hard to fake -- and it instantly clicked for me as the thing that explains half my professional life: why "trust me, I'm a great engineer" is worthless in an interview but a whiteboard gauntlet supposedly isn't, why nobody believes marketing copy but people do believe a generous free tier, why the most senior person in the room never bothers signaling. So for next month I want an activity where the group *feels* why cheap talk collapses and costly signals survive, instead of me just lecturing at them.

Design me a game for this. My rough idea that you're free to improve or scrap: some kind of repeated round structure where players privately know their own "quality" (honest vs. faker type, or high vs. low) and try to convince others to pick them as partners/invest in them, with made-up payoff points instead of real money. First phase should be pure cheap talk so the group watches communication dissolve into everyone claiming to be great and selection becoming basically random -- I want them to feel that frustration. Then in a second phase introduce some costly action players can take that costs *more* for the low type than the high type, and let them discover on their own that suddenly believable separation happens, and maybe even that the signal has to stay wasteful to keep working (if it gets cheap or useful for everyone, it stops meaning anything). Bonus if the game can produce a moment of countersignaling, where the clearly-established top performer conspicuously skips the costly signal and gets picked anyway.

Give me: concrete rules and setup I can print on a one-pager, the payoff numbers (and why those numbers and not others -- I want the math to actually force the dynamics, not just gesture at them), a rough timeline that fits 75 minutes, and a debrief script that maps what happened onto the handicap principle and onto interview/hiring stuff my audience will care about, only naming the theory at the end. Also tell me honestly what will probably go wrong the first time I run it -- e.g. engineers will immediately try to min-max the payoff table and treat it as an optimization problem rather than an experience, or someone will find an exploit that breaks the signaling equilibrium -- and how to handle that. And note which part of the real theory the game can't capture and I have to just say out loud.
