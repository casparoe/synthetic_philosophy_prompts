I run the graduate interview series for my department's philosophy journal, and our next guest is a philosopher of science who defends the orthodox Bayesian resolution of Hempel's raven paradox: observing a white shoe genuinely does confirm the hypothesis that all ravens are black, only to a minuscule degree, and our sense that this is absurd reflects a mistake about how confirmation works. Please draft eight to ten interview questions for the session. She has written on this topic for twenty years, so none of the questions should merely invite her to restate the resolution; each should press at a point where the position is genuinely exposed, and each should be accompanied by one follow-up question calibrated to the reply she is most likely to give.

Please include at least one question on each of the following pressure points:

1. The equivalence condition. The Bayesian treats evidence for "all non-black things are non-ravens" as evidence for "all ravens are black" on the ground that the two are logically equivalent. Ask whether that assumption, rather than anything about ravens or probability, is carrying the entire weight of the paradox. In the follow-up, press on whether logically equivalent reformulations of a hypothesis must always receive identical evidential support, even in cases where we would investigate them by entirely different methods and with entirely different expectations.

2. Indoor ornithology. Hempel himself accepted that the resolution licenses confirming ornithological hypotheses without leaving one's armchair, treating the counterintuitive flavor as a psychological illusion. Ask whether she accepts this consequence outright, and — if she bites the bullet — whether its very willingness to license armchair confirmation shows that the Bayesian notion of confirmation has drifted away from anything working scientists mean by evidence.

3. Background dependence. The degree to which a white shoe confirms the hypothesis depends on one's prior beliefs about the relative numbers of ravens and non-black objects. Ask whether this means the resolution converts what looked like a purely logical puzzle about confirmation into a contingent dispute about priors, and whether that constitutes a solution or merely a relocation of the problem. The follow-up should address the obvious rejoinder that all confirmation is background-relative.

4. Selection effects. Ask whether it matters how the white shoe came to be observed — sampled at random from the totality of objects, versus deliberately sought out as a non-black thing. If she grants that the method of selection alters the evidential import, follow up on whether the orthodox resolution then needs to be supplemented by a substantive theory of sampling, and what work remains for the Bayesian core.

5. The relation to Goodman's new riddle. Ask whether the ravens constitute a distinct problem about the logic of confirmation or simply the old problem of induction restated in ornithological dress, and what would count as solving one without solving the other.

Finally, include at least one question that asks her to name what she takes to be the strongest rival treatment of the paradox — for instance, the Popperian denial that positive instances ever confirm generalizations, or the proposal that confirmation is a three-place relation requiring an explicit relevance clause — and to explain why she rejects it. Avoid anything generic: no questions about her career, her intellectual biography, or how she first encountered the paradox.
