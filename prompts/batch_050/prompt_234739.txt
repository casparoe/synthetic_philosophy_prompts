So I cover language, cog sci, and AI for a general-audience magazine, and every couple of years I have to write something that touches the poverty of the stimulus fight, and it drives me nuts because the thing has been running since before I was born and as far as I can tell nobody has moved in decades. Nativists say kids end up knowing things about grammar -- the stock example is that question formation is structure-dependent, you get "the dog that is in the corner is hungry" → "is the dog that is in the corner hungry?" and never "is the dog that in the corner is hungry?" -- that they couldn't have extracted from the speech they actually hear, so some of it has to be built in. The other side (usage-based people, various empiricists, lately a lot of the neural net crowd) says the input is way richer than nativists claim, that the famous "no child ever hears X" claims keep failing when someone actually checks a corpus, and that general-purpose learning over enough statistics gets you there. And now with language models everyone in my mentions insists the debate is settled, except half say LLMs prove you don't need innate grammar and half say LLMs train on a thousand times more words than any kid hears so they prove the opposite.

My working hypothesis, and I'd like you to stress-test it rather than just accept it: the two camps are using "learned from the input" in different senses. One side means something like "no general learning mechanism with weak priors could converge on the right grammar from data of this quantity and quality," and the other means something closer to "the pattern is in principle statistically present in the signal." If that's right, a lot of the debate is people talking past each other, and the real disagreement is about what the burden of proof is -- which would explain why more corpora and fancier models never settle anything.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A straight empirical question that just turns out to be hard? A methodological fight about what counts as evidence (corpus counts vs. formal learnability arguments vs. adult intuitions vs. child experiments)? Dueling priors that can't be argued for? Or my hypothesis, that part of it is semantic and the residue is something else? Give me a diagnosis with reasons, grounded in what the sides actually argue, and tell me what -- a formal result, a model trained on genuinely child-scale input, better cross-linguistic acquisition data, whatever -- would count as progress given your diagnosis. Also: what would change *your* mind here? What argument or finding would lead you to a different diagnosis of the debate, and where is your own reasoning most likely to fall apart?
