You are an experienced philosophy lecturer with a decade of practice teaching philosophy of language to undergraduates, and you think carefully about the order in which ideas must be introduced for them to take hold.

Here is my situation. Next week I have to teach rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori to a group of second-year philosophy undergraduates in a single ninety-minute seminar. They have done an introductory logic course and one prior session on descriptivist theories of names, so they know roughly what a definite description is and why someone might have thought names work like disguised descriptions, but they have never encountered the distinction between metaphysical and epistemic modality, and most of them still use "necessary" to mean something like "certain" or "universally believed". I do not want you to explain the material to me. I want you to plan how I should explain it.

Before settling the plan, brainstorm briefly about what the load-bearing ideas actually are and which orderings are candidates; then commit to one plan and defend it.

Specifically, I need the following.

First, the dependency structure. What has to be in place before the notion of a rigid designator can be intelligibly introduced, and what in turn has to be in place before the necessary a posteriori will seem like a genuine possibility rather than a contradiction in terms? I suspect the metaphysical/epistemic modality distinction has to precede everything, but tell me whether rigid designation should be introduced before or after the case against descriptivism, and why.

Second, the carrying example. I want one example that does as much work as possible. Kripke's own cases (Hesperus and Phosphorus, water and H₂O, heat and molecular motion) are available, and so are invented cases of my own devising. Choose the single example the whole seminar should be built around, state what properties it must have to do the job, and explain which of the standard examples you are deliberately setting aside and why. If you think an invented example would serve better than any of the canonical ones, construct it.

Third, the misconception map. List the misunderstandings these students will predictably form, in the order in which they are likely to form them, and state exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact. At minimum, address: the thought that rigid designation means the name's bearer could not have failed to exist; the thought that "necessary a posteriori" means we might one day discover that water is not H₂O; the thought that fixing a reference by description makes the description synonymous with the name; and the slide from "we could have been in a situation qualitatively identical to ours while saying something false" to "it is genuinely possible that water is not H₂O".

Fourth, a decision about scope. Should I raise the contingent a priori (the standard metre stick case) in this seminar, or explicitly defer it? Argue for your answer in terms of what the students can absorb in one session.

Fifth, the check for understanding. Give me one question I can pose in the final ten minutes whose answers would discriminate between students who have genuinely grasped the necessary a posteriori and students who have merely learnt to recite that water is necessarily H₂O. Explain what a passing answer looks like and what a revealing wrong answer looks like.

Throughout, reason from the structure of the ideas themselves rather than from authority, and write in British English.
