I'm setting the final for my third-year course on rational choice and game theory, and I need one essay question on the centipede game and the backward induction paradox. Here's my problem: every year I get answers that recite the textbook line -- at the last node you'd take, so at the second-to-last node the other player takes, so it unravels to taking at the very first node -- and then add a paragraph saying this seems "paradoxical" because long centipedes offer big payoffs. That's revision-guide stuff and I want a question it can't answer.

What I actually want candidates to grapple with is the epistemic structure underneath: backward induction assumes common knowledge of rationality, but the moment a player finds themselves at a node that backward induction says should never be reached, they have evidence that the assumption has failed -- so what should they believe and do then? The question should force them to confront why passing can be rational for a player who wants to signal they're the passing sort, and whether that's coherent or whether it just eats the premise that made the argument go. Bonus territory if the question naturally pulls them towards the experimental finding that real people do pass, and whether that shows irrationality or just that the payoffs on the page aren't the payoffs in people's heads.

So: write the exam question itself (answerable in 75 minutes, worth 40 marks), then a model answer of around 700 words, then a marking scheme. The scheme should be built around five must-hit points with marks for each, a one-line note on how candidates typically bodge each one, and a clear statement of what separates a first-class answer from a 2:1 -- I'd like that distinction to turn on whether the candidate notices that the argument's own conclusion destabilises its premises at off-path nodes, not on whether they name-drop the literature.
