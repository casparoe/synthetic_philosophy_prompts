I'm a second-year undergraduate taking a course on the philosophy of social measurement, and I also sit on my university's Student Activities Funding Board. The board distributes roughly $150,000 a year among about forty student clubs, in two allocation rounds, plus occasional emergency requests. We are volunteers with day jobs as students, so each of us has maybe six hours to spend per round; a faculty advisor has to sign off on our final allocations, and clubs can appeal our decisions to the student government, so everything we do has to be defensible in writing.

Last year we tried to make funding objective by ranking clubs on measurable criteria: event attendance, membership growth, and events held per semester. It went badly. One club counted everyone who came for free pizza as a member; another split its programming into lots of tiny "events" to pad its count; a third was clearly inflating sign-in sheets, but we couldn't prove it. Meanwhile, a genuinely excellent small club with twelve devoted members scored poorly on everything and nearly lost its funding. We've now seen first-hand the thing my course keeps circling: once a measure becomes a target, people optimize for the measure rather than the thing it was supposed to track, and some of that optimization is outright gaming while some is technically honest adaptation that still corrupts the signal.

I want to turn that body of theory into an actual written procedure the board can run, because right now we're about to design next year's criteria from scratch and we'll keep having this problem every cycle. Specifically, I'd like:

1. A pre-adoption audit we can run on any proposed metric before we put it in the rubric: the questions to ask, in order, and what counts as a passing answer at each step. Include at least the following: how the metric could be gamed by a club acting in bad faith; how it could be corrupted by a club acting in good faith (e.g., optimizing for the proxy in a way that's allowed but drifts from what we actually value); what the metric would measure if everyone behaved perfectly; and how much it costs a club to comply honestly versus to fake compliance.

2. A short list of hard disqualifiers — red flags that should knock a metric out immediately regardless of its other virtues — and a separate list of fixable weaknesses, with the fixes (e.g., verification steps, delayed or random audits, capping the weight any single metric gets, rotating criteria between years, separating the measurement from the reward so clubs don't know exactly what's being counted when).

3. A rule for when the board should abandon measurement altogether and use holistic judgment on a given dimension, given that judgment is inconsistent and can hide favoritism — which is exactly what our faculty advisor wants the objective criteria to prevent.

4. Honest markings: where in the procedure the answer is a judgment call dressed up as a checklist item; which theoretical distinctions must survive the translation into procedure (I'd nominate the gaming-versus-honest-adaptation distinction, but tell me if I'm wrong); which distinctions can safely be dropped for a board of undergraduates with no statistics training; and what this procedure structurally cannot do — for instance, if the goal we're measuring toward is itself the wrong goal.

Then run the audit on three candidate criteria for next year: (a) event attendance verified by card-swipe at the door, (b) an annual member-satisfaction survey, and (c) events held per semester, which several board members want to bring back. At least one of these should be a criterion the procedure rejects even though I'll find that verdict unwelcome — my money is on the survey, since it seems like the most democratic option and I've been advocating for it — and in that case tell me what to put in its place that serves the same underlying purpose.

Everything adopted has to be explainable to club leaders on a single page, since a procedure we can't explain will lose every appeal no matter how sound it is.
