I'm an independent researcher applying for a small six-month grant from an EA-adjacent funder, and I need a two-page research proposal. The project sits at the intersection of the ethics of randomized controlled trials and formal decision theory — two literatures that, as far as I can tell from years of reading, mostly talk past each other.

Here's my half-formed idea. Clinical equipoise is standardly glossed as genuine uncertainty within the expert community about which arm of a trial is superior, and it's treated as the moral license for randomizing patients. But there's a tension I can't find anyone resolving cleanly: any well-run trial accumulates evidence as it goes, which means equipoise degrades over the course of the very activity it licenses — yet we regard stopping at the first hint of a difference as irresponsible and continuing to a pre-registered sample size as the ethical gold standard. Meanwhile, in decision theory, the problem of allocating participants across treatments of unknown effectiveness is recognizably a bandit problem, and bandit problems have well-understood solution concepts — response-adaptive allocation, regret minimization, explore-exploit tradeoffs — that look nothing like fixed-ratio randomization to a significance threshold. My suspicion is that "equipoise" is covertly doing two jobs at once: justifying exploration (why we may randomize at all) and functioning as a stopping rule (why we must keep going). Making those two jobs explicit, and translating the ethics of randomization into decision-theoretic terms, should either dissolve the standard puzzles — when may you start a trial, when must you stop, what do you owe the next patient enrolled — or show that equipoise is an unstable compromise between deontological scruples and consequentialist trial design.

Turn this into a proposal with the following components:

1. A precise statement of the question. It should be the kind of question that has a determinate answer, not "explore the relationship between equipoise and adaptive design."
2. Why the existing positions leave the question open — the two standard moves being, on one side, the "uncertainty principle" defense of fixed randomization, and on the other, adaptive-trial enthusiasm that treats the ethics as solved by efficiency gains.
3. The method: what I would actually do, month by month, for six months of desk research. This is a conceptual/theoretical project, not an empirical one, so the method needs to be something more specific than "read the literature."
4. What a result would look like — for instance, a decision-theoretic reformulation of the ethics of enrollment, or a demonstration that certain standard equipoise puzzle cases have no analogue in bandit framings and why — and what would count as the project failing.
5. An honest scope assessment: which parts are too big for six months and get cut first.
