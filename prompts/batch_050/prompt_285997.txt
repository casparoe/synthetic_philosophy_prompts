My sister had a baby last year through IVF, and they've still got three embryos frozen from the same cycle which they might use in a few years. Over Sunday lunch this turned into a surprisingly heated argument: my brother-in-law reckons that if they'd implanted one of the other embryos instead, they'd still have ended up with essentially "their daughter", just looking a bit different, whereas my sister insists that would have been a wholly different child and their actual daughter would simply never have existed. I'm doing a second-year metaphysics module at the moment and realised, embarassingly, that I couldn't settle it.

So: write me an essay on whether you could have originated from a different sperm and egg than the ones you actually came from. I know the broad Kripke-style idea that your origins are essential to you, but I don't want the essay to just state it and move on — I want it to properly grapple with the awkward cases. For instance: identical twins come from the very same sperm and egg, so those gametes plainly didn't have to produce *you* in particular; does that sink the whole view, or does it just show origins are necessary but not sufficient, and is that distinction even coherent? Second, it seems like an absurdly chancy business which sperm fertilised the egg — a different day, a different bicycle ride, and a different sperm wins — and the view seems to imply that the resulting child wouldn't have been you but a near-duplicate stranger. Is that a bullet worth biting? Third, embryos can now be frozen for over a decade: if the embryo you developed from had been implanted eleven years later, would that child have been you born late, or not you at all, and what does the answer suggest about which parts of your "origin" (the gametes? the zygote? the pregnancy?) are doing the essential work? Finally, with mitochondrial donation you can now get children with genetic material from three people, so "the" origin starts looking less like a tidy package — does the view even give a determinate verdict there?

Please weigh the main positions fairly, but actually come down somewhere — no "on the one hand, on the other hand" shrug at the end. Defend a verdict on whether one of the frozen embryos could possibly turn out to be the same person as my niece, and say what follows more generally for how we should think about the essentiality of origins.
