Ok so I've been chewing on this question for weeks and it's driving me a little nuts: when you look at a photograph of something, are you actually *seeing* that thing, or are you just seeing the photo? My aunt died last year and there's a picture of her on my fridge, and I caught myself thinking "at least I still get to see her every morning," which felt true and also obviously not true at the same time. So I started collecting cases and writing down my gut verdicts, and now I want to know if there's some principle behind them or if my brain is just a mess.

My verdicts:

- Photo of my aunt = I see my aunt (in some past-tense way). A super realistic oil painting of her, even a perfect one = I don't see her, just the painting. Feels like the difference is that the camera didn't have opinions.
- Mirror while shaving: yes I see my face, obviously.
- Live security camera feed of the parking lot at work: yes, I'm seeing the lot, that's the whole point of the camera.
- Same screen but the hard drive died and it's secretly replaying footage from last Tuesday: no, I'm not seeing the lot anymore, I'm being fooled. Even though the pixels could be identical to a live feed.
- A "photo of Bigfoot" that's actually a guy in a suit: I see a guy in a suit, not Bigfoot, no matter what the caption says. The photo shows what was in front of the lens.
- But here's one I can't defend: a photo of someone's reflection in a mirror — I'd say you see the person, not just their reflection, so camera-of-mirror is fine. Yet when my friend held his phone up to his laptop and photographed a photograph, I felt like he was NOT seeing the original scene, just re-seeing a picture. Two hops is fine if one is a mirror, not fine if both are photos?? I can't justify that.
- Heavy Instagram filter, or photoshopping a random tourist out of the background: I still see the beach and my sister, I just see them slightly wrong in spots. But an AI-generated image of my sister at a beach she never visited: I don't see anything real there at all, even if it looks exactly like a genuine photo would.
- Night-vision trail cam photo of a deer: I see the deer. Thermal drone image, same. X-ray of my hand: I think I... see my bones? Doctors look at scans and see tumors, that seems like seeing.
- Ultrasound of a baby: parents always say "we saw the baby today!" and I want to count it, but there's no light involved, it's sound getting bounced and converted, and if I count that I'm scared I have to say a seismograph printout is "seeing" an earthquake, which I definately don't want to say.

So is there one principle under all this? My best guess is something like: the image has to be causally hooked up to the scene in the right way, without a human's interpretation getting in the middle. That covers a lot, but I'm not sure it handles the picture-of-a-picture thing, or why the dead-hard-drive feed stops counting when the pixels are the same. Tell me what principle you'd give me, which of my verdicts I'd have to drop to keep it simple, and what it predicts for cases I didn't list — like a robot arm photorealistically copying a photo onto paper by hand, or those colorized old photos where the software invents the colors.
