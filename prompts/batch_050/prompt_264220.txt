I've been trying to pin down my own views on when several things compose a further thing — when there is, strictly speaking, one more object in the world than the parts we started with. Here are my verdicts on cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, lies behind them. A cat is a genuine object. A hammer is one too: the head and handle, once fastened together, compose something. A heap of sand poured from a bucket is not an object, just grains. Yet a sandcastle built from those same grains somehow is, and I can't fully justify the difference. Two strangers shaking hands compose nothing. A crew team rowing in unison does compose something — a team — though I admit it feels like a different kind of thing from the hammer. My left shoe and the top half of the Eiffel Tower compose nothing. A water molecule is an object; three billiard balls resting against each other in a triangle are not. A wooden ship whose planks are gradually replaced remains one object throughout, but those same original planks reassembled years later in a museum are, I think, a new object rather than the old ship restored. And a body that loses a finger is still the same body, which suggests the boundaries of composition can be fuzzy — yet I resist both the conclusion that every plurality composes something and the idea of a sharp cutoff. Is there one principle, or a small family of them, that captures most of this? State it precisely, say which verdict motivates each clause, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate; if no clean principle fits, tell me which verdict you would surrender first and why. Then apply your best candidate to two cases I haven't listed: a colony of ants, and a cloud. Finally, indicate how confident you are in the principle you settle on, and single out the specific point where you are least sure it tracks the truth rather than merely systematizing my prejudices.
