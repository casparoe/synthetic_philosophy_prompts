I am a PhD student in philosophy of psychiatry working on Ian Hacking's idea that human kinds are 'looping kinds': unlike natural kinds, classifications of people can change the people classified, because those people become aware of the classification and conform to it, resist it, or elaborate it -- which in turn forces the classifiers to revise the classification, and so on. In Mad Travelers Hacking adds that a 'transient mental illness' can flourish only in an ecological niche with certain conditions, which I gloss as: a medical taxonomy ready to receive the condition, a cultural tension it expresses, observability (the illness must be visible and legible as such), and some release it grants the sufferer from an intolerable situation.

Please analyze the 2020–2022 surge in sudden-onset, functional tic-like behaviors among adolescents -- especially girls -- during and after the COVID lockdowns as a case study for this framework. The salient facts as I understand them: movement-disorder clinics in several countries reported sharp increases in teenagers presenting with abrupt, severe tic-like symptoms rather different from typical Tourette's onset; clinicians noted that many patients had consumed large amounts of social-media content (especially TikTok) by creators displaying tics; and the wave subsided as lockdowns ended and clinicians changed how they talked about it.

Here is where I want you to go beyond the standard application. I am developing a distinction between first-person looping (the diagnosed person changes under their own diagnosis, Hacking's classic case) and what I want to call second-hand looping: people adopt or elaborate a condition's symptom repertoire from exposure to already-classified people, before or without ever receiving the diagnosis themselves. So: (1) Map the episode carefully onto the framework -- what exactly is the classification doing the work, who is looping, and where is the feedback mechanism located if the 'classifier' is arguably an algorithm rather than a clinician? (2) Test each of the four niche conditions against the episode and say which hold, which strain, and whether the framework needs a fifth condition for a networked era. (3) Say where the theory breaks or goes silent -- for instance, does it explain why the wave receded, or why most viewers of the same content did not develop symptoms? (4) Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: does this episode confirm looping effects, show they were always implicitly about institutions and so need revision, or reveal a case (widespread behavioral mimicry without formal diagnosis) that the theory never anticipated? Please be disciplined about distinguishing what the evidence cited actually supports from what a looping theorist would merely like to say.
