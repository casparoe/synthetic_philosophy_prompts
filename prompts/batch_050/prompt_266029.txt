I've recently been put in charge of running supplier negotiations at my company (mid-sized, we buy a lot of specialist kit and services), and I've just watched two deals in a row blow up in the same weird way. Both sides rushed to lock themselves in early -- one supplier announced publicly to our exec team that they'd already reserved production capacity for us, and in the other case our own procurement lead sent a "final terms, non-negotiable" email in week one. Each time, the commitment came before anyone had actually worked out what they wanted, and it felt like whoever committed first was trying to box the other side in. It worked, sort of, but both deals ended up worse than what was on the table at the start.

I've since learned there's a name for this general pattern: a commitment race, where parties rush to make hard-to-reverse moves (public promises, sunk costs, deadlines, exclusivity signings) because being first to credibly lock in shapes everyone else's options, even though everyone might do better if everyone stayed flexible a bit longer -- and when both sides race, you can get a chicken-game collision where two incompatible commitments crash into each other.

I want to stop this happening on my watch, so I need a reusable procedure I can run for every negotiation I oversee (roughly one or two a month, stakes from £50k to low millions, I have maybe an hour of prep per deal and my director signs off on anything structural).

So: act as a game theorist who's spent years advising on real commercial negotiations -- think in terms of commitment devices and credible threats, but write like a practitioner, not an academic. Think carefully about the failure modes first before settling on the procedure; I'd rather you sketch the ways this goes wrong and then design around them than hand me something naive.

Please structure the answer in British English with these sections, in this order:

**Diagnosis** (~300 words): how to tell a genuine commitment race from ordinary hard bargaining or someone just being decisive. Give me 3-5 tests I can run in the first meeting or two, and say what I'd observe if it's NOT a race.

**The intervention ladder** (~400 words): the actual procedure -- a sequence of interventions from lightest to heaviest (e.g. cooling-off norms, staged/reversible commitments, symmetry rules where both sides de-commit together, escalation to my director). For each rung, say when to climb to the next one.

**Hard cases** (~250 words): at minimum cover (a) one side genuinely can't reverse because of their own internal politics or regulation, (b) the person racing is my own boss or on my own side, and (c) slowing things down itself reads as weakness and costs us the deal.

**Worked examples** (~400 words): run the procedure on two or three realistic cases, including one where it gives an answer I won't like (e.g. it tells me to let the other side's commitment stand and negotiate around it, or to walk away from a deal my director wants). Don't flinch on that one -- show the reasoning.

**Where this breaks** (~150 words): the honest limits. When does this whole framework misfire, and what should I just do instead?

Please don't pad it with general negotiation advice -- I know how to negotiate, I specifically want the commitment-race-detection-and-defusing machinery. Cheers!
