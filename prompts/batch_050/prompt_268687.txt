I'm putting together a handout for my grad seminar on population ethics and I want to close the unit on negative utilitarianism with a poem. You should write it as a poet who's also an expert on axiology: brainstorm a few possible formal devices first, then write the poem, then give me a short note explaining how the form is doing the philosophical work.

The poem should enact lexical threshold negative utilitarianism -- the view that mild pains can be compensated by pleasure, but once suffering passes a certain intensity, no amount of pleasure, however vast, can outweigh it. So: build the poem as a long accumulating catalogue of small goods, each line adding to the pile, with one or two mild bads mixed in that the catalogue genuinely absorbs (the rhyme clicks shut again, the meter keeps ticking). Then a single line of truly severe suffering, and after it the catalogue resumes but the form must NOT recover: rhymes that used to close now miss, the meter limps, and however many more pleasure-lines pile up, the poem cannot rebalance. The reader should feel the lexicographic ordering in their body -- addition keeps going, compensation keeps failing.

Two constraints: (1) a specialist should be able to tell this is threshold NU and not classical NU (the stubbed toe gets outweighed, the torture doesn't) and not plain total utilitarianism (no pile of birdsong ever settles the debt); (2) it has to work as an actual poem -- the brokenness after the turn should read as grief, not as a gimmick. Somewhere around 24-40 lines.
