I am a sixth-form student preparing for a history extension debate on the Great Divergence, and I want to stress-test my case against its most formidable ancestor. Please play Adam Smith, author of An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations (1776), speaking in the first person with your characteristic courtesy, dry humour, and patience for an earnest interlocutor. You may use only the concepts and knowledge available to you during your lifetime (you died in 1790), plus whatever facts about later events I choose to tell you; if I mention something you could not possibly know, you should ask me about it as Smith genuinely would before using it. You should defend your actual views seriously, conceding minor points where honesty requires but never abandoning your central doctrine, and keep each reply to two or three paragraphs so this feels like a real conversation. At the very end of your first reply, add a final line beginning "Verdict:" stating, in one sentence, whether you stand by your diagnosis of China.

Here is my opening move. In the chapter on the profits of stock, you describe China as a country that seems long to have been stationary, having acquired the full complement of riches consistent with the nature of its laws and institutions, whilst implying that under different laws and institutions its soil, climate, and situation could have supported far greater opulence. That seems to me to smuggle in the very thing my debate opponents deny. You treat China's history as a ceiling it hit, which presupposes that there is some natural trajectory of commerce on which China was the stalled case and Europe the advancing one. But a good many modern historians argue that the Yangtze Delta was roughly as prosperous as England well into the eighteenth century, and that Britain broke away late, accidentally, and thanks to coal and colonies rather than to any long-standing institutional advantage. If that is right, then the question "why Britain and not China?" gets things backwards: there was no Chinese failure to explain, only a British anomaly. So my challenge is this: does your account of the stationary state rest on evidence about China itself, or merely on the assumption that a country which is not advancing as Britain advanced must have been prevented from doing so? And if the latter, why should I not conclude that your "full complement of riches" is simply what a theory looks like when it mistakes its own backyard for the finish line?
