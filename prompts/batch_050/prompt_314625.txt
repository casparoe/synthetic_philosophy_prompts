I draft policy for a professional licensing body, and I keep running into a pattern I have no word for. When a vague requirement has to be made operational — "substantial experience," "adequate command of English" — we fix a cutoff: five years, a particular test score. Everyone at the table knows the cutoff is partly arbitrary; six months or a few points either way would have served the purpose just as well. But then two things happen that don't sit well together. First, once the cutoff exists, candidates a hair below it get described in our minutes, and even in appeal decisions, not as falling short of our administrative line but simply as lacking substantial experience — as though the line had uncovered a fact about them rather than drawn one. Second, when a later committee revisits the cutoff, everyone feels free to move it, which suggests nobody ever believed in that fact. I've looked at the philosophical literature on vagueness and found roughly two families of views: one on which vague expressions secretly have exact boundaries that we merely cannot know, and one on which they can be made precise in many equally legitimate ways and a borderline case is settled only relative to a chosen sharpening. Neither seems to capture our practice: the first says the boundary was there all along, the second says all sharpenings stand or fall together, but we treat one particular sharpening as binding fact in the hearing room and as revisable convention in the drafting meeting. Does this pattern — an admittedly arbitrary sharpening hardening into apparent fact within one institutional context while remaining negotiable in another — have an established name? If so, how is it distinguished from plain stipulation, from the hidden-boundary view, and from an ordinary chosen sharpening? If not, propose a name and definition that mark its edges, including where it stops and mere hypocrisy or equivocation begins.
