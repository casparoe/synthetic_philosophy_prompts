Two colleagues in my department -- call them Ana and Bram -- co-supervise a graduate student whose thesis models how small firms set prices. The model assumes, among other things, that firm owners know the exact elasticity of demand they face and update it instantly when market conditions change. The student defends the model by showing that it predicts observed price responses to cost shocks better than two competing models. At the thesis meeting, Ana and Bram fell into a dispute that never got resolved, and I would like an outside adjudication of it, move by move.

(1) Ana opened by saying the elasticity assumption is simply false -- owners demonstrably do not know their demand elasticities, they learn slowly, and many rely on rules of thumb -- and that a model resting on a known falsehood about the very agents it describes cannot inform the thesis's policy chapter on price regulation.

(2) Bram replied that she was committing a classic methodological error: on the instrumentalist view associated with Milton Friedman's essay on positive economics, the realism of assumptions is irrelevant to a theory's worth; what matters is whether it predicts well, and this one does. He added that virtually all successful science idealizes -- frictionless planes, ideal gases, infinitely lived consumers -- and that no one demands realism from physics.

(3) Ana answered that the physics comparison backfires. Frictionless-plane idealizations are controllable: we know in which direction friction biases the result, we can add it back, and we can estimate the error introduced by omitting it. The elasticity assumption is not like that -- the model's headline prediction essentially follows from it, nobody knows how to relax it without rebuilding the model, and the fit could easily reverse once one does. She also argued that the policy chapter needs a mechanism, not a correlation: a recommendation to regulators about how firms will respond to a price ceiling is a claim about what happens under an intervention, and a model that gets the right answers for the wrong reasons in-sample may fail exactly there.

(4) Bram countered that a counterfactual prediction is still a prediction, so her distinction does not touch his position; the model should simply be tested on whatever quasi-experimental variation is available. He also accused her of ignoring the costs of realism: every parameter added to make the model psychologically plausible is one more degree of freedom that cannot be identified from their data, and a 'realistic' model with six unidentifiable parameters is worse than a false one with two identified ones.

(5) Ana's final move was to concede that realism is not free, but to insist there is an asymmetry he keeps missing: when the model eventually fails -- and she granted it has not failed yet -- the failure will not tell them whether the elasticity assumption or some other component is at fault, precisely because the assumption was never independently checked. A false assumption that is doing real work, she said, is not a simplification but an unmonitored liability.

(6) Bram closed by saying this proved his point: she could not name a single actual predictive failure, only hypothetical future ones, and methodological anxieties about models that keep predicting correctly are how disciplines talk themselves out of their most useful tools.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or right-but-incomplete, with a brief justification. Where a move rests on a distinction (prediction vs. intervention, controllable vs. uncontrollable idealization, simplicity vs. identifiability), state whether the distinction actually does the work the speaker needs it to do. Finally, identify what, if anything, both parties are missing -- for instance, whether there is a question about the model's purpose or about the relationship between assumptions and the interpretation of estimated parameters that neither raised.
