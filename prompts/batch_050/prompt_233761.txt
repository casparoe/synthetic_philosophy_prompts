I teach a first-year philosophy of mind seminar, 12 students, one 90-minute session, and I want to do Dennett-style qualia skepticism without lecturing. Design an activity -- I'm thinking something with blind tasting or color matching done twice with a delay -- where the students end up genuinely unable to say whether their experience changed between the two rounds or whether their memory/standards changed, i.e. they each become a Chase or a Sanborn without knowing those names yet. The point is they should feel in their own case that the two hypotheses (my qualia shifted vs. my memory or reaction shifted) are not just hard to distinguish but maybe not even two different things. Give me: the full procedure with materials and timing, what the facilitator should and shouldn't say beforehand, how to split them into Chase-camp and Sanborn-camp for the argument phase, and a debrief that names the thought experiment only at the end and maps what happened onto the claim that qualia might be an incoherent posit. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong (people peeking, people who just don't care about coffee, the clever student who tries to dissolve the problem by keeping written notes) and be honest about the one thing the activity can't reproduce -- presumably something about first-person authority that a taste test can't touch -- that I'll have to supply in discussion myself.
