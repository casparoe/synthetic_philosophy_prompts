Last night I had a long and unresolved argument with a friend about Daniel Dennett's "Quining Qualia," specifically the thought experiment of the two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn. Chase comes to dislike the taste of a coffee he once loved, though his perceptual discrimination is unchanged; Sanborn's discrimination has degraded, so the coffee now tastes different to him, though his standards are unchanged. Dennett uses the cases to argue that there is no fact of the matter about which changes are changes in qualia and which are changes in reaction or judgment, and hence that qualia, as standardly conceived, are an incoherent posit. My friend holds that the argument proves too much: if it worked, it would dissolve ordinary facts about perception generally, not just qualia. I suspect the argument is weaker than Dennett thinks for a different reason. My half-formed idea: the force of the thought experiment depends entirely on the claim that neither Chase nor Sanborn can tell, from the inside, which kind of change they have undergone — but this indistinguishability is established only by a memory-comparison test, and using memory comparisons as the criterion for sameness of taste already presupposes that there is nothing more to sameness of taste than passing such tests. If that's right, the thought experiment doesn't independently motivate qualia eliminativism; it quietly builds it in.

I want to turn this into a two-page research proposal for a one-semester graduate research project. The proposal should include: (1) a precise statement of the question — whether the Chase and Sanborn cases provide evidence against qualia that doesn't already assume a verificationist or dispositionalist account of experience; (2) why the existing positions leave this open, treating the standard realist reply (insisting on first-person authority) and the eliminativist position as both under-argued on this specific point rather than rehearsing the whole debate; (3) a method, broken into phases over the semester — reconstructing the argument in its strongest form, identifying exactly where the memory-comparison test enters, and checking whether my suspicion survives charitable reconstruction or collapses into a point Dennett already anticipated; (4) what a positive result would look like and what would count as the project failing, including the possibility that the charge of question-begging turns out to be answerable and I end up strengthening Dennett's argument instead; and (5) why this narrow question is a better use of a semester than a general assessment of qualia eliminativism.
