So I spent 40 years as a research chemist and only started reading moral/political philosophy in retirement, which means I keep forming opinions and then discovering there are whole literatures about them. Here's where I've landed on markets and what shouldn't be for sale, and I want to know if the package hangs together or if I've just collected five things that each sounded good in the book I read them in.

(1) A regulated market in kidneys from living donors should be legal. Competent adults may do what they like with their own bodies, the waiting lists kill people, and the "but the poor will sell" worry is a reason to regulate the market, not ban it.

(2) Anti-price-gouging laws should be scrapped. The guy who drives a truck of bottled water into a hurricane zone and charges triple is doing more good than the people wringing their hands about him, and the high price is exactly what gets the next truck on the road. This is just supply and demand doing its job and I'm unapologetic about it.

(3) Buying and selling votes should stay criminal, full stop, even between consenting adults, even at fair prices, even if it would make some elections more "efficient" by letting people who care more pay people who care less.

(4) My reason for (3) is that some goods are spoiled by being sold -- their whole value depends on them NOT being allocated by money, and once you put a price on them you've destroyed the thing you were trying to distribute. It's not about inequality or exploitation; if it were, we could fix it with a subsidy. It's that the sale itself corrupts the good.

(5) Generally speaking I think policy should be judged on outcomes, and visceral reactions of the "that's just wrong" variety -- what people call the yuck factor -- carry no evidential weight with me. Feelings aren't data.

Now, I suspect there's friction in here somewhere but I genuinely can't localise it. (1) and (3) both involve consensual transactions, so maybe that's a problem? And I have a vague sense (4) might cause trouble elsewhere, since a colleague pointed out that people say similar things about emergency supplies as I say about votes, but that seemed overwrought to me.

Please do an actual audit: go through the pairs, tell me which tensions are real and which are merely apparent, rank the real ones by severity, and for each say what would have to give. Don't just lay out the options and leave me to choose -- commit. Tell me which single commitment you'd revise first if it were your belief set, and what it would cost me. Also, one thing I specifically don't want is to be told all five are in equal tension with everything; if some pairs are fine, say they're fine and why.

Last line of your response should be exactly: \answer{...} with the single commitment (by number) you'd drop or weaken first inside the braces.
