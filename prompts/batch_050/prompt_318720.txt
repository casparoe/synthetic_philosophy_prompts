Two friends of mine have been arguing for weeks about the scope of the Euthyphro dilemma, and I would like a verdict on their exchange, point by point.

The first, Mara, holds that the dilemma is a narrowly anti-theistic argument. It refutes divine command theory by asking whether God commands actions because they are right, or whether actions are right because God commands them. If the former, morality is independent of God and the theory is false; if the latter, morality is arbitrary, since God could have made cruelty obligatory. Either way, she concludes, the theory collapses — but the argument's work ends there. It tells us nothing about secular ethics.

The second, Jon, replies that the dilemma is not an argument against theism at all but a general template that applies to any attempt to ground moral facts in something's attitudes or responses. Consider the secular view that an action is right if and only if it would be approved by an ideal observer — someone fully informed, impartial, and free of distorting bias. The same two horns appear: does the ideal observer approve of actions because they are right, or are they right because she approves of them? On the first option, the observer's approval is idle in the metaphysics, merely tracking independent moral facts; on the second, rightness again looks arbitrary, hostage to whatever psychology we build into the ideal conditions.

Mara concedes the parallel but insists on a crucial asymmetry. The arbitrariness horn is fatal to divine command theory specifically because of what God is supposed to be: a being whose will is sovereign over everything, value included. A secular theory never claims that its ground is sovereign in that sense, so it can take the first horn with good grace: yes, ideal observers approve of actions because they are right, and their approval functions as an epistemic instrument, a perfect detector of moral facts — which is all a modest naturalist ever wanted.

Jon answers that this retreat costs the secular theory its point. An approval condition that merely tracks independent moral facts is moral epistemology, not moral metaphysics; the theory was supposed to say what rightness consists in, and consisting in whatever the reliable detectors of rightness detect is no answer. He adds that the theist has an exactly parallel escape: identify the good not with God's commands but with God's necessarily loving nature, so that commands flow from a nature that could not have been otherwise and arbitrariness never arises. If that move saves divine command theory, then some analogous move — treating the ideal-observer conditions as constitutive of value rather than as detectors of it — should be equally available to the secularist, and Mara cannot have it both ways.

Mara's final move is to reject the dilemma's presupposition that the grounding relation must be either determination or detection. Between the two, she says, lies constitution: perhaps value simply is, or is constituted by, the responses of certain agents under certain conditions, much as the value of money is constituted by collective acceptance without thereby being either arbitrary or independent of human practice. Jon counters that the money analogy proves too much: money is precisely the sort of thing whose value is conventional and could have been otherwise, whereas morality is supposed to hold of necessity — so a constituted-but-contingent morality is just the arbitrariness horn under a new name.

Please adjudicate. For each round of the exchange, state who has the better of the specific point at issue and why. Then say which side, overall, has the stronger position on the question they began with — whether the Euthyphro dilemma is exclusively a problem for theists — and identify any distinction or option that both parties have overlooked.
