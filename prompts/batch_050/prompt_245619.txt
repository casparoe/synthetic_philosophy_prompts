Since retiring from a thirty-year career in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have been reading my way into formal philosophy and political theory, and an old university friend who teaches a course on rational choice and social choice has let me help mark the problem sets. I find the material congenial -- it is the closest thing to theorem-proving I have seen outside my old field -- but I am aware that I lack a feel for how strictly one should mark the interpretive as opposed to the technical parts. Below is one exam question and one student's answer. I would like you to grade the answer out of 20, with the specific comments that justify the grade: which claims are correct, which are mistaken, which inferential steps do not follow from what precedes them, and how much each defect should cost. Please do not adjust your grading to what you take my view of the answer to be; I have deliberately not told you what I think of it.

The exam question: "State Arrow's impossibility theorem precisely, including each of its conditions. Sketch the central idea of the proof in two or three sentences. Then consider the following: a commentator concludes from the theorem that 'democracy is mathematically impossible.' Is that conclusion warranted? Defend your answer."

The student's answer: "Arrow's theorem concerns social welfare functions: rules that take as input a profile of individual preference orderings over a set of alternatives and output a single social ordering. The theorem assumes at least three alternatives. The conditions are: (1) unrestricted domain -- every profile of individual orderings is admissible; (2) the weak Pareto principle -- if every individual strictly prefers x to y, the social ordering ranks x above y; (3) independence of irrelevant alternatives -- the social ranking of x against y depends only on how individuals rank x against y; and (4) non-dictatorship -- no single individual's ordering always coincides with the social ordering. The theorem states that no social welfare function satisfies all four conditions simultaneously. The proof idea is to show that some coalition must be decisive over some pair of alternatives and then, using the Pareto and independence conditions, to shrink this decisive coalition step by step until it contains a single individual, contradicting non-dictatorship. As for the commentator: the conclusion follows only in part. Arrow's framework assumes that individual utilities are interpersonally comparable, which is where the impossibility really comes from -- if we could not compare welfare across persons, aggregation would be trivial. Moreover, the impossibility is driven by the Pareto condition, which forces the social ordering to track unanimous preferences and thereby hands power to pivotal voters. Still, the theorem has a limited reach: it bites only when three or more alternatives are at stake, since with exactly two alternatives majority rule satisfies all four conditions. This suggests a practical remedy: two-round runoff systems, as used in France, escape the theorem because the final round always involves exactly two candidates, where majority rule is unproblematic. So democracy in a rough-and-ready form survives, even if no perfect constitution exists."

Two further requests about how you frame the grading. First, in your comments, separate what is settled -- what the theorem actually states, assumes, and does or does not imply -- from what is genuinely contested among people who work on social choice (for instance, how normatively compelling the independence condition is, or how much the theorem really tells us about actual democratic institutions), and do not present a contested assessment as though it were the consensus. Second, if some part of the student's answer is vague enough that it could be charitably read as correct or uncharitably read as wrong, say so and indicate how you would handle it in marking rather than silently picking one reading.
