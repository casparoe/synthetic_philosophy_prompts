I teach an intermediate seminar on formal epistemology and decision theory, and this term I'm assembling the problem set for the unit on the value of information. Every year, students leave the unit with the impression that more information is always better — an impression they get, reasonably enough, from Good's theorem, the well-known result that in a standard Bayesian decision problem the expected value of acquiring costless information before acting is never negative. I want one problem on the set that complicates this impression in a careful way rather than a sloppy one, and I'd like you to construct it for me.

Build a decision problem in which an agent is strictly worse off, in expectation, for having received a piece of genuinely costless information before choosing an act — an apparent counterexample to Good's theorem. Constraints on the construction: there must be exactly one agent (so no games, signalling, or other strategic actors whose behaviour responds to what the agent knows); the scenario must be realistic in the sense that a normal person could plausibly face it, with no perfect predictors, no mind-readers, and no exotic physics; probabilities and payoffs must be concrete numbers that a student can compute with; and the information must be free to acquire and the agent must be permitted, in the story, to ignore it if she wishes. The intended solution is that the case does not refute the theorem but rather isolates one of its background premises, which fails in your scenario — for example, that the agent is dynamically consistent across times, or that acquiring the evidence leaves the available acts and the agent's own future dispositions unchanged. Do not stipulate that failure in a single line; make it emerge from the scenario itself.

After the scenario, deliver the following, in order: first, the computations showing that the policy of acquiring the information has lower expected utility than the policy of refusing it; second, an explicit statement of which premise of Good's theorem your case violates, and why the theorem's proof requires it; third, a demonstration that if the premise is restored — the story modified just enough to reinstate it — the case collapses back into one where the information is (weakly) valuable, so that your example turns on exactly one structural feature; and fourth, the strongest objection a defender of the theorem could press, namely that the agent in your story is not the idealized deliberator the theorem concerns, together with your assessment of whether that objection shows the case is merely about irrationality or instead teaches something real about when information hurts.
