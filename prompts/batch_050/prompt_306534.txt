I'm a third-year philosophy undergraduate currently taking a seminar on personal identity, and for our final essay we're allowed to write on a film, provided the reading does real philosophical work rather than using the movie as decoration. I've chosen Christopher Nolan's The Prestige, because it seems to me to be quietly obsessed with the Ship of Theseus problem even though it never names it. Robert Angier's cloning machine manufactures a perfect copy of him at a distance each night, and every performance ends with one Angier dropping through a trapdoor into a water tank; the man who steps forward to take the bow has all of Angier's memories, his body down to the last detail, and his full conviction that he is the same man who walked on stage. My seminar covered the classic puzzle of the ship whose planks are replaced one by one, as well as modern variants involving gradual replacement, teleportation, and fission, and I want to ask whether the film's nightly duplication is better understood as Theseus-style gradual replacement compressed into an instant, or as something categorically different, since in the film nothing is repaired or replaced piecemeal -- a fresh whole is fabricated while the old one persists, at least briefly, alongside it.

Please give me a close philosophical reading of the film along these lines. In particular: does the case for the copy's being Angier rest on psychological continuity, and if so, why should the origin of that continuity (growth versus manufacture) matter, given that Theseus-style intuitions usually tolerate total replacement so long as the process is gradual and the resulting object is spatiotemporally continuous with its predecessor? Consider whether the film itself supplies an answer through Angier's own terror and his question about which consciousness he will wake up in, and whether that question is even coherent on a no-further-fact view of personal identity. I'd also like you to bring in the Borden and Fallon thread as a counterpoint: here identity seems to be maintained not by bodily or psychological continuity but by two people alternately occupying a single social role, which inverts the usual structure of the puzzle -- one continuous public person, two underlying individuals. Where does the film resist the Theseus reading altogether? I'm suspicious of my own instinct that the tank drowning settles the matter, since from the inside each copy's experience is seamless, and I'd like the essay to take seriously the possibility that the film's point is precisely that there is no fact of the matter the characters are wrong to care about.

A few constraints from my professor: I can't assume the reader has the film in front of them, so please anchor the analysis in the major plot events and the machine's mechanics as I've described them, rather than in specific lines of dialogue or scene-by-scene citation; and I can't use a bibliography, so please don't direct me to particular papers by name -- work the positions out from the arguments themselves. Finally, I'd like the reading to end by answering the question my seminar keeps circling: what, if anything, does dramatizing the duplication case add to the debate that the standard teleportation thought experiments don't already provide? My current hunch is that the film contributes the element of repetition and dread -- Angier chooses the procedure a hundred times over, knowing what it entails, which no one-shot thought experiment captures -- but I'm open to being argued out of that.
