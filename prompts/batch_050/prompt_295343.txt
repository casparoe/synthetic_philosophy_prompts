You are an epistemologist who works on knowledge norms and higher-order knowledge, and I'd like you to adjudicate a disagreement I witnessed between two colleagues after a reading group on Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument. Please think carefully, take the role seriously, and before giving verdicts, briefly brainstorm the strongest version of each side's position so I'm confident you're not adjudicating a caricature.

The background they agreed on: a condition is "luminous" if, whenever it obtains, one is in a position to know that it obtains. Feeling cold is the stock candidate example. The KK principle is the stronger general claim that knowing implies being in a position to know that one knows. They both know Williamson's argument: if a condition can fade gradually (a light dimming, a pain subsiding, a person slowly becoming cold), then knowledge of it requires a margin for error — in cases too close to the boundary, even a true belief that the condition obtains would not be safe, hence not knowledge — and luminosity is inconsistent with any such margin. That's where agreement ended.

Round one. Mara said the argument shows that nothing interesting is luminous, and that we should simply accept this: the intuition that you always know your own current feelings is just a prejudice produced by how rarely we're at a boundary. Dev said the argument has a sorites structure, and like all sorites arguments it must break somewhere; his diagnosis was that the margin-for-error premise is plausible for any single step but that chaining it across a long series of imperceptible transitions sneaks in exactly the kind of tolerance principle the sorites teaches us to reject. Mara replied that this misreads the argument: the premises aren't "for every step, knowledge survives the step" treated as one tolerance principle — each step is independently motivated by the safety condition on knowledge, and you can't reject a safety condition case by case without abandoning safety altogether.

Round two. Dev shifted ground: fine, he said, suppose luminosity fails for feelings of coldness, which involve discriminative capacities. That doesn't touch the cases that matter for the KK intuition, like knowing a trivial logical truth one has just proved, where there is no fading series and no boundary, so no margin is needed. Mara answered that this saves a triviality: the interesting luminous conditions were always the phenomenal ones — pain, feeling cold, seeming to see red — and those all admit of gradual fading, so the anti-luminosity argument covers everything anyone ever wanted luminosity for. Dev said that concedes his real point, which was that the argument is local, not global: it refutes luminosity only where discrimination is involved, and the philosophical folk wisdom that "Williamson showed nothing is luminous" overgeneralizes.

Round three. Mara then pressed harder on the safety premise: knowledge requires that one not easily have been wrong in similar cases, and near a boundary one easily is wrong — that's not a sorites move, it's just what knowledge is. Dev responded that safety conditions generate the same higher-order vagueness problems they were meant to avoid: if knowing requires a margin, then knowing that one knows requires a further margin, and so on, and we end up unable to say where knowledge ends at all — which he took to show the safety condition itself is the problem, not luminosity. Mara said that's a cost of any adequate theory of knowledge and not a refutation of hers; Dev said a theory of knowledge whose own boundary is unknowable had better have a good story about that, and safety doesn't.

Neither would move. Here is what I'd like from you:

1. A point-by-point verdict on the exchange: for each round, say who is right about the specific point at issue — whether the anti-luminosity argument is really soritical in structure, whether the margin-for-error premise can be resisted stepwise without abandoning safety, whether the argument generalizes beyond discriminative cases or is essentially local, whether restricting KK to proof-like cases is a trivializing retreat or a principled limitation, and whether higher-order vagueness is a genuine cost for the safety theorist or just the human condition.
2. An overall verdict, if one is available, or an explanation of why the dispute is actually several disputes that got tangled together.
3. What both parties are missing, if anything.
4. Finally, state explicitly what would change your verdicts: which argument, distinction, or consideration — if supplied or refuted — would lead you to rule the other way on the points where you were most confident, and where you take your own reasoning to be most likely to fail.
