I retired from physics (condensed matter) about six years ago and have spent a fair chunk of that time falling down the philosophy rabbit hole, mostly via podcasts and whatever my local library happens to have. Lately I've been chewing on the so-called gender-equality paradox: the finding that countries scoring highest on gender equality, places like Norway and Finland, seem to have *more* gender-skewed career choices, with relatively fewer women going into engineering and physics than in much less egalitarian countries. As an ex-physicist this bugs me, and I keep turning it over.

I've written down the main morals I've seen people draw from this finding (including some of my own half-baked ones). What I'd like is for you to sort them into tiers by how much philosophical weight you think each can actually bear, and give a sentence or three per item on why it lands where it does. Tier 1 = solid, Tier 3 = flimsy or outright fallacious, something like that. Here they are:

1. The differences must be largely innate, because when you remove external barriers and let people choose freely, the gender gap in interests shows up *bigger*, not smaller. Freedom is like a controlled experiment that isolates nature.
2. The finding can't tell us anything about individuals at all, because it's a correlation between country-level statistics. Inferring individual-level causes from group-level correlations is the ecological fallacy, and this is a textbook case.
3. In rich, egalitarian societies gender becomes a vehicle of self-expression rather than a constraint -- people signal who they are through gendered choices precisely because they feel free to. So culture is doing the work, just a different, subtler kind of culture than straightforward discrimination.
4. Since equalizing opportunities hasn't equalized outcomes, egalitarian policy has overshot and should be scaled back, at least the parts aimed at outcome ratios in fields like engineering.
5. The finding actually *vindicates* the egalitarian countries on the justice front: justice is about fair procedures and opportunities, not outcome ratios, and Scandinavia shows you can get the procedures right while outcomes stay lopsided.
6. The whole thing rests on wobbly measurement -- which equality index you use, whether you count the share of women among STEM graduates or the share of women who choose STEM, etc. -- so nobody should draw philosophical conclusions until the stats people stop arguing.

Two extra requests if you can manage them. First, tell me which two items on the list interact in the most interesting way -- e.g. where accepting one forces you to rethink another. Second, since I know this area is contested: please say how confident you are in your overall tiering, and single out the single point in your answer you're *least* sure of, with a line on why. I'd rather have a hesitant answer that knows where its weak spots are than a confident one that doesnt.
