I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm writing a long feature on paradoxes of rational choice. My editors have seen too many pieces that present the two-envelope paradox, gesture at one resolution, and stop. I want to do better: before I interview anyone, I want a specification I can hold any proposed resolution up against. Write that specification for me -- the complete list of desiderata a resolution of the two-envelope paradox must meet to count as settling the problem, with the consideration or case that motivates each one.

A few things I need the list to cover, though you should treat this as a floor, not a ceiling:

First, the paradox comes in at least two versions -- the no-peek version, where you reason about a fixed but unobserved amount in your envelope, and the peek version, where you open your envelope, see the amount, and must reason about a conditional expectation given a prior over the possible contents. Many resolutions I've seen in draft dissolve one version and silently leave the other standing. The spec should require that a resolution state explicitly which version it addresses and either handle both or explain why one reduces to the other.

Second, a resolution has to identify a fallacious step -- a specific inference in the switching argument that fails -- rather than merely announce a verdict. Saying the expected values come out equal, or that expectations are infinite, is a diagnosis only if it pins down where the reasoning breaks. Relatedly, the spec must forbid overcorrection: there exist priors under which switching is genuinely the better policy for every amount you might observe, so a resolution that "proves" switching is never warranted has refuted something true and must be disqualified.

Third, I want the spec to force clarity about the relationship between the paradox and infinite expectation: under what conditions on the prior does the always-switch conclusion arise, and is infinite expected value necessary, sufficient, or incidental? A resolution that leans on infinite expectations should be required to say whether the paradox survives in a bounded world (say, the amounts are known to be at most a million dollars), and if it doesn't survive there, to explain what was doing the work.

Fourth, there are choice-points where I suspect the desiderata themselves are contested rather than agreed: whether improper priors (a uniform distribution over all possible amounts) are legitimate tools or the source of the trouble; whether a resolution owes us a decision policy or only a diagnosis; whether treating the problem as a one-off decision versus a repeated game changes what counts as solving it. Mark each desideratum as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and say which camp.

Finally, two things I most want to see. One: if any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say so and prove it or sketch the proof -- a spec that cannot be met is itself a finding, and I'd lead the piece with it. Two: tell me which single requirement, strictly enforced, would knock out the largest share of the resolutions one encounters in the literature and in blog posts, since that tells me where the real fault line runs. A table is fine for the list itself, but I want the reasoning in prose.
