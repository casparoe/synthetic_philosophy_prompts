I help run a monthly evening workshop at a public library, loosely themed around visual design and everyday thinking; most attendees are adults without any formal training in philosophy, linguistics, or typography, and we usually have about twelve people for a ninety-minute session. Lately I've been turning over a question that I find strangely gripping: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? A printed lowercase a in a book, the a in my own handwriting, the a in a child's wobbly scrawl, the single-storey a on a street sign, and some ornate blackletter a look almost nothing alike as shapes -- and yet everyone recognizes them instantly as the same letter, while a subtly wrong squiggle that shares many of their parts is rejected as "not an a." I've tried writing down a precise rule ("a closed loop with a vertical stroke on the right") and immediately found counterexamples on both sides. I want the group to feel this difficulty themselves rather than just hear me describe it.

Could you design a runnable activity for this setting that makes participants run headlong into the problem of what constitutes the identity of a letterform -- the tension between defining a letter by its parts and rules versus something more elusive, a style or "spirit" that can survive radical changes of shape? I'd like them to discover the failure of rule-based definitions themselves: perhaps by having them produce letterforms under constraints, trade and judge one another's productions, and attempt to write a defining rule that then gets broken by the group's own drawings. The idea should emerge from what happens in the room, not from an introductory lecture, and I'd prefer they not hear terms like "family resemblance," "essence," or any philosopher's name until the debrief at the end.

For the debrief, please map specific moments of the activity onto the underlying conceptual problem: which moments correspond to the lure of a parts-based definition, which to the realization that recognition tolerates huge variation while still having boundaries, and which to the puzzle of whether the "spirit" of a letter is a fixed thing or something that shifts with context and neighboring letters. Please also flag what will predictably go wrong when this is run with real people (for instance, participants copying each other, someone gaming the judging by drawing near-misses, or the group converging too quickly on one safe style) and how the facilitator should handle each case. Finally, be honest about which aspect of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and must simply be stated aloud -- my guess is that something about the relationship between letter identity and overall alphabetic style won't survive translation into a game, but I'd like your judgment on that.
