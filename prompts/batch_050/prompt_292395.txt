Me and a mate have just spent the whole evening arguing about this and got nowhere, so I want an essay that actually settles it, not another balanced survey.

We were talking about Dennett's thought experiment with the two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn. Quick version in case it matters: Chase used to love the flavour of a particular coffee and now hates it, but he can't tell whether his tastes have genuinely changed or whether his memory of how it used to taste has drifted. Sanborn is in the same boat but lands on a different self-diagnosis, and the point is neither of them can check, even from the inside. Dennett's conclusion, as I understand it, is roughly this: if there's no fact of the matter accessible to anyone, including the subject, about which way the change went, then the idea of a private, ineffable quale sitting there unchanged while everything around it shifts is an incoherent posit, and we should just give up on qualia.

My friend thinks this proves far less than Dennett wants. Her line: all the case shows is that memory of past experiences is fallible, which nobody ever doubted, and from "we can be wrong about whether our experiences changed" it doesn't follow that there are no experiences, any more than "we can be wrong about whether the painting was moved" shows there are no paintings. I think she's missing the deeper point but I couldn't articulate why in the arguement.

Here's the wrinkle I want the essay to grapple with, because it's where we got properly stuck. I invented a variant on the spot (she hates it): imagine a future Chase whose brain is continuously scanned by a device that, we can just stipulate, reads off his moment-to-moment gustatory processing and writes it to a log, so there's a complete external record of what his sensory machinery was doing at every sip, and the memory worry is engineered away entirely. Now suppose the logs show the sensory processing is bit-for-bit identical across the years while his verdicts flip from love to hate. Questions for the essay: (1) does the device variant rescue qualia, by giving the "receptors changed versus reaction changed" question a definite answer the original case lacked? Or does it merely relocate the problem, since the log can record what the neurons did but never what the taste was like? (2) If the eliminativist replies that the device only ever records functional goings-on and never the quale itself, has the qualia defender thereby committed to something no possible evidence could reveal — and is that fatal, or is it just what qualia realists cheerfully believed all along?

So: write an essay on whether the Chase and Sanborn case supports eliminativism about qualia or only a modest fallibilism about introspective memory, using my device variant as the test case. Do consider the "Dennett proves too little" objection and also the opposite worry, that his standard proves too much (if nothing counts as evidence for a quale except first-person say-so, and first-person say-so is defeasible, does the same standard threaten other things we plainly accept?). But reach a verdict and defend it. I want something I can throw at her tomorrow, and "it's complicated" won't cut it.
