I teach a second-year philosophy of mind course (about 18 students, one 75-minute session) and we are about to spend two weeks on functionalism and multiple realizability. I want to open the unit with an activity, before they've read anything, that lets them feel the pull of Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary idea from Gödel, Escher, Bach: the thought that a colony of ants, none of which understands anything, might collectively constitute a mind that does -- that understanding could live at the level of the colony's patterns the way our own might live at the level of neural patterns, with the lower-level components as oblivious as neurons are. Design a live simulation in which the students themselves are the components. Concretely: each student follows a short, purely local rule card (if you see X, do Y) that never mentions any question, answer, or meaning, yet the room as a whole should be able to do something recognizably mind-like -- for instance, collectively producing coherent answers to simple questions posed to "the colony," or collectively navigating toward a goal no individual knows about. I'm open to your choice of mechanism (spatial arrangements, token-passing, gesture chains), but it must actually run in a seminar room with tables pushed back, no electronics, in the time available.

The deliverable: the exact rule cards (worded as the students would receive them), materials, setup, a minute-by-minute timeline, the facilitator's script for what to ask "the colony" and when, and a debrief sequence. The debrief matters most: it should move from what the students just experienced -- what it felt like to be an ant, the moment they realize the room answered something none of them heard -- to the real philosophical questions, namely whether pattern-level competence plus the right causal organization is enough for a mind, what more could reasonably be demanded, and where this puts pressure on Searle-style intuitions that no mere rule-following system could understand anything. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first two times I run this (students breaking character, rules producing deadlock, the colony's output being illegible) and how to design around or recover from each, and be frank about which feature of the Aunt Hillary thought experiment a classroom simulation cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply in discussion -- I'm guessing phenomenal consciousness is the main casualty, but tell me if you think it's something else. Keep the whole thing under 900 words.
