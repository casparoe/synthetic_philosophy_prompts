I have no formal training in physics or philosophy, but I read a lot of popular science, and I keep running into one sentence doing more work than it can bear. The most recent occasion was an argument on a forum about why we remember the past but not the future, and whether time could in principle run the other way. Someone ended the discussion by writing: entropy always increases, so the direction of time just is the direction of entropy increase, and there is nothing left to explain.

When I pressed for what that meant, I got a muddle of half-answers: entropy is disorder, entropy is the number of ways a system can be arranged, the universe is running down, the arrow of time is a law of nature, the arrow of time is an illusion produced by statistics. I suspect the slogan is being used to mean several quite different things at once, some of them obvious and some of them very strong, and I would like them pulled apart.

Could you set out the distinct precise claims that "entropy always increases" might be making, and likewise for the companion claim that the arrow of time is the increase of entropy? I have in mind candidates of very different strengths, though you may carve them better than I can: something near-definitional about what entropy measures; a statistical claim about what overwhelmingly happens in isolated systems; a strict empirical law with no exceptions; a historical claim about the whole universe, including why the past had lower entropy at all; a claim that entropy increase explains, or even constitutes, the direction of time itself; and a claim that it accounts for the other asymmetries people point to, such as memory, records, and cause and effect. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude or definition, a well-supported empirical claim, a substantive and contestable thesis, or simply false. I gather, for instance, that on the statistical reading the "always" is not literally correct, and I would like to know whether that is a pedantic footnote or a crack that swallows the whole slogan.

Then, most importantly: which of these readings would my interlocutor actually need in order to close off the question of why we remember the past rather than the future, and whether time could reverse? My suspicion is that the slogan only gets him his conclusion on the strongest reading, and that the strongest reading is also the least secure, quietly assuming rather than explaining the low-entropy past. But I may be wrong about that, and if a weaker reading suffices, I want to know how.

Two requests about how you answer. First, please write in continuous prose, like a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. If you lay out several readings, keep them clearly ordered in words within the prose, first, second, third, so I can follow the structure without any formatting. Second, since you will be giving verdicts about which readings are platitudes and which are false, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to reclassify one of the readings, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. And if some of the readings turn on open problems that even specialists dispute, say plainly which ones, rather than smoothing that over.
