I’m a philosophy professor revising the consciousness unit for a graduate seminar. A collegue who works mostly on political philosophy drafted the following blurb for the student handbook. It sounds broadly right to me, but a couple of moves make me suspicious, and I don’t want to teach a caricature:

> Illusionists about phenomenal consciousness are not claiming that we are zombies or that nothing mental is going on. They accept beliefs, desires, sensations, attention, introspection, and self-monitoring. What they deny is that any mental state has the special intrinsic qualitative character—the redness of red or the hurtfulness of pain—that introspection seems to reveal. On the standard version, the brain builds a compressed internal model of its own states, and this model represents them as having ineffable qualitative properties even though no such properties are instantiated. The so-called hard problem is therefore really the meta-problem: explain why physical systems judge, report, and behave as if they were phenomenally conscious.
>
> Thus, when I say that coffee tastes bitter, the state I am reporting is real enough, but strictly speaking nothing tastes bitter to me. Likewise, if pain’s badness is supposed to consist in its qualitative feel, illusionists must say that nothing is bad in that way; at most, pain has functional features that motivate avoidance and reports of badness. The familiar objection is that an illusion of consciousness must itself be consciously experienced, so the view refutes itself. Illusionists reply that misrepresentation need not be phenomenal: a system can falsely represent one of its states as qualitative without that representation itself having a qualitative character. Illusionism is therefore eliminativism about consciousness as such, although it can still permit talk of consciousness in a loose everyday sense.

Could you audit this sentence by sentence? I’m especially interested in which claims are load-bearing commitments of illusionism, which are optional explanatory stories, and which subtly shift the view into something easier to refute. Please distinguish the different senses of “sensation,” “experience,” and “consciousness” at work here, and say whether “nothing tastes bitter to me” follows if “taste” is understood functionally rather than phenomenally. Also, is the familiar objection question-begging, or does it expose a real instability in the idea of a nonphenomenal seeming?

Don’t just give me the anti-illusionist party line, and please no bibliography. Flag where illusionists themselves might disagree, and be explicit about what would change your verdict: for example, what argument would show that the objection does—or does not—assume precisely the phenomenal notion of seeming that illusionists reject.
