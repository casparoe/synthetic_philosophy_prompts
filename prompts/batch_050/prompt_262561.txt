so i'm a novelist, and my writers' group has been stuck for months on the usual blow-up -- one of our former faves got credibly accused of some genuinely awful stuff, and now half the group won't read him and the other half says that's philistinism. the anti-boycott side has a go-to move they wheel out every time, and it's an analogy: a novel is like a plate of food. if you find out the chef is a terrible person, the risotto doesn't suddenly start tasting worse, and refusing to eat it on those grounds is just confused -- you're judging the dish by something that isn't in the dish. they use this to argue we should all calm down, evaluate books purely on craft, and stop letting an author's conduct into our aesthetic judgments at all.

i want you to actually audit this analogy for me, not just pick a side. give me the explicit mapping first -- what plays the role of the chef, the dish, the flavor, the eating -- then go through where it breaks down. some stuff i've been chewing on that you should address one way or another: a meal doesn't say anything to you and a novel does; eating dinner doesn't put the chef's name on a syllabus or confer prestige on him, but reading-and-recommending kind of does that for an author; and it seems like a chef's cruelty can't get *into* the risotto, whereas an author's blind spots might actually get baked into the craft itself -- whose interiority gets rendered and who's just furniture in the story. but maybe those disanalogies don't all matter equally for the conclusion my groupmates want? separate the breaks that actually threaten the "just judge the craft" verdict from the ones that are technically disanalogies but don't do any damage.

then tell me whether their conclusion survives the audit, and if the analogy was quietly doing all the work, suggest what a fairer analogy would be -- my own candidate is that a novel is more like a freindship with someone charming you can visit but never confront, but you should stress-test that one too rather than just letting me have it.
