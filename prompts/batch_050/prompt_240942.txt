The argument, about whether "generations" like Boomers and Millennials are real things:

(1) Dana says generations are marketing fiction, not real kinds. The boundaries are arbitrary -- someone born in December 1996 and someone born in January 1997 get put in different generations -- and the differences *within* any generation dwarf the differences between them.

(2) Casey says fuzzy boundaries don't make a kind unreal: nobody denies mountains or childhood are real just because you can't say exactly where they start. And cohorts share formative experiences -- coming of age in the 2008 crash, the internet arriving mid-childhood -- that plausibly leave lasting marks on attitudes.

(3) Dana replies that those are period effects, not cohort effects -- the crash hit everyone alive at the time -- and that the "formative years" story is unfalsifiable: any 15-year window contains *some* big event, so you can retrofit a narrative onto any arbitrary cohort. If Millennial were a real kind, it should predict things, and it predicts less about you than your parents' income does.

(4) Casey counters that by that logic historians would have to give up "Victorians" too, and nobody thinks that's marketing fiction. Plus, generational labels are self-fulfilling: once people identify as Millennials and get stereotyped as such, the category becomes socially real even if it started out arbitrary.

(5) Dana says Casey's last point concedes the whole game -- a fiction that becomes socially real through self-identification is like money or fashion, real as a *social* fact but not a real kind in the population. Casey says that distinction is exactly what's confused, since social kinds are still kinds.

So: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each briefly, and then say where the disagreement actually lives -- is it empirical, or a fight about what "real kind" means?
