A friend and I spent all of last night arguing about consciousness, and I want to settle it in verse. She insists that somewhere in the mind there must be a place where all the sensory streams arrive, where experience is "presented" to the one who experiences it — a finishing line where it all comes together. I kept objecting that this just installs a little spectator inside the head who would then need a theater of its own, and that the real picture is messier: many parallel versions of events being continuously drafted and revised, with no single canonical showing and no audience.

Write a poem that enacts this dispute rather than merely describing it, using the following constraint: five stanzas of blank verse, in which the opening stanza confidently builds the inner theater (seats, screen, the viewer in the dark), and each subsequent stanza must open by rewriting the final line of the stanza before it — same scene, revised wording — so that by the end no line of the poem stands as the settled, official version of what happened. The revisions should not be corrections of errors but genuinely competing drafts, so the poem's own form makes the point that there is no final text and no one who reads it. The closing stanza should let the theater go dark without announcing a winner. Someone who knows the debate should recognize both positions in how the poem behaves, and someone who doesn't should still read it as a good poem about memory and self-revision.
