I edit the investigations desk of a regional news organization. We publish two or three substantial investigative pieces a month, and I make fifteen to twenty publish-or-spike decisions in that time, most of them with at most two days to decide, all subject to legal review, and all ultimately requiring my signature. My reporters keep landing on opposite instincts. Half of them are closet Cliffordians: nothing runs until the evidence would satisfy a hostile auditor, and they would rather kill a true story than publish an under-supported one. The other half are Jamesians: some questions cannot be settled in advance, waiting is itself a decision with costs, and sources, like James's potential friends, often respond only to someone willing to extend trust first. Both sides quote their philosopher at me. Neither can tell me, in advance of a specific case, what rule they are applying.

Write me the decision procedure I should adopt: a single ordered sequence of tests that a reporter can run on a claim before pitching it and that I can rerun before signing off. It should be drawn from both James and Clifford rather than splitting the difference between them -- Clifford's duties of inquiry and his insistence that a belief's legitimacy depends on how it was acquired, not on whether it turns out true; James's criteria for when believing ahead of the evidence is permitted, namely that the option be genuine in his sense (live, forced, and momentous) and that the matter be one the evidence cannot in principle settle in time. For each step, state the question to be asked, what counts as a passing answer, and the stopping rule: publish, spike, or investigate further, with a time budget attached to the last. Identify which distinctions from the originals must survive translation into newsroom language and which can be safely dropped, and defend each dropping. In particular, the procedure must keep separate: believing a claim versus committing the institution to it in print; evidence that is unavailable versus evidence that is merely ungathered; beliefs whose truth depends on whether they are held; and the asymmetry between the costs of publishing a falsehood and the costs of spiking a truth.

Then run the procedure on three of my actual pending cases. First, a whistleblower allegation of contract-rigging against a county official, resting on one credible source whose documents we have not yet obtained but plausibly could. Second, whether to send our best reporter to embed with a controversial sheriff's department for six months, a forced and momentous allocation decision where the information that would settle it cannot exist before we commit. Third, whether to launch an open-ended investigation into foster-care placement failures whose success depends on our believing, before any evidence, that families will trust us enough to come forward -- a belief that helps make itself true. At least one of these should come out in a way I will not enjoy, and I expect it is the first: explain, in the procedure's own terms, why spiking or delaying a story I am privately convinced is true can be mandatory, and why my private conviction is not an input the procedure may accept.

Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot decide: the points where it stops and hands the question back to me as a judgment about values rather than evidence, and the failure modes a reporter applying it mechanically should be warned about.
