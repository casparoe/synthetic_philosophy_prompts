I've been running an informal reading group with some friends from graduate school — a mix of economists, a physicist, and a mathematician — and our last two sessions on the St. Petersburg paradox ended in an argument I want adjudicated carefully. One member defended the classical resolution going back to Daniel Bernoulli in the early eighteenth century: because wealth has diminishing marginal utility, rational agents maximize expected logarithmic utility rather than expected monetary value, and the infinite expected payout of the game therefore translates into a modest finite price. Another member, who had recently read Ole Peters's work on ergodicity economics, insisted that Bernoulli arrived at the right answer for the wrong reason. On her telling, when wealth evolves multiplicatively over time, the time-average growth rate of a gamble equals the expected logarithm of wealth, so logarithmic "utility" falls out of the dynamics as a theorem rather than being postulated as a psychological fact about satiation. Nobody in the group could settle whether this is a genuine conceptual advance or a rediscovery dressed in new vocabulary.

Please act as an economist with deep expertise in decision theory and the foundations of probability, and think carefully through the comparison before committing to a position. In what sense, if any, does the time-average approach subsume, dissolve, or merely replicate expected utility theory? Work through the hard cases rather than restating the standard pitch: Does the ergodicity argument have anything to say about genuinely one-shot decisions, such as selling one's house or betting the firm on a single product launch, where no long run exists to average over? What happens to the derivation when the dynamics are additive rather than multiplicative — does the ensemble average quietly regain its authority there? Consider a village of ten thousand people who each play the repeated gamble but pool all proceeds and split them equally every round: does the ensemble average become decision-relevant for them even though the dynamics remain multiplicative, and if so, does that show the ergodicity framework is secretly about the size of the pool rather than about time? And how many repetitions are enough — if a gamble recurs only a handful of times over a lifetime, which average should govern?

Having weighed all of this, reach and defend a verdict: which of the two resolutions offers the deeper explanation of why the St. Petersburg game is not worth any arbitrarily large finite price, and does the ergodicity perspective earn its claim to have removed an arbitrary psychological assumption, or does it merely relocate the assumption into a choice of dynamics and time horizon?
