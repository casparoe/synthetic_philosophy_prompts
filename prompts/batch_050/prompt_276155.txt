At a going-away dinner last week I watched two colleagues — Dana, an economist by training, and Priya, who works in clinical research — argue about a mutual friend who had just finished a law degree he openly hated because, in his words, he couldn't stomach wasting five years of tuition. I'll reconstruct their four moves as fairly as I can, since I genuinely can't decide who won.

(1) Dana opened with the textbook line: a cost is either recoverable or it isn't, and if it isn't, it is irrelevant to any decision you now face. The only legitimate question is whether the future benefits of finishing exceed the future costs. Our friend's five years are gone either way; letting them tip the scale is the sunk cost fallacy in its purest form, and finishing the degree to "honor" them compounds the loss rather than redeeming it.

(2) Priya replied that this is too quick, because in real life the past investment does work that pure forward-looking analysis misses. First, finishing things is partly an investment in your own self-model: someone who quits a degree at year five teaches himself that he quits, and that damaged self-trust is a real future cost of quitting. Second, the very fact that he chose to invest five years is evidence — from his own earlier, perhaps clearer-eyed self — that the career was worth pursuing, and his current despair may be less reliable information than his past commitment. So treating the sunk investment as carrying weight isn't a fallacy; it's deference to evidence and to one's future character.

(3) Dana answered that Priya had proven the doctrine's point, not refuted it. Everything on her list — self-trust, signaling to oneself, the informational value of the original choice — is a future consequence of quitting or continuing, which the standard analysis already counts. The doctrine never said "ignore the past"; it said "ignore irrecoverable costs as such." Once you translate her considerations into forward-looking terms, there's nothing left over for the sunk cost itself, so the friend is still making a mistake if it's genuinely the money, rather than these downstream effects, that's driving him.

(4) Priya's final move was that this rescue makes the doctrine empty as advice. In every actual case, the hard question is precisely whether your forward-looking reasons are genuine or are motivated confabulations built on top of the sunk cost, and a rule that says "ignore the past except insofar as it bears on the future" gives no procedure for telling the difference. For creatures like us, a blunt policy of finishing what you start — which looks exactly like honoring sunk costs — may outperform case-by-case optimization that we will predictably corrupt. So the "fallacy" might be a rational rule for irrational agents, and condemning it in principle is a luxury of idealized reasoners.

Please adjudicate this move by move. Rate each of the four moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each rating in a few sentences, and then say what, if anything, both Dana and Priya are missing — in particular, whether the disagreement dissolves once the relevant distinctions are drawn, or whether a real dispute survives.
