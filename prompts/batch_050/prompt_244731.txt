My partner Nadia and I are in this informal reading group on hedonism (long story, it started as a book club), and she's become a real evangelist for one particular chunk of Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics, the bit where he goes after Plato's way of ranking pleasures:

"Plato's reason for claiming that the life of the Philosopher has more pleasure than that of the Sensualist is palpably inadequate. The philosopher, he argues, has tried both kinds of pleasure, sensual as well as intellectual, and prefers the delights of philosophic life; the sensualist ought therefore to trust his decision and follow his example. But who can tell that the philosopher's constitution is not such as to render the enjoyments of the senses, in his case, comparatively feeble?"

She loves this because she thinks it shows you can't settle what pleasure is by polling the person with the most refined palate for it. But here's the thing: I've been quietly tracking her actual verdicts on cases, and I can't figure out what rule she's running. Her calls so far: a backrub where the person finds it ticklish and wants it to stop, even though they agree the raw sensations are the same ones they'd enjoy on a better day — she says that's not a pleasure, not while they want it to stop. An objectless warm glow from morphine that the patient is perfectly content to let wash over them — pleasure, definitely, doesn't need to be about anything. Being totally absorbed in a chess game with zero reflective thought about how it feels — still a pleasure. A hoppy craft beer that tasted awful for years and now, chemically identical, she savors — it became a pleasure when she started savoring it, not before. The relief when a headache finally lifts — a genuine positive pleasure, not just the absence of something. And a spiteful little glow of satisfaction at a rival's misfortune that she'd disavow two seconds later — still a pleasure while it lasts, she insists.

Now the case we're arguing about: the last spoonful of an extremely rich chocolate mousse. She closes her eyes, says it tastes exactly as good as the first spoonful did, savors it completely — but she's utterly full, and if the bowl magically refilled she'd push it away without a flicker of regret, and she has no wish for even this mouthful to last a second longer than it does. Is that last spoonful a pleasure, on her view? I genuinely can't predict her, and I suspect that's because at least two different rules fit her whole track record: one where a pleasure is a feeling you're welcoming or taking to be good-as-a-feeling while you have it, and one where a pleasure is a feeling you want to continue. Those two split right down the middle on the mousse. So: what do you think she'll say, which rule is she actually running (or is it a third one I haven't spotted), what does each candidate rule predict here, and if her past verdicts genuinely underdetermine it, what's the further case that would settle which rule she holds? Bonus points if you can tell me whether whatever rule lands is consistent with the Sidgwick passage she keeps waving around, or whether she's accidentally committed herself to the kind of cross-person comparison he says is hopeless.
