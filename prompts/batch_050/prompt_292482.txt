Since retiring from a career in experimental physics I have been reading my way into the philosophical literature on rational choice, and I keep returning to Warren Quinn's puzzle of the self-torturer. The case, as I understand it: a device is implanted with a dial of a thousand settings; each advance of one notch produces an increase in pain too small for the subject to discriminate, and pays out ten thousand dollars; yet the difference between the lowest and the highest setting is the difference between comfort and unremitting agony, so that the subject who has turned all the way would gladly surrender most of his winnings to be returned to zero. Before I commit my views to paper for my reading group, I would like a consistency audit. Here is where I have landed:

1. Marginal verdict. At every setting, advancing one notch is a favorable trade: an imperceptible increment of pain for ten thousand dollars is a bargain, and a rational agent prefers each setting to its predecessor.
2. Transitivity. Rational preference over a fixed menu of outcomes must be transitive; a cycle is a defect in the agent, not a curiosity of the case.
3. Money pump. What establishes this is the money-pump argument: cyclic preferences expose an agent to a guaranteed loss, and that exploitability is precisely what makes them irrational.
4. Endpoint verdict. The self-torturer at the highest setting strictly prefers the lowest, and acts rationally in paying to be reset.
5. Foresight. A fully rational agent who understands the entire setup in advance will simply stop at some moderate setting; the puzzle is a cautionary tale about myopia, not a genuine problem for rational choice.
6. Imperceptibility. A change in pain that the subject genuinely cannot discriminate from the status quo is, considered on its own, no cost to well-being at all.

My training taught me to trust marginal analysis and to distrust arguments that trade on vagueness, which I suspect is what motivates several of these commitments. What I cannot tell is whether they cohere.

Please proceed as follows. Identify which of these commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. Rank the real tensions by severity. For each, state the cheapest revision and what it costs: which commitment must be abandoned, or how it must be restricted. If a pair looks problematic but dissolves on inspection, explain why rather than merely flagging it. Finally, say which single revision restores consistency at the lowest overall cost.

I would like the whole reply in no more than 700 words.
