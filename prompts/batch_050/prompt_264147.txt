I work as a policy adviser in the government of a mid-sized European country. Our prime minister has announced a headline ambition: within ten years, the country should rank first in the annual world happiness league table — the one based largely on a single survey question that asks people to picture a ladder with steps numbered from zero to ten and to say on which step their own life stands. I have been tasked with drafting the strategy, and I am torn about what to recommend.

A consulting firm has put three kinds of measure on the table. The first seems genuinely valuable on its own terms: properly funded mental-health services, more walkable towns, unemployment support that treats people decently. The second seems aimed at the number rather than the thing: a national advertising campaign encouraging citizens to count their blessings, school programs training children in positive self-evaluation, and a suggestion that the survey fieldwork be scheduled for the weeks just after a beloved national holiday. A third idea, floated only half-jokingly, notes that since the table reports averages, our score would rise if our most miserable residents were somehow never surveyed — or no longer residents.

My question is not really about the third idea, which is obviously unacceptable. It is about the middle category, and about the ambition itself. Suppose the surveys measure something real but partial — how people assess their lives when invited to step back and take stock. Is there anything wrong with policies that improve the answers without improving the lives? If the campaign works and reported life evaluations climb while nothing else changes, has the country become happier in any sense that matters? Conversely, suppose we genuinely improve how citizens' days feel to them, but the ladder score stays flat because expectations rise alongside circumstances. Would the policy then count as a failure, and by whose lights?

I am also unsure about the arithmetic. Measures that transform the lives of the worst-off may barely move a national average, while modest perks for the already-content could lift it noticeably. If the goal is the ranking, the incentives point one way; if the goal is the well-being of actual people, perhaps another. And there is a prior question I cannot settle: whether what makes a life good for the person living it is the kind of thing a single zero-to-ten judgment can capture at all, or whether it is only one thin strand of it.

What should I tell the prime minister? Which of the proposed measures, if any, would serve citizens rather than the scoreboard — and is there a defensible version of the ambition itself, or should I be arguing that the goal be quietly abandoned?
