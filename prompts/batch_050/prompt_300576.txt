Next month I'm giving a forty-minute talk at my company's internal engineering seminar defending the halfer position in the Sleeping Beauty problem: that on waking, Beauty's credence that the coin landed heads should remain 1/2 rather than drop to 1/3. My core argument is that waking conveys no new evidence — Beauty knew in advance, with certainty, that she would be woken — so there is nothing to update on, and the thirder case rests on a slide between a credence in a proposition and the fair price of a bet that is settled once per awakening rather than once per run of the experiment.

The audience is about twenty software engineers with no formal philosophy training. They are comfortable with probability as something you implement rather than something you axiomatise, most have strong (and mostly correct) intuitions drilled in by the Monty Hall problem, and our internal culture treats "I simulated it overnight and got 0.33" as a conversation-ender. Two attendees are data scientists with serious Bayesian training who will want to talk about updating and conditioning; one backend engineer has a habit of reducing every puzzle to expected payouts. Nobody in the room will have read the literature on self-locating belief, so objections will arrive in engineering vocabulary, not philosophical vocabulary.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I am actually likely to get from this audience — not the objections a philosophy journal referee would raise — in roughly the order they will come, each phrased the way one of these colleagues would put it. For each one: how damaging it is to my position, a reply I can deliver in under two minutes without a whiteboard, what I should concede immediately, what I should hold firm, and where my reply is at its softest. At least one entry should tackle the Monte Carlo objection head-on, since I need to explain why a simulation that counts awakenings rather than runs is not neutral evidence against me, and one should cover the variant where Beauty is told on waking that it is Monday, because I suspect the data scientists have read about it. Please also identify the weakest point in my own argument as I've sketched it — I assume it is the variant where tails produces a million awakenings rather than two, but tell me if something else is softer — and name the question I am hoping nobody asks. Where a graceful concession costs my overall position nothing, say so explicitly: I would rather give ground than defend everything.
