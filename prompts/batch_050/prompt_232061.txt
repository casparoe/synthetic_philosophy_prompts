Last week I had a long, unresolved argument with a friend about how the housing cooperatives I work for should be governed, and I want to turn the upshot of that argument into something usable. I draft standing rules for a federation of housing cooperatives -- noise policies, rules about the use of private units, common-area regulations, conduct expectations. These decisions recur constantly, the stakes are moderate but real (people live under these rules for years and boards must sign off on them), and I have no framework beyond intuition for a recurring class of disputes.

My friend's position was simple: if every single resident genuinely prefers one arrangement over another, no rule or principle should stand in the way of adopting it. Mine was that some matters are simply nobody else's business -- what you read, what you hang inside your own window, whom you have over -- even when everyone, for whatever reason, would rather you did otherwise. She then produced what I take to be a version of Sen's liberal paradox: there are cases where respecting each person's protected private sphere and honoring unanimous preference cannot both be done, so one of us has to give. I don't want to relitigate who won. I want a decision procedure I can actually run on a proposed rule, one that takes this conflict seriously instead of pretending it away.

Specifically: the questions to ask about a proposal, in order (does this matter fall within someone's protected personal sphere, and by what test? is there a genuine externality or only a meddlesome preference? is this one of the configurations where protecting the sphere and honoring near-unanimous preference genuinely collide?), what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, and the menu of legitimate moves when a collision is detected -- e.g., protecting the sphere and eating the preference cost, treating the right as waivable or tradable by its holder, restructuring so the decision is made at a different level -- with guidance on which move fits which kind of case. Mark clearly the points where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to a human being because the question is one of values, not of classification.

Then run the procedure on three real proposals from my files: (1) a rule banning all political or religious displays in windows visible from the street, where a survey shows nearly every resident prefers a display-free facade, including residents who would themselves like to display something -- each prefers their own display up but everyone else's down; (2) a quiet-hours rule restricting instrument practice to certain times; (3) a rule requiring staff at the co-op's front desk not to wear religious head coverings, supported by a large majority of residents who say it makes them more comfortable. I expect the procedure to protect (1), and I want you to tell me honestly if it does not -- include at least one case where it delivers a verdict I will dislike, and say so.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot settle at all. And throughout, indicate how confident you are in the load-bearing steps, and single out the one point in the whole framework where you are least sure of your ground.
