Having spent forty years in empirical science (physiology, mostly) before turning to philosophy in retirement, I find myself nagged by a question I cannot formulate precisely enough to investigate: which computations, if any, matter morally? Every physical system can, under some interpretation, be described as computing something — my desk calculator, the thermostat, the weather — and yet I recoil from the conclusion that pulling the plug on a simulation is indistinguishable, morally, from switching off a lamp, and equally from the conclusion that it is indistinguishable from cruelty to an animal. What I want from you is not a verdict but a specification. Before I read further proposals, I want to know what a theory in this area would have to deliver in order to count as adequate.

Please write the list of desiderata such a theory must meet, with the case or consideration that motivates each one. The purpose, so that adequacy has a target: a theory good enough to guide a cautious non-specialist in deciding where to direct research funding and what precautions, if any, current artificial systems warrant.

The list should address, at minimum, the following pressure points, though I expect you will find others:

- It must avoid both horns of what I understand is called the triviality or pancomputationalism problem: if every physical system implements every computation under some mapping, then either every computation matters (absurd) or the theory must supply a principled, non-gerrymandered account of which mappings are legitimate — without smuggling the answer in through the definition of 'implementation.'
- It must separate the question of whether a computation occurs from the question of whether that computation is morally significant, and say what additional property does the work, since I see authors slide between these constantly.
- It must handle a series of hard cases consistently: a lookup table that reproduces a brain's input-output behavior step for step; a simple feedback control system with genuine recurrent dynamics; an insect; a present-day large language model; and a patient under general anesthesia. A theory that gets the 'right' answer on one case by ad hoc stipulation but contradicts itself on another should be marked down.
- It must respect, or explicitly argue against, the distinction between sentience and sapience, and between having moral status and the degree of it, since conflating these seems to poison much of the discussion.
- It must not contradict anything well-established in neuroscience or physics, and it should say what empirical discoveries would change its verdicts — a theory consistent with every possible experimental outcome is, by the standards of my former profession, not a theory at all.
- It must offer some guidance under radical uncertainty, since no one will settle consciousness empirically before decisions must be made.

For each desideratum, state whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and say which camp. Most importantly: flag any pair or subset of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain why — if the specification itself is inconsistent, that is a finding, not a failure. Then, briefly and without allowing it to dominate, indicate how the main families of contenders (global workspace accounts, integrated information theory, higher-order theories, and the view that only biological systems can be sentient at all) fare against the list. I am more interested in the criteria and their motivating cases than in the scorecard; I can do the scoring myself once I know what I am asking of these theories.
