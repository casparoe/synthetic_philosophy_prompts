A colleague summarizes the Principal Principle as follows: “If an agent knows the objective chance of A, rationality requires their credence in A to equal that chance, except when they possess inadmissible evidence. So if a statistically reliable weather model assigns rain a 70 percent chance and you know this, your credence should be .7. The principle thereby defines objective chance as the number ideal rational agents would use, which is why nontrivial chances cannot exist in a deterministic world.” Which parts would a philosopher of probability accept, and which would they regard as distortions? In particular, separate knowledge of a model’s forecast from knowledge of an objective chance, explain the role of inadmissible evidence without making the exception unfalsifiable, and assess whether the principle offers a definition or theory of chance. Does the deterministic-world conclusion follow from the accurate parts?
