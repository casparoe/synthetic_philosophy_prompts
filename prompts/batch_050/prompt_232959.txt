I'm in a graduate seminar on weakness of will, and the professor opened the term by reading us a passage from Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, Book VII, in H. Rackham's translation: "In fact the unrestrained man resembles a state which passes all the proper enactments, and has good laws, but which never keeps its laws... whereas the bad man is like a state which keeps its laws but whose laws are bad." She treats this as the seminar's organizing image, and over the semester she's been giving verdicts on cases we bring her. Her track record so far: (1) A man who has resolved to quit smoking, who at the party thinks "all things considered, I should not smoke this," and smokes it anyway without ever changing his mind -- akrasia. (2) A woman who resolved to work tonight, but when a friend calls with a genuinely rare opportunity she deliberates afresh, sincerely concludes that going out is the better choice, and goes -- not akrasia; the professor called this a lawful repeal rather than lawbreaking. (3) A man who, in a flash of anger, insults a colleague before ever deliberating, and who has resolved many times to hold his tongue and regrets it the moment the words are out -- akrasia, though she noted he never formed any judgment at the moment of action at all. (4) A woman who judges all evening that she ought to leave the party to study, keeps saying "one more minute," and finds at midnight that the evening is gone -- akrasia, since the judgment remained hers throughout even as her attention drifted.

Here is the case for next week, which I'm supposed to comment on. Maria judges in January, calmly and after full deliberation, that she should spend the third weekend of March finishing her grant application rather than attending a festival she loves; the deadline matters and the festival recurs every year. She resolves accordingly. But as the weekend approaches, her assessment shifts: standing on the Friday morning with her bag packed, she sincerely judges -- with reasons she can articulate, about rest, morale, and the deferability of the draft -- that going is, all things considered, the better choice. She goes, misses the deadline, and by Monday judges that she was wrong. Notably, this reversal is no surprise to her: she knew in January, from long experience, that her judgment would swing once the festival was near, and it swings back once the deadline is past.

Please predict the professor's verdict on Maria. I'm less interested in the bare verdict than in the reasoning: which candidate rules fit her four earlier verdicts equally well -- for instance, a rule requiring action against one's all-things-considered judgment at the very moment of acting, versus a rule requiring only that one violate a resolution formed after deliberation, versus something else -- and which of those rules the new case finally pries apart. Tell me which rule you think she is actually running, drawing on her city-of-good-laws image and on her remark about lawful repeal, and say whether Maria's city is one that fails to keep its laws or one that repeals them on a schedule it could have predicted. Finally, name the one further case -- a variant of Maria's situation -- whose verdict would most cleanly confirm or refute your hypothesis about her rule.
