Last night, over a dinner that ran far too late, a friend of mine who works in computational linguistics talked me into an argument about generics — bare generalisations of the form 'ducks lay eggs' — and I have been turning it over ever since without finding the exit. His conclusion was that no generic sentence is ever literally true, and his reasoning ran as follows. (1) If a generic 'Ks are F' is literally true, then its truth must consist in some statable condition holding of Ks and F; otherwise, calling it true is an empty compliment. (2) The condition cannot be that all Ks are F: there are albino tigers without stripes, and yet 'tigers are striped' is plainly true. (3) It cannot be that most Ks are F either: only a small minority of mosquitoes carry malaria, and yet 'mosquitoes carry malaria' is true, whereas the majority of new books sold are paperbacks and yet 'books are paperbacks' sounds straightforwardly false. (4) Nor can the condition be that all normal Ks are F, since 'normal' here is being defined by the very generic at issue — a normal duck is, in part, an egg-laying one — so that proposal goes in a circle and states no condition at all. (5) If no statable condition makes a generic true, then 'true' applied to a generic marks no fact about the world; generics are at best useful shorthand, rules of thumb rather than truths. (6) Therefore no generic is literally true, and 'mosquitoes carry malaria' is exactly as much a fiction as 'mosquitoes carry measles' — both being loose talk that happens to be more or less serviceable.

What makes me think the argument proves too much is that last clause. Whatever the truth about generics, there is surely a real difference between the two mosquito sentences: one is something public health officials are right to assert and the other is not, and an account on which neither is true seems to me to have lost the distinction the whole inquiry was meant to explain. At the same time, I genuinely cannot see which premise to surrender. The examples in (2) and (3) look to me like data rather than premises anyone could honestly deny, and the circularity charge in (4) is one I levelled at my friend myself before he turned it to his own purposes. I have a nagging sense that something slides somewhere between (4) and (6) — as though the failure of every statistical or normative reading were being treated as the failure of every reading whatsoever — but I cannot name the misstep, and my friend denies there is one.

So: which premise or step do you reject, and why did it look so innocent? I would like the diagnosis to do three things. First, identify the point of failure precisely rather than gesturing at the general mysteriousness of generics. Second, explain what it costs to reject that step — in particular, I would like to keep the thought that generics are checkable against the world (we learn 'ducks lay eggs' from instances, and we would retract it given enough non-laying ducks), so please tell me whether the way out preserves that thought or spends it. Third, say a word about the obvious escape routes my friend will raise when I take your answer back to him: that generics predicate properties of kinds rather than of their members, and that generics about striking or dangerous properties are tolerated at very low prevalence in a way that neutral ones are not. Are either of these a genuine reply to his argument, or do they merely rename the difficulty?
