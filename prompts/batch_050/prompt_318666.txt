A funding officer must award five grants from eight hundred proposals before a fixed deadline. She cannot compare every feasible combination. She therefore uses a satisficing procedure: proposals are screened against explicit thresholds for methodological soundness, expected impact, and institutional fit; the first five proposals meeting all thresholds are shortlisted, with limited time reserved for checking close cases. A colleague argues that this is not merely imperfect but normatively indefensible:

1. A rational decision procedure for a finite agent should be assessed by the total value of its results, including both the quality of the option ultimately chosen and the costs of deliberation, search, and error.

2. If two procedures produce the same choice but one consumes more scarce time and attention, the more demanding procedure is, to that extent, less rational.

3. Where a maximizing procedure and a satisficing procedure recommend different options, the maximizing procedure’s recommendation is at least as good by the agent’s substantive standard, before deliberation costs are counted.

4. Consequently, satisficing can be rational only when the expected losses from failing to find the best option are smaller than the deliberation costs that maximization would avoid.

5. To know that this condition holds, the agent must estimate how much better the unexplored alternatives are likely to be. But reliably making that estimate requires information about the distribution and quality of those alternatives—information comparable to what maximization itself would require.

6. Hence satisficing is either irrational, because it stops without considering all alternatives, or practically unusable, because justifying the stopping point requires approximately global information.

7. Every bounded agent must nevertheless stop deliberating under incomplete comparison. If stopping cannot be rationally justified without global information, then every actual bounded decision is either irrational or arbitrary.

8. Therefore bounded rationality does not provide a genuine norm of choice. At most, it explains why agents predictably fall short of the real norm, namely maximization.

This conclusion seems to prove too much: it appears to make bounded rationality normatively empty just when it is most needed. Yet each step has some plausibility, especially the claim that a justification of stopping should not itself be prohibitively costly.

Which premise or inference should be rejected? Explain why that step initially looks compelling, what distinction or hidden assumption it depends on, and what must be given up if it is rejected. In your answer, also determine whether satisficing is best understood as an approximation to maximization under constraints or as a genuinely different standard of rational choice.
