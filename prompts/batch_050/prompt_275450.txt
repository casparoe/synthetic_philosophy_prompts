Write a dialogue, roughly 600–800 words, between two friends of mine who are each trying to persuade me how much of a modest inheritance I should stake in a legitimate betting opportunity that comes around repeatedly: each round offers even odds with a genuine 55% chance of winning, and I may bet any fraction of my current bankroll each time. One friend, an advocate of Kelly-style proportional betting, wants me to commit to a fixed-fraction policy forever; the other, an expected-value maximizer, thinks the Kelly approach is needlessly timid and that leaving expected money on the table is irrational full stop.

Both should be equally sharp, and neither should be a caricature: the Kelly proponent should not dismiss expected value as naïve without argument, and the expected-value proponent should not be a reckless gambler who has simply never heard of ruin. In particular, I want them to actually engage the hard questions: the Kelly advocate must face the charge that maximizing the expected logarithm of wealth is just an arbitrary choice of utility function dressed up as objective rationality, and explain what work repeated play, long-run growth rates, and the near-certainty of a deep drawdown at higher fractions are doing in the argument; the expected-value advocate must explain why the ensemble average is the right standard for someone who will live through only one actual sequence of outcomes, and respond to the claim that a sufficiently aggressive policy makes eventual ruin (or effective ruin) almost sure even though every individual bet is favorable. The dialogue should include at least one moment where each friend concedes that the other's position has real force, and it should end with them agreeing on what, exactly, they still disagree about, so that I can see where the genuine fork is rather than being left with a tie.
