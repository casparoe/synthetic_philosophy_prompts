Frankfurt's account treats bullshit as speech produced with indifference to whether it is true -- a category distinct from both lying and honest error. Debate about LLM outputs has mostly asked which of these three bins a hallucination falls into, and it tends to stall on the same point: bullshit, as Frankfurt defines it, seems to require an attitude of indifference, and it is contested whether LLMs have attitudes at all. I run a small research group with funding for two years of conceptual work on this topic, aimed eventually at informing how outputs get characterized in standards and policy documents (where 'hallucination', 'lying', and 'bullshit' currently get used interchangeably). Rather than settling the bin question directly, I want you to tell me which open questions underneath it we should work on, and in what order. Candidates you might consider include: whether bullshit is best analyzed as a property of speakers or of speech acts (i.e., whether there can be bullshit without a bullshitter); whether a system trained to produce plausible text instantiates indifference to truth functionally, even without attitudes; whether the lie/bullshit/error distinction does any practical work for trust, blame, or liability, or whether it is a philosopher's game; and what LLM confabulation would have to be like for the analogy to human confabulation in clinical psychology to be more apt than the Frankfurt analogy. For each question you prioritize, explain why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is in two years, and what would change your ranking -- for instance, what empirical findings about how these models work, or what policy developments, would move a question up or down the list. The deliverable is a defended agenda, not a survey of the debate.
