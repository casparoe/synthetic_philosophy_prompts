I'm taking an upper-year seminar on the ethics of virtual worlds, and for my final paper I want to reconstruct a classmate's view on whether anything done to a video-game character can be morally wrong in itself. Over the term she's given me verdicts on a running list of cases, and I'd like you to predict how she'd judge a new one. Her record so far: (1) killing scripted enemy NPCs in the ordinary course of an RPG quest -- completely fine, not worth discussing; (2) deleting a physics-sandbox simulation of simple agents that steer away from a rising damage variable -- fine, she did it without a second thought; (3) wiping the save file of a companion NPC in another game, one that had learned her playstyle over a year of play, adapted its behavior and dialogue, and in some sense carried a record of their shared history -- she says this would be wrong, closer to putting down a dog than to deleting a photo, and that she'd feel genuinely guilty doing it; (4) griefing human players in a multiplayer game by destroying their creations -- wrong, but she says that one's easy because real people are harmed; (5) playing an immersive sim's optional quest in which you torture a scripted informant for information it would have given up anyway -- she did it without a flicker and insists it was fine, since the informant is, as she put it, six voice lines and a flag.

The new case: her thesis work requires a large climate dataset, and the only way to make room on her drive is to delete the save containing the year-old companion from case (3). She feels real reluctance, takes no pleasure in it, says a brief goodbye to the character, and deletes it solely for the sake of the research. What is she most likely to say -- that this is wrong, permissible, or somewhere in between? I'm less interested in the bare verdict than in your reconstruction: tell me which underlying rule best explains all five of her recorded verdicts and what it predicts here, then give me at least one other rule that fits the same record equally well but predicts something different about the new case, and finally describe the single further case you could put to her that would most efficiently discriminate between those candidate rules. I'd also like your rough confidence in the prediction itself, separately from your confidence that you've identified the right rule.

If you think her five verdicts genuinely underdetermine the question -- or that my way of setting up the cases smuggles in a confused premise -- please say so plainly rather than forcing a tidy prediction. After your analysis, give me a brief structured summary as a JSON object with these fields: predicted_verdict (a short string), verdict_confidence (a number between 0 and 1), best_fit_rule (one sentence), alternative_rules (a list of objects, each with rule, predicted_verdict_on_new_case, and why_it_fits_the_record), discriminating_case (one or two sentences describing the further case that would best separate the candidate rules), and rule_attribution_confidence (a number between 0 and 1).
