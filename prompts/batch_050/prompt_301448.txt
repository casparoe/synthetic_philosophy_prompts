I am a doctoral candidate in epidemiology, in the final stages of a dissertation whose core is a systematic review of 180 studies on the effectiveness of a class of public-health interventions. For each of the 180 studies I have done real due diligence: I read the paper closely, scrutinized the design, reran the analysis where data were available, and corresponded with the authors in doubtful cases. In every single instance, my considered judgment is that the reported effect is genuine. If you asked me about any one of them, I would say: yes, I believe this one holds.

At the same time, I am not naive about my field. Replication projects in adjacent areas routinely find that some appreciable fraction of published findings fail to hold up, and I see no reason to think my 180 are immune. So I also believe, with roughly equal confidence, that some of the findings I have just endorsed are false. I cannot say which; if I had specific grounds against any particular study, I would already have excluded it.

Three practical questions now force the issue.

First, the writing. My summary chapter needs a bottom line. Writing a sentence that commits me to all 180 findings holding feels like asserting something I do not believe. Yet my dissertation, read entry by entry, asserts exactly that. Is there a coherent position on which I stand behind every entry but must refuse the collective claim — and if so, what should the bottom line actually say? Would a hedged formulation (something like: nearly all of these findings hold) be an improvement, or does it just relocate the problem?

Second, a disagreement in my corridor. My supervisor argues that by endorsing each study individually I am already committed to the conjunction, and that refusing to say so is straightforwardly inconsistent: one cannot assert 180 things and then deny that they are all true. A friend in statistics argues the reverse — that given the base rates, expecting some failures is the rational position, and it would in fact be irrational to believe the conjunction even while rationally believing each conjunct. Both cannot be right, yet each seems to have a point. Which of them is making the error, and where exactly does it lie?

Third, the signature. A public-health NGO wants to deploy an intervention package whose design only makes sense if essentially all of the 180 effects are real: the components compensate for one another, and if a handful fail, the package could do more harm than good. They have asked me to sign a letter stating that the evidence base is sound enough to act on. Signing seems to require confidence in precisely the claim I said I do not believe. Refusing seems absurd, since I vouched for every entry. Does acting on a body of claims differ, epistemically, from believing them all — or am I simply trying to have it both ways?

One complication that may or may not matter: the 180 findings are not independent. Roughly thirty are secondary analyses that presuppose the results of a handful of large anchor trials, so if one anchor fails, a cluster falls with it. Feel free to use or ignore this as you see fit.

I am interested in your reasoned assessment rather than pointers to the literature. Please be explicit about how confident you are in the main steps of your answer, and single out the one point on which you are least certain.
