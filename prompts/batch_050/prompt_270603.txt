You are an expert in rational choice theory writing for a small interdisciplinary workshop on formal epistemology and decision theory. Two of the workshop's participants -- a defender of orthodox expected utility theory (EUT) and a defender of Lara Buchak-style risk-weighted expected utility theory (REU, on which agents weight outcomes by their rank, so that worse outcomes can receive more or less weight than better ones independently of their probabilities) -- have agreed to an adversarial collaboration. Before drafting, think carefully about where each theory gets its intuitive force and which objections each side considers already answered. Then produce the document such a collaboration should yield: (1) a numbered list of claims both parties can sign, covering at least what standard representation theorems do and do not establish, which choice patterns (e.g., Allais-type behavior) are common ground, and whether risk attitudes toward known probabilities can always be redescribed as curvature of the utility function; (2) a table of residual disagreements, each formulated neutrally and worded so that each side would accept the statement of its own position; (3) an identification of the cruxes -- I suspect one concerns whether instrumental rationality (e.g., avoiding foreseeably dominated plans across time) constrains risk attitudes, and another whether the rank-dependence of REU is a psychologically and normatively independent dimension or a redescription of stakes; and (4) for each crux, a case, experiment, or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction or verdict recorded in advance. If part of the dispute turns out to be terminological -- for instance, about what "rationality requires" versus what is merely permissible -- flag it explicitly rather than smoothing it over.
