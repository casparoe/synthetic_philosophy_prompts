I've been drafting a statement of my epistemological commitments for a reading group on skepticism, and before I circulate it I'd like a stress test: do these five theses actually cohere, or am I papering over a contradiction? Here's where I've landed. (1) I know plenty of ordinary things — that I have hands, that my seminar meets on Thursday, that my car is parked where I left it — and I know these things even though I cannot rule out being a brain in a vat. (2) I think Nozick's tracking analysis is basically right: knowledge is true belief that is sensitive and adheres, and the sensitivity condition is the correct way to capture the idea that knowledge excludes luck. (3) I accept closure for known single-step entailments: if I know that p, and I competently deduce q from p while retaining my knowledge that p, then I know q. (4) I deny that I know my ticket in next week's million-ticket lottery will lose, however long the odds, and I take this to show that overwhelmingly justified confidence is not sufficient for knowledge. (5) Whether a belief counts as knowledge should not depend on which error possibilities happen to be conversationally salient when someone evaluates it; the same belief, formed in the same way, cannot be knowledge in one conversation and fail to be knowledge in another merely because somebody raised a skeptical hypothesis aloud.

When I showed this list to a friend, she said she was sure at least two of the items were pulling against each other but couldn't pin down which, and I've gone back and forth without getting anywhere. I'd like an audit, not a survey of the literature: go through the set and tell me which pairs or triples are in genuine tension, which only appear to be in tension but dissolve once you're careful about what each thesis actually says, and how severe each real conflict is. For each genuine tension, identify exactly what would have to give — which commitment, revised how — and note what the revision costs me elsewhere in the set. In particular, if two of my commitments are each individually standard in the literature but their usual motivations turn out to be incompatible, I want that spelled out rather than gestured at.

Please rank the conflicts by severity rather than treating them all as equally damaging, and end with a recommendation: if I want to keep as much of the spirit of this package as possible, which single revision cleans up the most, and what does my overall position look like afterward? One constraint: don't tell me to simply drop (1) — ordinary knowledge is the thing I'm least willing to sacrifice, though I'm open to hearing that this refusal is itself what generates the deepest problem, if you think that's the diagnosis.
