Alright, crowd-sourcing a thought experiment for my grad seminar on machine ethics, because the ones in the literature are all either trolley cases or paperclip nonsense and I want something that actually bites.

Here's the build order. Construct a scenario in which two artificial agents are deployed in the same role -- I'm picturing something like discharge planning or resource allocation in a hospital, but pick whatever setting works -- where one agent was built by explicitly encoding moral rules and principles (the programmers sat down and wrote the ethics in), and the other was trained to predict what a panel of thoughtful humans would judge in similar cases. The crucial spec: throughout their shared operational history, the two agents must have agreed on every single decision. No disagreement at all until the case you describe. Then, on your case, they diverge -- and here's the hard part -- the divergence must be such that a reasonable person in the seminar can't just side with one of them. Each verdict has to be genuinely defensible as the ethical verdict. If I read your case and immediately know which machine is right, you've failed the assignment.

Constraints: realistic near-future tech, no superintelligence, no novel physics of cognition, no cases where a patient dies no matter what (that's just trolley problems in a lab coat), and the scenario itself in one paragraph.

After the scenario, do the work: (1) state each agent's verdict and reconstruct the reasoning each would give, from inside its own architecture -- not "the rule machine follows rules" but the actual rule applied, and the actual pattern the imitator picked up on; (2) tell me which structural feature of the case generates the first disagreement after all that agreement, and show that if you remove that feature the two agents collapse back into agreeing, i.e. that the history of agreement was hiding exactly this fault line; (3) then the payoff question: have each side's defender argue that the other machine wasn't doing ethical reasoning at all in your case -- the rule-machine defender says pattern-matching to human judgments isn't ethics even when it matches, the imitation-machine defender says following rules you don't understand isn't ethics even when they're good rules -- and tell me honestly whether the history of perfect agreement is evidence that both were reasoning ethically all along, or evidence of nothing much at all.
