I teach a second-year undergraduate course in normative ethics, about 18 students, and I have a single 75-minute session. I want an in-class activity that makes the torture-versus-dust-specks dilemma -- the question of whether some finite number of individually negligible harms, spread across many people, can outweigh one catastrophic harm to one person -- land as a live problem rather than a curious thought experiment. The students will have read nothing on it beforehand, and I do not want the dilemma named, described, or even hinted at before the activity; the whole point is that they should generate it themselves through their own choices and only recognize in the debrief what they have done.

Design the activity in two connected stages. In the first stage, each student should make a series of small, private, real-stakes decisions -- stakes small enough to be genuinely negligible per decision (seconds of an irritating sound, drops of sour liquid, forfeited candies from an endowment you give them, or whatever you find workable without money), but such that the facilitator secretly aggregates the consequences of everyone's choices, and the aggregate, revealed at the end, is something no student would have accepted had it been offered to them as a single explicit tradeoff. Specify the exact rules, the materials, the payoff or penalty structure, how many decisions each student makes and on what information, how the aggregation is concealed during play without lying to anyone, and how the reveal is staged. In the second stage, I want a pairwise-comparison exercise that pushes the group toward an apparent intransitivity: a sequence of choices in which each individual step looks obviously correct (preferring slightly less of a bad thing, or trading a slightly-worse outcome for one affecting slightly fewer people), but where the sequence as a whole lands somewhere the group unanimously rejects at the outset. Spell out the rungs of the ladder, how you elicit and record the group's verdicts so that the intransitivity becomes undeniable on the board, and how to handle a group that notices the trap midway and refuses to keep stepping.

Then give me a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the activity onto the philosophical machinery: the difference between aggregating harms across persons and across time-slices of one person, the separability assumption each stage quietly relied on, the difference between the aggregation problem and the intransitivity problem (they are not the same, and students will conflate them), and the places where a utilitarian, a prioritarian, and a contractualist would each dig in their heels. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first two times I run this -- students gaming the micro-decisions, the stakes being too trivial to feel like harms at all, someone spotting the aggregation early and tipping off the room, the reveal reading as a trick and producing resentment instead of puzzlement -- and what to do about each. Finally, be candid about which features of the actual dilemma the activity cannot reproduce: the real thing involves harms of a kind and scale no classroom can ethically or practically stage, and the interpersonal stakes involve strangers, not classmates eating sour candy; tell me what I must supply in discussion to bridge that gap rather than letting the debrief overclaim.

Please write the whole answer as continuous prose, as if in a long letter to me: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold text, and no tables.
