Last night a friend and I argued past midnight about large language models and the philosophy of history, and I want the dispute adjudicated by three witnesses who cannot, strictly speaking, be called. The argument was this. My friend claimed that LLMs vindicate the sceptical wing of twentieth-century historiography: here is a machine that produces fluent, structured, emotionally resonant historical narrative without any contact with archives, sources, or evidence at all, which in her view shows that narrative form is something imposed on the past rather than extracted from it, and that the rhetorical construction Hayden White made much of is doing nearly all the work in human historiography too. I took the opposite side. It seems to me that LLMs demonstrate exactly the distinction the sceptics blurred: their narratives fail precisely where evidential discipline is absent, and we can detect the failures only by checking against the documentary record, which suggests that genuine history is constrained by sources in a way that pure narrative construction is not.

Here is what I would like from you. Reconstruct, as carefully and charitably as you can, how Leopold von Ranke, E. H. Carr, and Hayden White would each respond to the phenomenon of an LLM generating a polished account of, say, the Congress of Vienna, complete with plausible-sounding but occasionally fabricated references. In each case, go beyond the one-line caricature. For Ranke, consider his source-critical method and his ideal of showing the past as it essentially was: would he treat the LLM as merely a new species of uncritical chronicle, or as something that exposes a deeper problem about what the critical method was securing? For Carr, consider his claims that historical facts are constituted by the historian's selection and that history is a dialogue between present and past: would he count the LLM as a historian at all, and does his view predict that its outputs differ from human history only in degree? For White, consider his account of emplotment and the tropological deep structure of historical narratives: would he embrace the LLM as proof that narrative coherence is prior to and independent of evidence, or would he instead insist that his point was always about what disciplined, archive-based historians do, and that the machine is no counterexample to anything?

Then adjudicate the original dispute. State which of my friend's and my positions fares better once all three reconstructions are on the table, and identify the single point where my friend's inference from the machine's fluency to the autonomy of narrative is strongest and where it breaks. If you think the exercise dissolves the argument rather than settling it, say so and explain what question we should have been arguing about instead.
