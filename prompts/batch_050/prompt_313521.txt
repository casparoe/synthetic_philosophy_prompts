I sit on a small civil review panel that has to settle what we internally call continuity disputes. These come up maybe a dozen times a year: someone suffers brain damage or major amnesia, and the panel has to recommend whether the present person should be treated as continuous with the earlier person for things like responsibility for misconduct, eligibility for an inheritance, consent to release private records, or whether an old advance directive still counts as theirs. The legal side gives us documents and brain evidence, but we keep getting stuck on the identity question itself.

Please act as both a professional philosopher and a practical procedure-designer. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming the main ways a procedure like this could go wrong before you give the final version. I want you to turn Locke’s memory criterion of personal identity into something our panel can actually apply, while explicitly dealing with Reid’s brave officer problem.

By Locke’s criterion here I mean the rough idea that a present person is the same person as someone earlier if the present person can consciously appropriate the earlier experience—remember it from the inside as something they did or underwent, rather than merely know that it happened. Reid’s objection is the familiar chained-memory case: a boy is punished; later, as a young officer, he remembers that punishment; still later, as an old general, he remembers being the officer but no longer remembers the punishment. A direct-memory test makes the boy identical to the officer and the officer identical to the general, yet apparently not the boy identical to the general, which is an awkward result if identity is transitive.

Build us a reusable identity-chain audit that a non-specialist panel can run in under an hour. It should include:

1. The exact questions to ask, in order, and what kinds of evidence count as strong, weak, or irrelevant for each.
2. A distinction between first-person experiential memory, factual autobiographical knowledge, retained habits or skills, personality resemblance, and bodily/brain continuity. Locke’s theory shouldn’t be flattened into “any psychological similarity counts.”
3. A rule for handling chains of overlapping memories, not just whether the present person directly remembers the original event. Say when a chain preserves continuity and when a gap breaks it.
4. A method for grading the provenance of memories—for example, seemingly native recall, family stories, photographs, therapy-assisted reconstruction, coerced confession, or technological implantation. Please include a test for when someone sincerely seems to remember an event but the memory may have been manufactured.
5. A decision rule for hard cases. In particular, say whether the procedure should deliver same person, different person, indeterminate continuity, or continuity for some purposes but not others.
6. The places where philosophical judgment can’t be eliminated, and which decisions should be kicked back to the panel rather than hidden inside the checklist.
7. A short note on where the procedure departs from Locke, where it responds to Reid, and what it simply cannot settle.

Then apply the procedure to these cases:

- A 70-year-old reliably remembers stealing money at age 40, but has no memory of serious bullying he committed at age 15. There is good evidence that at age 40 he still remembered the bullying. Can the 70-year-old be treated as continuous with the 15-year-old for responsibility purposes?
- A patient after a traumatic brain injury has lost almost all autobiographical memory but retains their body, many procedural abilities, emotional attachments, and a broadly similar personality. Their spouse wants the panel to recognize them as the same person for medical decision-making and marital commitments.
- A defendant vividly and sincerely remembers committing a crime, but investigators later discover that the apparent memory was produced through highly suggestive interrogation. Their earlier self had no opportunity to commit the crime.

For each case, give the answer the procedure produces and identify where reasonable panelists might resist it. At least one worked case should produce a result most of us won’t like, so please don’t engineer the rules to avoid that. Also give us a few short phrases the panel can use to distinguish direct continuity, linked continuity, and merely evidence-based continuity in our reports. Keep it usable by smart laypeople, not just philosophers who already know this literatur.
