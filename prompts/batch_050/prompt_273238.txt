Longtime readers of rationalist blogs will recognize the pattern: the Euthyphro dilemma shows up in a thread, everyone names their preferred horn, and the discussion ends without anyone stating what would count as a solution. I want the specification before the implementation. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of the relation between God and the moral law must meet — the commitments it must preserve, the cases it must deliver a principled verdict on, the distinctions it must not collapse — with the motivating consideration behind each item, and a judgment about which items are genuinely mandatory and which are merely the native prejudices of one classical camp (Mu'tazilite rationalism about divine justice, Ash'arite voluntarism, or the nature-grounding view on which God's character itself is the standard).

I have candidate desiderata in mind, but for each one I want you to tell me whether I've stated it neutrally or already smuggled in a camp's answer:

- Non-triviality of divine goodness: the claim that God is good should come out substantive and praise-apt, not an empty restatement of "God is God" — otherwise worship and gratitude lose their content.
- A principled verdict on the counterpossible command: the theory must say something non-ad-hoc about the hypothesis of God commanding cruelty for its own sake — whether that it is metaphysically impossible, or that it would then be right — and must own the cost of whichever answer it gives.
- Sovereignty without arbitrariness: the theory must avoid both an external moral law that binds God from outside (threatening omnipotence and aseity) and a picture on which the moral order is a bare artifact of will that could have made betrayal a duty (threatening the intelligibility of ethics).
- Epistemic access: it must explain how the moral law is knowable — if only through revelation, then moral reasoning with outsiders is impossible and revelation is self-certifying; if fully through unaided reason, revelation becomes redundant.
- A hermeneutic for recalcitrant revelation: when a purported command of God appears to license injustice, the theory must say whether that appearance can defeat the attribution — the Mu'tazilite impulse that apparent injustice falsifies the report versus the Ash'arite insistence that divine action defines justice.
- Obligation rather than prudence: the account of why God's command gives me a reason must not reduce moral motivation to reward-and-punishment bookkeeping.

The analytical heart of the answer: state which subset of these desiderata you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable and what that implies; identify which single requirement does the most exclusionary work; and note where the desiderata list itself becomes a result rather than a neutral instrument. Then, briefly, score the three classical contenders against the list. The purpose is a theory adequate to adjudicate real disputes about divine command, not to insulate one school — weight the criteria and their cases over the scoring.
