I cover science and ideas for a magazine and I'm working up a piece on how big moral norms actually die, and I keep circling back to an argument I overheard between two historians I interviewed for it. One of them, call her Dana, was defending the view that moral revolutions mostly end through honor, not argument: everyone already knew dueling was irrational and wrong for a century before it stopped, and it only collapsed once it started looking ridiculous rather than noble, same with footbinding. The other, Marcus, pushed back hard. He said the honor story is just the last domino taking credit for the whole line: before honor flipped you had decades of sermons, pamphlets, changing law, shifting economics, and the honor code itself only flipped because those things had already changed what respectable people were embarrassed by. Dana answered that this proves her point, since none of those arguments persuaded anyone while the norm was alive -- people conceded the arguments and kept dueling anyway, so argument was causally inert. Marcus said conceding an argument and being unmoved by it isn't the same as the argument doing nothing, and that by her standard you'd conclude rain doesn't fill reservoirs because the reservoir only overflowed the day the dam gate opened. Dana's last move was that even if arguments soften things up, the actual trigger is always a shift in what's shameful, so honor deserves pride of place. Marcus said 'pride of place' is exactly what's at issue and she hasn't earned it.

I have to adjudicate this in the piece and I'm stuck. Can you go through their exchange move by move, say who's right on each specific point, and then say who's more right overall -- including what, if anything, both of them are missing about how these revolutions actually happen? And if you think the whole framing of their fight is confused -- like if 'what caused it' is the wrong question to ask about a norm collapsing -- please just say that instead of forcing a verdict.
