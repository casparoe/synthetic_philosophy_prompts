I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long-form column on the raven paradox -- the old puzzle that a hypothesis like "all ravens are black" seems, by elementary logic alone, to be confirmed by the sight of a white shoe, since the hypothesis is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens." In discussions with an editor, we converged on the suspicion that the whole puzzle rests on a hidden premise: that confirmation is a matter of logical form between hypothesis and instance, rather than of the process by which the instance came to be observed. To make that point vivid for readers, I want a constructed case that drives a wedge into the standard setup. Acting as a philosopher of science with real expertise in confirmation theory, please construct a thought experiment meeting these specifications: observing a black raven must *lower* a rational agent's credence that all ravens are black (or at minimum leave it unmoved), while observing a non-black non-raven, such as a white shoe, must *raise* it. Constraints: the scenario must be realistic -- a field study, an ornithology department, a museum collection; no demons, no hypnotists, no stipulated credences -- and the reversal must be generated by the observation- or sampling-generating process rather than by eccentric prior beliefs about raven coloration. Think carefully about the likelihoods involved, and brainstorm two or three candidate sampling arrangements before settling on the one you present, so that the final scenario is the version where the effect is cleanest and hardest to dismiss.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specification, and do so honestly: state the hypothesis, the two observations, and the relevant likelihoods under the hypothesis and its negation, so a reader can see the black raven failing to confirm and the white shoe succeeding. Then identify the single structural feature of the case that does the work, and tell me what happens if that feature is removed -- specifically, whether the case collapses back into the standard paradoxical situation in which both instances confirm. Finally, give the strongest objection available to a defender of instance-based confirmation (someone who insists a positive instance of a generalization always confirms it, at least slightly) and indicate whether you think the objection succeeds.

Format your reply as a numbered list of exactly six items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the scenario itself; (2) why the black raven fails to confirm, in likelihood terms; (3) why the white shoe confirms, in likelihood terms; (4) the structural feature doing the work; (5) what removing that feature does to the case; (6) the instance-confirmation defender's best objection and your verdict on it.
