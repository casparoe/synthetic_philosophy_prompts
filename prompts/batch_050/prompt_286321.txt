I recently became engaged, and in the course of drafting my wedding vows I fell down something of a rabbit hole in the philosophy of love — specifically, the debate between those who take love to be a response to the beloved's valuable qualities (an appraisal view, associated with Velleman) and those who take love itself to bestow value on the beloved, such that we love first and find reasons afterwards (a bestowal view, associated with Frankfurt). The puzzle arose quite practically: I found myself wanting to write something like "I love you because you are kind, quick-witted and steady in a crisis", and then realising that this seemed to commit me to something absurd — namely that, were I to meet someone kinder, wittier and steadier, consistency would demand that I transfer my affections accordingly, or at least regret not having met her first.

What I should like is a careful explanation of how the two views each handle, or fail to handle, three specific pressure points. First, the trading-up problem just described: does the appraisal view genuinely entail that love ought to be reallocated whenever a superior instantiation of the loved qualities comes along, or can the appraiser block this conclusion without smuggling in something to which the view is not entitled? Secondly, the problem of change: my fiancée will not be quick-witted for ever, and on the appraisal view it looks as though her ageing would give me reason to love her less, which seems precisely backwards. Thirdly, a problem that appears to cut the other way, against bestowal: if love generates its own reasons rather than responding to them, it becomes unclear how anyone's attachment could ever be criticised as misplaced — yet we plainly do say things like "he does not deserve your devotion" and mean them as more than expressions of taste.

Finally, I have come across Kolodny's suggestion that what grounds love is neither the person's qualities nor an act of bestowal but the relationship itself — the shared history. I should like to know whether this genuinely dissolves the difficulties above or merely relocates them: does a shared history fare any better against trading up, given that one can always ask why this history rather than a richer one I might have built with somebody else? Please explain all of this without assuming familiarity with the literature beyond what I have set out, and, if you can, tell me what it would actually be coherent for me to promise in the vows.
