I want you to analyze a real historical episode through the lens of the doomsday argument, and be honest in both directions: use the theory to read the episode, then use the episode to stress-test the theory.

The episode: in 1969 the astrophysicist J. Richard Gott visited the Berlin Wall, which had been standing for 8 years. He reasoned that, assuming there was nothing special about the timing of his visit, he was probably somewhere in the middle stretch of the wall's total lifespan, and on that basis announced 50% confidence that it would be gone within roughly the next 24 years. It fell in 1989, inside the interval. Gott then applied the same move to humanity itself in his 1993 Nature paper, getting 95% confidence that our species would last somewhere between about 5,100 and 7.8 million more years.

Here's what I want:

1. A careful mapping of the wall case onto the machinery of the doomsday argument. What exactly is the reference class, what assumption is doing the work (self-sampling? the Copernican/mediocrity principle?), and does the fact that Gott *chose* to visit a famous wall -- rather than being dropped at a random moment in its lifespan -- break the "no special time" assumption or not?

2. Gott's version runs on *durations*; the Carter/Leslie version runs on *your birth rank among all humans who ever live*. The wall case can't even be stated in birth-rank terms. So does the wall's fall actually lend any support to the human-extinction version of the argument, or are these two different arguments that share a slogan?

3. The confirmation question. One interval prediction came true -- but it was only a 50% prediction, and a method that issues 50% intervals is *supposed* to be right half the time. So how much, if anything, should a Bayesian update on this single hit? What would a real calibration test of the Copernicus method even look like (Gott apparently tried something like this later with Broadway show runtimes)?

4. A counterfactual twist I haven't seen discussed: suppose the wall were still standing today. Strictly speaking that wouldn't even contradict Gott's prediction, since it was only 50% confidence. So is the Copernicus method unfalsifiable in the way its critics allege, or is there a precise sense in which repeated application *is* testable even though no single application is? And what happens to the whole analysis if you swap in the self-indication assumption instead of self-sampling?

Throughout, please be clear about what's just settled math (the interval arithmetic, the Bayesian updating logic) versus what's genuinely contested philosophy (whether the mediocrity assumption is legit, whether the argument proves anything about extinction), and don't pass off a contested reading as the consensus. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase saying whether, on balance, the Berlin Wall episode counts as evidence for the doomsday argument, against it, or is basically neutral.
