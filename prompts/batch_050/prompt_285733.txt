In the first chapter of Arthur Conan Doyle's The Sign of the Four, Watson shows Holmes his published account of an earlier case, and Holmes responds: "Honestly, I cannot congratulate you upon it. Detection is, or ought to be, an exact science, and should be treated in the same cold and unemotional manner. You have attempted to tinge it with romanticism, which produces much the same effect as if you worked a love-story or an elopement into the fifth proposition of Euclid."

Write the first official memorandum, about 600 words, of the Department of Continuity, established in 1892 by the London publishers of Dr. Watson's narratives in response to complaints like Holmes's. The department's remit is to verify the accuracy of the accounts and adjudicate readers' corrections, and its inaugural circular should lay down policy on at least: the Jezail bullet which struck Watson's shoulder in A Study in Scarlet but which he nurses in his leg in The Sign of the Four; the report, in "The Man with the Twisted Lip," that Mrs. Watson calls her husband James though his name is John; and what the department may certify as fact on matters the narratives nowhere record, such as how many times Watson has been married. The memo must sustain a perfect Victorian-editorial deadpan and must never state that Holmes does not exist; yet every policy it adopts should quietly betray that the department's only evidence is the corpus itself -- that no letter of inquiry to Baker Street could settle anything, that two contradictory reports cannot both be right though neither can be checked against the man, and that on unrecorded matters there is nothing to be right about. The founding grievance, a complaint about his own portrayal lodged by a man whose existence the department cannot establish and whose authority over the text it cannot assess, should hang over the entire document. The humour must land, without ever naming them, the problems of empty reference, of truth and inconsistency in fiction, and of fictional indeterminacy. Begin with the memorandum itself, with no preface or commentary.
