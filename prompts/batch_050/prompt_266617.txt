For a graduate reading group in philosophy of physics — twelve to fourteen participants, one 75-minute session — design a structured activity that makes the probability problem in the Everett (many-worlds) interpretation of quantum mechanics felt in the body rather than merely stated on a whiteboard. The group has read a survey of the Born-rule problem and knows the vocabulary, but most of them still privately feel the objection must be a confusion — surely something has to be more likely, even if every outcome occurs on some branch. I want an activity that breaks that complacency.

The core experience I am after is this: participants should first be placed in a situation where branching is simulated literally (tokens, rooms, or roles multiplying according to some rule the facilitator controls), and where they are asked to state what they expect to happen to them or how they would bet. Naively they will count copies — weighting outcomes by how many instantiations of them exist. The activity should then be engineered so that this counting rule fails in a way they can see: for instance, by making the number of instantiations a matter of the facilitator's representational choices rather than of anything in the physics of the situation, so that counting gives different answers depending on how finely one chooses to individuate branches, while nothing that any participant could observe or care about has changed. Ideally the payoff structure should force them to discover that their betting behavior and their multiplicity bookkeeping come apart.

Please provide: the full rules and setup, including any materials and a payoff scheme that does not involve real money (chits, course points, or privileges are fine); a timeline for the 75 minutes; the exact moment at which the counting rule is meant to collapse, and what the facilitator should do if participants patch it with an ad hoc fix (they will); and a debrief of about twenty minutes that maps each stage of the activity onto the actual structure of the debate — where branch-counting corresponds to naive Everettian frequentism, where the facilitator's freedom in individuating branches corresponds to the arbitrariness objections, and how the decision-theoretic defenses (deriving rational credence from constraints on rational preference rather than from counting) and the self-locating-uncertainty proposals would each describe what the participants were actually doing when they bet.

Finally, be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce. In real quantum mechanics the amplitudes are not arbitrary annotations on the branches; they govern interference, which has no analogue in a room full of tokens. Tell me which feature of the problem the game therefore flattens, and what I should say in the debrief to keep participants from concluding that the Born rule is merely conventional.
