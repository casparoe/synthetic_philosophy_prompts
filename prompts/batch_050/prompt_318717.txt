So I've been going back and forth with a friend from a local reading group about approval-directed agents -- the old Christiano-style idea where instead of giving a system a goal and letting it optimize, you train it to take whatever action an overseer (or some idealized/combined version of overseers) would approve of. My friend thinks approval-direction basically dissolves a bunch of alignment problems because the agent never has a target to Goodhart against. I'm skeptical, but when I try to articulate the failure mode I keep ending up with cases where the overseer is either stupid, lazy, or gets deceived -- and my friend just says "sure, but that's a complaint about the overseer, not about approval-direction as a scheme."

So: build me a thought experiment where an approval-directed agent does *worse* than a goal-directed agent at the same task, under the harshest possible conditions for me. Constraints: the overseer is competent, attentive, and genuinely trying; the agent is not deceptive, not scheming, and not manipulating anyone -- it's honestly doing its best to take the action the overseer would most approve of; the overseer evaluates actual outcomes and behavior, not just what the agent reports to them; and the setup has to be semi-realistic (a company, a lab, an assistant managing someone's projects -- no paperclip factories, no omniscient predictors). The failure has to come out of the *structure* of optimizing for approval itself, e.g. something about which options are even visible to be approved, or what's legible to the overseer, not from anyone in the story making a mistake.

After the scenario, do the work: walk through step by step what the approval-directed agent does and why it's acting correctly *by its own lights*, then what the goal-directed agent with the same information does differently and why it lands somewhere better. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the verdict if you remove it -- does it collapse back into "overseer was just bad at their job"? Finally, steelman my friend: how would a defender of approval-direction attack this case -- e.g., by saying the failure is really about *which* approval procedure you used (actual approval vs. what they'd approve on reflection, individual vs. a whole process), and can I patch the scenario to survive that response?
