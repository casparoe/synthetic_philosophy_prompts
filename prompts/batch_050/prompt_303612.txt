Okay so I'm the best man at my best friend's wedding next month and I need a toast, and I want to do something a bit ambitious with it. The bride did her PhD on the Kripkenstein rule-following stuff — the whole plus/quus thing, where no finite stretch of someone's past behavior settles which rule they were actually following, since for all the evidence shows they might have been doing quus all along (quus works exactly like addition until some cutoff, then starts answering 5). The groom is a mathematician, and they actually met because he sat in on her rule-following seminar and objected, out loud, in week one, that quus is a perfectly well-defined function and the paradox is "a sociology problem, not a math problem." Roughly half the guests are philosophers who will catch every reference and grade me silently; the other half are both families, who I beleive think Wittgenstein is a physicist.

Here's the idea I want the toast built around: apply the skeptical paradox to the vows themselves. Ten years of relationship is, evidentially speaking, a finite table of past uses — it no more determines what "forever" means for these two than a finite list of sums determines plus over quus. For all the past shows, what they've been practicing could be quove (works exactly like love until the first Monday after the wedding, then becomes mild indifference toward a coworker). But — and this is the move that has to land — the paradox's actual lesson is that this underdetermination was never the threat it looks like. Meaning was never stored in some hidden fact that guarantees the future; it lives in the practice of going on together, and a vow is precisely a commitment to keep going on, made with no proof that "going on" is well-defined. I want the room to end up feeling that you can't derive a marriage from the evidence is the most romantic sentence in the English language, not the scariest. Ideally there's a callback to his week-one objection — something like: he spent ten years proving that the correct response to the paradox is to marry it.

You're a professional speechwriter who also happens to know the rule-following literature cold — think carefully first about what makes a philosophical joke land for grandmas and philosophers at the same time, then give me the toast as exactly five numbered beats, each two or three sentences, with no preamble or outro beyond the beats themselves. Beat 1 has to define quove in plain language a tipsy aunt can follow (no "skeptical paradox," no "epistemology," no "underdetermination" spoken out loud anywhere in the toast). The last beat should give me a real, short line to raise the glass on. Total spoken length around four minutes.
