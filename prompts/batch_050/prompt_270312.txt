I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long piece about consumer genetic testing and how the industry talks about risk. Everyone in this world -- the testing companies, the press releases, honestly most of my own drafts -- leans on the same analogy: your DNA is a code, or a program, or the instruction manual for you, and getting sequenced is like finally getting to read it. The marketing straight-up implies that once your genome is "decoded," the traits basically follow. I caught myself writing that a variant "codes for" a higher chance of some disease, which is exactly the kind of sentence my editor loves and exactly the kind I'm now suspicious of.

A molecular biologist I interviewed pushed back and said the code metaphor is fine up to a point but journalists stretch it way past its breaking point. She said something like: a code needs a reader, and in the cell the reader is itself made out of the thing being read. I probably mangled that, but it stuck with me.

So: how far does the DNA-is-a-code analogy actually hold? Give me the mapping first -- what plays the role of the alphabet, the words, the grammar, the reader, the message, the meaning -- and then where it breaks. A few specific worries I've collected from reporting:

- In a real code, the pairing of symbol to meaning is arbitrary and set by convention, and somebody designed the lookup table. Is the genetic code like that, and does it matter that nobody designed it?
- A program or instruction manual implies that if you just execute the instructions you get the output, but the same genome in different environments gives you different organisms.
- "Information" in the engineering sense doesn't care about meaning, but every journalistic use of the metaphor is about meaning. Is that a slide the metaphor lets people make without noticing?
- I've heard that a huge chunk of what the genome does is regulatory, more like punctuation and volume knobs than sentences. Does that fit the code picture or strain it?

Then the part my piece actually hinges on: does the deterministic conclusion the companies trade on -- spit in a tube, learn what you're made of, maybe learn your future -- survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the metaphor carrying that conclusion the whole time? And if the metaphor is doing too much work, what's a better one I could responsibly put in a lede? I've heard recipe, I've heard jazz score, someone once told me ecosystem. Tell me which you'd actually want a journalist to use, or whether the right move is to quit analogizing altogether.

Please answer in American English, and don't worry about pointing me to sources -- I want the thinking, not a bibliography.
