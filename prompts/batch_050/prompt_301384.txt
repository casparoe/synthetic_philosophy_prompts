A guy in my moral philosophy reading group cornered me after our last session with an argument about future generations, and four of us have been fighting about it in the group chat for a week now. The conclusion is obviously unhinged but nobody has managed to say exactly which step does the damage, and he's annoyingly good at patching every objection we throw at him. Here it is, cleaned up:

(1) A choice is wrong only if it's worse for someone than the alternatives — there has to be at least one person who ends up worse off than they would have been had you chosen differently. No victim, no wrong.
(2) Which particular person gets conceived is hyper-sensitive to circumstances: had the parents done anything at all differently in the weeks before conception — caught a different train, stayed at the party one more hour — a different child, or no child, would have been conceived.
(3) Any large-scale choice (an energy policy, a tax code, a war) shifts the circumstances under which thousands of people meet and conceive, so within a couple of generations the population that exists under option A is almost entirely different *individuals* from the population that would have existed under option B.
(4) So take a choice between Dirty Grid (cheap now, future people inherit a degraded climate but still have lives clearly worth living) and Clean Grid (painful sacrifices now, future people flourish). By (3), nobody who lives under Dirty Grid would have existed under Clean Grid. So Dirty Grid isn't worse for any of them than the alternative — for each of them the alternative was never existing at all, which isn't better than a life worth living.
(5) So by (1), choosing Dirty Grid isn't wrong. And this isn't just about policy: by (2) and (3), *everything* any of us does ripples forward and changes who gets conceived, so nothing we do now can wrong future generations, provided we leave them lives worth living.
(6) Worse, run it backwards: every living person's existence depends on a long causal chain that includes past catastrophes. Had some disaster a century ago been averted, different people would exist today. So the disaster wasn't worse for anyone alive now — it was a precondition of their existence — so nobody currently alive can coherently wish it hadn't happened.

His conclusion is that basically all of intergenerational ethics is void, and our intuitions about what we owe the future are just confusion. When we said "but the victims of the historical disaster were wronged," he happily agreed and said the argument only covers effects on people not yet conceived, which is exactly the part he wants. When we attacked (1), he said fine, but then you owe an explanation of why it isn't wrong to *fail to create* happy people, and we're all monstrously wronging merely possible people every day by not maximizing the population, which is at least as absurd as his conclusion. When someone attacked (2), he asked whether we seriously think the same child would have been born if the parents had conceived two months later. Our TA mumbled something about "non-identity" but the cases in our readings are all one-off choices about a single potential child, and this argument seems to prove enormously more than those.

So: which step do I actually reject? I'd *like* to keep (1), since the alternative seems to saddle me with duties to people who will never exist, and I obviously want to deny (5) and (6). But I want the diagnosis, not just a preference — where exactly does the reasoning slip (or if it's valid, which premise is cheapest to drop), why did that step look innocent, and what does each available exit cost? In particular I'd like to know whether something funny is going on with "worse for *someone*" — whether the victim has to be an identifiable particular person, or whether we could wrong whoever-ends-up-living under a description even when it's different people in each scenario — and whether (2) is smuggling in an overly fragile picture of what makes a person the person they are.
