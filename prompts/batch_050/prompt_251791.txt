I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I have been asked to give the after-dinner speech at the annual dinner of a learned society for the philosophy of science. This year's programme has been organised around Max Black's 1952 dialogue on the identity of indiscernibles — the thought experiment in which the universe contains nothing but two exactly similar spheres, alike in every intrinsic and relational respect, and the question is whether there are really two spheres or only one. Roughly half the room will be metaphysicians who know the dialogue inside out, including the moves involving Kant's incongruent counterparts and the distinction between qualitative and numerical identity. The other half will be spouses, editors and administrators, who have never heard of any of it.

I would like the speech (around eight minutes when read aloud) to be built on a conceit that does genuine structural work rather than serving as decoration. My suggestion, which you may refine but not abandon, is this: the metaphysician's puzzle is whether two perfectly indiscernible things can be two; the reporter's working faith is the mirror image — that no two events are ever truly indiscernible, and that the whole craft of journalism consists in finding the fact that tells them apart. The speech should then turn the screw, because journalism has its own two-spheres problem: the wire copy. When two hundred newspapers run the identical agency story, word for word, has the public read one story or two hundred? If the answer is one, does that vindicate Leibniz against Black, or does it show that Black's spheres win after all — and what should a profession that trades in the difference between one thing and two make of either outcome?

The speech must land differently, but successfully, for each half of the room: the metaphysicians should hear positions they recognise being handled accurately (no conflating qualitative with numerical identity, no treating the dialogue as a mere curiosity), while everyone else should follow every step without prior knowledge. It should be warm and after-dinner in register, not a lecture; it should contain exactly one good joke that turns on the actual content of the thought experiment; and it should end by drawing a moral about my trade that Black's dialogue genuinely licenses, rather than one I have simply stapled to it. Please write the speech.
