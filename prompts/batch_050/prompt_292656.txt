I am drafting a statement of position for a workshop on population ethics, and before circulating it I want to know whether my commitments actually hang together. Here is what I currently hold:

1. The relation "all things considered better than," applied to possible populations, is transitive and complete.
2. Small losses in average welfare can always be compensated for by sufficiently large gains in the number of people living lives worth living. Quality and quantity trade off against one another; neither enjoys lexical priority.
3. The repugnant conclusion is false. A very large population of people whose lives are barely worth living is worse than a much smaller population of people at a very high level of welfare.
4. The procreative asymmetry holds: we have strong moral reason not to bring into existence people whose lives would be not worth living, but we have no moral reason whatsoever to bring into existence people whose lives would be worth living, however good those lives would be.
5. Parfit-style non-identity cases admit of genuine moral verdicts: choosing the depletion policy over the conservation policy is worse, full stop, even though no one who exists in the depleted world would have existed had we chosen otherwise.

My questions are these. Which of these commitments genuinely conflict, and how severely? Please rank the tensions by seriousness rather than treating every pair as equally problematic. For each real tension, state precisely which of the numbered commitments generate it and what the least costly revision would be. For any pair that only appears to conflict, show why the appearance is misleading. I am not asking for a survey of population axiology; I am asking for an audit of this particular set, concluding with the single revision that would do the most to restore consistency and what I would lose by making it.
