So I'm on the conduct committee of a mid-sized community organization (rationalist-adjacent, coworking space plus workshops and events, a few hundred members) and we have to vote on a new misconduct policy next month. Backstory: over the past year we had a handful of members repeatedly trashing the norms — nothing illegal, stuff like flaking on commitments people were explicitly relying on them for, sniping at volunteers until they quit, one guy who apparently told three different versions of the same dispute to three different people. Our current process is entirely private: quiet conversations, warnings, eventually asking someone to leave. It mostly hasn't worked, because the repeat offenders just wait out the attention span of whoever's currently annoyed at them.

So now there's a proposal to move to public accountability: when the commitee finds someone violated the norms, we publish a short writeup naming them and describing what happened. The argument for it is basically that private processes have no teeth, and that reputational consequences are the only real enforcement mechanism in a community without contracts. The argument against (from one of my fellow committee members) is that this amounts to deliberately engineering shame as a punishment, that shame is corrosive — it makes people defensive or drives them out entirely instead of changing behavior — and that once you normalize it as a tool it gets weaponized against anyone unpopular.

I've read enough of the psych/anthropology-adjacent stuff on this over the years (the standard guilt vs. shame distinction — guilt as "I did a bad thing," shame as "I am bad"; the whole guilt-culture vs. shame-culture framing) to suspect both sides are half right, but I keep going in circles on it. Can you draft the memo I'm supposed to bring to the committee? What I need:

1. A clear bottom line: adopt the public-writeup policy, reject it, or adopt a modified version with conditions — and if modified, say exactly which conditions and why each one is load-bearing rather than decorative.
2. Actually engage with the guilt/shame question instead of treating it as window dressing. If shame-as-sanction really is as bad as the literature suggests, does that doom the proposal, or is there real work being done by the distinction between "shaming a person" and "making conduct legible to the community"? I genuinely can't tell if that distinction is a crux or a cop-out.
3. Take a position on whether an institution can even choose its moral-emotional regime in the first place, or whether you just inherit whatever the surrounding culture hands you and design around it.
4. Steelman the losing side properly, since I have to present this to people who hold it.

Also, because I'll be defending this in a room full of extremely argumentative people: say how confident you are in the main recommendation, and flag the single point in the memo you're least sure of, so I know where to expect the pushback to land.
