ok so my roommate hit me with this at like 1am and I have not been able to shake it. We were arguing about whether you can trust film critics and he escalated way past what I was ready for:

(1) Aesthetic properties are response-dependent. A film is moving because it's disposed to move a suitable audience; a joke is funny because it's disposed to amuse. Strip away how the thing lands on people and there's no fact of the matter left about whether it's moving or funny.

(2) How something lands on a person is something that person can only know by having the response. You have to actually be moved, amused, gripped, whatever. Nobody else can have your response for you or check it from the outside.

(3) So when a critic tells me the film is devastating, all their testimony can really report is how it landed on them, or on audiences they know about. It's evidence about other people's responses, not about the response-dependent fact insofar as that fact concerns me.

(4) Testimony that only informs you about other people's responses can't ground your own knowledge of the response-dependent property. So I can't know the film is devastating on the critic's say-so.

(5) But nothing in (1)–(4) is specific to art. Funniness, deliciousness, scariness, charm — all response-dependent in exactly the same way.

(6) So nobody has ever known that a restaurant is good, a joke is funny, or a horror movie is scary on the basis of reviews and reccomendations. Every Yelp-informed reservation, every comedy special you watched because a friend swore by it, all of it is epistemically groundless.

He's extremely pleased with himself and I hate it. (6) can't be right — I knew my favorite restaurant was good before I ever ate there, because three people whose taste I trust told me so, and I was right. But I also want to keep something like (1): the idea that beauty or funniness is out there in the object totally independent of us seems wrong to me.

So: which step actually breaks? I want a commited answer here, not a menu of options where I'm told to pick whichever feels right. Name the specific step where the argument goes off the rails, explain why that step looked fine when he walked me through it, and tell me honestly what I have to give up to escape the conclusion — if killing (6) costs me (1), say so and defend the trade.
