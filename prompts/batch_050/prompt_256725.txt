I am a second-year student in a seminar that pairs ancient theories of the good life with contemporary theories of well-being, and I am writing my term paper on whether Epicurus is best read as an early tranquilist. For context: tranquilism is a recent view in axiology holding that what makes an experience good or bad is exhausted by the presence or absence of craving — a state of intense pleasure accompanied by no felt lack is, on this view, no better than a perfectly neutral state, because in neither is anything missing. The flashpoint in my paper is this passage from Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus, which my seminar split over last week: "For the end of all our actions is to be free from pain and fear, and, when once we have attained all this, the tempest of the soul is laid; seeing that the living creature has no need to go in search of something that is lacking, nor to look for anything else by which the good of the soul and of the body will be fulfilled. When we are pained because of the absence of pleasure, then, and then only, do we feel the need of pleasure."

Half the class read this as the ceiling reading: once pain and disturbance are gone, the good is complete, and any further pleasure merely varies it without increasing it — which would make Epicurus a proto-tranquilist. The other half read it as the platform reading: freedom from pain is just the stable base from which positive enjoyments (friendship, conversation, bread and cheese) do real additive work, and the passage only says we shouldn't go chasing luxuries. Please adjudicate this. Specifically, I would like you to (a) explain what in the wording of the passage supports each reading, paying close attention to the claim that we feel the need of pleasure only when pained by its absence; (b) explain precisely how tranquilism differs from the view it is usually confused with, negative utilitarianism, since one trades on craving and the other on suffering, and show which of the two the ceiling reading would actually commit Epicurus to; and (c) state the strongest objection to each position as applied here — for tranquilism, the implication that a life of vivid but uncraved delight is no better than a blank one; for the platform reading, the question of why Epicurus insists there is nothing lacking once the tempest is laid.

I am not asking which reading is historically correct in some final sense — I suspect the text underdetermines that — but I want the interpretive and conceptual options laid out rigorously enough that my paper can take a position rather than just report the dispute. Please write for someone who has read the primary text and the basic secondary literature but keeps conflating absence of suffering with absence of craving, since that conflation is exactly what I am trying to stop doing.
