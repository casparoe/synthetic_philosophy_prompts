I am a novelist working on a legal drama set in the near future, and I want the plot's central trial to rest on a philosophically accurate tension rather than on Hollywood logic. The scenario is this: an autonomous delivery vehicle, operating exactly as designed and within its training data, misclassifies a pedestrian in an unusual shadow pattern and kills her. As I have researched how such cases are actually handled, I have noticed an asymmetry I cannot make sense of. When a human driver makes a comparable perceptual error, she may face criminal prosecution for negligent homicide, and the question of her culpability is treated as a live one. But when the vehicle's decision-making causes the death, responsibility in practice flows almost entirely into civil channels: the manufacturer settles a products-liability claim, insurers pay out, regulators perhaps demand a software patch, and no engineer, executive, or fleet operator is criminally charged -- not because anyone has established that no one was at fault, but because no single human's contribution seems to meet the bar for criminal culpability, and the machine itself is treated as a thing to be compensated for, not an agent to be blamed. Meanwhile, in other domains the same legal culture insists on keeping a human nominally in the loop -- military targeting, medical diagnosis -- as though a human signature could absorb responsibility even when the human exercises no real discretion. Proposals are occasionally floated to grant sufficiently autonomous systems some form of legal personhood precisely so someone can be held to account, and these are generally dismissed as absurd.

What I want to know is what this whole patchwork of practices presupposes about responsibility. Does the reflexive routing of machine-caused harm into civil rather than criminal liability presuppose that blame requires something machines lack -- consciousness, intention, an inner life -- or merely that punishment is pointless where deterrence and desert find no purchase, which is a different and more consequentialist commitment? Does the insistence on a human in the loop presuppose that responsibility can be conferred by title rather than by actual control, and is that consistent with the same system's refusal, in the autonomous vehicle case, to hold engineers criminally liable for decisions they collectively made? Reconstruct the implicit theory of responsibility that would make these practices jointly coherent, if any does; say whether the practices strictly presuppose that theory or are merely compatible with several rivals; and tell me what would have to change in the handling of my fictional case if the law instead took seriously the possibility that some harms are genuinely no one's fault -- that a responsibility gap exists and no doctrine should be bent to close it.

Please answer as a philosopher of technology with a firm grounding in legal theory, and think carefully about the distinction between what a practice presupposes and what it merely happens to fit. I do not want a survey of positions; I want you to commit: name the single implicit theory you judge the best reconstruction, defend it against the strongest rival, and take a definite stand on whether the practices are mutually consistent. Keep the entire response under 500 words.
