THE EXERCISE I SET (40 points: part a = 10, part b = 10, part c = 8, part d = 12)

In large markets, individual consumers seem unable to affect anything. Consider what I called the Batch Problem. A regional distributor supplies frozen chicken to several hundred stores. It orders from producers in batches of 10,000 birds, four times a year, based on total sales in the previous quarter. No individual purchase is registered as such; each is an anonymous increment to a sales figure in the millions. A skeptic argues: since no single purchase will ever change the size of any order, the consumer who buys a chicken causes no additional harm, and so does nothing wrong — at least as far as consequences are concerned.

(a) State the skeptic's reasoning as precisely as you can — call it the inefficacy objection — and explain why it generalizes beyond this case.
(b) Evaluate the expected-contribution reply: the claim that although any one purchase is very unlikely to change an order, the magnitude of the change when a threshold is crossed is correspondingly large, so each purchase carries an expected share of the harm. Does the reply succeed, and does it depend on the threshold's location being unknown or variable from the consumer's point of view?
(c) Some claim the same structure arises in large elections, so that whatever vindicates the consumer vindicates the non-voter, or vice versa. Assess whether expected-contribution reasoning transfers between the market case and the voting case, and whether any structural difference between them blocks the transfer.
(d) State and defend your own conclusion: does the inefficacy objection succeed in the Batch Problem? If not, what exactly is wrong with the individual purchase?

THE MEMBER'S ANSWER

(a) The inefficacy objection holds that where an outcome results from the aggregated actions of a vast number of people, the causal contribution of any one participant is so small as to be effectively zero, and an action that causes nothing cannot be wrong on consequentialist grounds. The objection generalizes because the same pattern recurs wherever effects are massively overdetermined or threshold-governed: one vote in a national election, one driver's emissions, one diner's purchase of meat. Derek Parfit's case of the harmless torturers is the classic illustration. Each of a thousand torturers performs an act that makes no perceptible difference to any victim, while together they inflict severe pain. Parfit's point, as I understand it, is that since no individual act makes a perceptible difference, none of the torturers does anything wrong considered individually; the wrong resides only in the collective pattern. That the objection survives even in this stark case shows it has real force and cannot be dismissed as a mere excuse for inaction.

(b) The expected-contribution reply grants the premise but denies the conclusion. My chance of being the purchase that triggers a reorder of 10,000 birds is roughly one in 10,000 — vanishingly small. The expected harm of my purchase is therefore a tiny fraction of a chicken's worth of harm: small, though admittedly not exactly zero. The reply thus weakens the objection but does not really answer it; it shows the harm is not literally zero, but it remains negligible for any practical purpose. The reply does appear to depend on the threshold being unknown to me: if I somehow knew the current batch sat far from its reorder point, my purchase would be plainly idle, and if I knew it sat one short, my purchase would carry enormous weight — but consumers are never in either epistemic position, so the average applies.

(c) The same structure clearly arises in elections. One vote among millions is either pivotal or idle, and almost always idle. Since expected-contribution reasoning vindicates the consumer, it vindicates the voter in exactly the same way: a tiny probability of decisiveness paired with enormous stakes. There is no relevant structural difference between the cases; in both, my act matters only at a threshold whose crossing I cannot foresee, and the same arithmetic governs each.

(d) My conclusion is that the inefficacy objection substantially succeeds. The expected-contribution reply shows only that each purchase carries a minuscule expected harm, and minuscule harms cannot ground serious duties. What is required of individuals is therefore not changes in personal consumption but support for collective and political remedies, since only coordinated action operates at the scale where real differences are made.
