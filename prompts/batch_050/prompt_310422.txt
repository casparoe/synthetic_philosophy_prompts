In my seminar on the ethics of historical memory, an argument broke out after class between two classmates, Elena and Marcus, and it has stayed with me because I genuinely cannot settle it. It was about whether the "man of his time" defence works for Thomas Jefferson.

Elena's position was that the defence is basically sound. Moral norms are largely absorbed from one's social world, she said, so expecting Jefferson to have freed his slaves and reordered his life accordingly is expecting him to have been morally abnormal — and we cannot make moral abnormality the minimum condition of decency.

Marcus replied that the defence only has force for people who lacked the relevant moral concepts, and Jefferson plainly had them. He described slavery as a grave injustice, understood enslaved people as rights-bearers, and anticipated that the practice would be judged harshly. The man-of-his-time defence excuses ignorance, Marcus said, not weakness of will; knowing better and failing to act is a different and worse failure, not the same one.

Elena pushed back on two grounds. First, knowing a norm is not the same as having a live practical option: Jefferson was deeply in debt, his household and political standing rested on slave labour, and the contemporaries who did free their slaves — Washington only at death — show how exceptional acting on the belief actually was. The defence, she insisted, is about what can fairly be demanded of someone in action, not about what they believed. Second, judging historical figures against the moral frontier of their own era condemns virtually everyone, including us: most of us eat factory-farmed meat while knowing the arguments against it. A standard on which everyone in history fails is a standard that distinguishes nothing and so does no work.

Marcus answered that the comparison to ourselves cuts the other way — we should simply accept that we are condemnable too. His stronger point, though, was that the defence is applied asymmetrically. When Jefferson is praised for the ideals of the Declaration, nobody says the credit really belongs to his era; the credit is treated as personal while the blame is outsourced to the period. Either the times explain the person, he said, or they do not.

Elena granted the asymmetry charge was clever but denied it lands: blame requires that a reasonable alternative was available, whereas credit requires only that the achievement was real and difficult. Much credit is simply credit for seeing something first, and that is compatible with the era explaining why others did not see it.

Marcus closed with a move I found harder to place. He said the whole dispute mistakes what judging the dead is for. The real question is not whether Jefferson, the man, merits our anger, but what we should honour in public space now — and that is a question about our present commitments, on which the man-of-his-time defence is simply irrelevant in either direction.

Could you adjudicate this properly? Tell me who is right about each specific point — the ignorance-versus-weakness distinction, the demandability of action, the condemn-everyone objection, the credit/blame asymmetry, and Marcus's final reframing — and give me a definite verdict on who is more right overall. Please commit to positions with reasons rather than laying out both sides and leaving me to choose, and if both are missing something, say plainly what it is.
