I'm teaching an undergraduate epistemology seminar this term, and a long-running disagreement with a colleague has pushed me to commit to a reading of Hume that I now want stress-tested before I put it in lecture notes. In Part I of Section X, "Of Miracles," of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Hume writes:

"All probability, then, supposes an opposition of experiments and observations, where the one side is found to overbalance the other, and to produce a degree of evidence, proportioned to the superiority. A hundred instances or experiments on one side, and fifty on another, afford a doubtful expectation of any event; though a hundred uniform experiments, with only one that is contradictory, reasonably beget a pretty strong degree of assurance. In all cases, we must balance the opposite experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the smaller number from the greater, in order to know the exact force of the superior evidence."

My position: this arithmetic of balancing is not a loose metaphor but the load-bearing structure of Hume's argument, and it delivers the strong, a priori conclusion he advertises when he calls his argument an "everlasting check" on superstition. On my reading, testimony derives its force from our experience of the conformity between reports and facts, which is always less than uniform, since people lie, err, and exaggerate; whereas the experience establishing a law of nature, in any case where a genuine miracle is at issue, is by hypothesis unbroken — a full proof. Deducting a merely probable counter-evidence from a full proof can at best bring the scales to zero, never past it, so no accumulation of testimony, however vast, could make belief in a miracle rational. I therefore take the standard move of reconstructing Hume in Bayesian terms and showing that sufficiently extreme likelihood ratios can swamp any finite prior to be a refutation of a different, weaker argument than the one Hume actually gave. What would count against my reading?

I would like the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and they should include the internal textual evidence: what to make of Hume's own later concession that he would accept universal and concordant testimony to eight days of total darkness over the whole earth, which on the arithmetic reading he seemingly ought to reject; whether the four empirical considerations of Part II sit awkwardly with an argument that was advertised as decisive in advance of any facts about how testimony actually behaves; and whether the passage's reliance on counting experiments — a hundred to fifty, a hundred to one — survives once independence is taken seriously, given that Hume himself treats concurring independent witnesses as compounding rather than merely adding. But I also want the conceptual and formal defeaters: the charge that the strong reading secures its conclusion only by defining laws of nature as exceptionless, thereby converting an evidential argument into a definitional truth; the question of whether "deduct the smaller number from the greater" is a defensible proto-statistics or an outright error no charity can repair; and an honest verdict on whether my reading has insulated itself, since any well-attested anomaly can always be re-described as showing that the law was misformulated rather than violated — and whether that immunity is a flaw in the reading or simply what a norm of belief was bound to look like. Where the defeaters diverge in force, say which of them a defender of the strong reading would herself concede as counting against it, and which she would dismiss as presupposing the Bayesian recasting I reject.
