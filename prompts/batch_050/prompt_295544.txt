I'm a PhD student in legal philosophy, and next month I'm presenting a paper at our jurisprudence workshop titled "Selective Legal Moralism." The paper tries to rehabilitate Devlin's side of the Hart–Devlin debate, but with a twist: I concede that Devlin's "social disintegration" argument fails as an empirical claim about society literally falling apart, and argue instead that a community may enforce through criminal law only those moral norms that are constitutive of ongoing cooperation itself — norms against perjury, corruption, and breach of trust, for instance, but not norms governing private sexual conduct. My claim is that this selective version escapes Hart's standard objections while preserving what was actually defensible in Devlin.

The audience will be mostly Hartian liberals and legal positivists — people who treat the debate as settled since the Wolfenden Report era — plus one or two legal moralists and a skeptical legal anthropologist who thinks both Hart and Devlin were parochially British.

Acting as an expert in philosophy of law who has sat through many such workshops, please prepare me for the question period. Think carefully, and begin by briefly brainstorming the full range of likely challenges before organizing them. Then give me: (1) the objections I'm most likely to face, ordered by likelihood and how damaging they'd be, phrased in the vocabulary each kind of audience member would actually use; (2) for each, a short reply I can give in under a minute, with a note on where that reply is weakest; (3) what I should concede up front to lower the temperature; and (4) the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks — which I suspect is whether my "constitutive of cooperation" criterion smuggles in a covert harm principle and thereby collapses my view back into Hart's — together with the best available answer to it.
