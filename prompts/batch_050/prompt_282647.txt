I TA for an intro philosophy of language course and the professor has asked me to put together a handout for the week we do Wittgenstein. The students always walk in assuming that every word must have a strict definition -- a neat list of necessary and sufficient conditions -- and then they hit the games example and get confused, and half of them leave thinking family resemblance just means 'definitions are hard, whatever.' What I want is a decision tree (or equivalently a taxonomy drawn as a tree) that sorts positions on the question: what unifies the things a general term applies to? I'd like the tree built from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names -- so start with the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then place the actual positions in that space.

The leaves should include at least: the classical view (real definitions in terms of necessary and sufficient conditions), Wittgenstein's family resemblance, cluster-concept views, prototype theory, exemplar-based views, the meaning-as-use / use-theory option, natural-kind essentialism as a view about some words but not others, and a deflationary 'nothing unifies them and that's fine' option. Each branch should be a concrete question a student could answer without knowing any of these labels -- e.g. cases like: can you define 'game' in a way that covers chess, ring-a-ring-a-roses, professional esports, and Calvinball; does 'gold' work differently from 'game'; if a Martian showed up with an activity that's clearly a game but shares no feature with all our games, would that change your view; does it matter whether competent speakers could state the definition or just reliably sort cases? Questions like that -- not 'do you believe in essences?' Please note any combination of answers that no named position occupies and say briefly why you think the slot is empty (incoherent? unattractive? just unexplored?). And flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate -- I suspect prototype and exemplar views will be a problem here, and maybe family resemblance vs. cluster concepts too, but tell me rather than papering over it.

Aim for a tree with maybe 6-10 branch questions, and for each leaf give one sentence on the most awkward thing that position is committed to -- like the classical view owing us a definition of 'art', or family resemblance threatening to dissolve the difference between a concept's boundary being fuzzy and there being no boundary at all. This is going on a two-page handout for first-years, so keep the jargon minimal but don't dumb down the distinctions themselves.
