So I'm a backend engineer and last week we were doing capacity planning for a launch, and it turned into a low-key argument with our finance guy. Our whole approach in infra is basicly: figure out the worst plausible case (traffic spike 10x-ing, a region going down, a dependency failing at the worst moment) and make sure the system survives it, even if the headroom costs money we'd probably never recoup on expected value. Nobody computes probability times loss — we honestly don't even have probabilities for a datacenter fire. Meanwhile the same company prices literally everything else on expected value: insurance, ad spend, even whether to ship a feature comes down to an A/B test about averages. Then I mentioned this to a friend who's a civil engineer and she said building codes work exactly the same way — safety factors everywhere, design for the worst credible earthquake, not the average one. But the same governments that demand that also run cost-benefit analyses on environmental regulation where all the harms get averaged out. So the same society is worst-case in one room and expected-value in the next.

My question is what the worst-case practice actually presupposes, philosophically. I went down a bit of a rabbit hole and it looks like there are formal versions of this — maximin (pick the option whose worst possible outcome is least bad), minimax regret — and people say these only really make sense "under ignorance," i.e. when you can't assign probabilities at all. So does the engineering/building-code practice presuppose that we genuinely have no probabilities in these cases? Or is it really a different commitment — that certain outcomes (the bridge collapses, the platform dies) are so bad that no probability discount makes them acceptable? Those seem like pretty different views and I can't tell which one the practice is commited to, or whether it even needs to pick.

And then there's the part that made me suspicious the rabbit hole goes deeper: apparently there's this whole Rawls vs. Harsanyi fight with the same shape. Rawls's difference principle gets defended with maximin-flavored reasoning from behind the veil of ignorance, and Harsanyi argued that if you don't know who you'll end up being, you should just maximize expected utility assuming you're equally likely to be anyone. So: is worst-case engineering secretly Rawlsian, and is Harsanyi's move available there too (just assign a probability to the disaster, however rough, and average)? Or is maximin in practice really just a heuristic for fat tails and model uncertainty, not a philosophical position at all? Reconstruct the implicit decision theory of the practice, tell me whether the maximin-here-expected-value-there split is coherent or whether one side has to give, and say what would concretely change — in engineering practice or in policy — if you swapped in the rival view. Give me your bottom line in one sentence as the last line.
