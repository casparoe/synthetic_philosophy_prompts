Mackie opens Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong with the flat declaration "There are no objective values," and a friend of mine thinks the argument behind it quietly detonates itself. Her reasoning: (1) If moral rightness and wrongness were objective features of the world, they would have to be intrinsically prescriptive -- facts whose mere recognition gives anyone who apprehends them a reason to act, regardless of their desires. (2) Nothing in the world is like that; no natural or supernatural fact has to-be-pursuedness built into it, and any faculty by which we supposedly detected such facts would be mysterious. So (3) there are no objective moral values. (4) But notice that (1) and (2) nowhere rely on anything distinctive about morality -- only on the claim that a fact could be categorically reason-giving. (5) Epistemic reasons are categorical in just this way: that the evidence strongly supports p is a reason to believe p whether or not believing p serves any of your ends, so evidential support would have to be an intrinsically belief-guiding feature of the world, and our grasp of it just as mysterious. (6) So the argument equally shows there are no objective epistemic reasons -- nothing is objectively evidence for anything. (7) But then there is no objective reason to accept premises (1) and (2), so the argument, if sound, gives us no reason to believe it. (8) Therefore either (1) or (2) must be false, and Mackie's queerness argument collapses. She concludes that moral realism survives by self-defense. I would rather keep (3) than accept (6), so I suspect something is off in (4)–(7), but I can't locate it. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does -- and if the fix is to deny (5)'s analogy, what does drawing that line cost an anti-realist who still wants Mackie's conclusion?
