I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind working on levels of explanation, and I'm designing a decision tree for our reading group that sorts people by their position on whether high-level, symbolic descriptions of a system ever do real explanatory or causal work, or are always just convenient glosses on the substrate. I want the tree built out of Hofstadter-style level-crossing examples rather than out of textbook labels.

Take as the starting vignette a system in the spirit of the careenium: a vast population of tiny interacting particles (the simmballs) whose local collisions are fully governed by micro-dynamics, but whose aggregate configurations form large stable objects (the simms) that behave like symbols -- a simm of one shape reliably producing a simm of another shape, in patterns that look for all the world like the simms are pushing each other around. The tree's trunk question is what to say about the sentence "the simms cause the outcomes": literal truth, useful fiction, or confused. Every branch node should be a question the user can answer by thinking about a concrete system, not by knowing terminology -- alongside the careenium, please use at least these three diagnostic cases somewhere on the tree: a chess engine where we explain a move by the position's strategic features rather than by transistor states; a DNA sequence where we say a codon "codes for" an amino acid; and a room thermostat, as a minimal case for testing whether the user grants symbolic status cheaply.

The leaves should land on positions in this neighbourhood: eliminativism about the symbolic level (only the micro-story is real), type-identity reduction (symbol-kinds are substrate-kinds), non-reductive functionalism (symbol-kinds are real because multiply realizable), epiphenomenalism about symbols (real but causally inert descriptions), weak emergence (the high level is explanatory but adds no causal oomph), strong or downward causation (high-level states genuinely constrain micro-events), and interpretivism (symbolic descriptions are projections of an interpreter, not in the system). I'd like the tree built from the logical space first -- questions about multiple realizability, explanatory autonomy, causal exclusion, and interpreter-dependence, in an order you justify -- with the positions located afterward. Note any unoccupied answer-combinations and say briefly whether they're empty because incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate (I suspect weak emergence and non-reductive functionalism will be a problem pair, but check my suspicion). Aim for eight to twelve questions and no more than ten leaves, and end with a verification step: one novel case (your choice) that the user answers to confirm the tree sorted them correctly, plus instructions for what to do when it doesn't.
