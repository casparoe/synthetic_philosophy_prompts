Two of my coworkers, Mara and Denis, got into an extended argument at lunch about the upcoming election, and I stayed out of it — but I'd like a verdict now. Mara announced that she intends to vote for a minor-party candidate whose platform she actually endorses. Denis said that, given the tight race between the two major candidates and the large moral difference he sees between them, the only defensible vote is for the lesser evil, and anything else is self-indulgence. Here is how it went, as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) Denis opened with an expected-value argument: a single ballot has only one causal power, namely the tiny chance of being decisive, but where it is decisive the stakes are enormous, so the expected value of a lesser-evil vote swamps the small but guaranteed expressive satisfaction of a sincere vote. (2) Mara replied that the argument proves too much: if tiny probabilities multiplied by huge stakes settle what one must do, then he is equally obliged to skip work to canvass, fund marginal campaign ads, or drive strangers to the polls — yet nobody considers declining those a dereliction. So his real view is not expected value at all but a special duty attached to the ballot, which needs its own defense. (3) Denis accepted this and offered one: voting for the lesser evil is a duty of non-complicity. If the greater evil wins and he could have cast a marginally effective vote against it, he shares responsibility — like refusing to add his sandbag to a levee. (4) Mara answered that the analogy fails: each sandbag does measurable work regardless of what others do, whereas a vote contributes only in the astronomically unlikely event of a tie, so there is no participation in an outcome to be complicit in. She added that her sincere vote is not causally inert either — it signals policy demand and incrementally builds the minor party's future viability, small expected effects his own framework is committed to counting. (5) Denis conceded that single-vote effects are symmetric in kind but insisted the asymmetry in stakes decides: the gap between the major candidates is near-term and certain in size, while her party-building payoff is remote and speculative. Moreover, expressive signaling is available off the ballot — donations, advocacy, conversation — whereas the tie-breaking function is the ballot's unique competence, so the ballot should be reserved for it. (6) Mara's final move was that "unique competence" simply relabels the question: whether the ballot's proper use is exhausted by its tie-breaking role is exactly what is at issue, and she accused him of building his conclusion into a definition.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible only on a further assumption, and justify each rating briefly. Then identify what, if anything, both parties are missing — is there a consideration or distinction neither raised that would change the analysis? Finally, give an overall verdict: who is more right, and on which specific point is the loser of the verdict nonetheless correct?
