For several years I have been keeping a private record of decisions, mine and those of friends and colleagues, that attracted the standard advice to cut one's losses, and I have come to think the cases lumped together under that advice fall into two quite different piles. I am writing to ask whether the second pile has an established name in the literature on practical rationality, or in economics, and if not, how you would carve it out.

The first pile is clear-cut. A friend keeps paying rent on a failing shop because of how much she has already sunk into renovating it. The renovation money is gone either way and has no bearing on whether next month's rent is worth paying; continuing for that reason is simply a mistake, and I understand the standard diagnosis of it.

The second pile is what I lack vocabulary for. Three examples. A doctoral student in her fifth year, whose project is going badly, judges that finishing even a mediocre dissertation will demonstrate to future employers, and to herself, that she completes what she starts, and that this demonstrated fact has real value spread over a career. A man who has paid for a long course of physical therapy finds it tedious and painful, but knows his own temperament: if he lets himself abandon this, he will abandon the next thing too, and he treats finishing as an installment on becoming a person who finishes. And a boat-builder, halfway through a hull whose resale value as a half-hull is essentially zero, keeps going not out of sentimentality about what he has spent but because nearly all the value of everything spent so far materializes only upon completion.

In each case the prior expenditure is genuinely unrecoverable, yet continuing seems defensible, for what look like at least four distinct reasons: the past investment is evidence about the venture's prospects, since it was made by someone who knew the venture best; abandoning carries reputational costs that are real future losses, not attachments to the past; sticking to a standing policy of finishing protects against a predictable future weakness of will; and some expenditures are like the hull, worthless unless the project is completed, so that the money is not fully gone until one quits. Yet when I describe these cases, people invoke the standard fallacy and stop thinking, which seems to me to flatten distinctions that matter.

So: is there an established concept for the defensible members of the second pile, and a literature on when deference to sunk costs is rational rather than fallacious? How does this territory relate to what gets called escalation of commitment, which as far as I can tell is also treated purely as a pathology? Is there a term for the boat-hull configuration specifically, where an expenditure's value is contingent on completion? And if no adequate name exists, please coin one, define it precisely, and mark its edges: in particular, give me boundary cases in which what presents itself as a defensible reason of one of my four kinds is actually the genuine fallacy wearing a disguise.

Please write your answer as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one would write a letter.
