I teach a course on rational choice and occasionally dabble in the literature on program equilibrium — the game-theoretic result that, in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma where each player is a computer program with access to the other's source code, mutual cooperation is sustainable as a Nash equilibrium (via programs of the form "cooperate if the other program behaves as I do, defect otherwise"). I've come to hold a fairly strong view about its significance: I maintain that this result, and its extensions, dissolve the classical puzzle of rational cooperation in one-shot interactions. On my view, the prisoner's dilemma only ever looked paradoxical because we smuggled in an assumption of mutual opacity — agents who cannot verify anything about each other's deliberative structure — and once that assumption is dropped, as it will be for advanced AI systems, open-source agents, and arguably even for humans who can signal and detect psychological traits, the tension between individual rationality and cooperation largely evaporates. The dilemma, I claim, is an artifact of an unrealistic informational assumption, not a deep fact about strategic rationality.

A colleague in my department thinks I'm wildly overreading a fragile formal curiosity, and we've agreed to actually settle this rather than repeat ourselves. So: what would count against my position? Please map its exposure as precisely as you can. I want the candidate defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be — formal results, thought experiments, and real-world observations alike. Some things I suspect belong on the map, but I'd like you to assess them and add what I've missed: the fact that cooperation is merely one equilibrium among many in such games, and nothing in the standard result selects it; the apparent dependence of the construction on exact syntactic matching or perfect simulation, and whether "noisy" transparency (approximate code similarity, imperfect interpreters, agents under resource bounds) restores defection; worries that undecidability and non-halting behavior make the cooperative programs unusable in the general case; and the objection that granting agents access to each other's source code simply changes the game being played rather than solving the original one. I'd also like to know what defenders of program equilibrium themselves concede would undermine it, not merely what its critics say, and whether any of the relevant defeaters have arguably already obtained — either in formal follow-up work or in the observed behavior of actual interacting software agents.

Finally, please be willing to reach an uncomfortable verdict if warranted: has my position, or the position of program-equilibrium enthusiasts generally, been insulated by qualifications — retreating to "cooperation is possible under transparency" whenever "cooperation is robust under transparency" is challenged — to the point where it no longer risks anything? If so, say whether you think that matters for a thesis of this kind, or whether an existence result about rational cooperation is significant even if it is unfalsifiable in practice. What I would personally find most persuasive is a formal result or a class of realistic agent designs under which transparency systematically *fails* to yield cooperation, but I'm prepared to be moved by less.
