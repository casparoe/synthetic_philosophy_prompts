Question: Devlin argues that society is entitled to enforce its shared morality by law. Reconstruct his argument, then set out Hart's case against him. Does Hart succeed?

Student answer:

The Wolfenden Report (1957) recommended decriminalising homosexual acts between consenting adults in private. Its reasoning was that private harmless conduct between consenting adults is not immoral, and so falls outside the law's proper business. Devlin's 1959 reply argues that society is not a mere aggregate of individuals but a community bound together by a shared morality — this common morality is the cement of society. It follows that the suppression of vice is as much the law's business as the suppression of subversion: an attack on the shared morality threatens society itself, and the test of what counts as immoral is the reaction of the ordinary right-minded person, the man in the jury box, including what he finds intolerable, indignant and disgusting.

Hart responds on two levels. First, he distinguishes positive morality, the code a society actually accepts, from critical morality, the standards we use to evaluate that code, and insists it must be argued, not assumed, that deviation from the positive code threatens social disintegration — Devlin offers no empirical evidence for this. Second, Hart's opposition to enforcement follows from his legal positivism: because law and morality are conceptually separate, and there is no necessary connection between what law is and what it ought to be, the legal enforcement of morality as such is ruled out, and Mill's harm principle governs instead.

Hart largely succeeds. Devlin's cement metaphor assumes what it needs to prove, and his jury-box standard would freeze the positive morality of the moment into law, leaving no room for the moral criticism Hart's distinction makes possible.
