So I write historical fiction, and my current book is set during a made-up coup in something like 1840s Europe -- a general takes the capital, the old parliament scatters, and by winter everybody's just sort of going along with the new government because the trains run and nobody wants to get shot. My writing group has been stuck for three months on what I thought was a craft question: should the novel treat the general's regime as legitimate-at-the-end or as illegitimate-all-along? But it's clearly not a craft question anymore, because we've all read the same chapters, we all agree on exactly what happens in them, and nobody has moved an inch. Half the group says legitimacy is just what people stop resisting -- power settles in, becomes habit, becomes law -- and the other half says no, force can produce obedience but it can never produce legitimacy, legitimacy is a moral property the general never acquires no matter how smoothly the trains run.

The thing that made me think there's something deeper going on is that I finally read the bits of Pascal's Pensées everyone kept gesturing at, the fragments on justice and force where he says, roughly, that since we can't make what's just strong, we end up making what's strong just -- and that custom is basically the whole of equity, that we follow the majority not because they have more reason but because they have more power. The weird part: both halves of my group claim Pascal agrees with them. The cynics read him as saying legitimacy-talk is a post-hoc story power tells about itself, full stop. The moralists read him as diagnosing a corruption -- he's describing how people *behave*, not endorsing it, and the whole point is that real justice stands apart from whatever the strong call just. Both readings seem honestly available from the text, which is maybe a clue about my group's problem too.

So here's what I want from you: don't tell me who's right about the general. Diagnose the stall. Is my group having a factual disagreement (about how obedience actually turns into authority, in history or in readers' heads), a verbal disagreement where 'legitimate' means two different things and we could fix it by splitting the word, a disagreement about which cases count as evidence, or bedrock intuitions about whether there's any moral residue left over after you've explained obedience through power and habit? And does the Pascal passage cut one way on *that* question -- i.e., is he himself dissolving the concept, reforming it, or keeping two senses of justice running in parallel and letting them collide? Given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually move my group: a distinction, a sharper question, an example nobody's considered, or an argument that the impasse is permanent and I should just write the novel so both readings survive in it.
