I am editing a philosophy magazine's annual issue in which famous thought experiments are retold in the idiom of an older text, and I would like a contribution built around the Chinese Room. The conceit is that John Searle's 1980 scenario — a monolingual English speaker in a room who produces fluent Chinese answers by following an English rulebook for manipulating Chinese symbols, and who is thereby supposed to show that running the right program never amounts to understanding — is itself a piece of Cartesian natural philosophy: an imaginary world containing nothing but matter in motion, inspected for signs of a mind. As an epigraph and stylistic model, the piece must use this passage from Part V of René Descartes's Discourse on the Method:

"I resolved to leave all the people here to their disputes, and to speak only of what would happen in a new world, if God were now to create somewhere in the imaginary spaces matter sufficient to compose one, and were to agitate variously and confusedly the different parts of this matter, so that there resulted a chaos as disordered as the poets ever feigned, and after that did nothing more than lend his ordinary concurrence to nature, and allow her to act in accordance with the laws which he had established."

Write the piece as a mock summary, in the manner of Part V, of an unpublished treatise by an invented seventeenth-century natural philosopher — call him Monsieur S. — who describes a new world containing only a room, a man, slips of inscribed paper, slot-holes, and great volumes of rules, all agitated according to fixed laws, and who then reports, with perfect seriousness, that he has traced every motion in the system and nowhere found any understanding of Chinese. The satire should exaggerate the real structure of Searle's argument rather than invent a straw version: the author's scrupulous attention to the man inside the room, his serene unconcern with whether the room plus its contents might jointly do what no part does alone, and his confidence that whatever cannot be seen in the motions of the matter cannot be there at all. The irony must stay deadpan and remain legible without winking: by the final section, the author's own exhaustive inventory of the system's flawlessly appropriate behavior should leave the reader feeling the pressure of the Systems Reply — that he has described everything except the one thing his method forbade him to look for — even though the author never notices. Do not name Searle or any other real person; Monsieur S. and his treatise are fictions. Roughly 700 words, not counting the Descartes passage, in six or seven short numbered sections imitating a scholar's summary of a lost book.
