I sit on the mission review panel of a mid-sized national space agency, and we have recently adopted a charter principle that is causing us real trouble in practice. The principle has three clauses: first, that no mission may make an irreversible change to a celestial body while significant open scientific questions about that body's history or potential for life remain unanswered; second, that where an intervention is reversible in principle, the burden is on the proposer to show the reversal is achievable with technology that already exists rather than technology that is merely anticipated; and third, that the interests of future human generations in encountering unaltered environments count as a standing objection, though a defeasible one, against any permanent alteration. The board that wrote this intended it as a commitment, not a slogan, but every proposal we receive forces us to improvise an interpretation on the spot, and our rulings have become inconsistent enough that mission teams are starting to forum-shop between panel members.

Please turn this principle into a decision procedure my panel could actually run on a proposal, given that we handle roughly a dozen proposals a year, each must receive a ruling within six weeks of submission, and our panel has expertise in engineering and biology but not in philosophy. I want the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a rejection, and where it must hand the decision back to a human because the question is one of values rather than of fact -- I expect there to be several such handoff points, particularly around what makes a scientific question "significant" and "open," and around how much weight the future-generations clause gets against concrete present benefits. Please also tell me which distinctions in the principle have to survive the translation into checklist form and which can safely be collapsed, and where the procedure is most likely to misfire.

Then run the finished procedure on three proposals: a crewed landing in a Martian region where orbital data suggests recurring subsurface brines, with the crewed program office arguing that delaying means losing a decade of political support; a privately funded pilot to release engineered microbes on a small icy moon currently judged sterile, aimed at testing whether biological crusts could eventually thicken a thin atmosphere; and a mining operation that would dismember a small asteroid whose unusual structure some planetary scientists believe preserves a record of early solar system chemistry, though this is contested within that community. At least one of these should be a case where the procedure delivers a ruling I will not like, and I would like you to say plainly which one that is and whether the uncomfortable outcome reflects the principle working as intended or a defect in how I have operationalized it.
