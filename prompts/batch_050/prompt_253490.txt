Next month I am giving a forty-minute paper at a metaphysics colloquium defending a position I call two-stage primitivism about the unity of facts and propositions, and I want a Q&A prep sheet. My view, in brief: the regress associated with Bradley shows only that the unity of a state of affairs cannot be a further constituent of it, since a relation tying a to F-ness would itself need tying to both, and so on indefinitely. So for facts I am a primitivist: states of affairs have constituents, but their unity is not among them, and exemplification is not a constituent-style relation at all. For propositions, however, I deny that primitivism is needed. Propositions, on my account, are unified derivatively: they represent possible facts, and their unity is inherited from what they represent rather than being an intrinsic tie among their parts. The regress is therefore blocked at two different places by two different moves, and the second half of the paper argues that the moves do not interfere — in particular, that representational unity does not covertly reintroduce a constituent tie inside the proposition.

The audience is not friendly in any uniform way, which is why I need help. It includes: a truthmaker theorist working broadly in Armstrong's line, who will accept constituent ontology and press me on whether a primitive, non-constituent tie is an explanation or just a refusal to explain; a trope theorist who thinks the entire problem is an artifact of assuming facts are built out of universals and particulars, and who will try to absorb my paper into her framework as a confused restatement of it; a Fregean philosopher of language who thinks the unsaturatedness of senses already dissolves the proposition half and that my representational move reinstates a problem Frege solved; and one quiet member of the audience who takes Bradley's own conclusion seriously — that the regress shows relations, and perhaps thought's relational structure itself, to be somehow unreal — and who tends to ask exactly one devastating question per colloquium. The rest are graduate students and generalists whose sympathies I could win or lose.

Prepare the prep sheet. Give me the eight to ten objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by the product of how likely each is and how much damage it does to the paper, and phrased the way each person above would actually phrase it, in their vocabulary rather than mine. For each objection: the best reply I can give in under two minutes, a frank note on where that reply is soft, and whether the right move is to hold the line, concede a point while protecting the thesis, or reframe the question. Tell me which objection I am secretly hoping nobody raises, and why it is dangerous to the conjunction of my two moves rather than to either move alone. Flag any objection that is likely to arrive wearing one audience member's vocabulary while really being another's argument in disguise, since those are the exchanges where I am most likely to answer the wrong question. Finally, tell me which fight I should explicitly decline to have during Q&A and bank for the published version, and give me a sentence or two I can use to decline it without sounding evasive.
