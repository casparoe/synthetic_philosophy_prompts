I'm prepping a grad seminar on diachronic rationality and there's a case I want but can't seem to build cleanly myself. Construct a thought experiment in which an agent has genuinely strict cyclic preferences over three ordinary options -- prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, all at the same time, no preference change over the course of the story -- and yet cannot be money-pumped: there's no sequence of trades they'd willingly walk through that leaves them holding less money with nothing to show for it. Constraints: realistic setting, no demons, no perfect predictors, no exotic anything; payoffs a normal person could actually face (concert tickets, apartments, job offers, that sort of thing); the scenario itself in one paragraph; and -- this is the part I keep failing at -- the immunity to pumping can't come from the agent being dim, or from some obviously broken quirk like refusing all trades on principle. It has to fall out of something at least defensible about *how* they choose.

After the scenario, do the demonstration. Take the standard pump (they hold C, you offer B for C plus a small fee, then A for B plus a fee, then C for A plus a fee, and around again) and walk through step by step why it stalls against this particular agent. Then tell me exactly which structural assumption of the money pump argument the case violates. I expect it's somewhere in the vicinity of: the argument assumes choices get made one at a time without the agent treating the sequence as a single object, or that preferences over outcomes fully settle choice dispositions, or that a rational agent can't adopt a binding policy and stick to it. Whichever it is, name it precisely, and say whether the pump's defender can just fold that assumption into the definition of rationality or whether doing so begs the question against the cyclic-preference agent.

Also: what happens if you remove the load-bearing feature -- does the case collapse back into an ordinary pump? And give me the best counterattack from someone who thinks strict cyclic preferences are irrational full stop: what would they say the agent's immunity actually costs them, and is the cost one the agent should happily pay?

The point of the case, for the seminar, is to press on whether the money pump argument proves anything about preferences as such, or only about preferences *plus* a particular way of making choices -- so a scenario that just stipulates the agent can't be pumped, rather than generating the immunity from the setup, is cheating and I'll know.
