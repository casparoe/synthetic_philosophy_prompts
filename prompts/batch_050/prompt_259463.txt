For my philosophy of mind course we've just covered heterophenomenology — the method, associated with Dennett, in which a consciousness researcher treats a subject's introspective reports the way an anthropologist treats a native informant's talk about the gods: the reports are recorded, transcribed, and interpreted as data about what the subject believes and finds it natural to say, but they are never taken as authoritative descriptions of what the subject's experience is actually like. If a subject in a psychophysics experiment says the afterimage looked green, the heterophenomenologist treats this as a text to be explained, not as a window onto a green afterimage. Please act as a professional philosopher of mind and think this through carefully, starting by brainstorming the possible readings before settling on your analysis. My question: what philosophical commitments about consciousness does this method actually presuppose? For instance, does it require denying first-person incorrigibility, or does it require the stronger claim that there is no fact about experience beyond what the subject believes? Reconstruct the implicit theory of consciousness the method only makes sense under, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and tell me what would have to change in the practice of consciousness science if the presupposed view were replaced with one on which sincere self-reports really are incorrigible.
