so i'm TAing an intro phil seminar this semester and we're in the middle of the James vs. Clifford unit on the ethics of belief. last week a student (business major, naturally) stayed after class to inform me that William James was "basically the first LinkedIn thought leader," and the worst part is i haven't been able to unsee it since. he was especially smug about this passage from James's essay "The Will to Believe," which he'd screenshotted onto his phone:

"Finally, if I were Dr. Nansen and proposed to you to join my North Pole expedition, your option would be momentous; for this would probably be your only similar opportunity, and your choice now would either exclude you from the North Pole sort of immortality altogether or put at least the chance of it into your hands. He who refuses to embrace a unique opportunity loses the prize as surely as if he tried and failed."

write me the opening chapter, about 600-700 words (the passage doesn't count toward that), of a fictional bestselling decision-coaching book by an invented guru -- call the book something like "The Nansen Protocol" -- who has built his entire empire on this passage and on James's taxonomy of hypotheses and options. the comedy should come from him deploying James's actual framework with total accuracy and zero shame: he knows liveness isn't an intrinsic property of a hypothesis but a relation to the individual thinker, measured by willingness to act, so week one of the program is devoted to *making* dead hypotheses live (vision boards, hype playlists, an app that scintillates) so that more of life qualifies as a genuine option; he coaches clients to receive a timeshare salesman's "this offer expires at midnight" as a textbook forced, momentous option; and at some point he has to deal with James's own umbrella example -- where James himself says the choice isn't forced because you can just stay home -- which the guru dismisses as agoraphobic loser talk, even though this requires him to quietly break with his source. never let him misquote or strawman James, though: his citations are accurate, and that's precisely what's damning about him. include a back-cover blurb from a satisfied client who hasn't declined a unique opportunity since 2019 and can no longer afford groceries, plus one hostile footnote where the guru feuds with the long-dead W.K. Clifford, whom he's read just enough of to make a shipwreck joke about. underneath all the jokes, the reader should be left sitting with the genuine problem the passage raises: James's machinery tells you what to do once an option is live, forced, and momentous, but says almost nothing about how to tell real momentousness from manufactured urgency -- and the piece should leave it ambiguous whether that's an exploit in James or just the human condition.
