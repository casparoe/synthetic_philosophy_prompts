I've read enough rationalist-adjacent writing about organizations over the years -- Coase on why firms exist, principal-agent problems, the endless blog discourse on "high-agency" people, and lately the debate about algorithmic management at gig platforms -- to notice an assumption that runs through all of it without ever being stated, let alone defended. I want you to dig that assumption out.

The practice I have in mind is the near-universal division of labor between deciding and executing. Concretely: organizations sort people into roles where some individuals do the foreseeing -- setting strategy, planning, prioritizing, defining what counts as done -- and others do the implementing, often under instructions so detailed that the implementer's own judgment is treated as a defect rather than a resource (think of a delivery driver's app, which does the routing, pricing, and scheduling, leaving the human to supply what one might call physical execution). Hiring pipelines formalize this early: "leadership track" versus "individual contributor," assessments of "strategic thinking" versus "execution skills." The justifications offered are always local and pragmatic -- comparative advantage, coordination costs, the impossibility of everyone deliberating about everything.

Now consider Aristotle, Politics, Book I (Jowett translation), where he argues that some human relations of rule are natural rather than conventional:

"For that which can foresee by the exercise of mind is by nature intended to be lord and master, and that which can with its body give effect to such foresight is a subject, and by nature a slave; hence master and slave have the same interest."

My questions:

First, what does the modern practice actually presuppose about the distribution of foresight across persons? Reconstruct its implicit theory. Does it require anything like Aristotle's claim -- that the capacity to foresee by the exercise of mind is unevenly distributed in a way that is natural, fixed, and person-bound -- or does it presuppose only the weaker, role-bound claim that foresight and execution are most efficiently *separated*, wherever the capacity happens to sit? Be careful to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with: a practice can look Aristotelian from the outside while resting on no view about natural kinds at all.

Second, take the standard economic justifications one at a time -- comparative advantage, coordination costs, specialization gains -- and ask which of them license the practice *without* Aristotle's commitment and which smuggle the commitment back in. I have a suspicion that comparative advantage does less work here than rationalist-adjacent writers assume, since it explains why a division is efficient without explaining why the same people reliably end up on the deciding side, but I want you to evaluate that suspicion rather than accept it.

Third, Aristotle packs a second claim into the passage: that ruler and ruled "have the same interest." Does our practice presuppose that too -- is the mutual-interest claim load-bearing in the justification of decide/execute divisions, or is it an independent and possibly false addition? What follows for the practice if foresight's distribution turns out to be largely an artifact of training and role occupancy rather than nature -- does the practice collapse, or merely change its justification?

Where you think the practice is philosophically underdetermined -- compatible with several rival theories of deliberative capacity -- say so explicitly rather than forcing a verdict. But where a justification quietly requires Aristotle's stronger claim, name it.
