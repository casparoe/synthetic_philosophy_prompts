I'm a TA for an introductory philosophy of mind course, and in two weeks I have a single fifty-minute section to teach Dennett's brain-in-a-vat puzzle from "Where Am I?" to first-years who have read the piece but, on past form, mostly remember it as a quirky science-fiction story about a brain in a tank of fluid. The story, as they'll have read it: the narrator's brain is removed and kept alive in a vat while his body, controlled remotely through radio links, goes about its business; later a perfect computer replica of the brain is built as a backup, and a switch on his belt can transfer control of the body between the biological brain and its duplicate, with the flipping of the switch making no felt difference at all. I don't want the piece explained to me -- I want you to plan how I should explain it. What has to be in place conceptually before the puzzle can bite at all (I'm guessing students first need to feel the ordinary confidence that they are located where their eyes and body are, and to see that the story pries apart three questions they normally run together: where is my body, where is my brain, and where is my point of view)? How should I stage the narration itself as the carrying example -- at which moments should I pause the story and put "where is he now?" to the room, and what should I do when the class votes differently after the brain's removal than after the first flip of the switch?

Then give me the misconception map: the errors this audience predictably walks in with or forms on the spot, and the exact point in my sequence where each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact. My own guesses are that some will treat the puzzle as a factual riddle with a hidden right answer (the real Dennett is in the vat, or in the body, and the task is to figure out which); that some will take it as covert propaganda for an immaterial soul; that some will dismiss it because the neuroscience is fantasy; and that the sharpest students will insist the switch must have made some difference that simply couldn't be detected. Tell me which of these I've got right, which I've missed, and how the ordering of the lesson should absorb each one. Finally, give me one question I can pose in the last five minutes that would show the idea has landed -- something that separates students who have grasped that the question "where am I?" can fail to have an answer even when every physical fact is settled from students who merely enjoyed the story -- and tell me what I should openly refuse to go into at this level (fission, split-brain cases, the wider personal identity literature) and say so.

Lead with the spine of the fifty minutes in a single first sentence -- the one-line shape of the whole lesson -- then give the plan, with no preamble and no restating of my request.
