Some background so you know where I'm coming from: I spent about fourty years as a marine biologist, mostly benthic ecology, which meant a lot of time staring at corals, sponges, and squirmy things in sediment cores. Since retiring I've joined a philosophy of biology reading group at the community college (my wife says it's an excuse to drink wine on Tuesdays, and she's not entirely wrong). Lately we've been chewing on what makes something one organism rather than a bunch of things cooperating, and the group keeps circling two rival criteria: on the one hand, metabolic or physiological integration -- shared metabolism, mutual dependence, coordinated function -- and on the other, the evolutionary "unit of selection" style account, where being an individual is about having a shared reproductive fate, bottlenecks between generations, policing of cheaters, that sort of thing. Problem is, the usual exhibit cases -- lichens, the Portuguese man o' war, aphids and their bacteria -- have been discussed to death and everyone in the group has a rehearsed take on them by now.

So here's what I'd like from you. Construct a thought experiment: a single living system, describable in one paragraph, where the two criteria come apart in the direction I find genuinely tricky -- the metabolic/physiological criterion says it clearly IS one individual organism, while the evolutionary criterion says it is NOT (it's two or more lineages cooperating, or a community, not a Darwinian individual). Constraints: ground it in real biology, no science fiction. You can loosely base it on real systems -- obligate endosymbioses, synthetic bacterial consortia, squid and their light-organ bacteria, whatever works -- and a plausible lab-engineered system is fine as long as it doesn't violate anything we actually know. It also has to be stable over many generations, not a one-off fluke that falls apart by Tuesday.

After the scenario, do the bookkeeping for me: state what verdict each criterion delivers and why, name the single feature of your case that's doing the work of pulling them apart, and tell me what happens if you remove or reverse that feature -- does the evolutionary criterion flip to "one individual" and the disagreement collapse? Then a practical coda, because honestly this is where I care: suppose conservation officials have to decide whether destroying the system kills one organism or several (it matters for how they count what's lost). Which criterion should govern that call, and does your answer change depending on whether the partners have been co-evolving for ten million years or were assembled in a lab last year? I'd rather you commit to an answer than give me "it's complicated," though you can flag the complications on the way.
