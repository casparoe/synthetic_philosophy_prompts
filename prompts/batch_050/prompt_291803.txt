I'm a software engineer at a mid-size company (~400 people) that makes, among other things, hiring software and some warehouse automation stuff. Zero philosophy background here. A few months ago we had an ugly near-miss: our resume-screening tool silently auto-rejected a pile of qualified candidates for two weeks because of a data pipeline bug, and when leadership went looking for whose fault it was, everyone had a story for why it wasnt theirs -- the vendor sold us the model, the PM just wrote the spec, the data team just maintained the pipeline, legal said nobody asked them. Nobody was exactly lying, which was the disturbing part. In the aftermath I got voluntold into a new role: before any system that makes consequential decisions ships, I run a "responsibility review" and either sign off or escalate to our risk committee. I'll do maybe 3-4 of these a quarter and can spend about half a day on each. I need an actual repeatable procedure, because right now I'm winging it and my reviews are just vibes plus a spreadsheet.

Here's what I think I understand from reading pop-science pieces on the "responsibility gap" idea, and please correct me in plain language where I'm off: normally, blaming someone for an outcome only makes sense if they had real control over it and knew (or should've known) what they were doing; opaque, vendor-built, constantly-updating systems erode both of those for everyone in the chain, so you can get harms that no one is legitimately blameworthy for. Also "responsibility" seems to smush together at least three different things -- being the actual author of a decision (it reflects your judgment), being answerable for it (you can explain why it happened), and being on the hook for it (sanctioned, fired, sued) -- and a system can be fine on one dimension and totally broken on another. And I gather there's a known failure mode where a company adds a nominal "human in the loop" who approves hundreds of decisions an hour, which isn't real control, it just creates a designated blame-absorber for when things go wrong.

So: write me a runbook I can actually follow. The questions to ask about a deployment, in order; what counts as a passing answer to each, in terms of observable evidence (org charts, logs, override rates, review-time-per-decision) rather than assurances; where the procedure hard-stops at "don't ship this as-is" versus where it hands off to the risk committee because the question is really about values, like how much irreducible risk is acceptable; and an honest section on where this procedure will misfire or can be gamed. Then run it on two cases: (A) the vendor resume screener from above -- auto-rejects with no human review before rejection, our team can't inspect the model, appeals land with one overwhelmed HR generalist; and (B) my own baby, a fleet speed-governor for warehouse robots with one human supervisor watching twelve camera feeds with an emergency stop button, where our own testing suggests humans mostly can't react fast enough to prevent the failures that actually matter. If the honest answer is that my system fails its own review, say so plainly, don't soften it. Throughout, separate what people who study this stuff broadly agree on from what's genuinely contested -- I've seen conflicting takes on whether this "gap" is even a new problem or just the old diffusion-of-responsibility story with fancier computers, and on whether a gap is always a reason not to deploy or sometimes a price worth paying, so don't present a contested position as the consensus. At the very end, give me the verdicts in machine-readable form, like \answer{case A: ..., case B: ...}.
