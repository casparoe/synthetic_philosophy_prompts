Here's an episode I want run through the machinery of judgment aggregation: the Brexit endgame, roughly June 2016 to late 2019. The salient facts as I understand them, so we're on the same page: the referendum was a binary, Leave or Remain, Leave won 52-48, and the Leave side never committed to what Leave concretely meant -- Norway-style single market membership, a Canada-style trade deal, a clean no-deal break all got floated at different times by different people, nobody signed up to one. Then in 2019 the Commons (which was majority Remain-leaning to begin with) voted down May's withdrawal deal three seperate times, the first defeat being the biggest in modern Commons history, and in the indicative votes that spring it also failed to find a majority for anything else -- customs union, Common Market 2.0, a second referendum, revoking Article 50, no-deal all lost, some of them narrowly.

So the mapping I want: treat Leave as a conclusion and the various exit models as mutually exclusive premise-sets, and tell me how far discursive-dilemma-type machinery -- premise-based versus conclusion-based aggregation, majority inconsistency over a connected agenda -- actually explains this mess. Some specific things I want pinned down:

- Is this even the discursive dilemma, or a different pathology wearing it's clothes? A majority for a disjunction with no majority for any disjunct is not, as far as I can tell, a paradox by itself. So say exactly which formal result (if any) the episode instantiates, and which features are just ordinary preference aggregation doing ordinary non-paradoxical things.
- The classic doctrinal paradox runs premises-to-conclusion inside one institution with a fixed set of judges. Here the conclusion was locked in first by one body (the electorate) and the premise-work was dumped on a different body (Parliament) with different composition, different incentives, and no obligation to honor the first body's aggregation method. Does the theory have anything to say about splitting the agenda across bodies like that, or does it just go silent?
- Judgment aggregation assumes honest, separable judgments over a fixed agenda. Brexit had strategic voting everywhere, options appearing and disappearing, and MPs whose preferences over packages plainly weren't separable from who proposed them or what it did to their party. Which of these does the most damage to applying the theory at all?
- Run it in both directions. What does the episode confirm? The rhetoric of the period treated the referendum result as the output of a group agent with a coherent judgment set, and that seems like a point for the theory's diagnosis. And what does it show the theory misses? My hunch is the binding constraints were time pressure under Article 50, party discipline, and the fact that the EU was a strategic player sitting at the table with its own agenda -- none of which the framework has vocabulary for. Tell me if I'm wrong.

I already know the intro version of the discursive dilemma, so don't re-derive the three-judge example for me. I want the careful mapping and then the verdict: is Brexit the theory's poster child, a case it half-explains, or a case people reach for the theory when it doesn't actually apply?
