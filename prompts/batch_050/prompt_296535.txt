So I teach a second-year undergrad course on the philosophy of emotions, and next week I've got one 75-minute seminar on the question of whether love is responsive to reasons -- the whole appraisal vs. bestowal tangle, roughly the Frankfurt/Velleman/Kolodny territory, though the students haven't read any of it and I don't want to just lecture the positions at them.

What I want from you is not the seminar itself but the plan for it. Specifically:

The order of ideas. There's a dependency structure here and I want it thought through -- e.g. I'm fairly sure they need to feel the pull of "I love her *because* she's funny and kind and brilliant" before the replaceability/trading-up worry can get any grip, and the trading-up worry has to land before bestowal looks like anything other than madness. Tell me if I've got that wrong and why, and what else has to be in place before what.

The carrying example. I need one case that does most of the work for the whole session. My instinct is to avoid a generic "imagine your partner" -- half the room has never had a serious partner and the other half will just get sappy. Should the case be romantic at all? Would a parent and adult child, or a decades-long friendship, carry it better? Whatever you pick, say what the example needs to include (the qualities have to be genuinely generic, I think, or the replaceability worry doesn't fire) and what it must NOT include.

The misconceptions, in the order they'll occur to the students, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off rather than letting it fester. The ones I'm bracing for: that the appraisal view makes love into a hiring decision; that bestowal means you could love literally anything, like a lamppost; that this is a psychology question about what causes people to fall in love; and that reasons for *staying* in love are the same thing as reasons for *falling* in love. Tell me which of these I'm right to worry about, which I'm wrong about, and what I'm missing.

A check for the end. One question I can put to them where the answer shows whether they understood the distinction or just memorized two labels. It should be something where "it depends" is the wrong kind of answer but a definite verdict with a reason is the right kind.

And what I should explicitly refuse to cover in 75 minutes and say so out loud -- I suspect the full machinery of relationships-as-reasons is one of these, but you tell me.

Oh and one wrinkle I want built in somewhere: a student last year asked whether the appraisal view means you're obligated to dump your partner if someone objectively better comes along, and I fumbled it. Where does that land in the plan and how do I not fumble it this time.
