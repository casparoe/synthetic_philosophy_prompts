Paul Benacerraf posed a dilemma for the philosophy of mathematics: the natural way to read mathematical statements treats them as straightforwardly true or false, with numbers and sets as genuine objects, but then mathematical knowledge becomes mysterious, since we stand in no causal contact with such abstract entities; and the views that make mathematical knowledge easy to explain seem to distort what mathematical claims mean. Suppose a philosopher constructs a parallel dilemma for morality: take moral statements at face value as true or false independent of anyone's attitudes, and it becomes hard to explain how our moral beliefs could reliably track such facts; take a view on which moral knowledge is unproblematic -- say, that moral claims record what we would endorse under informed and impartial reflection -- and morality seems to lose the objectivity that realists prized. Compare the two dilemmas in detail. Identify at least two genuine asymmetries between the mathematical and moral cases -- for instance, concerning the role of causal explanation, the possibility of error, or what would be lost by going anti-realist -- and argue on balance for whether the dilemma is more tractable in one domain than the other. Do not merely catalog standard responses to each; the point of the exercise is the comparison itself. Please write your answer in American English.
