I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist ruins himself by increments: each step he takes is defensible in the moment, yet the sequence of steps is catastrophic. To get the psychology right, I described the dollar auction to two friends over dinner. In this game, an auctioneer offers a one-dollar bill to the highest bidder, with the twist that the second-highest bidder must also pay her bid while receiving nothing. Bidding typically opens at a few cents, climbs past fifty cents, and often sails beyond a dollar, because once someone has bid ninety-nine cents, the person at ninety-eight prefers losing two cents by bidding a dollar to losing ninety-eight cents by folding.

My friends fell into an argument I could not resolve, and I would like you to adjudicate it.

The first friend, a psychologist by training, held that the auction simply dramatizes the sunk cost fallacy. A rational agent, she said, treats irrecoverable losses as irrelevant and compares only future costs to future benefits; anyone who bids two dollars for a one-dollar bill because he has already committed money is making a textbook error, and the auction's value is precisely that it isolates the bias. The tragedy of my protagonist, on her view, is a flawed decision procedure.

The second friend, an economist, replied that this reading gets the logic backwards. Once inside the auction, he argued, each bid is locally rational: at every stage, the marginal comparison genuinely favors one more bid over folding. The irrationality, if there is any, lies in entering the auction at all—and even entry can be rational for the first mover if she expects others to recognize the trap and stay out. The auction, he said, is not a portrait of a bias but of a structural trap: individually sound moves aggregating into collective ruin, which is why clear-eyed people in bidding wars, military escalations, and litigation keep doing it.

The psychologist answered that locally rational steps that foreseeably compound into disaster cannot be called rational without emptying the word of meaning; an agent who can see the whole arc and still climbs it step by step is exhibiting exactly the myopia she meant. The economist countered that "myopia" names the phenomenon without explaining it, and that labeling escalation a fallacy cannot account for why sophisticated, well-advised decision-makers escalate most. The psychologist then pressed what she took to be her strongest point: people escalate even with no rival—continuing to read a bad book because they are three hundred pages in, pouring money into a solo venture long after its prospects have soured—so the engine must be internal, not competitive. The economist responded that even solitary cases involve an implicit contest: the present self against past investments of identity, reputation, and announced intentions, so structure is doing more work than she admits.

Neither would yield, and they parted having talked past one another on at least one point I could not quite locate. Who is right about which specific claims here? In particular: is escalation within the auction best understood as error or as correct local reasoning in a poisoned structure; whose account better handles solitary escalation; and is there a deeper distinction—about what rationality evaluates, whether single choices, policies, or dispositions—that both of them are missing? Finally, since my own stake is literary: what does your verdict imply about whether I should portray my protagonist as making mistakes or as reasoning well?
