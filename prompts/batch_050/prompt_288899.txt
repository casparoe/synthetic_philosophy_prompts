ok so I just got laid off. junior dev, three years in, and the official explanation was that our team's output is now "covered by AI-assisted tooling." Which is rich, because I spent the last year doing code review on generated code, so arguably I helped train my own replacement. Anyway, I did the thing where you cope by reading theory at 2am, and I went through the big machinery chapter in Capital vol. 1 (the "Machinery and Large-Scale Industry" one) and the passage in the Grundrisse that everyone calls the Fragment on Machines, and now I have Opinions, but I also suspect I'm projecting my situation onto an analysis of 19th century textile mills.

Here's what I actually want. Restate Marx's account of machinery and automation in the vocabulary of my working life, which I know cold: code review, seniority ladders, per-seat SaaS pricing, velocity metrics, technical debt, on-call, the shift from writing code to approving generated code. Specifically translate these four things:

1. his claim that the machine doesn't lighten work but intensifies and deskills it. Does that map to me going from writing code to rubber-stamping copilot output? What plays the role of the hand-loom weaver versus the machine minder, and is that even the right mapping?
2. relative surplus value, i.e. the machine makes the same working day produce more rather than making the day shorter. My guess is this maps onto per-seat pricing, where the tool's price is calibrated to capture the productivity gain instead of giving me back my time, but tell me if I'm wrong.
3. the machinery-as-a-weapon bit, where capital deploys machines against labor, to break strikes and discipline workers. Is there a version of this where the threat of the tool matters more than the tool?
4. the "general intellect" passage, where he seems to suggest that eventually direct labor stops being the measure of wealth and the whole system of value becomes incoherent. Half of tech twitter treats this as Marx predicting github copilot. Does it actually say that, or is that a stretch?

Then the part I care about most, the residue report: what didn't survive the translation? Two suspects I want you to rule on. First, the labor theory of value is doing a lot of load-bearing work in Marx's version, and I don't think it has any counterpart in how my industry actually prices things, where the marginal cost of one more inference is basically zero. Second, his machines don't learn from the worker, but I literally spent a year correcting the tooling that replaced me, which feels like it either supercharges his point or breaks the analogy completely — I can't tell which. And one more: Marx's whole story runs through exploitation of the worker, but in my case the "victim" of the automation is a line item in the company's cost structure. Is there any way to say that in his vocabulary, or is the concept of exploitation as surplus extraction just untranslatable here, and is that my framework's defect or his?

Be honest where the mapping is forced. If the right answer is "he's talking about physical machines that wear out and transfer their value into the product, and your situation differs in ways X and Y," then say that instead of flattering the analogy.
