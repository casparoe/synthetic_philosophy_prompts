I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long essay on whether societies can deliberately steer their dominant moral emotion — and whether social media amounts to an uncontrolled experiment in exactly that. The essay hangs on one episode, and I'd like you to analyze that episode through the guilt–shame framework so I can pressure-test my argument before I file.

The episode. In December 2013, a communications director at a large American media and internet company posted a joke on Twitter before boarding a long flight from London to Cape Town. The joke played on AIDS statistics and race, implying she, as a white person, was safe from infection. She had fewer than two hundred followers. While she was in the air and unreachable for roughly eleven hours, the tweet spread globally, a hashtag speculating about her landing trended worldwide, strangers gleefully anticipated her destruction, and her employer publicly distanced itself before her plane touched down. She was fired within days. In the years since, the episode has become the canonical case study of internet pile-ons, and public sentiment has largely swung back toward sympathy for her — she has been recast less as a villain than as a casualty of the crowd. (If the specific case doesn't register for you, work from the facts as I've just stated them; they're accurate as far as my reporting goes, and nothing below requires more detail than this.)

The lens. Take the standard distinction: guilt is distress at what one has done, keyed to one's own standards and typically repaired by confession and amends; shame is distress at what one is in the eyes of others, keyed to an audience and typically repaired by concealment, exile, or transformation of the self. On top of that distinction, I want you to test a hypothesis I'm considering for the essay, which I'll call the audience-without-community hypothesis: shame sanctions work, when they work, only when the shamed person belongs to and values the observing community, because shame's sting is the threat of exclusion from a group one needs. Networked shaming, the hypothesis says, supplies an enormous audience but no community — the target belongs to the crowd in no sense — so it can produce humiliation, fear, and material ruin, but not conscience; worse, because repeat exposure to anonymous shaming selects for people who feel no shame before strangers, it trains the population toward shamelessness rather than virtue.

Here is what I need the analysis to do:

First, do the mapping rigorously. Which emotion was the pile-on actually trying to produce in its target — shame, guilt, or something the distinction doesn't cleanly name? Which did it plausibly produce in fact, and what would count as evidence either way, given that we can only observe her behavior from the outside? Note that she later apologized and expressed remorse; say whether that fits a guilt reading, a shame reading, or neither, and be careful not to let the available evidence simply ratify whichever reading you prefer.

Second, test the audience-without-community hypothesis against the episode rather than assuming it. Identify the details that support it — and then identify the detail that cuts hardest against it: the sanction that actually bit was her employer firing her, which is not exclusion from a valued moral community but a material consequence delivered by one actor under pressure from the crowd. Does that rescue the hypothesis (the crowd worked through a community after all), refute it, or reveal it to be vacuous? Take a position.

Third, put the crowd on the couch. The guilt–shame framework is built to describe wrongdoers, not punishers. What emotion best describes the spectators' gleeful participation — and if the framework has no home for it, is that a gap in the framework or evidence that moralistic punishment was never a guilt-or-shame phenomenon to begin with?

Fourth, answer my essay's headline question: can a culture choose its moral emotion? Treat networked shaming as a deliberate, decentralized cultural technology — an attempt by many participants to raise the price of certain speech by shifting enforcement from internalized guilt to public shame. Given that the long-run aftermath of this episode was sympathy for the target and widespread revulsion at the pile-on itself, what does the case suggest about whether such a shift can be steered, and whether steering it toward shame is even desirable? Here I want you to engage the further question of which emotion is the better moral emotion for a liberal society: does this episode vindicate shame's defenders (who praise its reach where law and conscience can't follow) or its critics (who note that shame punishes visibility rather than wrongdoing, and falls hardest on the inarticulate and unlucky)?
