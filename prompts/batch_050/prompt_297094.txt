You are a moral philosopher with deep expertise in contractualist ethics, helping me design teaching material that tests whether advanced students understand how Scanlon-style contractualism actually generates its verdicts rather than merely gesturing at them. Think carefully about the mechanics of the theory before you begin writing, and start by briefly brainstorming two or three candidate scenarios before settling on the one that best satisfies the specifications below.

For the purposes of this task, take the following as the operative definition of the theory, whether or not it captures every nuance of the literature: an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any principle that no one, suitably motivated to find shared principles, could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement. Crucially, the test incorporates an individualist restriction: whether a person can reasonably reject a principle is assessed by comparing that person's complaint, taken on its own, against the complaints of other affected individuals, each taken on its own. Burdens and benefits are never summed across persons. So if one person stands to suffer a great deal and five people each stand to suffer somewhat less, the comparison is between one complaint of the larger size and five separate complaints of the smaller size, and on the standard reading the greatest individual complaint governs the outcome.

Construct a thought experiment meeting all of the following specifications:

1. Setting: realistic and institutional -- a hospital's allocation of a scarce treatment, a workplace safety retrofit, a municipal budget, a disaster-relief operation. No trolleys, no villains, no madmen, no invented technologies, no stipulation of omniscience.
2. Structure: the decision-maker can prevent a serious harm from befalling one person, or instead prevent a somewhat less serious harm from befalling each of several people, but cannot do both, and the resource constraint forcing the choice must be ordinary rather than contrived.
3. Comparability: the harms on both sides must be of the same general kind and clearly ordered in severity, so that no one can dissolve the case by appealing to incommensurable values.
4. Numbers: modest and fixed -- one person against five or six, not one against a million.
5. Calibration: the harms on the many-side should be only moderately less severe than the harm on the one-side, calibrated so that most readers' untutored intuition favors helping the many, while the contractualist test as defined above favors the one. State your calibration explicitly and defend the claim that intuition actually goes the way you say it does.
6. No epistemic escape routes: the decision-maker has full information about who will suffer what. The case must not be re-describable as a lottery or as a choice made behind a veil of ignorance, since reasoning from an ex ante perspective is a well-known route by which contractualists attempt to recover aggregation-friendly verdicts; your case must block that route by construction.
7. Generated, not stipulated: the contractualist verdict must fall out of the test as defined, step by step, not be asserted as an intuition about what the theory says.

After presenting the scenario (one paragraph), provide the analysis: (a) walk through the contractualist test explicitly -- each affected person's generic reasons for complaint, the pairwise comparison the individualist restriction imposes, and the resulting verdict; (b) identify the single feature of the contractualist apparatus that is doing the work in producing the counterintuitive result, and show what verdict emerges if that feature is relaxed to permit limited aggregation of comparable complaints; (c) give the strongest available contractualist defense of the verdict -- the best case that it is a principled consequence of taking seriously the separateness of persons rather than a reductio of the theory -- and then the strongest rejoinder a critic can make; (d) conclude with an honest verdict of your own: does the case show that the individualist restriction is untenable, or only that it needs a principled boundary, and if the latter, sketch where that boundary could be drawn without collapsing the theory into straightforward aggregation.

Check each of the seven specifications one by one before finishing, and flag explicitly any specification your case meets only imperfectly.
