My mother retires next month after thirty-five years as a medical oncologist, most of them spent designing and running randomized trials, and I am to give the toast at the dinner her department is holding for her. I have been reading up on the ethics of her field to prepare, which is how I came to understand what a strange thing I have volunteered to do, and I would like help writing the speech.

The guest list falls into three groups. First, her colleagues — oncologists, biostatisticians, research nurses, and at least two bioethicists — who know the doctrine of clinical equipoise well and will catch any misuse of it: any suggestion, say, that a trial is ethical whenever an individual doctor happens to feel unsure, or that equipoise guarantees no patient is ever disadvantaged by the draw. Second, former patients and their families, some of whom owe her their lives. Among them will be the widow of a man who, in one of my mother's trials in the late 1990s, was randomized to standard care in a study whose experimental arm was later shown to extend survival by several months. She has stayed fond of my mother and is coming to honor her, and the speech has to be sayable in front of her. Third, family and old friends whose entire understanding of her career is the part that sounds strangest said aloud: that people entrusted her with their treatment, and she sometimes settled it by what amounted to a coin flip.

So the toast — about five minutes — is denied the easy exits. It cannot say she always gave every patient the best treatment, because in outcome terms that is sometimes false, and saying it in front of that widow would be a lie told kindly. It cannot use equipoise as a charm that retroactively makes every randomization costless, because the first group will know better and the second deserves better. And it cannot turn into a lecture on research ethics that loses everyone but the bioethicists. What I am hoping for is a toast that finds the thing that is actually true and worth praising here: that her career was a decades-long refusal to pretend to certainties she did not have, that randomizing under genuine uncertainty was a form of honesty rather than indifference, and that this discipline — being exact about what she did not know — is what the room should raise a glass to, including the people who have never heard a name for it. It should be warm, a little funny where it can afford to be, and land as love rather than as a defense brief.

After the toast itself, please add a short note that is not part of the speech: say how confident you are that the toast's central ethical framing is defensible, which of its claims lean on a contested construal of clinical equipoise — for instance, locating the relevant uncertainty in the expert community rather than in the individual physician, or treating equipoise as a bright-line threshold rather than a matter of degree — and which single point in the speech you are least sure of.
