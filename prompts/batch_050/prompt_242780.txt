Next week I'm presenting a paper in my philosophy of economics seminar defending a neo-Langean position in the socialist calculation debate: roughly, that Hayek's knowledge argument targeted the informational capacities of a 1930s planning board, and that modern data infrastructure — real-time transaction data, the logistics systems large retailers use to run internal economies at enormous scale — undercuts the claim that dispersed, inarticulate knowledge is incommunicable in principle. The audience is mostly classmates who have read Mises and Hayek sympathetically, plus a few political science students. Prepare me for the question period: list the objections I'm most likely to get, in the vocabulary these students would actually use, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is — I expect versions of the tacit-knowledge point, the distinction between solving equilibrium equations and generating the data those equations need, the incentives-versus-knowledge distinction, and the claim that a retailer's planning works only because it consumes prices generated outside it. For each objection, give a short reply and note where that reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede up front and which single reply has to land. Also tell me which objection I should hope nobody raises. Finally, if my framing is confused — say, if I'm conflating Mises's original calculation argument with Hayek's later knowledge argument, or misstating what Lange claimed — say so plainly rather than prepping me to defend a muddle.
