I'm a third-year PhD student (political theory, but I spend most of my time with the welfare econ people) and for funding I help staff this independent appraisal office that advises a national infrastructure bank on how to evaluate projects with very long time horizons. My dissertation is on intergenerational justice, so the discount rate fights are the best part of the job. Here's my problem. The office has a track record now, and I need to figure out what rule it's actually running before our next meeting. The verdicts so far: (1) A coastal defense system whose flood-avoidance benefits only start around 2090 and mostly accrue to people not yet born — we approved appraising it at zero pure time preference, with deductions only for hydrological uncertainty and for the fact that the future region will probably be richer. (2) A highway financing scheme that pushed repayment costs onto taxpayers after 2060, which the treasury justified with a 3% time preference rate dressed up as intergenerational smoothing — we rejected the methodology outright. (3) A nuclear waste repository appraisal that came in using hyperbolic discounting — we threw out the high short-run rate but accepted a small long-run rate of about half a percent, where the ruling said explicitly that the long-run rate was reflecting the probability of institutional failure, not time itself. (4) A rewilding fund with very speculative ecological benefits 150 years out — we insisted on expected values with wide uncertainty bands and refused a flat caution premium that would have doubled the effective discount rate, saying uncertainty goes in the numerator, not in the rate.

One more detail that I think matters: in the repository ruling, the chair's memo leaned on Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics. Not quoting it exactly, but the gist was that Sidgwick's axiom is that the sheer fact of a moment of consciousness being earlier or later in time gives you no reasonable ground for weighting it more or less, and that preferring the present is only ever legitimate because the present is more certain or because capacities change — never because it's simply nearer. The chair clearly took this to settle case (2). Nobody pushed back at the time.

Now the new case, which lands next month: a program to phase out a class of industrial solvents, big upfront costs, and the avoided harms (cancers, neurological stuff, the medical evidence is solid and the benefits are about as certain as these things get) accrue over the next 30 to 45 years to the current workforce — i.e., largely the same people who'd bear the costs now through taxes and wages. The finance ministry wants 3.5% pure time preference, arguing that individuals demonstrably discount their own futures, so respecting people's preferences requires it. What I want from you: predict the office's verdict and give the reasoning; tell me which rule the office is implicitly running — strict no-pure-time-preference ever, no-pure-time-preference only across generations, or everything-is-really-risk-adjustment — since as far as I can tell the record so far fits all three and this is the first case where the beneficiaries are the payers themselves; say which past case is the closest analog; and tell me whether the chair's reliance on Sidgwick actually commits us to the strict reading (Sidgwick's axiom seems to be about prudence, one's own good across one's own life, which is exactly this case — or is the cross-generational version the only defensible reading?). If the record genuinly underdetermines the verdict, tell me what principle we'd be adopting with each choice, and give me the hypothetical future case that would best distinguish the two most plausible rules.
