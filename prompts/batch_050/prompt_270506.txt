I work at a small AI safety research org, and at a recent reading group we got stuck on something that seems obvious once you say it out loud. The dominant method for making language models behave ethically is reinforcement learning from human feedback: human annotators are shown pairs of model outputs and asked which is better, a reward model is trained to predict those rankings, and the language model is then optimized against the reward model. Separately, some labs write explicit "constitutions" -- short lists of principles the model is trained to follow -- which seems like a very different mechanism sitting on top of the same pipeline. Our argument was about what this whole apparatus quietly takes for granted, and I want that spelled out carefully.

So: what metaethical view does RLHF-based alignment, as actually practiced, presuppose? It seems to assume that acceptable conduct is recoverable from aggregated human comparative judgments -- that if you collect enough "this response is better than that one" verdicts from contractors working from a few pages of guidelines, the resulting gradient points toward the good, or at least toward the permissible. Does that commit the practice to a form of preference-based or response-dependent theory of value, and rule out any robust moral realism on which the majority of annotators could be systematically wrong? Or is the practice in fact compatible with realism, treating human judgment as merely fallible evidence of an independent moral truth rather than constitutive of it -- and if so, is there anything in the pipeline that could ever detect the case where the evidence and the truth come apart? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, and to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with.

Three further things to address. First, annotator disagreement is real and is handled by aggregation or by guidelines that narrow the discretion; what view of moral disagreement does that presuppose -- that it's noise around a shared core, or that the guidelines get to stipulate which side counts? Second, is the combination of preference-learning with a written constitution coherent, or do the two layers presuppose inconsistent pictures of where ethical content comes from? Third, if we dropped the implicit view and instead built the pipeline on a thoroughgoing moral realism -- or, alternatively, on an expressivist view that annotator rankings are just attitude-expression -- what would concretely have to change in how the system is trained and evaluated?
