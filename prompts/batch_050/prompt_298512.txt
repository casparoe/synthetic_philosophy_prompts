"The tripartite or JTB analysis, which goes back to Plato's Theaetetus, says that knowledge is justified true belief: S knows that p iff p is true, S believes p, and S is justified in believing p. Gettier's 1963 paper showed this can't be right by presenting cases where all three conditions are met but the belief clearly isn't knowledge. His most famous example is the stopped clock case, where you look at a clock that happens to be showing the right time, so you have a justified true belief about what time it is without knowing, because the clock is broken. What all these cases have in common is that the justification works through a false lemma: the subject infers the true belief from something false, so they get lucky. Zagzebski showed that this luck structure is unavoidable, so no analysis of knowledge can ever work, which is why most epistemologists have given up on the project. One prominent response is Nozick's sensitivity condition: S knows that p only if, were p false, S would not believe p. This handles the Gettier cases because in the stopped clock case, had the time been different, you would still have believed it was that time, so your belief isn't sensitive. However, sensitivity faces the objection that it implies we don't know sceptical hypotheses are false, and worse, it makes knowledge non-closed under known entailment, which many find unacceptable. So the response identifies the right diagnosis but its remedy is too costly."
