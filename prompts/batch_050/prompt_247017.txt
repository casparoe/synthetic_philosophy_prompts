The question (they had 25 minutes): "Set out Locke's account of personal identity over time. Then explain Reid's brave officer objection and assess whether it succeeds. If the objection works, does the neo-Lockean appeal to psychological continuity (overlapping chains of memory rather than direct memory) rescue the spirit of Locke's view?"

The student's answer:

"Locke holds that personal identity over time consists in consciousness, specifically memory: person P2 at a later time is the same person as P1 earlier iff P2 can remember P1's experiences from the inside. His famous case of the prince and the cobbler, where the prince wakes with the cobbler's memories and vice versa, is meant to show that bodily continuity is irrelevant -- what matters is where the memories go. So sameness of person is psychological, not physical, and this distinguishes persons from substances like animals or masses of matter, whose identity conditions Locke treats seperately.

Reid's brave officer objection runs like this. Suppose a boy is flogged at school, later becomes a young officer who performs a brave deed, and still later becomes an old general. The officer remembers being flogged; the general remembers the brave deed but has forgotten the flogging. Reid takes this to show that memory is not necessary for personal identity, since the general is obviously the same person as the boy even though he can't remember the flogging. Reid also objects that Locke's account is circular, because we can only tell which memories are genuine -- as opposed to merely apparent -- by checking whether they belong to the same person, which presupposes the very notion of personal identity we're trying to define.

The objection seems to me decisive against Locke, and it also suggests that bodily continuity must be part of the correct account after all, since that's what in fact ties the general to the boy when memory fails. However, the neo-Lockean move to psychological continuity -- overlapping chains of directly connected memories, where the general remembers the officer who remembers the boy -- does fix the problem, because on the chain view the general counts as the same person as the boy even without remembering the flogging, which restores the spirit of Locke's view that identity is psychological rather than bodily."
