A small but growing number of research disputes are now settled through what is sometimes called adversarial collaboration: two researchers or groups who publicly hold opposing positions agree, before any data are gathered, on a joint experimental design, on what each possible outcome would show, and often on a neutral arbiter to supervise the work. Some practitioners extend the same format beyond science. Pairs of partisan commentators occasionally co-write a single piece stating where they actually agree; foundations have funded joint fact-finding exercises between advocacy organizations on opposite sides of a policy question, with both sides pre-committing to publish the results whatever they show; and there are proposals to make such co-designed studies a standard step before regulators rule on contested technical claims.

The practice interests me because of an apparent tension. On its face, it treats the other side as a rational interlocutor whose mind can be changed by evidence. Yet the elaborate scaffolding — the arbiter, the pre-registered predictions, the pre-commitment to publish — seems designed precisely for interlocutors who cannot be trusted to update honestly on their own. And the format is conspicuously applied to factual disputes embedded in value conflicts, while almost never to the value conflicts themselves.

What does this practice presuppose about the nature of political and intellectual disagreement? Please reconstruct its implicit theory: what view of good faith, of belief formation, and of evidence does it only make sense under? Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely sits well with. For instance, does it require that most disagreement stems from asymmetric information or divergent priors rather than motivated reasoning, or would it make equal sense in a world where motivated reasoning is the norm? Does it presuppose that the participants, as opposed to their audiences, are the ones whose beliefs are at stake? Say whether the presuppositions of its parts are consistent with one another — in particular, whether the assumption that one's opponent will reason honestly coheres with the institutional design's evident expectation that they will not — and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories of disagreement would license it equally well. Finally: if we replaced its implicit theory with the view that position-taking on contested topics is primarily an expression of group loyalty rather than evidence-responsive belief, what would have to change in how these collaborations are designed, and would anything worth keeping survive the replacement?
