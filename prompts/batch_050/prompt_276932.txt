So I work on an ML platform team and I've been reading a bunch of AI safety stuff lately, mostly the scalable oversight corner of it. Nobody on my team can agree on anything about it, and half the arguments seem to come from people talking past each other because they never actually located the disagreement. So here's what I want:

Build me a decision tree / flowchart that sorts someone through the space of positions on how you supervise an AI system that's smarter than its supervisor. The end leaves should be actual named proposals from the literature — I want at least these to show up as leaves: AI safety via debate (and I want cross-examination as a separate leaf if you think it actually separates from plain debate), iterated amplification / recursive reward modeling (you can treat these together or split them, but tell me which and why), plain RLHF as the "this doesn't scale but here we are" leaf, weak-to-strong generalization, and whatever the "scrape a bunch of human demonstrations and fine-tune" position is called. Feel free to add a leaf or two I haven't thought of.

The rules for the tree:

- Every node has to be a question someone can answer from their beliefs about ML, systems, or humans, not from knowing the literature. So NOT "do you believe in debate?" but something like "if a superhuman model can always find the most persuasive argument rather than the most true argument, does the most persuasive argument still tend to be the true one?" — questions where the named positions genuinely give different answers.
- Order the tree so the earliest splits are the most load-bearing ones. My guess is the whole space hinges on like 2-3 cruxes (something about whether truth is easier to verify than to produce, something about whether you can decompose hard oversight into easy pieces, maybe something about whether the human even stays in the loop at deployment time) but I'm happy to be told my cruxes are wrong.
- The tree should be built from the logical space first, names second. So: enumerate the answer combinations, then place the actual proposals in the space, then flag any combination nobody occupies and say whether it's incoherent, just unpopular, or an actual gap someone should publish on.
- If two leaves can't be separated by any belief-level question (e.g. if the difference between two proposals is purely an engineering detail), say so explicitly instead of inventing a fake distinguishing question.
- Cap it at like 8-10 questions max depth, this is going on a wiki page not in a dissertation.

Also, for each leaf, one or two sentences on what evidence would make someone at that leaf want to move to a neighboring leaf — like what empirical result kills their position or saves it. Written for engineers, so skip the philosophy seminar voice.
