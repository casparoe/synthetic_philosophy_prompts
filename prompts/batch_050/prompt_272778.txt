In Consciousness Explained, Dennett discusses the color phi phenomenon: two spots of different colors are flashed in sequence at different locations, and subjects report seeing a single spot move across the screen, changing color midway. Since the subject seems to perceive the color change at a position that could only have been identified after the second spot was presented, the question arises of how the brain handles the intervening stretch of experience. Dennett offers two possibilities, each modeled on a method of totalitarian revision. On the Stalinesque model, the brain stages something like a show trial: the experience of the moving, color-changing spot is fabricated before anything reaches consciousness, so the subject never experiences anything amiss. On the Orwellian model, the record is rewritten after the fact: the subject really does experience the first spot stationary and unchanged, but the memory of it is overwritten within a fraction of a second, so all subsequent report is of the revised version. Dennett's own conclusion is that, at these timescales, there is no fact of the matter about which model is correct, and hence no fact of the matter about exactly what the subject experienced when.

A colleague of mine leans on this same analogy to defend the opposite conclusion. She argues that both kinds of regime presuppose a determinate original event: there is always what actually happened, regardless of whether the public ever learns of it, and so Dennett's own analogy commits him to a determinate fact about what was experienced, with the revision merely concealing it. On her reading, the dissolution thesis refutes itself by the very analogy used to motivate it.

Please audit this analogy rather than adjudicate Dennett interpretation in general. Specifically: (1) Give the explicit correspondence table: what in the brain plays the role of the staged trial, the rewritten archive, the censors, the event itself, and crucially the public to whom information is or is not revealed. (2) Identify which disanalogies matter for the dissolution thesis and which are idle: for instance, regimes presuppose a determinate audience and a determinate moment of publication, archives persist for years while the relevant neural processes unfold within a few hundred milliseconds, and archival revision leaves physical traces whereas nothing in the brain need distinguish a content never experienced from one experienced and forgotten. (3) Assess my colleague's move: does the analogy's presupposition of a genuine original record get inherited by the brain case, or is the absence of a privileged observer precisely the feature the analogy was constructed to bracket? (4) Test both models against a second case: the familiar sort of dream that appears to build up, with apparent narrative preparation, to the very stimulus that wakes the sleeper, as in the classic report of a long dream culminating in an execution at the instant the bedboard falls on the dreamer's neck. Does each model yield a different verdict about such dreams, and is there any evidence, behavioral or memorial, that could in principle discriminate between them? (5) If the totalitarian analogy cannot carry the dissolution, propose a better one, for instance a document that passes through multiple drafts with no authoritative final version, and say whether that better analogy still supports Dennett's conclusion or only something weaker.
