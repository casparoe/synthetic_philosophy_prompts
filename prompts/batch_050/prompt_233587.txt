A popular book on the physics of time gives this account of the Rietdijk–Putnam argument: "Special relativity tells us that simultaneity is relative: observers in relative motion disagree about which distant events count as happening now. Rietdijk and Putnam turned this into a proof that the future is already determined. An observer walking past me can regard as present an event that still lies in my future, so that event must be just as real as my present; and what is real for one observer is real simpliciter. Hence the whole of spacetime — past, present, and future — exists all at once: the block universe. It follows that the flow of time is an illusion, and since the future already exists, free will is impossible."

I have a physics background and no quarrel with the relativity, but I suspect something philosophical is being passed off as physics here. Audit the summary: which steps would a philosopher of physics accept as given by special relativity itself, which require extra premises the book does not state, and which of the book's conclusions — determinism, the unreality of temporal flow, the impossibility of free will — actually follow once the argument is stated carefully? In particular, is there a reading on which the argument establishes something about the reality of future events without establishing that the future is fixed, and if so, where does the book's version go past it?
