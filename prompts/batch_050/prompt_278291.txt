So I did a master's in public policy and now work at a state environmental agency, and the cost-benefit analysis we run on every major regulation has been bugging me for a while now, philosophically I mean. How it works: we monetize everything using willingness to pay, either from surveys or from revealed-preference studies. Reduced asthma cases, avoided flood damage, and yes, statistical lives get a dollar figure too. Then we just sum across everyone affected, and if the benefits exceed the costs the rule passes. Distribution formally doesn't matter -- a dollar of benefit to a wealthy homeowner counts the same as a dollar of benefit to someone poor, because willingness to pay already builds in ability to pay. Sometimes we tack on a qualitative "equity analysis" at the end, but it can't change the verdict. My office defends all this as the only objective way to do things, as opposed to politics.

Here's what's confusing me though. The same government -- sometimes my own colleagues in the economic development department down the hall -- justifies other policies in completely different terms. Industrial subsidies get defended as strengthening the national economy, infrastructure as securing the region's future, trade restrictions in terms of national interest, and everyone talks about inflation and GDP growth as if the economy were a patient that can be healthy or sick. Nobody ever asks whether the people who benefit from "energy security" would collectively be willing to pay more for it than it costs -- and if you tried to run that argument through our CBA framework, I'm pretty sure it would fail or at least be inadmissible, because "the nation's strength" isn't in anyone's willingness-to-pay column. My roommate from grad school, who did philosophy, says the economists' approach assumes there's nothing to society over and above individuals and their preferences, and she likes to throw Thatcher's old line about there being no such thing as society at me.

So what I want to know: what does our cost-benefit practice actually presuppose about what a society is and what has value? Does it only make sense if you think welfare attaches exclusively to individuals and that adding it up across people is meaningful -- ruling out group-level goods, or the idea that a community could have interests nobody has, or rights that can't be traded off against money? Or could someone who rejects all that still run exactly the same spreadsheet, treating it as a mere decision heuristic rather than a theory of the good? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory, but also be careful to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where it's just underdetermined. And then: if we concluded that the holistic talk down the hall is the correct view (or that rights constrain aggregation), what would concretely have to change about how we do CBA -- distributional weights, rights screens, scrapping willingness to pay, or could most of it survive intact?
