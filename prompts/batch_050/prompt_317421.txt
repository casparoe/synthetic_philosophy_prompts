After forty years in experimental physics, I have in retirement joined a philosophy reading group, and the organizer has asked each of us to write down our philosophical profile on the ontology of mathematics so that the others can attack it. I found this surprisingly difficult. Physics trained me to be relaxed about whatever entities the formalism needs, but the moment I try to say, in my own voice, what numbers and functions actually are, I find myself pulled in several directions at once. Before I present my profile next month, I would like an audit. Here are my commitments, stated as honestly as I can manage. First, I am broadly Quinean about ontology in science: the entities I am warranted in believing in are those over which our best-confirmed theories indispensably quantify — I believed in the Higgs boson, so to speak, because the Standard Model could not do without it, and I see no principled reason to apply a different standard elsewhere. Second, I cannot make sense of how anyone could have knowledge of objects that are causally inert and outside space and time; if something cannot affect us or our instruments, even indirectly, then belief about it seems unconstrained in a way that knowledge cannot be. Third, statements of pure mathematics — that there are infinitely many primes, or that a given Diophantine equation has no solutions — are objectively true or false whether or not anyone ever proves them, and in many cases we plainly know them. Fourth, the indispensable use of mathematics in physics does not by itself establish the truth of the mathematics used; a formalism can do its predictive work as a mere instrument, as I take it we already grant in other corners of physics. Fifth, mathematical truths, unlike truths about Sherlock Holmes, are necessary — they could not have been otherwise — and this necessity is a large part of what separates mathematics from fiction.

What I want is not a lecture on platonism and nominalism in general but a diagnosis of this particular set. Please go through my five commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which pairs only appear to be; where a pair looks inconsistent but is not, say so explicitly and explain what dissolves the appearance. For each genuine tension, rank its severity, state what the cheapest revision would be, and say what that revision would cost me — for instance, whether it would force me to abandon something I care about elsewhere, such as my picture of scientific knowledge or my conviction that arithmetic is not up to us. Finally, if you had to recommend a single change that would make the whole profile as coherent as it can be while staying recognizable as the view of a working physicist, tell me which commitment to amend and why.
