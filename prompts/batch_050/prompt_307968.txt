For my ancient philosophy reading group I'm presenting the Epicurean argument about death, and I built a version around two lines from Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus that aren't the usual "when we are, death is not" bit:

"Whatsoever causes no annoyance when it is present, causes only a groundless pain in the expectation."

"And even as men choose of food not merely and simply the larger portion, but the more pleasant, so the wise seek to enjoy the time which is most pleasant and not merely that which is longest."

Here's my reconstruction:

(1) Something is bad for me only if there is some time at which it makes me worse off -- that's just what the first line says.
(2) Before I die, death hasn't arrived, so it isn't making me worse off yet.
(3) Once I'm dead, I don't exist, and a nonexistent person can't be made worse off -- nothing can annoy them when present.
(4) So there's no time at which death makes me worse off; by (1), death is not bad for me.
(5) If being dead isn't bad for me, then bringing it about instantly and painlessly can't be bad for me either, since it just swaps my current state for a state that harms nobody.
(6) So an instantaneous, painless, secret murder of a sleeping hermit with no friends harms no one.
(7) But (6) is absurd, so something in (1)-(5) has to give.

I'm pretty attached to (1) -- how can anything hurt me if I never so much as notice it? -- and I really dont want to accept (6). But here's the twist that's bugging me: the second line makes it look like Epicurus would happily swallow a milder version of the consequence anyway (no reason to prefer forty more years of pleasant life to one). So two questions: which step do I actually reject and what does rejecting it cost me? And does the first line really commit Epicurus to (1), or is he saying something narrower that my reconstruction overreads?
