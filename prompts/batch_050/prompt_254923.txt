You're an expert in measurement theory and the pedagogy of AI safety who has written university-level exams before. Think carefully about this one. Before drafting anything, brainstorm 3-4 candidate exam framings and give me one line each on why the easy/obvious ones won't discriminate between students, then pick the best and commit to it.

Build the exam around this scenario (which i made up, so definately don't go looking for it):

The city of Vellis pays its bus drivers a bonus on their on-time arrival rate, measured by GPS pings at scheduled stops. In year one, drivers stop waiting for late-transferring passengers and skip stops where nobody is visibly waiting. In year two the transit authority adds a rider-satisfaction component from in-app surveys; drivers start being conspicuously friendly in the minutes before the survey prompt fires and keep a box of candy by the door. In year three the authority, having read the same blogs we have, switches to a secret audit metric — a composite of anonymous spot-checker reports and schedule adherence under unusual conditions like rain and street festivals — and tells drivers only that a hidden quality index now sets the bonus. Within eighteen months the spot-checkers' reports have drifted rosy (drivers have worked out who some of them are and where they board), and the hidden metric and actual rider experience have visibly decoupled.

The exam question (one question, 90 minutes, closed book, 20 marks, aimed at smart adults with no formal training) should have three parts:

(a) Walk through the scenario year by year and say which variant(s) of Goodhart's law — using the standard four-way split of regressional, extremal, causal, and adversarial — best explain each failure, and flag any point where the taxonomy strains or two variants overlap.

(b) I'm coining a term for what happens in year three: "Goodhart leakage" — my claim is that any audit metric, even a secret one, eventually degrades once its outputs feed back into real decisions, because people infer the metric's shadow from who gets bonused and who doesn't. The question should ask candidates to defend or attack this claim, and to say what would distinguish genuine leakage from ordinary adversarial Goodhart.

(c) Candidates propose one concrete fix for Vellis and then pre-mortem their own fix: the most likely way it gets gamed within two years.

Deliverables:
1. The exam question as it would appear on the paper, with mark allocations for (a)-(c) summing to 20.
2. A model answer of ~600-800 words, written at the level of the best student you've ever had, not at the level of a textbook.
3. A marking scheme built around exactly five must-hit points, each with its mark value, and for each one a single line on the most common way candidates fumble it — e.g., treating adversarial gaming as the only real Goodhart, assuming piling on more metrics is always an improvement, or writing a fix in (c) and then refusing to actually attack it.

One hard constraint: nothing in the question or model answer should be answerable by reciting the taxonomy. The exam is testing whether candidates can tell the difference between a metric being gamed and a metric that was only ever a shadow of the goal in the first place.
