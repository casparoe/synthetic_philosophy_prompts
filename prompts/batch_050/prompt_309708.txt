In the discussion of games in the Philosophical Investigations (around §§65–67), Wittgenstein defends the unity of our concepts by analogy with family resemblance: there is no single feature running through all games, only a network of overlapping and criss-crossing similarities, just as members of a family resemble one another in build, features, eye colour, or temperament without any one trait being shared by all. The analogy is routinely made to do heavy anti-essentialist work: because concepts are like families, the Socratic demand for necessary and sufficient conditions is misplaced, and a concept can be perfectly usable despite having blurred edges. I would like you to audit how far the analogy actually holds. Give the mapping explicitly: what plays the role of the family members, the shared traits, the relative who groups them. Then press on the disanalogies. The most serious one, it seems to me, is that actual families are unified by an underlying essence after all, namely genetic relatedness, of which the visible resemblances are mere symptoms; does that smuggle essence back in through the back door and undercut the anti-essentialist lesson, or does it simply show that Wittgenstein's concern was the criteria by which we learn and apply concepts, not whatever hidden structure may underlie them? Consider also that families have sharper boundaries than the analogy suggests (one either is or is not related), yet resemblance admits of degree; and that families include members by marriage and adoption, who may share no traits and no genes with the rest. Say whether the anti-essentialist conclusion survives the audit, which of the disanalogies matter for its intended use and which are idle, and if the analogy fails, what better one would carry the same argumentative weight.
