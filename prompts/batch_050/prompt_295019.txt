Having retired after forty years in molecular biology, I have lately developed a serious interest in philosophy, and I now help run a reading group on decision theory at my local university, where I occasionally grade short written exercises. Below is an examination-style question I set for the group, followed by a member's answer. Please grade the answer out of 100, and justify your grade with specific, point-by-point comments that identify what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong, and why each error matters. Present your assessment as exactly five numbered points, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding remarks: the first point should state the numerical grade, and the remaining four should each address a distinct strength or weakness of the answer.

The question I set was the following. Some philosophers claim that deliberation crowds out prediction: while you are genuinely deliberating about what to do, you cannot assign probabilities to your own prospective actions, because treating your eventual choice as something already to be forecast would settle the very question that deliberation is supposed to resolve. Is this claim correct? If so, explain what it implies for decision theories that assign probabilities to an agent's own acts; if not, explain how an agent can consistently hold credences about her own choice while it is still under deliberation.

The answer I am grading reads as follows. "The claim is correct, and it is best understood as a point about the practical difficulty of self-knowledge: human beings are simply very poor at forecasting their own behaviour, so any probabilities they assign to their own acts would be unreliable, which is why deliberating agents ought not to bother. The slogan that deliberation crowds out prediction is due to Savage, who used it to motivate his framework of subjective expected utility, in which probabilities attach to states and outcomes but never to acts. This framework shows that a complete decision theory can and must avoid probabilities over the agent's own options. A further virtue of the view is that it dissolves Newcomb-style problems for evidential decision theory: if the agent cannot treat her own prospective act as evidence during deliberation, then the act cannot function as a symptom of a prior state of the world, so there is nothing to weigh against causal considerations. One might object that at the end of deliberation the agent assigns probability one to the act she is about to perform, and that this is still a probability assigned to her own choice; but this is harmless, since probability one merely records the outcome of deliberation rather than competing with it. Hence the claim stands: while deliberation is open, prediction of one's own act is excluded, and decision theory should be built accordingly."
