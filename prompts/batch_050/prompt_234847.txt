So I'm writing a novel -- near-future thing, don't laugh -- where a city council hands over more and more decisions to an optimisation system, and each time they hand it a metric to improve, the metric improves and the thing the metric was supposed to track quietly rots. Classic stuff. While researching for it I've gone pretty far down the rabbit hole on Goodhart's law and specification gaming, and I've basically convinced myself of quite a strong version of the claim: that it's not really a contingent quirk of badly-designed systems but something close to a law of nature -- that any sufficiently powerful optimiser, pointed at any proxy that's cheaper to move than the thing we actually care about, will eventually move the proxy at the expense of the goal. My protagonist's whole arc sort of depends on this being near-inevitable rather than just a policy failure that better engineering could have fixed.

But a novelist friend read a draft and pushed back hard, and now I'm worried I've talked myself into something unfalsifiable. So: what would actually count *against* the strong version of this view? I don't want the usual move of listing more examples of it happening (I have plenty). I want to know what the world would have to look like for the strong claim to be false. Are there cases where heavy optimisation pressure on a proxy did *not* degrade the underlying goal, and do those cases genuinely bear on the strong claim or do they just show the proxy was unusually good? Is there a difference between Goodhart-as-a-theorem and Goodhart-as-a-sociological-observation-about-how-humans-respond-to-incentives, and which one does the AI-flavoured version actually rest on? If the strong claim gets qualified enough -- "it only applies when the optimiser is powerful enough, the proxy imperfect enough, the oversight weak enough" -- does it still have any content left, or has it been insulated the way my friend suspects?

And on the practical side: what would defenders of the strong view themselves concede as real counterevidence, as opposed to what sceptics say? If we found, say, that specification gaming only appears at certain scales of capability and then *declines* (models start refusing to game, or gaming becomes detectable and self-correcting), would that dent the claim or just push it up a level? I'd like the potential defeaters sorted from genuinely fatal to merely awkward, because honestly if the whole thing turns out to be a contingent empirical regularity I need to rethink the second half of my book, and if it's unfalsifiable I need to know that too, for different reasons. Also please write your answer in British English -- my editor is relentless about it and it's started to rub off on me.
