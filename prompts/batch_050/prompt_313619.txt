I'm a PhD student in cognitive neuroscience (so, adjacent to this but not in the phil mind weeds myself) and I keep getting dragged into the same argument at lab meetings and it never goes anywhere: is the brain literally a computer, or is that just a metaphor we've all gotten too comfortable with?

Here's the thing that bugs me. Everyone in the room seems to agree on all the actual science -- nobody is disputing anything about spikes, synapses, tuning curves, whatever -- and yet you get one camp saying cognition just IS computation and any theory of mind will be a computational theory, and another camp saying the brain is a dynamical system, it's embodied, it doesn't manipulate symbols, the computer metaphor is a historical accident we'd be better off without. And then there's a third move that confuses me most, from the philosophy side: apparently there are arguments that any sufficiently complex physical system implements basically any computation (a rock, a wall, etc.), which would seem to make "the brain computes" trivially true and therefore empty, and computationalists respond with accounts of what makes a computation *real* -- causal structure, teleofunction, whatever -- and then the other side disputes those. So the whole debate might hinge on what the word "computation" even means.

My hypothesis is that this isn't one disagreement but at least two or three stacked on top of each other: a semantic one about what "computation" picks out, an empirical one about which mathematical formalisms best explain neural activity, and maybe a methodological one about what a good explanation in cog sci has to look like. And people keep sliding between them mid-sentence.

Am I right about that? Diagnose the debate for me: what's verbal, what's substantive, where is the hidden empirical question (if there is one) actually doing work, and is there anything -- a formal result about implementation, a kind of experiment, a conceptual distinction -- that would genuinely settle any of the layers? I want the diagnosis with reasons, grounded in what the sides actually argue, not a survey of positions, and if part of it is just unresolvable bedrock intuitions, say that honestly rather than pretending there's a fix.
