I'm a backend engineer, and years of distributed systems work has drilled the Two Generals Problem into me: two generals on opposite hills, messengers who might get captured, and no finite number of acknowledgment round-trips ever produces real common knowledge of the attack time. Fine, internalized, moved on. But recently a coworker explained the centipede game to me at lunch and I fell down a bit of a rabbit hole, and apparently the standard justifications for stuff like backward induction and iterated elimination of dominated strategies leans on an assumption called "common knowledge of rationality" -- everyone is rational, everyone knows everyone is rational, everyone knows that everyone knows that, out to infinity.

My immediate gut reaction was: hang on, that's exactly the thing the Two Generals says you can't have. If common knowledge is basically the consensus concept from distributed systems, and consensus over a lossy channel is provably unreachable, how do game theorists get to just *assume* it? But then I second-guessed myself, because the generals' result is specifically about *establishing* agreement through messages, and maybe the game theory assumption isn't about establishing anything -- it's just describing a state that either holds or doesn't, like when the rules of chess get announced out loud to both players in the same room. So the analogy I've been carrying around is "common knowledge = consensus, therefore common knowledge of rationality is as unattainable as consensus over a flaky network," and I've been using it (in my head, and once somewhat unwisely in a slack argument) to claim that backward-induction conclusions like the centipede's first-move take are built on sand.

Can you audit this analogy for me? Start with the correspondence table: when the target is two players sitting across a table who both know the rules of the game, what plays the role of the generals, the messengers, the channel, and the attack time? Then where the mapping breaks -- specifically whether "unattainable" in the systems sense translates to "unreasonable to assume" in the game theory sense, and whether rationality (a fact about what's going on inside someone's head) behaves differently from the attack plan (a fact created by a decision and then communicated) in a way that matters. Then the verdict: does the impossibility result actually undermine common knowledge of rationality as an assumption, does it leave it untouched, or is the truth somewhere more annoying in between -- like, maybe actual humans only track three or four levels of "they know that I know," and it's specifically the results that need the full infinite hierarchy that are fragile? And if there's a better analogy for what common knowledge of rationality actually is (a public announcement? a fixed point? a protocol everyone compiled against?), give me that one and tell me what extra it buys.
