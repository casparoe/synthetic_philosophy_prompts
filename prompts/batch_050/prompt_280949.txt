Write a satirical in-house style guide: an internal memo from the Ministry of Public Information of an unnamed fictional country, addressed to newspaper editors and radio broadcasters, setting out the approved rules for how the current food shortage may be described. Around 600-700 words, in deadpan bureaucratic prose, formatted with numbered rules grouped under short section headings (for example: Approved Terminology; Weather Attribution; Trade and Transport Matters; Handling of Foreign Correspondents; Statistical Guidance; a brief closing FAQ for editors with questions).

The humor must come from getting the political economy of famine right, not from exaggerating it. The rules should calmly enforce what the literature on famines says real governments actually do:

- All shortages must be attributed to drought, flood, or blight, regardless of what the meteorological records show; there is a standing rule that rainfall figures are never to be published alongside food-price figures.
- The word "famine" is prohibited in all media. Approved substitutes include "a regionally concentrated episode of food stress" and "an adjustment in household entitlements."
- Grain continuing to leave the affected region by rail and boat is to be described as routine commercial activity; any suggestion that exports be halted is defeatist and economically illiterate.
- Food availability in the aggregate remains adequate, and editors are to say so, without addressing who can currently afford it.
- Independent newspapers will be temporarily suspended for the duration of the weather, in the public interest.
- Mortality statistics may be published only relative to a baseline that the ministry will supply later.

A reader who knows the standard critique of the food-availability-decline view of famines should be able to reconstruct that critique from the rules alone: that famines are characteristically failures of entitlement, purchasing power, and distribution rather than of aggregate supply; that exports, hoarding, and price spikes routinely continue through them; and that a government answerable to a free press and an electorate behaves differently from one that is not. The memo must never state any of this, name any theory, or break character to wink at the reader.

Do not name real countries, leaders, scholars, or historical famines. The fictional ministry should feel as though it could belong to a wartime province in 1943, an island in the 1840s, or a collectivized countryside in 1960 without being identifiable as any of them. The target of the satire is the style of discourse that launders political choices into acts of nature, not any particular regime.
