EXAM QUESTION:
(a) Using one of Gettier's two original cases, explain what a Gettier case is and what it is supposed to show about the analysis of knowledge as justified true belief. (b) Consider the "no false lemmas" response: knowledge requires that the subject's justification not essentially depend on any false premise. Does this addition solve the problem raised in part (a)? Defend your answer, and consider at least one possible counterexample to the fix.

STUDENT'S ANSWER:
(a) In his 1963 paper Gettier presented two counterexamples to the traditional analysis of knowledge as justified true belief. In the first case, Smith and Jones have both applied for the same job, and Smith has strong evidence that Jones will get it (the president of the company has told him so) and also that Jones has ten coins in his pocket. From these Smith infers: the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket. As it turns out, Smith himself gets the job, and by sheer coincidence Smith also has ten coins in his pocket. So Smith's belief is justified and true, but intuitively he doesn't know it -- the belief is only true by luck, because it rests on the false premise that Jones will get the job. Gettier's second case works the same way: Smith infers "either Jones owns a Ford or Brown is in Barcelona" from his justified belief that Jones owns a Ford, and the disjunction happens to be true only because Brown is in fact in Barcelona. From these cases Gettier concludes that knowledge cannot be given any reductive analysis at all, since justification and truth can always come apart through luck.

(b) The no-false-lemmas response adds a fourth condition to the analysis: S knows that p only if S's justification does not essentially depend on any false premise. This clearly handles both of Gettier's cases, since Smith's reasoning passes through a false premise in each ("Jones will get the job" and "Jones owns a Ford"). It also handles the other standard examples in the literature, like the fake barn case: Henry is driving through countryside filled with barn facades, stops, and correctly believes "that's a barn." The no-false-lemmas condition correctly rules this out as knowledge, because Henry's belief rests on the false premise that the structures around him are genuine barns. The condition might be thought too strong -- maybe some everyday inferences pass through harmless falsehoods -- but as a response to Gettier-style counterexamples it seems to succeed. So the Gettier problem is solved by requiring that justification not route through any false lemmas.
