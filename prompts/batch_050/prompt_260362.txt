I have recently been thinking about mental causation after deciding to leave a long-term relationship. It felt obvious that my reasons, reservations, and eventual resolution caused my decision; yet when I tried to explain this to a friend with a neuroscience background, she replied that the behaviour was already fully accounted for by neural events. I now find it difficult to say what causal work is left for the mental level without either denying the completeness of physical causation or treating the mind as a redundant extra.

Write a proposal for a one-semester undergraduate philosophy term paper of roughly 5,000–6,000 words on the causal exclusion argument. The paper should investigate a deliberately narrower question: can a mental state count as causing behaviour not by producing an additional physical effect, but by channelling or selecting which sufficient neural process becomes operative? Develop and assess a distinction between production causation, where one event generates another, and routing causation, where a higher-level state constrains the path by which lower-level processes lead to an outcome. Use original examples rather than relying on standard cases, and do not provide a bibliography or literature citations; positions may be described generically, as I shall supply the references later.

The proposal should state the thesis question precisely, explain why familiar responses to causal exclusion leave this routing option underexplored, and justify the proposed method in comparison with simply defending mind-brain identity, accepting epiphenomenalism, or denying causal closure. It should include an outline of the intended argument, the strongest objection that a routing cause is merely a redescription of neural causation, and what would count as success or failure. Finally, give a realistic section-by-section plan for a semester and identify any parts of the project that would need to be excluded to keep it manageable.
