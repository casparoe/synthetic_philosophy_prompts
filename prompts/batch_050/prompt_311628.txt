So I'm a third-year philosophy undergrad and longtime reader of LessWrong/Slate Star Codex, and I need to hand in a proposal for a semester term paper (about 6000 words, one semester). The topic grew out of something I keep running into on rationalist blogs and forums: the claim that certain skills — riding a bike, research taste, noticing when you're confused — can't be learned from books because the relevant knowledge is tacit and resists being put into words. Then in my philosophy of mind seminar we did the classic knowing-how vs. knowing-that debate: Ryle's regress argument against the idea that intelligent action is steered by contemplating propositions, and the intellectualist comeback (Stanley and Williamson style) that knowing how to F is really just knowing, of some way w, that w is a way to F, grasped in a practical rather than theoretical way. What struck me is that the two discourses barely talk to each other, and that the analytic debate seems weirdly stuck on one-person cases — the ski instructor who knows how but can't do it anymore, the chick sexer who can do it but can't say how.

My half-formed angle: shift the debate from possession to transmission. If someone wrote a genuinely complete manual for a skill — every rule, cue, and corrective spelled out — and a novice who studied it could perform the skill without any practice or feedback, what would that show? Intellectualists seem commited to full linguistic transmission being possible in principle; Ryleans seem committed to the manual only ever being a scaffold for practice. But when I tried to pin down what the two sides actually predict about such cases, I kept suspecting they slide between transmitting the information and transmitting the ability, and that the disagreement might partly dissolve once those are separated. So the paper question would be something like: does the intellectualism debate have any real stake in whether knowledge-how is fully communicable by description alone, and if not, what does that tell us about what the debate is actually about?

Please turn this into a proper proposal. I need: the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; why the standard arguments on both sides leave it open; what I'd actually do over the semester (which cases to analyze, in what order, and what the argumentative steps are); what a positive and a negative result would each look like; and an honest assessment of where this could blow up into something too big for six thousand words.
