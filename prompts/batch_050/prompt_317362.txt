I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun reading Charles Sanders Peirce. I would like to test whether I understand how his epistemology might extend beyond the scientific problems of his own period.

Imagine a near-future laboratory accused of fabricating an experiment. The prosecution produces high-resolution videos of the apparatus, computer logs, calibration records, and images of the relevant detector traces. The defense does not deny that such traces could have occurred. Instead, it demonstrates that current generative systems can produce physically consistent videos, detector outputs, metadata, and calibration histories from a brief textual description. Even the laboratory’s cryptographic signing system is not decisive: the defense shows that a stolen signing key, a compromised logging server, or a generated record inserted before archival would produce the same evidence.

The case raises a broader problem. Historically, recordings have served as what I shall call an epistemic backstop: although they are not infallible, the cost and difficulty of fabricating them have allowed them to discipline testimony and resolve disputes. Generative technology weakens this backstop in at least three ways. It creates persuasive false records; it allows possessors of genuine but damaging records to dismiss them as fabrications; and it increases the cost of adjudication because every record may require investigation of its provenance, generation process, and institutional custody.

What would Peirce be likely to say about this development? In particular:

1. Would he regard it as a qualitatively new epistemic crisis, or merely an especially rapid version of familiar problems concerning testimony, instruments, forgery, and the interpretation of signs?

2. Would his account of signs imply that no recording was ever self-authenticating in the first place? If so, would the loss of the epistemic backstop mark the removal of a practical shortcut rather than the collapse of a foundational source of knowledge?

3. How would Peirce balance his fallibilism against the practical need to reach verdicts before inquiry has run its course? His remarks about inquiry and the long-run convergence of opinion may apply to an indefinite scientific community, but courts, regulators, and laboratory directors must act under deadlines.

4. Would he favor technical attempts to restore the backstop—cryptographic provenance, tamper-evident instruments, registration of datasets before analysis—or would he regard these as only partial signs whose authority likewise depends upon communal practices of checking and interpretation?

5. What procedural remedies would follow from a Peircean view? I can imagine redundant measurements using independent physical principles, public calibration standards, adversarial replication, separation between data collection and analysis, and incentives for exposing defects in one another’s methods. Which of these would most plausibly reflect his conception of inquiry rather than merely contemporary scientific good practice?

6. The most troubling consequence may not be that false evidence becomes easy to manufacture, but that true evidence becomes too easy to contest. How would Peirce respond when universal fallibilism is strategically exploited to create an asymmetry in which inconvenient records face unlimited scrutiny while preferred beliefs face none?

Please reconstruct the position Peirce would most likely take, rather than simply attributing to him a generic pragmatist distrust of certainty. Where his views admit of competing developments, identify the tension and state which resolution you consider more faithful to his overall philosophy. I am interested in the quality of the inference, not in bibliographical references.
