A recent episode of a philosophy podcast summarized T. M. Scanlon's contractualism as follows: 'Scanlon holds that an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any principle that anyone affected by it could reasonably reject, which in practice hands every affected person a veto over any principle that burdens them. And because each person's complaint must be weighed on its own, contractualism cannot aggregate lesser interests: a million people spared mild headaches could never outweigh one person's death, since no single headache sufferer has a complaint comparable to the person who stands to die. This is the famous aggregation problem, and it shows the theory breaks down in emergencies -- in a mass-casualty triage situation, contractualism would forbid saving the greater number, since each of the few left to die could reasonably reject a principle that sacrifices them for the many.' I know Scanlon does emphasize the complaints of individuals, so parts of this ring true, but I suspect at least the final inference is too quick. Which parts of this summary would Scanlon or a sympathetic contractualist accept, which are subtle misrepresentations or overstatements, and does the conclusion about triage actually follow from the accurate parts? Please indicate how confident you are in each of your main judgments, and single out the one point in your assessment that you are least sure about.
