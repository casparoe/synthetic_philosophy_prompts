So I somehow ended up on my town's historical commission — I argued too much at a town hall meeting about a plaque and someone decided that qualified me for public service. Our chair, Marta, has been doing this for twenty years and votes in a way that feels eerily consistent, but I cant pin down the rule. This is bugging me extra because my reading group just did Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, the one on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, with the three modes — monumental (the past as inspiring model for present greatness, at the risk of falsifying it), antiquarian (preserving and revering everything that made you, at the risk of mummifying the town), and critical (judging and condemning the past so life can go on, at the risk of sawing off the branch you grew from) — and I keep suspecting Marta is secretly running that playbook. There's a vote coming up where I really want to know what she'll do.

Her record, as best I've reconstructed it from old minutes and gossip: in 2011 she pushed to spend real money restoring a derelict 1890s bandstand that nobody famous ever touched, on grounds that a town owes something to the people who built for it. In 2014 she voted to strip a segregationist mayor's name off the public library, over loud objections that this erased history; her line was roughly that you can remember a man without keeping him on the door. In 2016 she championed commissioning a heroic bronze of a local strike leader and, I heard, waved off complaints that the design smoothed out the woman's messier politics — a town that can't look up to anyone stops doing anything. In 2019 she voted *against* landmarking an entire block of unremarkable postwar storefronts, saying a town that keeps everything has no room to build. In 2021 she voted to keep the statue of our town founder but move it off the courthouse steps into the history museum's courtyard with new signage. And in 2023 she voted against sandblasting an offensive 1920s frieze off the old post office, and instead got a counter-mural funded next to it.

The new case: that founder statue got vandalized twice this year and is now in storage, and a sculptor has proposed melting it down and recasting the very same bronze into a memorial for the mill workers who died in the founder's factories. Reusing the metal roughly halves the cost and the foundry slot only exists this spring. Options on the table: reinstall with a new plaque, do the 2021 museum move, or the melt-down. What I want from you: predict her vote and the rationale she'll give at the meeting. Tell me which rule best explains the whole record — mode-triage (she asks what the town needs right now: model, anchor, or judgment), never-destroy-only-add-or-move, or honors-are-revocable-but-artifacts-aren't — and show me how the other two rules *also* fit all six votes, because I think they do. Name the closest precedent and exactly where it stops helping. Then give me the hypothetical case that would actually seperate the two rules this vote can't distinguish. Finally: if I want the melt-down to pass, what's my best argument in her own idiom, and what Nietzsche-flavored worry is she going to throw back at me?
