So I spent last month backpacking and staying in hostels, and I kept having the same argument with a guy I met. He travels for a living, and he's convinced that national character is real and useful: he claims he can predict, way better than chance, stuff like whether a shopkeeper will bargain, whether strangers will queue properly, whether "I'll be there at 7" means 7:00 or 7:45, just from knowing what country he's in. I pushed back that this is all stereotype plus confirmation bias plus the fact that he's mostly noticing institutions and wealth levels, not "character." But honestly I can't fully convince myself, because some of his predictions did seem to land. His credence that "national character generalizations are often true and useful" is around 0.9; mine is more like 0.3.

Give me your own credence in the following proposition, stated precisely enough to have a truth value: knowing a person's nationality provides predictive information about their everyday behavior and dispositions that goes beyond what you'd get from knowing their country's wealth, institutions, and the situation they're in, and this extra information is reliable enough to justify at least some low-stakes generalizations (like how to approach a first business meeting). But I mostly want the decomposition, because I suspect my disagreement with this guy isn't about the headline number at all. Break it into sub-questions like: how likely is it that within-country clustering on behavioral/psychological traits is real and not a measurement artifact of self-report surveys; how likely such clusters survive controlling for wealth, institutions, urbanization, and religion; whether observed clusters reflect anything deep about people or just equilibria that would flip within a generation if incentives changed (call this the "institutional equilibrium" worry -- the thing where a population looks collectively honest mainly because cheating is currently punished); whether the kind of predictions my hostel friend makes are the kind national character could even in principle explain; and whether any of it licenses generalizing about individuals rather than distributions. Also factor in the selection effect problem: travelers mostly meet service workers, other travelers, and people who chose to interact with foreigners, so the "nationals" being sampled are wildly non-random.

Format: a table with columns: sub_question, your_probability, justification (one line), and load_bearing (high/medium/low -- how much the final number moves if this term moves). Below the table, give me your combined credence with one sentence on how the terms combine, then two more fields: weakest_link (the term you're least sure of) and what_would_move_it (one piece of evidence or argument that would shift it). Finally, tell me whether my 0.3 and his 0.9 most likely disagree about the empirical clustering question or about the generalization-licensing question.
