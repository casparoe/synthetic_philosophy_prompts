So I run this informal philosophy-and-TV group in my department (mostly grad students, plus a couple of undergrads who wander in for the pizza), and next week we're doing The Good Place. I've watched the whole thing twice now, and the reading I keep circling back to is karma — not in the vague "what goes around comes around" bumper-sticker sense, but karma as an actual theory of moral desert, the kind you get in classical Indian philosphy where actions generate fruits (karmaphala) that have to be cashed out eventually, if not in this life then in a later one. I want you to read the show through that lens, seriously, as though it were a long thought experiment about whether moral desert can even be computed.

Some things I'd want the reading to actually take on. The afterlife's points system is basically karmic bookkeeping made bureaucratic, but classical karma theories usually need rebirth to make the ledger balance — the show collapses everything into a single lifetime's tally, and I wonder what that does to the theory. Tahani raised billions for charity and still ended up in the Bad Place because her motives were vanity and a lifelong grudge against her sister; Buddhist karma famously makes intention (cetana) pretty much the whole ballgame, so is the show implicitly Buddhist about desert rather than outcome-focused? And then there's the late-series twist that nobody has qualified for the Good Place in over five hundred years because modern life is too entangled — buy your grandmother flowers and you come out negative because of pesticide runoff and exploited farmworkers upstream in the supply chain. That struck me as a dramatization of the old worry that the fruits of action are invisible and untraceable (the adṛṣṭa problem), except the show treats that as fatal to the system rather than as a reason to trust the cosmic accountant. Does that objection genuinely cut against karma as a desert theory, or only against a crudely consequentialist scoreboard that a real karma theorist would disown anyway?

Last thing: the show's eventual fix is to scrap desert altogether and rebuild the afterlife so everyone gets as many tries as they need to become a better person. Read that ending against the desert literature — is the show conceding that moral desert itself was the mistake, the way critics argue karma ends up blaming people for suffering they couldn't possibly have deserved? And where does the show resist the karma reading entirely — a defender of classical karma would presumably say that points handed out by demons with clipboards has nothing to do with karma as a causal-moral law woven into the universe. I want a reading that's faithful in both directions: not a plot recap with karma labels pasted on, but also not a distortion of the Indian material to make a network sitcom look smarter than it is. If the show dramatizes something the philosophy of desert misses, tell me what. Honestly, Doug Forcett alone — the guy who figured out the point system as a teenager and spent his whole life miserably optimizing it — feels like a better objection to desert-maximizing morality than most journal articles I've read, and I'd love to see that spelled out.
