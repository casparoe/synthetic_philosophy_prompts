So i'm drafting a short essay for a blog-ish thing on disagreement in economics and philosophy, and I'd love comments before I post it. Here's the draft:

---

Economists often assume that rational agents share a common prior -- before anyone learns anything, everyone assigns the same probabilities to everything, and disagreements only ever come from different information. Defenders say this isn't an extra assumption at all but just part of what rationality means: if two Bayesians with common knowledge of each other's posteriors still disagree, then at least one of them failed to update properly, since Aumann's result shows rational agents can't agree to disagree. So common priors fall out of Bayesianism for free.

But I think this defense confuses two things. Aumann's theorem assumes the common prior as an input; it doesn't derive it. What the theorem really shows is that *given* a common prior, disagreement can't be common knowledge between rational agents. That's a conditional, not a justification of the antecedent.

A better defense, I think, comes from the no-trade results: if people really had different priors, we'd expect endless speculative trade purely from disagreement, and since we don't observe that, common priors must be roughly true. The problem is this argument proves too much -- we plainly do observe huge volumes of speculation, so by the same logic common priors are empirically false, and the whole framework collapses. My tentative conclusion: the common prior assumption is best read not as a claim about what people beleive but as a modeling choice that buys you tractability at the cost of ruling out the most interesting cases of disagreement by fiat.

---
