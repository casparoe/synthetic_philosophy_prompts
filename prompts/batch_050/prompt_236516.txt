I am a third-year undergraduate in a course on classical American pragmatism, and next week I have to run a twenty-five-minute seminar section for about a dozen classmates on Charles Sanders Peirce's 1878 paper How to Make Our Ideas Clear. The passage I have chosen to anchor the session is the moment where Peirce, having worked through the dispute between Catholics and Protestants over the sacrament, writes: "Our idea of anything is our idea of its sensible effects; and if we fancy that we have any other we deceive ourselves, and mistake a mere sensation accompanying the thought for a part of the thought itself." I want the section to teach the pragmatic maxim through this sentence, because most of the class will have skimmed the maxim's official statement and retained only a vague slogan about meaning and practical consequences, and I suspect the sacrament passage is where the doctrine actually shows what work it does.

Please do not explain the passage to me; plan how I should teach it. I need the dependency structure first: what has to be in place before this sentence can be heard correctly, and in what order. For instance, does the distinction between a sensation that merely accompanies a thought and the content of the thought itself need to come before any examples, and do I need the earlier grades of clearness from the paper, or can I smuggle in just enough of them? Second, advise me on the one example that should carry the session: should it be Peirce's own wine-and-bread case, or something from later in the paper such as the diamond left in cotton wool, and what must that example have and what must it lack if it is to show the maxim as a rule for clarifying concepts rather than as a claim that only laboratory-testable statements mean anything? Third, map the misconceptions this audience will predictably form: that Peirce is saying truth is whatever is useful, that he is dismissing the sacrament dispute as mere superstition rather than making a point about what the dispute could even amount to, that he is a crude verificationist, and that the maxim is a theory of truth rather than a rule of method. Tell me at exactly which point in the sequence each of these should be headed off, since I think some are best killed early and others should be allowed to form and then corrected. Fourth, give me one question I can pose in the last five minutes whose answers would show whether the maxim has actually landed rather than been memorized, and finally tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, such as the semiotic machinery or Peirce's later renaming of the doctrine, and how to say so without losing the room.
