For years I've been arguing about this with people in my life, and the debate never moves: suppose I need to get out of a dinner invitation, and instead of inventing a false excuse I say something strictly true but calculated to mislead -- "I have a deadline this week," knowing they'll conclude I'm busy that night when actually the deadline is easily manageable. Everyone I've asked agrees that the outright lie would be wrong. But they split cleanly on the true-but-misleading version: half say it's exactly as bad, since the listener walks away deceived either way, and half say it's clearly better, because you respected the truth and the listener bears some responsibility for the inference. What strikes me is that nobody on either side ever cites new considerations -- both sides seem to agree on everything that happened, and then just diverge. My own suspicion is that the dispute is really about what the wrongness of lying consists in: the harm done to the deceived person versus a violation of a duty the speaker owes regardless of consequences -- and that once you see that, the surface question is settled by whichever deeper theory you already hold. Am I right that this is the real structure, or is there something else at issue -- a disagreement about what listeners are entitled to assume in conversation, say, or about what counts as causing a false belief? And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what progress would even look like here: is there a distinction or a kind of case that could settle it, or are the two sides committed to starting points that argument cannot reach?
