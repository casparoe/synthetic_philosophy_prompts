I teach a seminar on technology and society, and every year the same stalemate recurs. One group of students reads the determinist canon and concludes that technologies develop according to an inner logic and remake societies around themselves; another reads the social-constructivist literature and concludes that every apparently necessary feature of a technology was a contingent choice by identifiable people, so nothing is ever determined by the artifact itself. Both sides know the other's standard cases — the low bridges on the Long Island parkways, the mechanical tomato harvester, the QWERTY keyboard — and both sides have a rehearsed rebuttal for each. A decade of scholarship in the middle, including Langdon Winner's own work, seems not to have settled anything either: Winner argued that artifacts can have politics, then later complained that social constructivism opens the black box of technology and finds it empty. Nobody has moved.

Here is a passage from Winner's essay "Sow's Ears from Silk Purses: The Strange Alchemy of Technological Visionaries" (in *Technological Visions*, 2004), which is not one of his famous lines but which I suspect is diagnostic:

"... the arrival of any new technology that has significant power and practical potential always brings with it a wave of visionary enthusiasm that anticipates the rise of a utopian social order ... this has been the recurring theme: celebrate! The moment of redemption is at hand."

My hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the determinism debate has stalled because both sides are really arguing about how to respond to this recurring wave of visionary enthusiasm — the determinists half-believing it, the constructivists defining themselves against it — rather than disagreeing about any checkable claim. If that is right, the disagreement is not over facts about particular technologies at all.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, with reasons, not a survey of positions. Specifically:

1. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? A factual dispute about causal pathways (do technical properties constrain social arrangements, or only enable them)? A verbal dispute trading on the ambiguity of "determines" and "politics"? Two camps answering different questions — one about large-scale historical patterns, one about particular design decisions — under a single heading? Or a dispute about which cases count as evidence, where each side's chosen examples are invisible to the other's framework?
2. Does the Winner passage support my hypothesis, undermine it, or cut across it? Take a position on what the passage, read carefully, implies about why the debate keeps regenerating with each new technology.
3. Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves part of the dispute, an agreed method for adjudicating cases like the parkway bridges, or an argument that no resolution should be expected because the disagreement is doing work for both sides? If you think part of the dispute is genuine and resolvable, say which part and what would resolve it.

Ground the diagnosis in the actual structure of the two positions as they are standardly argued, not in caricatures of either.

End your answer with a final line beginning "Diagnosis:" followed by one sentence naming the kind of disagreement this is and what, if anything, could move it.
