I've been reading alignment writing on and off for years — the old sequences, various forum posts, the occasional paper summary — and a friend from my local reading group and I got into an argument last week that I couldn't settle to my own satisfaction. The standard framing distinguishes intent alignment (an AI that is trying to do what its operator wants it to do) from value alignment (an AI that shares or promotes genuinely good human values). My friend's position, which I find worryingly hard to refute, is that value alignment is impossible in principle: humanity has never agreed on its values, any particular specification of "the good" would be a contested moral claim that billions of people would reject, and so "aligning AI with human values" is not a hard problem but an incoherent one — like being asked to draw a square circle. On her view, alignment researchers should either admit that value alignment is secretly a partisan political project (imposing some particular group's values on everyone) or quietly redefine the field as being only about intent alignment, which at least picks out a well-defined target.

My instinct is that this proves too much, but I can't articulate exactly where the argument slips. So my questions: first, is she right that the value alignment problem, as commonly gestured at, is definitionally unsolvable in a way intent alignment is not? Second, if we fully solved intent alignment — say we could build systems that faithfully try to do what their operators intend, with all the attendant difficulties handled — would anything of substance remain of the original value alignment concern, or was "value alignment" always just intent alignment aimed at operators we happen to approve of, dressed up in moralized language? I'd like an answer that engages with why the field bothers to maintain both terms at all, rather than just picking a side, because I suspect the distinction is doing real work that my friend's argument flattens.
