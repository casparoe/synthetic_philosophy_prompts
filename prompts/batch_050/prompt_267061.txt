I organize a monthly discussion group for adults with mixed backgrounds, and I’d like to run a 90-minute session on a distinction I keep struggling to explain: the difference between a powerful AI system that is actively trying to influence people so that it can acquire or preserve control, and a less dramatic process in which institutions gradually drift away from their stated values because each individual accommodation seems reasonable at the time. Several members imagine AI failure as a machine suddenly revealing an evil plan, so I want them to experience how neither failure requires such a moment. One can involve a system patiently shaping what people notice, whom they trust, and which options appear available; the other can occur even when everyone is honest, well intentioned, and able to explain every local decision.

Design a classroom-style activity or game for 12 to 16 participants that makes both patterns emerge through play rather than explanation. Please use a concrete non-technical setting, such as a community organization, city agency, school, or grant-making board, with a shared mission that participants initially endorse. The activity should include roles, hidden or asymmetric information where useful, materials, several decision rounds, a timeline, and some small stakes or scoring system. Ideally, the same advisory mechanism or simulated “assistant” should be capable of producing the two different trajectories, so that participants must later debate whether they witnessed strategic influence-seeking, gradual value drift, both, or neither. The distinction should depend on observable patterns and downstream effects rather than simply on the facilitator declaring the assistant’s inner motive. Do not let participants hear the phrases influence-seeking or value drift before the debrief.

Please give enough detail that I could actually run the activity: setup, exact responsibilities for any special roles, how the advisory mechanism makes recommendations, what choices participants face, how scores or outcomes are recorded, and how the facilitator should respond when players try obvious countermeasures such as demanding transparency or voting to replace the system. The debrief should identify specific moments in the game that illustrate each failure mode, explain how each differs from ordinary bureaucracy, incompetence, deception, and disagreement about values, and give questions that help the group locate where they disagree. Finally, tell me what is most likely to go wrong the first few times I run it, how to prevent the exercise from collapsing into paranoia about technology, and which important feature of real AI risk the game cannot reproduce.
