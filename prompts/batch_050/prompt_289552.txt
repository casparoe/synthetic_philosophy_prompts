So I did a stint as a research assistant for a foreign policy think tank (nothing glamorous) and I keep noticing two things in how people there use history that I don't have words for, and I'm hoping someone does.

First one. Someone picks a historical case to argue with — Munich '38, Vietnam, the run-up to WWI, whatever — and at first it's doing honest work: here's a past situation, here's what happened, maybe the same logic applies. But then at some point the analogy quietly changes jobs. It stops being evidence and becomes a filing system. Every new fact about the current situation gets sorted into the analogy's roles: this leader is the Hitler figure, that treaty is the piece of paper, domestic critics are the appeasers, the early warning signs are the "signals we missed last time." And here's the part that really gets me — once the filing system is running, nothing can count against it. If the current leader does something Hitler never did, that just shows he's being cleverer this time. The analogy becomes unfalsifiable not because anyone defends it as unfalsifiable but because every incoming fact gets pre-processed into a supporting role before anyone evaluates it. Is there a name for that specific move — the flip from analogy-as-argument to analogy-as-sorting-machine? It's clearly cousins with confirmation bias and motivated reasoning, but those are about evidence generally, and this feels more specific: it's about a *narrative template* doing the filtering, and the template is historical.

Second thing, maybe related. Whole cohorts seem to pick their analogy as a reaction to the previous cohort's analogy. The people who grew up on "Munich proves you must stand firm" run policy for a while, overreach, and then their successors grow up on "Vietnam proves you must stay out" — and then *their* successors react against *that*. It's like each generation's big historical lesson is just the negation of the last generation's big mistake, and the actual current situation is sort of an afterthought. I've seen "generational forgetting" floated but that's about forgetting, and this is almost the opposite — everyone remembers history vividly, just always the same one chapter, chosen by trauma rather than by resemblance to the present.

Do either of these have established names? If they do, tell me what the nearest concepts are and where my description doesn't quite fit them. If they don't, coin something and give me boundary cases — like, when is reasoning from a historical case legitimate vs. when has it become the filing-system thing, because presumably the answer isn't "never use analogies."

Also curious whether the two patterns connect: does the seesaw thing in part two *feed* the filing-system thing in part one, since the chapter you picked by trauma is exactly the one you'll defend to the death?
