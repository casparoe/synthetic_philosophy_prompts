I was at a department mixer last week (I do admin work for a biology program, so I'm around these conversations without really being in them) and two of the grad students got into it over the human chin of all things, and then roped me in as a judge even though I barely followed half of it. I'm writing it up here because I genuinely can't tell who won and it's bugging me. Tell me who was right about what.

It started with Priya saying the chin is the textbook case of a spandrel -- it's just what happens when the jaw shrinks back during human evolution while the lower face grows a certain way, and asking "what's the chin FOR?" is exactly the mistake that old Gould and Lewontin paper was warning about, like asking what the curved triangles under a cathedral dome are for. Marcus pushed back that this confuses two things: sure, the chin might be a byproduct, but byproduct claims also have to be earned, and the way you earn them is by first checking whether adaptationist explanations work -- adaptationism is a research strategy, not a foregone conclusion. He also said "it's just development" isn't a real answer, because developmental systems themselves evolved, so you've just moved the question back a step.

Priya's counter was that adaptationist stories are basically unfalsifiable in practice -- when one functional story fails you can always invent another, whereas drift is the null hypothesis and the null shouldn't need defending. Marcus said drift isn't free either: neutrality makes its own predictions about things like how much variation a trait should show, and those often fail too, and calling something a "constraint" is just as much a just-so story unless you've actually measured the developmental system. Her last move was that even so, the burden of proof is asymmetric: claiming a trait has a function is a stronger claim than claiming it doesn't, so spandrel-by-default is the methodologically safer starting point. He replied that there's no neutral default at all -- assuming spandrel is also a hypothesis and it shapes what people bother to measure; he claimed researchers ignored chin biomechanics for ages precisely because the byproduct story made it seem settled.
