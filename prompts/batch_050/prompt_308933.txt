I've spent the past semester in a reading group on political obligation, and I keep discovering that my considered judgments about civil disobedience don't obviously cohere with one another. Rather than adopt a theory from the literature wholesale, I'd like to work from my verdicts on cases and see what principle, if any, they amount to. Here they are, as honestly as I can report them. First: stopping at a red light at three in the morning on completely empty roads — I do stop, and I think I genuinely ought to; running it strikes me as wrong, if only mildly, and not merely imprudent. Second: a young man who secretly evades conscription into a war he reasonably judges to be deeply unjust, say by leaving the country quietly — I judge this fully permissible, even admirable, and I don't think he owes anyone a public statement or submission to punishment. Third: activists who openly blockade a munitions factory supplying that same unjust war, identify themselves, and accept arrest — permissible and praiseworthy, and somehow the paradigm of justified disobedience. Fourth: someone who sneaks into the same factory at night and disables equipment, harming no one and achieving roughly what the blockade achieves — I find I judge this unjustified in a reasonably just, democratic state, though I concede the cause and the covertness are shared with cases where I rule the other way. Fifth: a citizen who quietly withholds the portion of her taxes that funds the war — I judge this unjustified, even though it looks structurally similar to the draft evader: covert, nonviolent noncompliance with an unjust imposition. Sixth: jurors who refuse to convict a defendant under a law they conscientiously judge unjust — I judge this permissible, even though it is covert, even though it frustrates the law's application rather than merely exempting the dissenter, and even though the jurors accepted an official role.

So the tensions are these: the draft evader and the tax resister seem like the same shape of act, yet I split them; the blockade and the sabotage share a cause and differ mainly in publicity, yet the draft evader shows I don't think publicity is necessary; and the red light case suggests I believe law as such carries some independent weight, which sits awkwardly with how readily I excuse violations elsewhere. What I'd like from you: the most precise principle (or small family of candidate principles) that captures as many of these six verdicts as possible, stated with enough care that I can see which case motivates each clause. If no clean principle saves all six, tell me which verdict you would overturn first and why, rather than papering over the conflict with ad hoc qualifications. Please also address whether the principle needs a clause restricting it to reasonably just states, or whether the obligation to obey simply scales with a regime's justice. Finally, apply your best principle to two cases I haven't resolved: a restaurant owner in a segregated town who quietly, continually serves integrated parties in violation of a local ordinance, and a doctor who covertly provides a treatment the state has banned on what she reasonably regards as captured, corrupt regulatory grounds. If your principle gives a verdict on these that I should find surprising, say so plainly.
