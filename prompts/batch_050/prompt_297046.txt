A friend of mine who took an ancient philosophy course summarized Aristotle's sea battle discussion in De Interpretatione 9 for me like this: "Aristotle considers the following argument. If it is true today that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, then the battle cannot fail to happen -- it is necessitated by the prior truth of the prediction -- and deliberation about whether to fight is pointless. Since Aristotle holds that deliberation is evidently meaningful, he concludes that statements about contingent future events are neither true nor false until the event occurs. He thereby refutes logical fatalism by rejecting bivalence, though at the cost of abandoning classical two-valued logic in favor of a three-valued one."

I have two worries I can't adjudicate. First, the step from "it is true that there will be a battle" to "the battle is necessitated" strikes me as a move Aristotle might be diagnosing as a fallacy rather than endorsing, but my friend presents it as the argument's sound engine. Second, I've heard that reading Aristotle as denying bivalence is one interpretation among several, not the settled meaning of the text, and that three-valued logic is a much later development. Which parts of my friend's summary would specialists accept, which parts present a contested reading as established fact, and does his closing verdict -- refuted fatalism, at the price of classical logic -- survive once those corrections are made?
