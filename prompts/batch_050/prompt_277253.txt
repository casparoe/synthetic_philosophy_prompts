I am grading the answers this week, and I would like an independent, rigorous assessment of one of them before I return it.

The exercise quoted this sentence from Book I, chapter 2:

"The premisses must be primary and indemonstrable; otherwise they will require demonstration in order to be known, since to have knowledge, if it be not accidental knowledge, of things which are demonstrable, means precisely to have a demonstration of them."

Question: Explain what Aristotle is claiming here and why he takes it to follow that a science must rest on indemonstrable first principles. Then consider whether his condition, generalized from demonstration to justification of any kind, tells against (a) coherentism, the view that beliefs are justified by their mutual support within a web, and (b) infinitism, the view that a belief can be justified by a chain of reasons that neither repeats nor terminates.

Student's answer:

Aristotle's claim is that demonstration cannot go all the way down. A demonstration establishes its conclusion from premises, but if those premises are themselves demonstrable, then knowing them in the non-accidental sense would require demonstrating them in turn, and so on. Either this continues without end or the chain stops at premises that are primary and indemonstrable. Since Aristotle regards the endless option as untenable, he concludes that scientific knowledge must rest on basic truths known without demonstration. In modern terms, this makes him a foundationalist: knowledge has a two-tier structure, with indemonstrable first principles at the base supporting everything derived from them.

The passage also has consequences for the two main modern rivals to foundationalism. Coherentism is essentially the circular case that Aristotle's condition excludes. If beliefs justify one another mutually, then each premise is in effect demonstrated from others in the system, so nothing in the web is primary, and a circle of demonstrations cannot produce knowledge, since its members are never prior to one another in the required sense. Aristotle's priority condition therefore rules coherentism out directly.

Infinitism fares no better. If every premise required a further reason, justification would have infinitely many links, and a finite mind cannot possess, let alone survey, infinitely many reasons. No such chain could ever be completed, so on the infinitist picture no belief would ever be fully justified. Aristotle's demand that the chain terminate is therefore not an optional assumption but something forced on us by our finitude.

So the passage commits Aristotle to the view that the first principles of a science must be known in some non-demonstrative way, though this particular sentence does not explain the mechanism by which we know them.
