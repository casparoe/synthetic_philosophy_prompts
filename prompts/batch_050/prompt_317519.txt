**Not All Races to the Bottom Are the Same Race**

Ever since Scott Alexander's Meditations on Moloch gave a name to the phenomenon, "multipolar traps" have become a standard piece of vocabulary: situations where everyone would prefer a collective outcome, yet competition among many actors drives each of them, rationally, toward an outcome all of them deplore. Arms races, clickbait journalism, grade inflation, working-hours escalation, doping in sport. The standard moral drawn from such cases is that the cure is coordination: a treaty, a regulator, a norm, a cartel of the virtuous. Get everyone to agree, bind everyone to the agreement, and the trap closes.

I want to argue that this moral is too coarse, because it treats structurally different situations as if they were one. Multipolar traps vary along at least two axes that change what a remedy would even look like, and lumping them together leads to prescriptions that fail predictably.

The first axis is the observability of defection. In some traps, each actor can see whether the others are holding the line: an arms control regime can in principle be verified; a league-wide doping ban can be tested for. In others, defection is effectively invisible, because it happens inside organizations and gets aggregated away. A newsroom that degrades its standards does not defect in any single observable act; it defects through a hundred small editorial decisions, each defensible, whose sum is clickbait. Call this *defection laundering*: competitive pressure rarely forces anyone to defect outright; more often it makes defection untraceable by diffusing it across intermediaries, metrics, and inheritance of decisions made by predecessors, so that no individual ever experiences themselves as crossing a line. Where defection is observable, agreements plus verification can work. Where it is laundered, there is no decision point at which to intervene, and remedies aimed at decision points — pledges, codes of conduct, ethics review gates — will mostly produce compliance theater.

The second axis is the reversibility of the loss. Some goods eroded by competition can be rebuilt: a compressed work week can be restored; prices can rise again after a price war. Others are closer to one-way stocks: trust in an institution, an ecosystem pushed past a threshold, a talent pipeline that collapsed a decade ago and therefore supplies no senior mentors today. This matters because of what I will call the *ratchet asymmetry*. Our institutions are mostly designed to prevent descents — to hold a line — and very rarely to climb back up. But the coalition needed to reverse a ratchet is different from the coalition needed to hold one. The beneficiaries of a descent are typically organized, incumbent, and few; the beneficiaries of restoration are diffuse, uncertain about what they have lost, and often not yet born or not yet hired. Preventing a race to the bottom and reversing one are therefore not the same project at different times; they are different political problems with different constituencies, and conflating them is one reason restoration efforts so often amount to nostalgia rather than strategy.

Putting the two axes together yields four types of trap, and my claim is that anti-Moloch interventions should be evaluated not by whether they "achieve coordination" in the abstract but by whether they change the underlying structure: making laundered defection visible (say, by forcing metrics into the open or shortening the chain between decision and consequence), converting irreversible losses into recoverable ones (say, by maintaining redundancy the market would not pay for), or — where neither is possible — deliberately shrinking the number of players so that defection becomes attributable again. Coordination is sometimes the means to these ends, but it is not itself the end, and coordination achieved over an unchanged structure tends to decay.

Two further implications, stated briefly. First, appeals to individual virtue are usually derided in this literature as naive, but they have a specific, limited function in laundered-defection traps: the person who refuses the small, deniable, individually rational degradation is often the only place where a diffused process acquires a visible decision point at all. Virtue does not solve the trap; it converts an invisible trap into a visible one, which may be a precondition for anything else. Second, the reversibility axis suggests a triage principle that I suspect will be unpopular: irreversible-stock traps deserve disproportionate preventive effort even when the probability of descent is lower, because the expected cost of being wrong is asymmetric. We intuitively do the opposite, attending to the crises already underway.
