Since retiring from my university post in experimental physics, I have joined a small philosophy reading group, and we have spent the past month working through Plato's Meno. What has stayed with me is not the doctrine of recollection but the value problem raised near the dialogue's end: why knowledge should be prized above mere true belief, when both guide action equally well. Socrates' own example is that a man who truly believes the road to Larissa will get you there just as reliably as a man who knows it; his answer is that true beliefs, like the statues of Daedalus, are fine possessions only so long as they stay put, and that they tend to run off unless tethered by an account of why they are true. Our group divided sharply over this. Half of us found the tethering reply satisfying; the other half pressed what I gather is called the swamping problem: if truth is the destination, then a true belief arrives there all the same, and the tether appears to add nothing a traveller should pay for -- much as an excellent espresso is no better for having come from a supremely reliable machine rather than a lucky one.

I would like to mark the end of our meetings with a villanelle that takes this dispute seriously, and I would be grateful if you could write one for me. The two refrains should be voiced respectively by true belief and by knowledge -- the one insisting, in ever so slightly different words each time it returns, that it reaches Larissa just as surely; the other answering, each recurrence, with the image of the tether and the statue that might bolt. The form itself should do philosophical work: the refrains circling one another and failing to converge should enact the dialectic, and when they finally stand side by side in the closing quatrain, the swamping worry should remain audible rather than be resolved -- our discussion ended, honestly, in exactly that unresolved place. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows the dialogue and the contemporary literature on the value of knowledge will recognise the Daedalus image, the Larissa example, and the reliability objection for what they are; but it should also succeed as a poem for the two members of our group who skipped the secondary reading entirely. Please resist the temptation to end on a tidy moral in favour of either party.
