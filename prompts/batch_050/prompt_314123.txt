So I'm working on a piece about the uncomfortable bits of canonical liberal thinkers, and I keep circling around John Stuart Mill. Here's what's tripping me up. In Considerations on Representative Government, in the chapter on how free states should govern dependencies, Mill basically argues that the rule of one people by another is a contradiction in terms — that a foreign power can't actually govern a subject people, only hold it down and use it, treat it like a place to extract revenue for the folks back home. He comes across remarkably anti-colonial, honestly more than I expected.

But here's the thing. Mill spent something like 35 years as an official at the East India Company — he literally drafted the Company's dispatches to India — and in 1858 he argued publicly that the Company, not the Crown or Parliament, should keep running the place. So if he'd already concluded that government of one people by another "cannot exist," wasn't his whole career one long exercise in the thing he said was impossible? Either he was being deeply cynical, cashing the paycheck while writing denunciations of the system that paid him, or there's some carve-out I'm not seeing.

My editor wants me to resolve this cleanly for the piece: was Mill simply a hypocrite, or does the argument in that chapter leave room for something like the East India Company — some special kind of foreign rule that dodges his own objection? If the latter, please explain what the carve-out actually is and why he thought the Company qualified. Also flag it if I'm just misreading what he meant in that chapter — I read a fair amount of philosophy but I'm definately not a specialist, and it's possible the claim is narrower than it sounds.

Whatever the answer is, end with a final line that starts "Verdict:" and sums up in a few words whether Mill was contradicting himself or whether I'm misreading him.
