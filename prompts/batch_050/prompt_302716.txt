I am a retired organic chemist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been working his way through the classics of evolutionary biology and, more recently, through the philosophical literature on the units of selection. In the course of reading Charles Darwin's The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex (second edition, 1874), I was stopped short by the following passage from Chapter V:

"We can see, that in the rudest state of society, the individuals who were the most sagacious, who invented and used the best weapons or traps, and who were best able to defend themselves, would rear the greatest number of offspring. The tribes, which included the largest number of men thus endowed, would increase in number and supplant other tribes. Numbers depend primarily on the means of subsistence, and this depends partly on the physical nature of the country, but in a much higher degree on the arts which are there practised. As a tribe increases and is victorious, it is often still further increased by the absorption of other tribes."

What puzzles me is the pivot that occurs between the first and second sentences. The first reads like textbook individual selection: the sagacious man out-reproduces his tribemates. The second appears to relocate the action to the tribe, which "increases" and "supplants" its neighbours, and the fourth complicates matters further, since absorption is plainly not biological reproduction. I cannot tell whether Darwin is sliding carelessly between levels, reporting the same causal process at two grains of description, or quietly committing himself to something a modern gene-centred theorist would have to disown.

Please write a dialogue of roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words (the passage above does not count towards this) between two fictional interlocutors: Dr Eleanor Vane, who defends a gene- or individual-centred account of selection, and Professor Marcus Okafor, an advocate of multilevel selection theory. The two meet, let us say, as co-panellists lingering after a public lecture, and fall to disputing what this passage actually shows.

I should like the following conditions observed:

- Both characters must be fully competent. Neither is to be a strawman, and each should concede at least one point the other genuinely earns.
- The conversation must turn on the quoted text rather than on the general debate. Have each speaker press the other on particular phrases — for instance "rear the greatest number of offspring", "supplant other tribes", and "the absorption of other tribes" — and demand an interpretation of them, not merely of Darwin in the abstract.
- I would like them to grapple, in terms a newcomer can follow, with at least three specific difficulties the passage raises: whether a tribe's being "supplanted" constitutes differential group reproduction in the relevant sense; whether the fact that tribes grow by absorption and by imitation of "arts", rather than solely by begetting, moves the claim out of the reach of genetic selection altogether (and whether that helps or hinders Professor Okafor); and whether a kin-selection or inclusive-fitness redescription of the first sentence dissolves the second, or merely redescribes it at a lower level.
- There should be real back-and-forth, with interruptions and short exchanges, not alternating set speeches.
- I do not want a contrived resolution. Have them end by agreeing, explicitly, on what observation or formal result would distinguish their two readings of Darwin — if anything would — and if they cannot agree even on that, let the reader see why.

Please write in British English, with each speaker's name marking their turns.
