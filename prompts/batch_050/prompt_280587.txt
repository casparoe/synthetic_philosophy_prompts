Most modern constitutions contain a procedure for their own amendment, and many go further: they declare certain provisions unamendable. The U.S. Constitution conditions amendment on supermajorities of Congress and the states and adds that no state may be deprived of its equal representation in the Senate without its own consent; the German Basic Law makes its commitments to human dignity and democracy permanently ineligible for amendment; and the Indian Supreme Court, since the early 1970s, has asserted the power to strike down amendments that were enacted through the constitution's own amendment procedure, on the ground that they damage the constitution's basic structure. Notably, the entrenching provisions are typically not themselves subject to the stricter rule they announce: the clause that protects the Senate rule can in principle be amended by the ordinary process, and it is contested whether an eternity clause can be removed at all, and if so by whom.

Reconstruct the philosophical theory of legal validity that this family of practices presupposes. In particular: (1) Does the practice of substantively limiting the amendment power presuppose that constitutional validity is not purely procedural -- that a measure can satisfy every formal requirement for amendment and yet fail to become law? What conception of a constitution, as opposed to a mere rule of change, does that require? (2) The basic-structure doctrine seems to distinguish between amending a constitution and destroying or replacing it. What theory of the identity of a legal system over time does that distinction presuppose, and can it be drawn without smuggling in the judges' own substantive preferences under the guise of structure? (3) There is a self-reference puzzle in the background: if the amendment procedure is itself a constitutional provision, is it amendable by its own lights, and can an entrenching clause coherently entrench itself? Say whether the practice as described rests on a confusion of levels -- between the power to change rules and the rules governing that power -- or whether it can be made coherent, and on what terms. (4) Finally, take the rival view seriously: a purely procedural theory on which whatever issues from the prescribed amendment process is valid constitutional law, full stop. State what institutional features of current practice that theory would have to condemn or abolish, and whether anything important would be lost beyond the doctrines it explicitly rejects.
