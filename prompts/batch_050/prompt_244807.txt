I chair the configuration review board at a mid-sized software company. Once a month we vote on proposed changes to our flagship product, and almost every proposal has the same shape: it adds something a real customer segment asked for, and it costs a little -- one more item in a settings menu, one more field in a form, one more modal dialog, a half-second of extra load time. Each change, considered against the product as it currently stands, is obviously worth approving. But over six years of approving these, the product has become the thing our own sales team apologizes for: nobody can find anything, onboarding takes a week, and churn is up. If you offered me a single trade -- the clean 2019 product, minus every feature we added since -- I'd take it instantly. Yet I can't point to a single vote I regret. A colleague who did a philosophy minor told me this is a known structure, something about a puzzle involving a dial with a thousand settings where each notch is an imperceptible increase in pain paired with a cash payment, and apparently there's a literature on whether rational preferences even have to be transitive. I don't need the puzzle explained to me; I need it turned into policy.

Write a decision procedure our board can actually run, grounded in whatever the philosophical literature on these stepwise-bad-sequences cases ( Quinn's puzzle, the debate about cyclic preferences, sorites-style reasoning about vague thresholds) genuinely supports. Constraints: we have ninety minutes a month, five voting members, none with formal training, and each proposal arrives as a one-page summary with projected benefit and projected cost. The procedure needs to say, in order, what we check for each proposal; how to handle the fact that each individual cost is too small to measure reliably on its own; how to keep the board from re-approving the same trade every month under slightly different descriptions; and what to do about the fact that our comparison baseline keeps silently shifting to whatever the product looked like last month. It should distinguish the cases where the right fix is refusing a proposal from the cases where the right fix is re-pricing the comparison (i.e., the problem isn't the vote, it's what we hold fixed when we vote), since I gather the literature disagrees about where the rational failure actually lives.

Be explicit about which steps are mechanical and which require judgment that no procedure can eliminate, and about what the procedure will not catch -- I assume there are real failure modes here (e.g., genuinely transformative features that look like just another notch, or costs that are small per-step but interact nonlinearly) and I'd rather know the blind spots than discover them. Then run the procedure on three worked cases: a proposal to add a sixth export format (benefit: one enterprise client asked; cost: one more menu item and a maintenance burden); a proposal to add an AI assistant panel (benefit: large and strategic; cost: significant new complexity, not incremental); and a proposal to remove two little-used features (which by the procedure's own logic might look like reversing past votes -- tell me whether it is). At least one of the three should come out with an answer my board will hate, and if none does, I suspect the procedure is just ratifying whatever we were going to do anyway.
