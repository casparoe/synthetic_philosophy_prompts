I am a retired experimental physicist who spent forty years in condensed-matter research, and in retirement I have been reading philosophy, where I encountered the literature on the cognitive penetration of perception -- the claim that what one believes, expects, or hopes can alter not merely one's interpretation of what one sees but the seeing itself. This is not an academic curiosity for me. I now volunteer as a referee for two journals and sit on an ad hoc data-integrity panel at my old institute, and a recurring class of dispute lands on us: a researcher's reported observation is challenged as contaminated by expectation. A student reads ambiguous features on a detector image as the predicted signal; an experienced microscopist insists she can see a structure that blinded colleagues cannot find; a field team identifies a phenomenon that, skeptics note, exactly matches the hypothesis their grant depends on. These disputes are decided today by seniority and tone, and I would like something better.

Please write me a procedure our panel could actually run on such a dispute, grounded in what the philosophy and psychology of perception genuinely establish about this problem. It should be a sequence of questions we ask in order, with guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer to each. I want the procedure to force the distinctions that matter and that we currently blur: between expectation altering what is perceived, expectation altering only what is attended to or remembered after the fact, and expectation altering only the report; between trained perceptual expertise, which can be epistemically legitimate, and contamination, which is not -- and how one tells them apart when both present as a veteran's confident eye; and between cases where penetration, if it occurred, would merely add noise and cases where it would make the observation circular -- where the same beliefs that motivated the hypothesis also shaped the perception offered as evidence for it, so that the observation cannot count for or against anything. For each stage, say which safeguard actually addresses that failure mode and which does not: blinding of the observer, blinded re-analysis by an independent rater, preregistered criteria for what would count as the phenomenon, calibration against known samples, and so on. I am told blinding is a panacea, and I doubt it, since it does nothing about the analyst's own priors at the interpretation stage.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases. First, a doctoral student who, after months of failed runs, sees the predicted signature in a noisy spectrum on the day before a deadline. Second, a senior researcher whose trained judgment reliably detects subtle structures that novices and blinded reviewers miss, and whose track record of later vindication is strong -- I want to know whether the procedure protects her or condemns her, and whether that verdict is a feature or a defect. Third, apply it to a case from my own career: a measurement I made in 1989, seeing a small anomaly I had predicted, which was never replicated but which I have privately always believed was real. I expect the procedure to rule against me, and I want it applied honestly anyway, with no special pleading for the panel's own chair.

Finally, be explicit about the procedure's limits. Mark each point where the questions run out and the panel must simply exercise judgment; say what the procedure cannot settle even in principle -- for instance, if the very distinction between perception and judgment is contested, or if all observation is in some degree theory-laden, whether the procedure presupposes a view of perception that not everyone accepts, and if so, which one, and what follows for a panel whose members don't share it.
