Okay so my school has two ancient rules everyone hates: the library closes at 4pm sharp even during finals week, and underclassmen can only check out two books at a time. Student council (which im on) wants both gone. Our history teacher, who's on the faculty commitee, hit us with the Chesterton's fence thing — you know, if you find a fence in a field and you don't know why it's there, you're not allowed to tear it down until you figure out why someone built it. His point: rules like this presumably solved some problem once, so hands off until we know what problem.

Annoying part is, we actually did the homework. The 4pm close apparently traces back to some janitorial contract dispute in the 90s, and the two-book rule was a response to one kid stealing a pile of books in like 2004. Both original reasons are completely dead. When we brought this back to him, the teacher moved the goalposts: he said the original reason doesn't really matter, because rules that survive for decades tend to pick up hidden jobs along the way — like maybe the two-book limit is secretly why there's always something on the shelf to browse and why teachers can assign a book knowing copies actually exist, and we won't find that out until every popular book is permanently checked out.

Meanwhile my friend Priya thinks the fence analogy is rigged from the start. Her version: a lot of school rules aren't fences, they're barnacles. Nobody put them there for a reason, they just grew, and "we've always had it" isn't a purpose, it's just drift. You don't hold a hearing before scraping barnacles off a boat.

So: can you audit both analogies? What maps onto what (fence, farmer, field, tearing it down — what are these actually standing in for?), where does each one break, and what does each one quietly smuggle in? Specific stuff I'm stuck on:

1. The fence picture assumes somebody designed the rule on purpose; the barnacle picture assumes nobody did. What if the truth is weirder — the rule was never designed, but it's not pure drift either, because genuinely useless rules would've gotten repealed at some point? Is there a better third analogy for that kind of thing?
2. My teacher's hidden-jobs move — does that make the fence argument basicly unfalsifiable? How would you ever check whether a rule has a hidden function *before* removing it, or is there no honest way?
3. Two disanalogies pulling opposite directions: a fence costs nothing to leave standing, but a rule costs people something every single day it exists. On the other hand, you can rebuild a fence, and maybe you can't rebuild a tradition once you've killed it. Which of these actually matters for our decision?
4. Fixed up as much as possible, does the fence argument support my teacher's bottom line (leave it alone), or only something weaker like "do the homework first" — which, again, we already did?
