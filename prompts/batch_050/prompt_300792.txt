Please comment on it: (a) whether the asymmetry I claim between the two stock examples is accurate and fairly stated; (b) whether my concluding argument actually engages the indispensability argument as philosophers standardly understand it, or whether I have in fact attacked a different thesis; and (c) what you take to be the single weakest inferential step, with a suggestion for how it might be repaired.

Two cases, one lesson?

Philosophers defending the indispensability of mathematics to science typically reach for two biological showpieces. First, periodical cicadas emerge every thirteen or seventeen years; because these are prime numbers, a brood intersects as rarely as possible with predators whose own population cycles are shorter and periodic. Second, honeybees build hexagonal combs, and the hexagonal tiling partitions a plane into equal-area cells with the least total perimeter.

I want to argue that these cases are run together too quickly, and that only one of them really helps the platonist. In the cicada case, the explanatory work appears to be done by arithmetic itself: the fact that thirteen is prime is not a physical fact, and no amount of further biological detail will displace it from the explanation. The honeycomb case is different. Wax minimisation is a physical process driven by surface tension and energetics; the mathematics of the honeycomb conjecture describes the outcome but does not produce it. Had the physics been different, the same theorem would still be true, but irrelevant to bees. So the hexagons are explained by physics wearing mathematical clothes.

From this I draw a conclusion about the indispensability argument. That argument says we ought to believe in mathematical entities because they are indispensable to our best science. But if the honeycomb case shows that apparently indispensable mathematics is dispensable in principle — replaceable, given patience, by purely physical explanation — then the argument's key premise is weaker than advertised. Only cases like the cicadas, where the mathematics is unreplacable, carry any weight, and such cases are rare. The platonist's evidence base is therefore far thinner than the familiar two-case rhetoric suggests.
