I am a PhD student in political theory designing a fifty-minute introductory seminar on Machiavelli’s distinction between fortuna and virtù for first-year undergraduates who have heard his name but have not read him. Act as a professional philosopher and an experienced teacher of early modern political thought. Think carefully before drafting: begin with a brief brainstorming section identifying the conceptual dependencies, likely misconceptions, and points at which the lesson might go wrong; then provide the teaching plan itself.

I want a plan for the explanation, not a miniature lecture. Organise the session around the following invented case:

A newly elected mayor inherits an empty treasury, a divided council, and intelligence that a regional war may interrupt the city’s grain supply within the year. She can borrow heavily to build a grain reserve, spend the same money placating rival factions, or wait for clearer information. In one continuation, war begins and the reserve saves the city. In another, peace is signed and the costly reserve becomes a political scandal. In a third, news of the borrowing causes hoarding and a grain-price panic before any war begins.

You may refine the case, but preserve three features: the central uncertainty is beyond the mayor’s control; the same decision can lead to different outcomes; and students can separately evaluate the quality of the decision, its outcome, and the mayor’s moral character.

The plan should specify:

1. The order in which the ideas should be introduced, explaining what must already be understood before the terms fortuna and virtù are named. In particular, determine whether students first need distinctions between choice, circumstance, outcome, and moral worth.

2. How the carrying example should be used. Identify the point at which it should be introduced, which details should be revealed only gradually, and when the class should return to each continuation.

3. The misconceptions students are likely to form, in the order they are likely to arise. Include at least: fortuna as sheer bad luck, virtù as ordinary moral virtue, virtù as mere ruthlessness, success as proof of virtù, and fortuna and virtù as two independent quantities rather than a relation between circumstances and capacities. For each misconception, state exactly where in the sequence it should be anticipated and what question or exercise would correct it.

4. How to convey the more demanding point that virtù concerns the fit between an agent’s dispositions, methods, timing, and circumstances. Make clear why virtù can increase the likelihood of success without guaranteeing it, and why fortuna can function as both constraint and opportunity. Avoid reducing the lesson to the slogan that political success is partly a matter of luck.

5. A final diagnostic exercise using a new, compact case. Explain what students must be able to distinguish for the exercise to show that the concept has landed, rather than merely that they have learned the vocabulary.

6. What should deliberately be left out of this first presentation, and why.

Do not provide a bibliography, citations, chapter references, or verbatim passages. Do not present Machiavelli as claiming simply that good ends justify bad means. Use British English and keep the whole response under 1,000 words.
