I serve on the retirement-benefits committee of a mid-size university. Five years ago our employee savings plan switched from opt-in enrollment to automatic enrollment at a 3% contribution rate: participation jumped from 62% to 94%, and almost no one ever changes the default rate. Our plan consultant argues that the data settle what employees want -- choices reveal preferences, he says, and the fact that 94% stay put shows the default arrangement suits them; moving to a required active choice (every employee must pick a rate, with no default at all) would merely impose hassle. A faculty representative on the committee objects that a preference manufactured by the form cannot then be read back off the form -- inertia, not endorsement, may be doing the work -- and she points to an internal survey in which a majority of employees, asked directly, said they would prefer to contribute around 6%. Draft the decision memo I will circulate before our vote: recommend one of the three options (keep automatic enrollment, return to opt-in, or adopt required active choice), put the recommendation first, take the consultant's revealed-preference argument seriously rather than dismissing it, and state explicitly what, if anything, this committee may legitimately infer about employees' good from their behavior. Maximum 450 words.
