I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I help run a monthly reading group at my company on philosophy of mind as it bears on AI. Our discussions keep stalling on the same dispute: when one of our models emits text, or when a vending machine accepts a coin, is there anything in the system that genuinely means something, or is all the meaning located in the heads of the people who built and use it? I'd like a handout in the form of a decision tree that sorts the live positions on original versus derived intentionality. Please anchor the tree in Dennett's two-bitser case: a vending machine designed in the US to detect quarters ends up in a country whose coins are physically indistinguishable from quarters but differently denominated, and it now happily "detects" those. Dennett's point, as I understand it, is that there is no deeper fact about what the machine's internal state really represents beyond its functional role and its history, and that the same holds for us — our intentionality is equally derived, just from natural selection rather than from engineers. Critics in Searle's orbit hold that the intentionality artifacts and symbols borrow from their makers is categorically different from the original intentionality of minds, and that no amount of functional organization crosses that gap.

Build the tree from the logical space of the debate, not from a list of famous names: first identify the questions on which the positions genuinely come apart, then locate the actual positions in the space those questions carve out. Candidate branch nodes might include things like: does fixing all of a system's present functional organization fix what its states represent, or can two functionally identical systems differ in content? If history matters, must the relevant history be natural selection, or can design by engineers confer content in the same way? And does inheriting meaning from designers and users count as a system really meaning something, or only as our treating it as if it did? Every node should be phrased as a question a reader could answer without already knowing which view it leads to. The leaves should include at least: Searle's biological naturalism, Dennett's interpretationism, a teleosemantic view on which selection history fixes content, an informational or causal-covariance view in Dretske's style, and a computational functionalism that grants machines content on the same basis as brains. Note any coherent-looking combinations of answers that no one actually defends, with a sentence diagnosing whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect, and flag any pair of leaves your tree can't genuinely separate rather than pretending it can.

Finally, run the finished tree on two test cases and show its work: the two-bitser itself (does its coin-detecting state mean quarter, the foreign denomination, or is the question ill-posed, according to each leaf?), and a large language model of roughly the kind my company deploys (does anything in it mean something, and if so, in whose virtue?). Throughout the handout, please be explicit about which branch questions are treated as effectively settled by everyone in the debate — I suspect there are very few — and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this, so the reading group can see where the tree's sorting depends on positions that are still live rather than on shared ground. Don't present any of these views as the consensus if there isn't one.
