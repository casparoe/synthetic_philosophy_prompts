You are a professional philosopher with expertise in game theory, collective action problems, and social norms. Think carefully through the following argument before answering; begin by briefly reconstructing its logical structure.

A colleague of mine uses Moloch-style reasoning to argue that moral restraint is irrational in competitive settings, and I think the argument proves too much, but I can't locate the misstep:

(1) In a multipolar race to the bottom, whether the bad outcome occurs depends only negligibly on my own conduct: if I exercise restraint, some competitor simply captures what I forgo, and the race continues as before.

(2) If my choice makes no difference to whether an outcome occurs, the badness of that outcome cannot give me a reason to choose otherwise.

(3) So in such races, the badness of the outcome gives me no reason for restraint; my only reasons for restraint would be prudential side-effects like reputation or punishment.

(4) In anonymous or one-shot competitive settings, those prudential side-effects are absent.

(5) Therefore, in anonymous multipolar races, unrestrained defection is rational for every participant.

(6) But norms of mutual restraint arise only if someone restrains themselves when defection would be individually rational; so if (5) is correct, such norms could never emerge in the absence of a central enforcer.

(7) Yet norms of mutual restraint plainly have emerged without central enforcers — among long-distance merchants, rival villages, and competing researchers.

(8) Hence the argument errs somewhere, though each of (1)–(5) seems individually compelling.

Which step do you reject? Explain why that step looked plausible, and what it costs to reject it — and briefly compare the cost of rejecting it against the cost of rejecting one of the other premises instead.
