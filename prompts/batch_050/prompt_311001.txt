The essay question: "State Bostrom's simulation argument (the trilemma) precisely. Then assess the following claim: if we take seriously a substantial probability that we ourselves are simulated, we should become less 'fanatical' about the far future -- that is, less willing to let the astronomically large value of humanity's long-run potential dominate our moral priorities. Does the simulation argument support this claim? Defend a verdict."

The student's answer:

"Bostrom's simulation argument runs as follows. Given substrate independence -- the thesis that minds don't require carbon-based neurons and could in principle be implemented on other substrates -- a sufficiently advanced ('posthuman') civilization would have enormous computing power, enough to run many detailed simulations of ancestors like us. The argument is a trilemma: at least one of the following is true. (1) Almost no civilizations at our stage survive to become posthuman. (2) Posthuman civilizations almost never run large numbers of ancestor simulations, whether from lack of interest, legal prohibitions, or something else. (3) We are almost certainly living in a simulation, because if the first two disjuncts fail then simulated minds like ours would vastly outnumber non-simulated ones, and by an indifference principle over observers we should take ourselves to be in the majority. Bostrom concludes that each disjunct has probability of roughly one third, and so we should assign substantial credence to our being simulated.

Does this dampen fanaticism about the far future? The fanatical longtermist argument is that the far future could contain astronomically many lives, so anything affecting humanity's long-run trajectory swamps all near-term considerations in expected value. I think the simulation argument undermines this in two ways. First, if we are simulated, our simulation is presumably of limited duration and fidelity: the simulators could shut it down at any point, so the 'astronomical future' available to us is far smaller than the longtermist calculation assumes -- you cannot count trillions of future lives in a universe that might be switched off a few centuries of basement time from now. Second, if we are simulated, then our experiences are not fully real in the morally relevant sense: simulated lives are not grounded in base reality, so the value at stake in our future should be discounted accordingly. Since the argument tells us to assign substantial credence to being simulated, the expected value of the far future is much lower than longtermists claim, and near-term priorities regain their proper weight.

So the simulation argument, on my reading, is a genuine counterweight to longtermist fanaticism: it both shrinks the stake (simulations are fragile and short-lived) and devalues the stake (simulated lives matter less than base-reality ones)."
