So I co-host a small philosophy of science podcast and we've managed to book a guest I've wanted for ages: a philosopher-physicist who's probably the most committed defender going of objective Bayesianism in the Jaynesian mould — the view that, given your evidence, there's a uniquely rational prior, and that the maximum entropy principle (relative to the right constraints and the right measure) is the machine that finds it. He's sharp and he's heard every objection before, so I don't want questions he can answer on autopilot.

Draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he'll most likely give. Here's where I want to press, roughly in this order:

- Start with the Bertrand paradox / parameterisation-dependence problem: maxent gives different answers depending on how you carve up the possibility space. He'll say transformation groups fix the measure — I want the follow-up to ask what happens in realistic inference problems where there's no natural symmetry group lying around, which is most of them.
- A question on where the constraints themselves come from: if I pick my moment constraints based on my data, isn't the "objective" prior just smuggling my judgement back in through the side door? Follow-up should push on whether there's any non-circular way to say which expectations are the right ones to hold fixed.
- Something on the Jeffreys prior as a rival claim to the "unique rational prior" crown — does he take maxent and Jeffreys to coincide in the nice cases, or does he have to say one is wrong?
- A question on updating: Jaynesians treat maxent as an updating rule that generalises Bayes' rule and Jeffrey conditionalisation, but there are cases where maxent updating and strict conditionalisation pull apart when new evidence bears on old constraints. Ask him which one blinks.
- The marginalisation paradox / inconsistency under coarse-graining worries: if your posterior depends on how finely you partition the outcome space, in what sense was the prior objective?
- One question about the subjectivist charge (de Finetti-ish) that objectivity talk is just intersubjective agreement dressed up — and one practical question: what does he actually tell a working scientist to do with a high-dimensional problem where maxent is computationally hopeless?
- End with a falsifiability-style question: what result or case would make him give up the uniqueness claim, and if nothing would, is this a thesis or a temperament?

Keep the tone curious rather than prosecutorial — it's a friendly show — but don't let the questions be softballs. And please write the whole thing in British English, since that's how the show scripts everything.
