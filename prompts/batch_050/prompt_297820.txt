Ok so I got into a genuinely heated argument with my friend Mara last night (at a bar, which probably didnt help) about whether her housing co-op should have put it to a vote of the residents whether the foundation repairs were actually necessary, instead of just deferring to the structural engineer's report. She walked me into this argument and I couldn't find the exit, but the conclusion is nuts. Here it is, reconstructed as best I can:

(1) On any yes/no question where there's a fact of the matter, each person has some probability of getting it right, and for most practical questions ordinary, attentive people are right more often than not.

(2) If every voter is right more often than not, and the voters make up their minds independently of each other, then the bigger the group, the more likely the majority is right — and as the group gets large, the probability the majority nails it goes to 1. (This is just the Condorcet jury theorem, she looked it up on her phone mid-argument.)

(3) So for any yes/no question with a fact of the matter, there's some group size where a majority vote settles it with near-certainty.

(4) A small panel of experts is also just a group of fallible people, each right with some probability less than 1, so the panel's answer is only ever probable, never certain.

(5) Near-certain beats merely probable. So given enough voters, a mass vote beats a small expert panel — on any question whatsoever.

(6) So the co-op was wrong to defer to the engineer: they should have polled a few thousand people in the city about whether the foundation was sound and gone with the majority.

(7) That is obviously deranged. You don't settle structural engineering by referendum.

Something is clearly broken but I can't locate it. (1)+(2) to (3) looks like just math, and (4) seems undeniable — experts aren't infallible, that's Mara's whole reason for distrusting deference in the first place. My gut says something slips in the move to (5), since the theorem compares bigger and smaller groups drawn from the *same* pool of voters and (5) swaps in a totally different electorate, but Mara says that's quibbling because surely 10,000 slightly-informed people swamp 3 engineers however you slice it.

So: which step should I reject, and why does it look so convincing? If I get to keep things, I'd like to keep (2) since it's a theorem, and I'd like to keep some version of the idea that crowds can outdo experts sometimes (she kept yelling "wisdom of crowds" at me and I don't think she's entirely wrong about that). What does each way out cost? And if the problem is the independence clause in (2), I want to actually understand why that matters so much — the residents would all be forming their own opinions, it's not like they'd be copying each other's ballots.
