I'm a staff software engineer at a mid-sized company, and next week I get twenty minutes in front of our internal platform's governance council to argue that a charter I helped write is incoherent and needs amending. Some background: our ML platform team runs an opt-in system where engineers can allow their own committed code to be used as training data for an internal code-completion model. When we set it up, we adopted two rules that seemed obviously compatible: a unanimity rule (any change to the training configuration that every affected engineer strictly prefers over the status quo gets adopted) and a personal-domain rule (whether your own code enters the corpus is your call and nobody else's). Then a real dispute broke out. One engineer, call her Anika, is a privacy hawk: her first choice is that no one's code be used at all, but if someone's must be, she would rather it be her own than her teammate Bruno's. Bruno is an ML enthusiast: his first choice is that Anika's code be included, since it's stylistically the data he most wants the model to learn from; his second choice is that only his own code be used; his last choice is that no one's is. Run the two rules and they collide: both engineers prefer "only Anika's code" to "only Bruno's code," so the unanimity rule mandates the former, but the personal-domain rule lets Anika refuse and lets Bruno opt in, delivering exactly the configuration both of them rank lower. I stumbled across Sen's liberal paradox while trying to figure out which of them was wrong, and my thesis to the council is that neither is wrong — the charter is — and that we therefore have to weaken one clause. I'll be proposing that we restrict the unanimity rule so it can't reach into anyone's protected personal domain, while keeping the personal-domain rule absolute.

The audience matters here: eight senior engineers, most of whom treat unanimous consent and individual control over one's own data as near-axioms and will instinctively look for a redefinition or a technicality that makes the conflict go away; one engineering manager who doesn't care about the logic and mostly wants the Anika-Bruno dispute to stop consuming sprint time; and our data-protection counsel, who thinks entirely in consent-withdrawal mechanics and keeps trying to recast the whole thing as a compliance question. Prepare me for the question period. Give me the seven or eight objections I'm actually likely to get from this audience — not the ones an academic philosopher would raise — ordered by how much damage each would do if I handle it badly, every one phrased the way a skeptical senior engineer or a privacy lawyer would actually phrase it. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is soft or question-begging. Tell me which concessions I should make up front to buy credibility, which points I should not concede even under pressure, and finish with the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
