I sketched this argument after a reading group did Lewis on conversational scorekeeping and its been annoying me ever since, because the conclusion seems mad but I can't locate the break. (1) When someone says something whose presuppositions aren't already common ground, hearers normally just accommodate them — the scoreboard updates silently, so long as nobody says "hang on a minute". (2) Whether a bit of content counts as presupposed or asserted isn't fixed by the sentence: "my sister's coming to dinner" delivers the sister one way, "I've got a sister and she's coming to dinner" delivers it the other, but either way that content ends up in the common ground unless someone challenges it. (3) So accommodation doesn't actually care about the presupposed/asserted distinction — whatever a speaker puts forward, however it's packaged, goes onto the scoreboard by default. (4) But then assertion is just presupposition with extra steps: every utterance updates the score in the same way, and hearer acceptance never really has to happen. (5) That's plainly false — asserting that it'll rain tomorrow does conversational work that presupposing it doesn't, and people get challenged, contradicted and held to account for their assertions all the time. Something has to give. If I'm forced to choose I'd rather keep the scorekeeping picture than (5), but I want to know which step is doing the damage and what rejecting it costs.
