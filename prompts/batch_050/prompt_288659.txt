I recently joined my university's Committee on Commemoration and Naming, which decides whether buildings, scholarships, and statues named for historical figures should retain their names. I came in expecting to rely on some version of the familiar "man of his time" defence, but studying our past decisions has left me unsure what principle, if any, this committee actually applies, and I have a case coming up that I'd like to reason through before the vote.

Our recent precedents, in summary: we removed Jefferson's name from a residence hall, judging that his lifelong ownership of enslaved people, including the mother of his own children, could not be bracketed off from the legacy the naming celebrates, even though slavery was not what the naming was for. We kept Churchill's name on the international studies centre, judging that his leadership against Nazism was the legacy being honoured and that his grave failures, including his government's conduct during the Bengal famine and his recorded racial views, were not the reason for the commemoration. We removed a statue of Columbus, judging that the wrongs he personally directed and the colonization he inaugurated are inseparable from the achievement for which he was celebrated. We kept the name of a nineteenth-century founder whose early fortune came partly from investments in slave-trading ventures, on the grounds that he personally opposed slavery, divested when he understood the source of the returns, and funded abolitionist causes. And we removed the name of a pioneering scientist who publicly endorsed eugenics in the 1920s, a view then common in his field, judging that lending his name to a science building today would signal acceptance of a position he actively promoted rather than merely held.

The new case concerns a twentieth-century public health figure after whom our tropical medicine institute is named. He is famous, justly, for sanitation and vaccination campaigns that saved enormous numbers of lives, and that work is the reason for the naming. The newly surfaced problem: in the 1940s he ran a series of non-consensual experiments on institutionalized orphans, exposing some to infection to study disease progression. The experiments were legal at the time, were not far outside the research norms of his era, produced publishable results he used, and were never secret, but the subjects were children who could not refuse, and several suffered lasting harm. If the committee wants to be consistent with its own record, what should it decide? I'd like you to identify which precedents pull in which direction, what the two or three most plausible rules are that jointly fit all five prior verdicts, which rule the committee is most likely actually running, and what each possible decision here would commit us to going forward. If the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, say so, and describe the hypothetical future case whose resolution would most clearly reveal which principle we have in fact adopted.
