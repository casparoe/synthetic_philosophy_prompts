So at a birthday dinner last week two friends of mine got into it for basically an hour and I've been going back and forth on who won ever since. Curious what you make of it.

It started because Priya said she'd rather have $1000 today than $1100 a year from now, and she stressed she meant even if the future payment was totally guaranteed, no inflation, no risk of the person flaking. Daniel said that was straightforwardly irrational. She said it's just time preference, everyone has it, economists literally build it into their models as a discount rate. He said economists also have models of people buying lottery tickets and smoking, modelling something isn't the same as endorsing it. She said okay but a year from now is genuinely less certain than now, not because of the payer but because of her -- she could die, her circumstances could totally change, so discounting is just pricing in risk like anything else. Daniel said fine, do the math: her chance of dying in the next year is a tiny fraction of a percent, and the payment was stipulated as guaranteed, so risk justifies a discount of basically nothing, whereas her stated preference implied something like a 10% rate. The gap, he said, is pure preference for sooner over later, and that's just a bias, like preferring chocolate cake because it's closer to you on the table.

Then he got a bit smug and brought up Sidgwick. In the Methods of Ethics, he said, Sidgwick lays out a principle of prudence that's supposed to be self-evident: roughly, that the mere fact of one moment coming before another in time isn't a rational reason to care more about what happens at the earlier moment, and that you should be impartial between all the times of your own life, with only an allowance for how certain the different goods are. So, Daniel said, her 10% was exactly what Sidgwick ruled out.

Priya read over his shoulder and said the quote actually helped her, because of the certainty allowance and the word "mere" -- Sidgwick is only ruling out preferring a time *just because* it's earlier, and her preference isn't bare like that, it's about her being 26 now with a cheap apartment and no responsibilites, when an extra grand genuinely changes her life, versus 27 when she'll probably be earning more anyway. Money at different ages isn't the same good, she said, so Sidgwick's principle doesn't even apply. Daniel said that's a fair point in general but it wasn't her actual reason -- she'd already agreed to the guaranteed version of the case, and besides, by her logic she should *reverse*-discount if she expects to be richer later, not discount. She said no, diminishing returns means the money does more good now even if she's richer later, wait, she said that wrong, she means even just not knowing what her life will look like makes later money worth less to her now. Then she tried a different move: preferences about ends aren't the kind of thing that can be irrational at all, only inconsistent ones are, and preferring present goods is an end, not a means. Daniel said but it's inconsistent in a way: when she's 40 she'll wish 26-year-old her had taken the 1100. Priya said of course 40-year-old her will say that, 40-year-old her is the one who'd get the money, every version of her prefers the slice that's good for them, and there's no neutral referee self that gets to overrule the present one. That's where it kind of stalled out.
