I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about distributive justice by consulting my verdicts on a set of cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, unifies them. My verdicts: (1) Someone who clears and farms unowned land while plenty of comparable land remains for others is entitled to the crop — just. (2) That same homesteader, decades later, owns the only reliable well in a drought and charges whatever the market will bear — unjust, even though every step in the chain of title was clean. (3) A basketball star who grows rich because millions of fans each freely pay a small surcharge to watch him play, starting from a distribution I take to be fair — just; this is Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain case, and I share his verdict. (4) A sequence of a hundred individually voluntary, informed, uncoerced trades and gifts that ends with one person owning all the food in a village whose other residents then starve — unjust, even though no single step was wrongful. (5) A person who bought land in good faith that was, unbeknownst to them, stolen from its original owners two centuries ago — the current owner's claim is compromised, and some rectification is owed. (6) A rescuer who charges a stranded hiker an enormous but genuinely consented-to fee for the rescue — wrong, despite the consent. Is there a single principle, presumably a historical one in the neighborhood of Nozick's entitlement theory with clauses for just acquisition, just transfer, and rectification, that delivers all six verdicts? If so, state it precisely, identify which case motivates each clause (in particular, what proviso on acquisition or transfer handles 2 and 4, and whether consent suffices for 6 or a separate anti-exploitation clause is needed), and say what it predicts about a case I haven't listed: taxing the basketball star's earnings progressively to fund public goods. If no principle delivers all six, tell me which verdict to abandon and defend the choice; I suspect 3 and 4 cannot both survive a simple principle, so say explicitly which one you would overturn. In your answer, separate what is genuinely settled among people who know this literature, such as the structure of Nozick's argument, from what remains contested, such as whether any workable version of the Lockean proviso exists, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. End with a final line beginning "Principle:" that states in one sentence either the unifying principle or, if none works, the verdict you would overturn and why in a clause.
