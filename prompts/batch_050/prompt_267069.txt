Construct a thought experiment about distributive justice that produces a genuine three-way split: luck egalitarianism, prioritarianism, and sufficientarianism must each recommend giving the same scarce, indivisible resource to a different recipient. Two theories agreeing against a third does not count.

Constraints on the scenario:
- Realistic and policy-shaped: one resource (a funded rehabilitation slot, a grant, a triaged treatment), three or four named individuals, and an institutional allocator (a hospital board, a benefits tribunal), such that a reader could plausibly encounter the case. No desert islands, no omniscient distributors, no trolleys, no stipulated intuitions.
- The split must be generated by the theories' standard commitments — responsibility-sensitivity, priority to the absolutely worse off, and the threshold — derived from facts the scenario states explicitly (each person's well-being level, the causal source of each person's disadvantage, where the threshold sits). Do not stipulate what any theory concludes.
- To force the interesting direction of disagreement: the person whose disadvantage stems from option luck must have taken a gamble that was reasonable ex ante, not reckless; the brute-luck victim must not be the worst off in absolute terms, so that luck egalitarianism is pushed toward favoring someone who is better off; and the threshold must fall between two candidates so sufficientarianism peels off from both rivals.
- One paragraph for the scenario itself.

After the scenario: (1) derive each theory's verdict step by step, showing which stated facts each verdict turns on; (2) for each pair of theories, identify the single feature of the case whose removal or alteration would collapse their disagreement — say explicitly whether the case then degrades into a standard two-way contest; (3) give the strongest objection a defender of each theory would raise against the case (for instance, that it misdescribes how that theory individuates disadvantage, handles responsibility attributions, or sets its threshold), and assess whether the objection defeats the case or merely relocates it; (4) state, in one or two sentences, what the case shows about whether these theories can be pulled apart in realistic allocation settings without stacked examples.

The purpose of the exercise is to test whether the three views genuinely diverge outside artificial setups, so a case that engineers sympathy rather than deriving verdicts counts as failure.

End with a final line in exactly this form, naming the recipient each theory selects: \answer{Luck egalitarianism → [name]; Prioritarianism → [name]; Sufficientarianism → [name]}
