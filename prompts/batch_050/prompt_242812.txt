Having retired after four decades as a population geneticist, I have spent the past two years reading philosophy, and I am now preparing an application for a one-year visiting fellowship in a philosophy department. My project concerns a claim I keep encountering in debates over gene drives and de-extinction: that an ecosystem's value depends partly on its causal history, so that a habitat restored to functional perfection, or a species reconstructed by genomic engineering, is worth less than the original it replaces even when no observer could tell the two apart. Most discussion of gene drives centres on risk and on consent of affected communities; most discussion of de-extinction centres on animal welfare and the diversion of conservation funds. The causal-history claim underlies both debates yet is rarely examined directly, and I cannot tell whether it states a genuine truth about value or merely dresses a preference for the familiar in theoretical vocabulary. Write the two-page proposal: state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explain why neither the intrinsic-value literature nor the ecosystem-services framing settles it; set out what someone with my background could actually accomplish in a year, including which distinctions to draw, which thought experiments to construct, and which empirical facts about gene-drive containment and genomic reconstruction to use as test cases; and say what would count as the project failing. I shall supply the citations myself, so please do not invent any.
