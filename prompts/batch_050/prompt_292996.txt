So two of my friends (both psych majors, annoyingly) got into it at a party last weekend and it went on for like an hour. I'm curious who was actually right, point by point.

It started because Priya said when she imagines her old apartment she can "walk around" in it and notice stuff, like she pictured the kitchen and "saw" that the window was to the left of the sink, which she'd never explicitly thought about before. Dan jumped on this: he said she can't have discovered anything, because anything she "sees" in the image she must have already put in there -- you can't be surprised by your own mental image the way you're surprised by a real photo. A photo contains information nobody put in deliberately; an image is just stuff you already know, rearranged.

Priya said that's wrong, because there's research where people rotate imagined shapes or scan across imagined maps and the response times scale with the distance or angle, like the image actually has spatial layout. If it was just a list of facts, why would it take longer to "scan" further? Dan said that's easy to explain away: people know how the world works, so if you ask them to scan an image they unconsciously mimic how long it would take to scan a real thing. The experiment measures their knowledge of timing, not a picture in the head. Priya said that tacit-knowledge move is unfalsifiable -- any result can be redescribed as "people know how it would go." Dan said no, it's falsifiable in principle: if scanning time were insensitive to how far apart people *believe* things are, that would kill his view. Priya conceded that but said his view still can't explain cases where people combine two images and get something new -- like mentally pushing two simple shapes together and suddenly realizing the combination looks like an umbrella. You couldn't have put the umbrella in, because you didn't know it was coming.

Then Dan switched to a different argument, which is the part I really couldn't call. He said neither of them was right, because both assume the image is either a stored picture or a stored description. His idea: imagery is more like a video game engine. Nothing is stored rendered; the engine generates detail only when you "look" at a region, on demand, which is why images feel rich but fall apart when you probe them (he said: try to count the stripes on an imagined tiger -- you can't, because there are no stripes until you decide there are). So Priya's "discovery" cases and Dan's "you only know what you put in" cases are both exactly what a lazy generator would produce.

Priya pushed back and said the engine metaphor is just doing his job for him with fancier words: a generator that produces detail on demand still needs rules for what to produce, and rules are basically descriptions, so he hasn't escaped the propositional view, he's just hidden it inside the engine. Dan said no, the difference is the engine *computes* consequences you didn't store, which descriptions don't do. Priya said a description plus general knowledge computes consequences too. Then they both turned to me and I had nothing.
