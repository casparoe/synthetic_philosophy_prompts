I'm in a philosophy of rationality seminar (there's a behavioral econ guy co-teaching it, which makes it messy) and we just did the sunk cost fallacy. Everyone in class seemed way too confident that it's ALWAYS irrational to factor sunk costs in, so I started collecting cases where my gut disagrees, and now I can't figure out if my gut is inconsistent or if the simple story is wrong. Here's where I land:

Paying $14 for a movie ticket and then sitting through a movie I hate: irrational, obviously, I should just leave. Finishing my PhD four years in even though I'm miserable, just because of the four years: irrational, same structure. BUT: I bought a year-long gym membership instead of paying per visit precisely BECAUSE I know the money being spent will guilt me into going, and that seems... smart, actually? Like I'm using the fallacy on myself on purpose. And here's another one: a company keeps funding a failing project for one more quarter because canceling it right before investor meetings would tank their credibility, and the investors will find out eventually either way -- that seems rational to me, not a fallacy. Also: finishing a novel draft I've stopped believing in, not because it'll be good, but because I've never finished anything long before and I want to know I can. That feels fine too. And my roommate keeps her ex's concert ticket "because it cost so much" which feels irrational again but honestly I'm not sure I can say why it's different from the gym case.

So: is there an actual principle that sorts these the way my gut does, or am I going to have to give some of them up? My hunch is something like -- it matters whether the past investment is still doing causal work (changing my motivation, changing what others expect of me, generating information) versus just sitting there making me feel bad -- but the gym case and the movie case have basically the same structure on that story and I judge them differently, so my hunch is probably broken. If you can state a cleaner principle, tell me which of my verdicts it keeps and which it overturns, and then tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed: a country staying in a war because "the dead must not have died for nothing," and me rereading the same awful book every summer because I first read it at a cabin with my grandpa. Also, if the right answer is that honoring sunk costs is just always irrational and the gym thing is self-deception that happens to work, I'd want that argued, not assumed, because the self-deception clearly DOES work and I'm not sure why that doesn't count.
