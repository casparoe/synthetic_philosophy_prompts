I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of mind course, and we've just spent two weeks on Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of kinds of minds: Darwinian creatures whose behavior is fixed by natural selection, Skinnerian creatures that learn by reinforcement, Popperian creatures that test hypotheses in an inner environment instead of in the world, and Gregorian creatures that further equip themselves with culturally transmitted "mind-tools" like language, notation, and explicit argument. The taxonomy was presented to us as a way of distinguishing genuine grades of intelligence, but I've noticed that in our seminar discussions — and in everything I read online — debates about whether some animal or some AI system is "really intelligent" seem to go nowhere. The people arguing all seem to know the same facts (the same experiments on corvids, the same benchmark results for language models), yet nobody ever updates. My hypothesis is that the disagreement has stalled because the participants are implicitly ranking candidates by different rungs of a Dennett-style ladder: one side demands Popperian inner simulation before they'll count anything as intelligence, the other thinks flexible Skinnerian learning already qualifies, and the dispute about facts is just a proxy war over which rung deserves the word. Is that diagnosis right, or is something else doing the work — a factual disagreement about mechanisms, a verbal dispute about the word "intelligence," or a deeper disagreement about what an explanation of mindedness has to deliver? Please give me your own diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, with reasons, and say what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a new distinction, a decisive experiment, a formal criterion, or an argument that no progress should be expected. I'm less interested in a verdict on any particular animal or AI system than in the structure of the impasse itself.
