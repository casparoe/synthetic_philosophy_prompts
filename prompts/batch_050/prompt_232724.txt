I'm a software engineer by trade — distributed systems, mostly — with no formal philosophy background, but I volunteer as a peer grader for a free online course on the ethics and metaphysics of AI. One of the homework answers that landed in my queue is below, and I'd like your help grading it before I submit my marks.

The homework question was:

"Some philosophers hold that a digital mind has moral status only if it is phenomenally conscious — only if there is something it is like to be it, with experiences that feel good or bad. Others hold that moral status can rest on functional capacities alone: having stable goals and interests, being able to pursue them, and being able to be benefited or harmed in a functional sense, whether or not any inner experience accompanies this. Explain what is at stake between these two positions. In doing so, address (i) the principle of substrate independence and its role in the debate, and (ii) how the fact that digital minds can be copied, paused, and rerun complicates the question of their moral status."

Here is the student's answer:

"Both positions agree that moral status concerns what a being is owed for its own sake, not for its effects on us; they disagree about what grounds that status. The phenomenal-consciousness view says the ground is experience: a being matters morally because things can go well or badly for it from the inside, and pleasure and suffering are the clearest cases. A digital mind that merely simulates distress without feeling anything would, on this view, have no more moral standing than a character in a video game. The functional-capacities view says this demands too much: what matters is that the system has interests — stable goals it can be helped or hindered in pursuing — and can be harmed in the sense of being damaged, frustrated, or destroyed. On that view, a digital mind could merit moral consideration even if we could not confirm it has any inner life, since the presence of experience is exactly the kind of thing we cannot verify from outside.

Substrate independence is usually invoked at this point to break the tie in favor of the functional view. The principle says that consciousness does not depend on the material a mind is made of: if a silicon system has the right functional organization, it can be conscious just as a biological one can. It follows, the argument goes, that we should treat digital minds as conscious whenever they are functionally sophisticated enough, and so the consciousness-based position effectively collapses into the functional one for any advanced digital mind.

On copying: this is where the positions pull apart most sharply. A digital mind can be copied perfectly, paused, and resumed. The functional-capacities view handles this naturally: if each copy has the same goals and interests, each copy matters, and running ten copies of a suffering system creates ten times the grounds for concern. The consciousness view has more trouble here, because there seems to be no additional fact of the matter about how many streams of experience exist. Either way, though, both views should agree that deleting one copy is not seriously wrong as long as other copies continue to run: nothing is permanently lost, since the mind's memories, personality, and projects all survive elsewhere, and the interests of the remaining copies are unaffected. This suggests that duplication generally lowers rather than raises the moral stakes for digital minds.

On balance, I find the functional view more defensible, mainly because it gives us something checkable to work with, whereas the consciousness view leaves us unable to act on its own criterion."
