I recently lied to a good friend about something trivial (said I was already on my way when I hadn't even showered yet) and felt basicly zero guilt about it, which bothered me more than the lie itself. That sent me down an Augustine rabbit hole and now I've half convinced myself he's right. As I understand his view: a lie is saying something you believe is false with the intent to deceive, jokes and irony aren't lies since there's no such intent, and every actual lie is a sin -- even lying to a murderer about where his victim is hiding. His reasoning, as I get it, is partly that lying corrupts the liar's own soul and partly that once lying for good ends is permitted, testimony in general (including scripture) loses its authority.

Here's what I want: what would count against this position? Not just the obvious "but the murderer at the door!!" case, which he'd plainly bite the bullet on, but what it would take to show the view is actually wrong rather than merely unpalatable. Which intuitions or cases would a committed Augustinian concede as genuine trouble? Does his definition of lying do the load-bearing work, such that showing that deception via misleading-but-true statements is equally bad would break the view -- or would he just extend the ban? And is the position insulated by the fact that he grades lies by gravity, so no case can ever function as a counterexample? Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.
