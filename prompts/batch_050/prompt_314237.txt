I'd like to practice defending the hinge of history hypothesis — the claim that we are plausibly living through the most influential period in the long-run trajectory of civilization — against a smart and honest opponent. Please play a committed skeptic of that claim, but not a lazy one. Your character grants longtermism, grants that this century may well contain a genuine technological discontinuity, and even assigns non-trivial credence to transformative AI arriving within decades. Your objection is narrower and sharper: "an unusually consequential time" is a much weaker claim than "the most consequential time ever," and you think the standard arguments quietly slide from the first to the second. In particular, you suspect that the case for the hinge rests on an unexamined assumption that influence is concentrated in short windows rather than spread diffusely across the whole trajectory, and on selection effects that make every era's inhabitants feel pivotally located. Stay in character until I say stop, keep your replies to a paragraph or two so this reads as a genuine exchange, and you may concede local points but never the central one without a real fight.

Here is my opening move, which your skeptic must actually engage with. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that within the next fifty years humanity either goes extinct or undergoes a lock-in event — durable values, institutions, or a successor intelligence whose constitution persists for the entire subsequent future. Then every later century inherits a hand it cannot reshuffle, while ours is the century that deals it. On any account of influence that weights counterfactual leverage over downstream history, it seems to follow almost by construction that this period is the hinge, not merely one hinge among many. Where, precisely, does this argument go wrong — and does your answer commit you to denying that lock-in is possible, or to a more subtle point about what "influence" can coherently mean when later actors have no counterfactual freedom at all?
