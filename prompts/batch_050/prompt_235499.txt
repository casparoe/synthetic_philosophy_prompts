I'm a novelist working on a book about a woman who grew up in Osaka and moves to small-town Ohio, and I've been boning up on the whole shame-culture vs guilt-culture thing because I want her interior conflicts to feel real and not like a fortune cookie. A pop-anthropology book I picked up at the airport (I wont name it, but it sells itself as a fun guide to "how other cultures tick") lays it out like this: "Anthropologists divide societies into shame cultures and guilt cultures. In shame cultures, mostly in East Asia, people behave because others are watching and wrongdoing only counts if you're caught, so morality is basically external and conformist. In guilt cultures, like the West, people carry an inner moral voice, so they do the right thing even when nobody sees. Ruth Benedict showed this in the 1940s, and her scheme is still the standard framework in anthropology today. The upshot is that guilt is the morally superior emotion, because it's the only one that works when you're alone -- and a society that wants honest citizens should raise children on guilt, not shame."

Some of that matches other stuff I've half-absorbed, but the "only counts if you're caught" bit made me put the book down for a second, because my aunt in Kyoto seems to feel shame plenty when nobody's watching, and I know Americans who cheat the moment they're unobserved. Also "guilt is superior and societies should pick their emotion" smells like a value judgment wearing a lab coat. But I'm a novelist, not an anthropologist, so I cant tell where the scholarship ends and the TED-talk begins.

Could you go through that summary sentence by sentence and tell me what's a fair statement of the shame/guilt distinction, what's an overstatement or a conflation, and what's just wrong -- including what Benedict herself actually claimed versus what this book pins on her, and whether her dichotomy is really "still the standard framework"? And separately: even granting the accurate parts, does the conclusion follow that guilt is the better moral emotion and that a culture could just choose to raise kids on it? If shame turns out to do real moral work too, I want to know, because it'll change how I write my protagonist's two competing inner voices.
