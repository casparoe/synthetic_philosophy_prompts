I am a PhD student in pragmatics. My dissertation studies how politicians in broadcast interviews avoid making commitments they can later be held to. Its empirical core is a corpus of about 450 question–answer exchanges transcribed from archived interviews — text only, no audio. Two graduate research assistants will do the first-pass annotation; I adjudicate disagreements in a weekly meeting; we can spend at most ten minutes per exchange. Every utterance that arguably conveys more than its literal content must receive exactly one label from this set: what is said / entailment, presupposition, conventional implicature, generalized conversational implicature, particularized conversational implicature, strategic indeterminacy, judgment required.

The sixth label is my own addition, and my examiners will attack it. I coined it for utterances that seem designed to let different audiences draw different conclusions, where no single inference can be shown to have been invited by the speaker. I need the annotation manual built on Grice's account of conversational implicature rather than gesturing at it, because the manual is what my dissertation's central claim stands or falls with.

Please produce the following, in this order.

1. Brainstorming. Before designing anything, list the standard candidate tests for conversational implicature — cancellability, non-detachability, calculability, reinforceability, and any others you take seriously — with two or three sentences each on how they fail in practice: for instance, the way metalinguistic stress spoils cancellation as a diagnostic; the fact that non-detachability presupposes that annotators can find a synonymous paraphrase, which is itself a judgment call; and the worry that calculability rationalizes inferences after the fact rather than discovering them. If you think a test is unreliable enough that it should be demoted from a decision step to a tie-breaker, say so here.

2. The procedure. An ordered sequence of steps for annotating one utterance: for each step, what counts as a pass or fail, which label it outputs or which step it routes to, and the default to apply when tests conflict. Mark explicitly the points where the procedure must stop and hand the case to a human, and say what the annotator should record at each such judgment point, since those records drive our adjudication meetings. Then tell me which Gricean distinctions must survive the translation into procedure and which can be dropped: in particular, advise whether the generalized/particularized split is worth its likely cost in inter-annotator agreement, whether presupposition belongs in the same flowchart or a separate module, and how the procedure should treat my strategic indeterminacy category given that Grice ties implicature to an inference the hearer can work out. Note that our transcripts lack prosody, so irony and other flouts that live in intonation need an explicit rule even though the transcript is all we have. Close this section with a short paragraph on what the procedure cannot do — for example, whether it can ever tell us what a speaker actually intended.
