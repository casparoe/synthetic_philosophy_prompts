I'm a novelist, and my current book has me in a fight with myself about how moral norms actually die. Let me give you my gut verdicts on the real cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, sits underneath them.

Dueling among English gentlemen: every argument agianst it — it's murder, it's unchristian, it's an insane way of settling anything — was on the table for centuries, nobody budged, and then within a few decades it went from something a gentleman *had* to do to something faintly embarassing. So my verdict: arguments don't kill norms, honor does. A practice dies when doing it stops protecting your status.

Atlantic slavery: here my instinct flips. All the moral argument feels downstream of the economics to me — the Williams-style story where slavery became dispensable to the powerful first and shameful second. Interests do the killing; moral argument writes the obituary.

Footbinding in China: ends in about a generation after centuries of practice, largely because reformers deliberately rebranded it as a national humiliation rather than a mark of refinement. That's honor again, like dueling — except this time the honor shift was engineered, by people making arguments. Which seems to break my first verdict.

So: is there one principle that captures all three — some general claim about when moral argument actually works on a practice and when it's just decoration — or do I have to drop one of these? Tell me which verdict the best principle can't save. And here's why I care: in my book, a decent, status-conscious merchant in an invented port town keeps up a dueling-like practice, and I need him off it by the end. My editor wants him argued out of it in one great scene. Which lever does your principle say a man like that really moves for?
