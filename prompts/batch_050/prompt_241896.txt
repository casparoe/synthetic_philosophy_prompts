A guy in my decision theory reading group cornered me with this last week and I still don't have a clean exit. Setup first, since it's short. Two urns, 100 balls each. Urn 1 is exactly 50 red and 50 black. Urn 2 is some unknown split of red and black -- you know it's 100 balls, nothing else. Most people, me included, would rather bet on red drawn from urn 1 than red drawn from urn 2, and would *also* rather bet on black from urn 1 than black from urn 2. He says fine, now watch:

(1) A rational agent's preferences over bets have to be representable as maximizing expected value relative to some single probability assignment. If no probability assignment makes sense of your choices, your preferences are inconsistent, full stop -- that's what Dutch book arguments establish.

(2) No single probability assignment rationalizes the Ellsberg preferences. Preferring red-from-urn-1 to red-from-urn-2 says you assign urn 2's red probability less than 1/2; preferring black-from-urn-1 to black-from-urn-2 says you assign it more than 1/2. Contradiction.

(3) So the Ellsberg preferences are irrational.

(4) But nearly every real decision -- taking a job, buying insurance, choosing the drug with fifty years of data over the experimental one, even grabbing an umbrella -- involves outcomes at least as ambiguous as urn 2's composition. Nobody hands you the probabilities in real life either.

(5) So preferring the option whose risks are better understood, in any real-world decision, is irrational for exactly the same reason as in (3).

(6) But paying more for insurance with a clear payout structure, or wanting the treatment with the long track record, is just prudence. It cannot be a consistency failure on par with refusing a free dollar.

(7) His conclusion: either ordinary prudence under uncertainty is irrational, or (1) is false -- and if (1) is false, Bayesianism as a theory of rational belief goes down with it, since (1) is just Bayesianism stated in betting terms.

He swears all the action is between (1) and (3), but I have a nagging feeling something slipped in between (4) and (5) and I can't name it. Which step do I actually reject, why does that step look so innocent at first, and what does rejecting each of the candidates cost? For the record I badly want to keep (6), and I'm attached to (1) because I like Dutch books, so if the fix is somewhere in (2), (4), or (5) I'd be delighted -- but tell me if I'm wrong to be.
