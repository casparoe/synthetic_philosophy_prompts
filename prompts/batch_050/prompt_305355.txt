I've been a reader of the rationalist blogs for over a decade, and one lesson I've absorbed is that beliefs are supposed to cash out in action: a belief you wouldn't bet on is doing no work. Recently, though, I tried to make that lesson precise using some ideas from mainstream epistemology, and the argument I ended up with seems to prove far more than I can accept. I'm hoping for a diagnosis.

Background, in case the terminology varies: by "pragmatic encroachment" I mean the thesis that whether a subject knows that p can depend on the practical stakes, holding fixed the subject's evidence. The standard motivation is bank-style cases: when nothing rides on it, "I know the bank is open on Saturday" seems true; when a missed payment would be catastrophic, the same sentence, same evidence, seems false. And the standard mechanism is the knowledge-action principle: you know p only if p can properly serve as a premise in your practical reasoning, i.e., only if you are in a position to rationally act on p.

Here is the argument.

(1) Rational action is a matter of expected value. So whether it is rational to act as if p depends not only on your confidence that p but on the stakes: for any fixed level of confidence short of certainty, there are stakes high enough that acting on p is irrational.

(2) You know p only if you are in a position to rationally act as if p.

(3) Therefore whether you know p can vary with the stakes, holding your evidence fixed. (This is just pragmatic encroachment, so far so good.)

(4) Stakes need not be known to the subject to affect what it is rational for her to do. Suppose Omega -- the usual superintelligent predictor from the decision-theory literature -- has, without telling anyone, wired an enormously costly outcome to whether the 6:15 train is running. Acting on your belief that the train is running now carries huge expected costs if you're wrong, whether or not you know about the wager.

(5) Your evidence about the train schedule is exactly what it was before Omega's wager. So by (1)-(3), in the wager scenario you do not know the train is running, despite having the same evidence that, in the low-stakes case, sufficed for knowledge.

(6) Your actual situation right now is, from the inside, indistinguishable from the wager scenario: nothing in your evidence rules out that some hidden high-stakes wager attaches to any of your ordinary beliefs. Epistemically, you cannot distinguish "no hidden stakes" from "hidden stakes everywhere."

(7) Therefore you do not, right now, know that the train is running -- and by parallel reasoning you know almost none of your ordinary beliefs, since hidden stakes could attach to any of them without leaving a trace in your evidence.

Conclusion: once you take hidden stakes seriously, the knowledge-action principle collapses into near-global skepticism. The only things you can know are propositions you're certain no agent could possibly care about -- which is to say, almost nothing.

This conclusion is absurd. The argument was supposed to be a friendly formalization of the rationalist insight that beliefs are for actions, and instead it seems to show that the more a belief matters, the less you can know it -- that knowledge survives only where nothing is at stake. Somewhere I must have gone wrong.

So: where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I'd like you to name the specific step, explain why it looked valid (I suspect several steps are valid under one reading of their terms and not under another), and then price the alternatives: if the fix is to restrict (2), what does that cost the belief-action connection; if the fix is to restrict (4), what notion of rationality are we left with; if the fix is a contextualist semantics for "knows" rather than genuine pragmatic encroachment, how does that handle the hidden-stakes case without collapsing into skepticism anyway. If forced to choose, I'd rather keep (2) -- the idea that knowledge just is the state that licenses treating p as settled in deliberation is the whole reason I find epistemology worth caring about -- and I'd obviously rather deny (7). But I want to know what I'm paying for keeping them.
