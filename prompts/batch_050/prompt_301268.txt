I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general audience, and at the moment I'm working on a column about probability paradoxes that break people's intutions. For the section on the two-envelope paradox I wanted an interactive widget where readers can actually play the game, so before building it I coded up a quick simulation to sanity-check the famous switching argument. Here's my setup: you get an envelope containing some amount A (drawn randomly), and then the other envelope contains either A/2 or 2A, fifty-fifty, decided by a virtual coin flip at the moment you decide whether to switch. And the famous reasoning checks out beautifully. Over a few hundred thousand runs, the strategy "always switch" beats "always keep" by almost exactly 25%, matching the expected value of 1.25A that the paradox argument predicts.

Here's my problem. A mathematician I interviewed for the column told me the standard switching argument is fallacious and the paradox was basically resolved decades ago -- something about conditional expectations and needing a prior over the amounts, honestly she lost me a bit. But my simulation shows switching genuinely does win, by exactly the margin the "fallacious" argument says it should. So as far as I can tell, either the mathematicians are wrong, or the so-called fallacy is actually sound reasoning and my code proves it, or -- and this is the option I suspect is right but can't articulate -- my simulation and the original paradox are somehow not the same game. If it's the third one, what's the actual difference between the two setups, where exactly does the original argument go wrong in a way my code doesn't capture, and why does that difference flip the conclusion? I'd love an answer pitched at a smart reader who took one stats class, because "paradox that turns out not to be a paradox once you code it up" is kind of the whole hook of the piece and I don't want to botch it.
