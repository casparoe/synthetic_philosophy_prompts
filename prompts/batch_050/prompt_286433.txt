I teach an undergraduate course on historical methods, and for our final unit I want to spend one seventy-five-minute seminar on the question historians of economic development keep asking: why did industrialization take off in Britain rather than China? The students (about twenty second-years, no economics beyond intro) have read a short piece arguing that the very question is malformed because it assumes China was on the same track as Britain and merely stalled, and they've read a piece that treats the contrast between the two cases as the only way to identify what actually mattered for industrialization. I don't want to lecture them on the Great Divergence itself; I want to teach them how to evaluate whether a contrastive "why X and not Y" question is well posed in history at all, using this as the running case, and then let them apply the skill to other famous contrastive questions (why the scientific revolution in Europe, why the Spanish conquered the Inca rather than the reverse).

Please plan the explanation rather than give it. I need the dependency structure: which notions have to be in place before the students can even see that "why Britain and not China?" is a different question depending on what you silently take the foil to be (China as a whole? the Yangzi Delta? India? an imaginary China that was "almost" Britain?), and in what order to build those notions so that the level-of-aggregation problem and the choice-of-foil problem emerge as instances of one general point rather than two separate nitpicks. Tell me the one example I should build the whole session around -- I'm tempted to use an everyday contrastive question like asking why one sibling went to university and the other didn't, since everyone can feel how the answer changes with the implied comparison, but I'd like you to either endorse that choice with reasons or propose something better, and to say what the carrying example must and must not contain. Then give me the misconception map: the errors the students will predictably make (I anticipate at least: that criticizing a question's framing is just dodging it; that a contrastive question commits you to thinking the foil was "on track" and failed; that picking the right unit of comparison is an empirical matter only, with no judgment involved; and that any answer to "why Britain?" must be a single factor), each one placed at the exact point in the sequence where I should head it off, and how. Include one check I can run in the last ten minutes that would show whether they can construct and critique a contrastive question themselves rather than merely repeat the critique they read, and note honestly what I should leave out at this level -- for instance, how much of the actual Pomeranz-style reciprocal comparison method, or the formal philosophy of contrastive explanation, can be gestured at without derailing the session.

I'd like the whole plan as continuous prose, in American English, written the way you'd write a long email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Order of ideas, carrying example, misconception placement, final check, and what to omit should all be woven in rather than sectioned off.
