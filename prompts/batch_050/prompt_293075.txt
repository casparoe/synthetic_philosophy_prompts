I work on distributed systems, mostly databases and replicated state machines, and I've been reading the linguistics literature on presupposition, accommodation, and conversational scorekeeping (the tradition running through Stalnaker's common-ground picture and Lewis's rules for how a conversation's "score" evolves). It strikes me that the whole framework might be statable in vocabulary I actually think in, and I'd like you to attempt that translation seriously rather than gesture at it.

Here is the target vocabulary, all of which I use fluently: a conversation is a sequence of transactions against a shared, replicated store (the common ground). Each participant holds a local replica and there is a synchronization protocol. An utterance is a proposed transaction. Declarative content is a state update; presuppositions are preconditions that must already hold in the state for the transaction to commit. Accommodation is something like an automatic, silent migration that extends the state so the precondition passes. Felicity and infelicity are commit success and failure. The "hey, wait a minute" phenomenon is a client raising an error on a precondition violation and initiating repair. Scorekeeping rules are the default update policies of the protocol -- the way the state drifts to keep utterances processable, the way an apparently failed update can be reinterpreted rather than rejected. Local contexts for conjunctions and conditionals look like transaction composition, where the second statement in a conjunction runs against the state as modified by the first.

Please do the following:

1. Give the systematic translation. Work through the core apparatus -- common ground, assertion as update, presupposition as requirement on prior state, global versus local accommodation, the distinction between at-issue and not-at-issue content, the behavior of presuppositions embedded under conditionals, negation, and modals, and Lewis-style scorekeeping -- and assign each a counterpart in the store-and-transactions model. Where a choice of counterpart matters (for instance, whether accommodation is a silent migration or an implicit compensating transaction that other participants can roll back), flag the choice and its consequences.

2. Then give the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Three categories, please:
   - What translates cleanly, with no distortion you can detect.
   - What translates only under strain -- where you had to pick an implementation the linguists leave open, or where the translation quietly builds in an assumption the linguistic framework deliberately avoids (I suspect the normativity of infelicity, the deniability of accommodated content, and the difference between rejecting an utterance and repairing the context will land here, but correct me).
   - What has no expression in the target vocabulary at all.

3. Finally, a verdict on the residue. For each item that failed to translate, say whether this indicates that my store-and-transactions model is missing a dimension the phenomenon requires (and which one), or whether it indicates that the linguistic framework is tracking a distinction that is less substantive than the terminology makes it look. If the answer differs across items, so much the better -- I'd rather have a mixed verdict with reasons than a clean one without.

One constraint: keep the translation honest. Don't let the database model's machinery quietly decide open questions in the pragmatics literature (for example, whether accommodation is mandatory repair or optional charity) and then present the outcome as a translation rather than a stipulation. Where the model forces a decision the linguists leave open, say so explicitly.
