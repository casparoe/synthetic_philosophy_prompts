I'm a novelist in the final year of work on a manuscript about mourning and machine imitation, and I've run aground on an argument between two of my characters that I cannot make sound like anything but a position paper. The setup: my protagonist, Mara, a widow, subscribes to a service that trains a conversational agent on her late husband's two decades of emails, text messages, and voice notes. For a year it consoles her. Then the company pushes an update, and the agent begins producing jokes, opinions, and turns of phrase the husband never expressed — plausible extrapolations rather than recombinations. Mara discovers that she prefers the updated version, and is quietly horrified that she prefers it. Her sister insists the whole apparatus is a desecration that has trapped Mara in a loop; Mara's grief counselor replies that rereading old letters and keeping a saved voicemail were never condemned, and that maintaining an ongoing inner relationship with the dead is a recognized and healthy form of grieving rather than a failure to move on. I've drafted their confrontation three times now, and each version flattens into two spokespeople, which makes me suspect the real dispute has a structure I haven't located.

From my reading around the ethics of these technologies, the public and scholarly debate seems to have stalled in much the same place my fictional sisters have. One side treats griefbots as straightforwardly harmful: they impersonate the dead without consent, commercialize the vulnerable, and freeze mourning by removing every natural occasion for acceptance. The other side replies that this is moral panic dressed up as principle — that the objections reduce to unease about novelty, that the dead cannot be wronged by a statistical model any more than by a memoir, and that what matters is whether the bereaved are helped, which is an empirical question the critics haven't answered. My working hypothesis is that the debate keeps circling because the word griefbot conflates two morally different devices — one that only recombines what the dead actually said, and one that generates what they might plausibly have said — and because the two camps harbor irreconcilable pictures of whether mourning has a destination it can be prevented from reaching, which strikes me as a dispute about what grief is for rather than about anything either side could settle with data.

What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure, with reasons, not a verdict on which sister is right. Is this fundamentally an empirical dispute (does sustained interaction with such agents predict worse or better outcomes for the bereaved, and by what measure of 'better'), a conceptual one (about whether posthumous misrepresentation can harm the dead or what authentic mourning requires), a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue the other out of, or several different debates wrongly sharing one name? If my conflation hypothesis is wrong, show me where. And tell me what would count as genuine progress given your diagnosis — a longitudinal study of bereaved users, a principled distinction between recombining and generative agents, an agreement about the telos of grief, or something else — or, if you think no such progress should be expected, make that case. The ending of my novel depends less on who wins than on whether the argument could, in principle, be won.
