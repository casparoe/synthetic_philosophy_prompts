Ok so background first: I manage a small warehouse team, zero philosophy training, and a few months ago I got in trouble for saying something to my boss that was technically true but painted a totally false picture (I told him "Marcus handled the late shipment" — Marcus did handle it, by being the guy on shift when it went out late, and I just let my boss draw the obvious conclusion). Everyone keeps telling me well at least you didn't LIE, and I got chewing on that, and now I've got this argument that seems to prove way too much and I want to know where it breaks.

(1) What makes deceiving someone wrong is that you deliberately get them to believe something false, usually so you benefit and they get burned. So how wrong a deception is should be fixed by what you intended, plus what false belief they end up with, plus what happens because of it.

(2) The only difference between lying and misleading is the route the false belief takes into the person's head. With a lie it travels through the literal meaning of the sentence; with misleading it travels through what any normal listener would infer from the sentence.

(3) The route can't itself carry moral weight. If I poison someone it doesn't matter morally whether the poison was in the red capsule or the blue one — route is nothing next to intent and outcome.

(4) So if intent, resulting belief, and fallout are all held equal, misleading is exactly as wrong as lying. Same wrongness, full stop.

(5) Now here's where it runs away from me. When I stay quiet and let someone keep a false belief I could fix with one sentence, and I stay quiet on purpose because the false belief suits me, the intent and the belief and the fallout can again be identical to the lying case. The only difference is the route is now their own inference from the situation instead of their inference from my sentence. By (3), route doesn't matter. So deliberate silence is exactly as wrong as lying too.

(6) Which means: knowingly letting my coworker keep believing the safety inspection is Thursday when it's Wednesday is morally identical to flat out telling him it's Thursday. And I just don't buy that. At all. That conclusion is definately wrong, I'm just not sure why.

So the argument seems to prove that lying, misleading, and strategic silence are all one moral category, and I refuse to accept it, but I can't find the loose screw. Which premise do I throw out? What did that premise have going for it that made me put it in in the first place, and what does it cost me to drop it? And if the answer is that the "route" actually can matter morally, explain why the capsule thing in (3) doesn't already settle that the other way, because that analogy still feels solid to me.
