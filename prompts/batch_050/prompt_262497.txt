I'm a third-year PhD student in the philosophy of physics, and I run a small graduate reading group on the Everett interpretation. We've spent this term on the probability problem, and I've noticed that our discussions keep going in circles because participants talk past one another: one member thinks the Born rule simply falls out of rationality constraints, another insists there is no uncertainty in Everett at all and so nothing to derive, a third keeps reverting to naïve branch counting whenever we're not looking. I want to hand out, at the start of next term, a one-page decision tree that takes a reader from the bare commitment that the universal state evolves unitarily without collapse to the position on probability they actually hold, so that we can at least argue about the same things.

Please construct the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: start with the questions on which the positions genuinely come apart, then locate the positions in the space those questions define. The diagnostic questions I have in mind include (though you should reorganise, merge, or replace them as you see fit): whether probability in Everett requires genuine uncertainty at all, or can instead be a measure of how much an agent should care about each successor; whether rationality constraints alone (symmetry, Dutch-book avoidance, indifference to the physical realisation of an amplitude) suffice to fix squared-amplitude weighting; whether the vagueness and arbitrariness of branch individuation under decoherence — the 'branch dust' problem, where interference never quite vanishes — undermines any counting-based measure; and whether self-locating uncertainty about which branch one is on can be made coherent given that all branches equally exist. Where possible, phrase each node as a question a graduate student who knows the formalism could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and use at least two of the following concrete cases as the vehicles for nodes: an unequal-amplitude splitting (coefficients 1/√3 and √(2/3)) in which the agent is offered bets; a Sleeping-Beauty-style case where the coin is flipped only in some branches; and a case in which decoherence leaves no determinate answer to how many branches there are.

The leaves should include the Deutsch–Wallace decision-theoretic derivation, Zurek's envariance argument, the Sebens–Carroll self-locating-uncertainty treatment with the epistemic separability principle, naïve branch counting (even if the leaf is nearly empty of serious defenders), the 'caring measure' approach associated with Vaidman and Saunders, the Albert–Kent-style sceptical position that probability talk simply has no place in Everett, and any frequentist or frequency-operator view you think deserves a leaf. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate (I suspect the decision-theoretic and envariance leaves will be hard to tell apart from the outside, so if they share a leaf, say why). Where the space of answers contains an unoccupied combination — for instance, someone who grants squared-amplitude weighting for all practical purposes but denies this is probability in any robust sense — note it and give a sentence diagnosing whether the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Aim for eight to twelve nodes in total, and keep the whole thing compact enough to fit on one printed page with a short accompanying key.
