I recently started helping out at a tenants' union where everyone talks nonstop about renters' rights, and at a family thing my brother-in-law (did a PPE degree, very pleased with himself) told me Bentham demolished that whole way of talking back in the 1790s. He sent me a summary from a politics blog he said was basically right:

"Jeremy Bentham's critique of the French Declaration of the Rights of Man is the original demolition job on human-rights talk. His argument: rights are created by laws, full stop, so talk of rights that people have before or independently of any legal system is meaningless — empty noise that can be filled with whatever the speaker happens to want. And because natural rights are meaningless, no rights-based line can ever be drawn against a government: the only real question about any law is whether it promotes the general happiness. Hence his famous sneer about nonsense on stilts, and hence the problem utilitarians have had ever since making room for inviolable rights."

Then in a fit of diligence I actually opened the source, Bentham's Anarchical Fallacies, where he goes through the Declaration article by article. Early on he writes:

"The description of the persons, of whose rights it is to contain the declaration, is remarkable. Who are they? The French nation? No; not they only, but all citizens, and all men. By citizens, it seems we are to understand men engaged in political society: by men, persons not yet engaged in political society - persons as yet in a state of nature."

That reads to me like his target is specifically the idea of rights people have before political society exists, which seems narrower than the blog's version. So is the summary fair? Break it down: what's accurate, what's a subtle overreach or conflation, and what would Bentham himself say about rights against a government that already exists (he can't have thought utility just rubber-stamps everything a state does, surely?). And does the closing dig at utilitarians follow from anything the accurate parts establish? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question.
