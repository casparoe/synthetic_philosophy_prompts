Read Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day as a case study in the reliability of introspection. Stevens spends the whole novel telling us what he feels and why he does what he does, and he seems to get it wrong almost everytime -- he reports professional satisfaction where the reader sees grief, loyalty to Lord Darlington where the reader sees evasion, and he keeps correcting his own memories a few pages after stating them with total confidence. Here's what I want untangled: philosophers disagree about what kind of mistake is even available here, and the novel seems like a test case. On an inner-perception picture of self-knowledge, Stevens has a faulty instrument -- the grief is in there somewhere and he misreads it. On an interpretationist picture, he knows his own mind the same way the reader knows it, by interpreting behavior from the outside, and he's just a terrible interpreter of himself, the same way he's a terrible interpreter of Miss Kenton. And on a more constitutive picture, when Stevens sincerely says he's content, that partly makes it true, in which case maybe he's not wrong at all -- he's just living a much thinner emotional life than we wish for him, and our judgment that he's deceiving himself says more about us than him. Which model does the novel itself actually push you toward? Work through at least three concrete episodes -- his conduct on the evening of his father's death, his later account of dismissing the two Jewish housemaids, and the final scene on the pier at Weymouth -- and say which picture of self-knowledge best makes sense of each, and whether Ishiguro seems to think Stevens's failures of self-knowledge are corrigible or structural. And then the bigger question: does the book dramatize something the philosophy papers tend to miss, namely that unreliable introspection might not be a defect in a person but a feature of a social role -- that being a truly great butler more or less requires being opaque to yourself? I'm writing an essay on this and keep flip-flopping between readings.
