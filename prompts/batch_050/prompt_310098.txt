I'm a software engineer; I spend part of my time building moderation and trust-and-safety systems, which has pushed me into thinking about informal social punishment in general -- pile-ons, boycotts, call-outs, deplatforming, professional ostracism, the whole toolbox by which groups sanction members without anything like a court. Before I read any particular normative theory of when this kind of sanction is justified, I want the specification first. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of informal social sanction must meet, with the case or consideration motivating each one.

Concretely, I want the list to cover at least the following pressure points, though you should add any I'm missing: (a) the theory must distinguish refusing to associate with someone from punishing them, and say whether that distinction survives at scale, when thousands of individually blameless unfollows aggregate into someone losing their livelihood; (b) it must handle error rates -- formal punishment systems tolerate elaborate procedure partly because the sanctioned can't appeal a hashtag, so what does a theory owe the wrongly targeted, and does it matter that informal sanction has no mechanism for correcting false positives; (c) it must say something about proportionality when no one is in charge of the dose, since each participant may reasonably claim their individual contribution was tiny; (d) it must take a position on whether sanctions need an exit ramp -- apology, rehabilitation, statute of limitations -- or whether permanent reputational residue is an acceptable outcome; (e) it must address whether deplatforming should be evaluated as punishment of the speaker, as protection of the audience, or as something else, since the justification presumably differs across these framings; (f) it must reckon with the fact that informal sanction falls hardest on the marginally employed and is shrugged off by the already secure, so a theory that is fair in principle may be regressive in application.

For each desideratum, give me the motivating consideration and say whether it's genuinely non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp (retributivists, free-speech absolutists, harm-reduction types, and so on). What I most want flagged: any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied -- for instance, whether demanding both low false positives and rapid response to genuine harm is structurally impossible in a system with no centralized adjudicator, the way certain guarantees are incompatible in distributed systems. If the list itself is overdetermined, that's a result I'd rather know about up front. Finish with a brief note on which single requirement eliminates the most positions people actually defend. A table for the summary is fine, but the substance should be in the reasoning.
