I'm preparing a seminar on Jain epistemology for a mixed audience of philosophy students and film-studies students, and the screening I've chosen is Kurosawa's Rashomon. I'd like a careful reading of the film through the lens of anekāntavāda, the Jain doctrine of many-sidedness, together with its companion ideas: nayavāda (the theory of standpoints, each of which captures a genuine but partial aspect of a thing) and syādvāda (the sevenfold scheme of predication, in which every substantive claim is prefaced by an implicit "in some respect" — syāt — so that the same thing can truly be said to be, not to be, to be indescribable, and so on, depending on the standpoint taken). The film famously presents four incompatible accounts of the same events in the grove — the bandit Tajōmaru's, the wife's, the dead samurai's (delivered through the medium), and the woodcutter's — and the standard classroom reading treats it as a parable of relativism, self-serving memory, or the unknowability of truth. I want to test whether the Jain framework yields a better, or at least a different, reading than that standard one.

A few things I specifically want the reading to address. First, the crucial disanalogy I keep circling: anekāntavāda is not relativism. It presupposes a single reality of which each standpoint grasps a real aspect — the blind men in the classic parable are all touching the same elephant. Rashomon arguably suggests something more radical: that the accounts are not partial truths but fabrications, each shaped by the teller's need to preserve a self-image (honor, shame, cynicism), and possibly that there is no stable fact of the matter to which they answer. Does the film dramatize many-sided truth, or does it dramatize the breakdown of the very realism that makes many-sidedness intelligible? Second, take the four testimonies one by one and try assigning each the status it would have under syādvāda: which could be charitably heard as syāt-qualified truths, and which resist that charity because their distortions are motivated rather than perspectival? Third, the ending — the woodcutter taking the abandoned infant — is often read as a humanist consolation tacked onto an epistemically bleak film. Is there a reading on which it does philosophical work instead: a standpoint that is chosen rather than discovered, something closer to an ethical naya than an epistemic one?

Please structure your response in four sections, in this order: "The framework applied" (roughly 400 words — map the Jain apparatus onto the four testimonies as precisely as the material allows, without pretending the fit is cleaner than it is); "Where the film resists" (roughly 300 words — the honest points of friction, especially the motivated-lying problem and whether anekāntavāda has the resources to distinguish a standpoint from a self-serving distortion); "The ending as standpoint" (roughly 200 words — your treatment of the woodcutter's final act); and "Verdict" (roughly 150 words — whether the Jain lens illuminates the film better than the relativist reading, and what, if anything, the film shows about many-sidedness that the philosophical arguments alone don't). Don't quote dialogue; work from the film's well-known structure and scenes.
