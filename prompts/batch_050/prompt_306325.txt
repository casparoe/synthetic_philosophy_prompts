I hold a view I think of as complexity-sensitive naturalism: computational tractability is a legitimate constraint on theories in physics, cognitive science, and even epistemology, such that a theory which requires an agent, a brain, or nature itself to solve intractable problems in the course of doing what the theory says it does is thereby defective and should be revised or rejected. For instance, I take this to rule out models of human reasoning that assume we compute optimal Bayesian updates over exponentially large hypothesis spaces, and to bear against any physical theory whose predictions could only be extracted by solving NP-hard instances. What would count against this view? Please separate decisive defeaters from merely awkward ones: for each candidate counterexample or argument, say whether it would sink the position or only force a qualification, and whether it targets the thesis itself or only its usual applications. I'm especially interested in the objections a careful defender would themselves concede -- for instance, that the view trades on worst-case complexity when nature and brains operate in the average case, that tractability is an asymptotic notion applied to fixed-size phenomena, and that every historical invocation of intractability (factoring, simulation of quantum systems) has had to retreat as algorithms and hardware improved. Finally: is there a real risk that the position is insulated rather than supported -- that "intractable" gets redefined case by case so that nothing could falsify it -- and if so, what commitment would have to be stated up front for the view to be genuinely at risk?
