I sit on the claims committee of a small mutual health fund. Our charter commits us to pay for treatments that correct a malfunction — cases where some part or process of a member's body is not doing its job — and it explicitly excludes enhancements: improvements to a body that is already working as it should. The charter nowhere defines "its job," and two current claims have split us into camps that cannot even agree on the principle dividing them.

The first claim comes from a member whose kidneys retain sodium with unusual efficiency. In the environments where human beings spent most of their history, salt was scarce, and retention of this kind is presumably why the trait persisted; her kidneys are doing, very effectively, the thing kidneys of that kind were shaped by that history to do. On a modern diet, however, the same retention produces chronic hypertension, and she asks us to cover a treatment that dampens it. One camp argues that nothing in her has gone wrong: the organ is performing exactly the role it was formed to perform, so there is no malfunction to correct, only a mismatch between an old design and a new environment, and the fund does not pay to redesign the design. The other camp argues that a part is doing its job when its activity serves the health of the whole it belongs to, and a kidney whose ordinary activity reliably damages the organism that houses it is failing at its job, whatever its history.

The second claim runs the same dispute in the opposite direction. A member asks us to fund a therapy that would restart, in adulthood, his production of the enzyme that digests milk sugar — production that shut down in his childhood, as it historically has in most human beings. The camp that denied the first claim finds itself wanting to approve this one: by the contribution-to-the-owner standard, the capacity would plainly serve him. The camp that approved the first claim wants to deny this one as the rewriting of a working design rather than a repair. Meanwhile a majority of the committee, myself included, has the intuition that the first claim should be paid and the second refused — and we cannot state a principle that delivers both verdicts at once.

So my questions are these. Which of the two standards — the history of what a part was shaped to do, or the contribution it currently makes — is the better reading of "malfunction," and what does each reading cost us as a general policy? If contribution is the standard, what fixes the relevant sense of contribution — survival, health, the member's own goals? Does any choice short of the member's own goals avoid arbitrariness, and does the choice of the member's own goals collapse the malfunction/enhancement line entirely? And is there a principled basis for the combination of verdicts our majority wants, or should we accept that one of our intuitions has to go?
