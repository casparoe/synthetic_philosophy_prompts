In the value theory reading group I'm part of, we keep circling the same question and I'm starting to suspect the problem is the question itself rather than the answers. The issue is whether "better than" is transitive, and it arises through spectrum arguments: you construct a long sequence of cases in which each neighboring pair differs so slightly (a headache of marginally greater intensity, a lifespan one minute longer, one more hangnail endured) that it seems plainly worse than its predecessor, and yet chaining enough of these tiny steps together yields a verdict almost nobody accepts -- say, that a year of agony for one person is better than a century of mild discomfort for many. Roughly three families of responses exist in the literature. The first keeps transitivity and bites a bullet, insisting that somewhere in the spectrum a small step really is neutral or reversed even if we can't see which one. The second keeps transitivity by appeal to lexical thresholds or vague boundaries, so the chain breaks somewhere even if the break is unknowable. The third, associated with figures like Stuart Rachels and Larry Temkin, rejects transitivity itself and holds that goodness comparisons can be essentially tied to the particular pairs being compared rather than to positions on a single scale. Everyone in our group can state all three positions cleanly, everyone agrees about which verdicts are counterintuitive, and nothing in years of published exchanges has budged anyone.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about logic at all but a disguised disagreement about what "better than" reports: one side implicitly treats value comparisons as readings of an underlying magnitude (so transitivity is as secure as transitivity of length), while the other treats them as sui generis comparative judgments answerable to nothing beyond the pair in front of you. If I'm right, the impasse is about the metaphysics of value wearing the mask of a puzzle about inference rules. But I'm not confident, and I want this tested rather than assumed.
