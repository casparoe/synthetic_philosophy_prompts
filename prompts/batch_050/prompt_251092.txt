I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and our reading group has been stuck for weeks on the color phi phenomenon (two differently colored dots flashed in quick succession at different locations; people report one dot moving smoothly across the screen and changing color partway along its path, apparently before the second dot could even have registered). Two of us keep butting heads over what this shows about the timing of experience, and honestly its getting old, so I've volunteered to draft the joint statement an adversarial collaboration between them should produce.

The two camps:

1. The fact-of-the-matter camp: whether the case is Stalinesque (the brain fabricates the motion-and-color-change experience in some pre-conscious editing stage, so the first dot is never actually experienced as stationary) or Orwellian (the first dot genuinely is experienced as stationary, and the memory is instantly rewritten) is a real question, and one of the two happened, even if no experiment could in principle tell us which.

2. The deflationist camp (roughly Dennett-style): once you get serious about what fixes phenomenal content, the two hypotheses stop differing in any testable or even conceivable way; there's no further fact about when the experience "happened," and the question only looks substantive because both sides are still picturing a theater with a finish line for consciousness.

What I need from the document:

- A numbered list of claims both sides can actually sign: about the experimental facts, about what first-person reports can and can't establish, and about what neural timing evidence (backward masking, Libet-style referral experiments, postdiction work) would or wouldn't show.
- The remaining disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording. My guess is the real cruxes are whether there's a determinate moment at which content becomes conscious, and whether unprobed content counts as experienced at all -- but make them check.
- For each crux, one experiment, case, or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance (e.g., what each would predict about reports in probe-at-different-delays variants of the paradigm).
- If some part of their dispute turns out to be merely verbal, flag exactly which part and why.

Format it as the agreed-claims section first, then a disagreements table.
