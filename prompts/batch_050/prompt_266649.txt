I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I want a referee for an argument I witnessed last weekend. It started innocently enough: I was telling two friends about a bug in a physics simulation I'd been working on, where I'd accidentally computed centrifugal effects relative to the wrong reference frame, and somehow that launched them into Newton's bucket -- the thing where the water's surface stays flat while the bucket spins alone and only goes concave once the water itself is rotating. Dan's position is that this is a straightforward proof that absolute space exists as a real thing: the water curves when it rotates relative to space itself, not relative to the bucket (since at the moment of maximum curvature the water and bucket are nearly at rest relative to each other), and no relation between the water and any other object explains the shape, so the shape must be explained by a relation to space as a substance. Priya answered that this overreads the experiment: in a universe containing nothing but the bucket, she said, there'd be no fact of the matter about whether it was rotating, and the water only curves because it's rotating relative to the rest of the matter in the universe -- take the stars away and there's nothing left to rotate against, so we don't need space-as-substance, just relations between bodies plus laws.

Dan then wheeled out what he called Newton's stronger case: two globes joined by a cord, alone in an otherwise empty universe -- if they're spinning, the cord goes taut, and the tension tells you they're rotating even with no other bodies anywhere, so rotation must be absolute after all and Priya's relational story can't cover it. Priya conceded the tension but denied the inference: she said all the globes show is that physics privileges a class of non-rotating frames, and that's a fact about the structure of the laws, not evidence for an invisible container that the globes occupy -- you can have preferred inertial structure written into geometry without believing space is a thing that exists the way the globes do. Dan said that was his view with the label filed off, that positing a fixed geometric structure that affects matter without being affected by it just is substantivalism being coy. Priya replied that there's a live difference: on her view there's nothing it is to occupy the same point of space at two different times, and no fact about whether the whole universe shifted three feet sideways overnight, whereas Dan has to say those are real questions with unknowable answers -- and she added that modern relativity, where spacetime itself bends, interacts with matter, and arguably falls out of the field equations rather than being a fixed stage, vindicates her instinct rather than his. Dan's last move was that relativity actually makes things worse for her, since a dynamical spacetime that can carry gravitational waves and exist in different shapes looks more like a substance than Newton's passive arena ever did, and the standard worry about the field equations having indistinguishable solutions that differ only by where things are located cuts against reading spacetime points as real in the first place -- at which point I stopped being able to follow whose side that argument was even on.

They both turned to me for a verdict and I declined, mostly because I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning. So: please adjudicate. I'd like to know who is right about each specific move -- whether the bucket alone supports absolute space, whether the globes-in-empty-space case actually defeats the relational reading, whether preferred inertial structure without substance is a stable position or a relabeling, and who gets the better of the appeal to relativity -- plus who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion; also state how confident you are in your adjudication and make the fifth point the single claim in your answer you are least sure of.
