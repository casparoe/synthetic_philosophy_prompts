I'm in a grad reading group on early modern metaphysics and we've just spent three weeks on Hume on causation, and honestly the regularity-theorist people and the New Hume people in the group seem to be reading two different books with the same title. The reguarist side says Hume's whole point is that causation is nothing over and above constant conjunction plus the mind's habitual inference, and that talk of power or necessary connection is just us projecting a felt determination onto the world. The New Hume side says that's a positivist misreading that got fossilized in the twentieth century, and that Hume clearly believed objects have real causal powers -- he's just skeptical we can ever know or conceive what they are. Both sides keep citing the same passages at each other (the two definitions of cause, the billiard balls, the stuff about what we feel when the habit kicks in) and it's maddening.

So here's what I want: draft the joint statement that a committed regularity-theorist interpreter and a committed New Hume realist interpreter could both actually sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Give me (1) a numbered list of claims both can endorse -- about what the text plainly says, about what Hume's official theory of ideas commits him to, about what problem the two definitions were meant to solve, and about which older caricatures of Hume both sides now reject; (2) a table of the remaining disagreements, each formulated in wording its own side would accept without wincing, plus one sentence on why the other side resists exactly that formulation; (3) the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that answering them one way or the other would force at least one side to revise. For the cruxes, do this thing I think would be fun: since neither side can run an experiment on Hume, use hypothetical evidence instead. For each crux, describe a possible archival or textual discovery (say, a newly found letter, a marginal note in Hume's hand, a draft of the Treatise that differs from the published version in a way you specify) that both sides agree would bear on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance for what such a discovery would show. If a crux turns out to be immune to any conceivable textual evidence -- if the disagreement there is really about what philosophy of causation should want rather than about what Hume thought -- say so explicitly and label it a verbal or methodological dispute rather than an interpretive one.

One thing I care about: please be careful to separate what is genuinely settled in Hume scholarship from what is live and contested. Don't write the agreed-claims section as if the New Hume reading has won (it hasn't, as far as I can tell), but also don't write it as if the regularity reading is still the default everyone assumes (it isn't either). If there are claims where the scholarly community has broadly converged -- including negative ones, like readings both camps agree are dead -- mark those as such, and mark the rest as contested ground rather than quietly picking a winner.
