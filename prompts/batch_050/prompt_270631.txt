I teach an undergraduate course in political philosophy (about 18 students, one 75-minute session) and will be covering Sen's liberal paradox — the result that a social decision procedure cannot simultaneously guarantee even a minimal form of individual rights (each person decisive over at least one pair of alternatives differing only in their own private sphere) and respect unanimity (the weak Pareto principle), without generating a cycle or landing on an outcome everyone considers worse than an available alternative.

Design an in-class game that makes students run into this conflict before they ever hear it stated. The basic shape I have in mind is the classic two-reader structure: each participant receives a preference card ranking the possible joint outcomes, where the rankings are "meddlesome" — each person cares intensely about what falls within the other's private sphere (what the other reads, wears, eats), not merely their own. The rules of the game should encode the two principles directly: one rule granting each player final say over their own sphere, and one rule requiring the group to accept any change every member prefers. Played honestly under these rules, the group should find itself unable to reach a stable, acceptable outcome — and should feel that the impasse is the rules' fault, not theirs.

The deliverable should include: the exact preference tables on the cards (and a note on why the meddling must be mutual — a variant where only one side meddles should visibly fail to produce the paradox); roles, materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline; instructions for handling the predictable moment when students spontaneously coordinate on the Pareto-superior outcome, which the design must make them experience as overriding someone's right rather than as a happy solution; and a debrief mapping each stage of play onto the formal conditions of the theorem, including which condition each "fix" the students propose would abandon. Finally, be candid about what a game with real, negotiating choosers cannot reproduce about an impossibility theorem that quantifies over all possible preference profiles, and how I should supply that gap.
