Draft the joint statement that two camps in the debate over moral deference could both sign after an extended, good-faith adversarial collaboration. Camp One, the Unificationists, holds that deference to moral testimony is epistemically and morally on a par with deference to testimony on any other subject: if testimony from a reliable source can transmit knowledge or justified belief about geography or arithmetic, it can do the same about right and wrong, and nothing distinctively defective marks a person who adopts a moral view on another's say-so. Camp Two, the Deference Skeptics, holds that there is something distinctively defective — whether epistemically, morally, or in the agent's character — about forming a moral belief simply by deferring to another's testimony, no matter how reliable the testifier.

Structure the statement in four parts:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of at least eight claims both camps can sign, none of them vacuous. These should include the status of the stock contrasting cases (say, readily accepting from a friend that the nearest post office is two streets away versus balking at accepting from that same friend that it is permissible to end a friendship over a petty slight); whether either side denies that moral testimony can transmit propositional justification; whether both accept that some reliance on others' moral judgments is unavoidable in practice; and whether both concede that the felt asymmetry between the stock cases is a datum to be explained rather than a verdict on who is right. Each agreed claim must be phrased so that neither side could read it as smuggling in its own position.

2. Disagreements, precisely stated. Each residual disagreement in a formulation its own side would accept. Require, at minimum, that the Skeptics state where the alleged defect is located — in the justificatory status of the deferred belief, in the agent's understanding, in the agent's character or practical identity, or in the appropriateness of acting on the belief — and commit explicitly to which of these their view requires and which it does not; and that the Unificationists state whether they take the felt asymmetry to be illusory, an artifact of confounds (moral questions being harder, testifiers less often reliable), or real but benign.

3. Cruxes and agreed tests. For each crux — a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp — give the case or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Include, at minimum, these two stipulated probes. First, the Oracle: a system certified, across millions of audited trials, to predict a person's own idealized, fully informed reflective moral verdicts far more reliably than any human adviser; each camp must say in advance whether adopting the Oracle's deliverances would instantiate the alleged defect and what that verdict implies. Second, the Transparent Deputy: a person who defers on a moral question while fully grasping and being able to rehearse the testifier's reasoning; each camp must say whether understanding without independent endorsement removes the defect, and what that answer entails for where the defect is located.

4. Concessions in principle. A short closing paragraph, signed separately by each camp, stating what result would move it to the other side.

Write the statement in formal American English, in the measured register of a published collaboration, and let the drafting do the work: formulations that are neither empty nor slanted, and a clean separation of disagreements that are genuine from ones that turned out to be about words.
