My flatmate has a confident but entirely unanalysed ear for generic sentences, and I have been keeping a record of her verdicts. She accepts 'ducks lay eggs' (even though only mature female ducks ever lay), 'mosquitoes carry malaria' (even though only a small minority of mosquitoes do), 'lions have manes' (adult males only), and 'tigers are striped'; she rejects 'birds are blue', 'cars are red', and 'ducks are female' (roughly half of all ducks are female, yet she insists the sentence is plainly false). A purely statistical rule is therefore out, but more than one subtler rule seems to fit her record equally well: one on which a generic is true when the property is in some sense characteristic of the kind, and one on which it is true when the property is sufficiently striking or hazardous and the kind is at least capable of exhibiting it.

Her new case is 'vending machines kill people'. People are occasionally crushed by toppling vending machines, so the capability is there, but killing is hardly what vending machines characteristically do, and the proportion of vending machines that ever kill anyone is minuscule. Please predict her verdict. In doing so, identify the candidate rules that fit her existing record, state what each predicts for this case, and then commit: I want your best guess as to which rule she is actually running and the verdict it yields, with reasons, rather than an even-handed menu of possibilities. Finally, name a further generic, not appearing anywhere above, whose verdict would best discriminate between the surviving rules.

Give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion: the candidate rules, their respective predictions, your committed verdict, your reasons for preferring it, and the discriminating case.
