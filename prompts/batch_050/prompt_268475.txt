Professional gamblers who use Kelly sizing will routinely pass on bets with positive expected value if the stake is big enough -- a 60/40 coin flip paying even money is +EV, but they'll only bet 20% of their roll on it, and they'd refuse to stake everything even though the expectation of doing so is higher. Meanwhile plenty of trading desks and intro decision-theory textbooks talk as if maximizing expected value per play is just obviously the rational thing. So the same bet gets two different verdicts depending on the practice. What is the Kelly practice actually presupposing? Reconstruct the implicit view: that you're living through one long sequence of plays, so what matters is the growth rate along your own trajectory through time, not the average outcome across parallel copies of you, and that these come apart whenever losing changes what you can stake next. Say whether that's a rival to expected-utility maximization or just a special case of it with log utility, whether the practice rules anything out or is equally well licensed by several theories, and what would have to change in how the gamblers bet if we told them the ensemble average is what really counts.
