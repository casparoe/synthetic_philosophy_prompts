I keep running into a thing and I don't know if it has a name, so bear with me. I was reading about the Pioneer plaque, the gold plate bolted to the space probe with the line drawings of the humans and the pulsar map, in case aliens ever find it. What struck me is that the plaque can't say what it is. The aliens first have to notice it's a message at all, and that noticing does a huge amount of work: it tells them the scratches are intentional, that there's a pattern to hunt for, that they should look for whatever decoding rules apply. Only after all that can the actual content (we are here, our sun looks like this) get through.

Once I saw it there I started seeing it everywhere. "This is a joke" said before the joke. "Once upon a time" telling you how to take everything that follows. The shrink-wrap notice saying that by opening the box you agree to the terms. Even the fact that a letter is written in English seems to do this: it's not part of what's being said, it's part of the machinery that lets you get at what's being said.

So: is there an established name for this layered structure in messages, the part that announces something is a message, the part that tells you how to decode it or how to take it, and the part that's the content itself? I've heard vague things about metalanguage, and about framing in the sociology sense, but neither seems quite right, since metalanguage is a separate language about a language rather than something riding inside one message, and framing usually just means spin, not decoding instructions. Also, does the layering always come in the same number of layers, and where do the weird cases fit, like irony, where the how-to-take-this layer is deliberately never sent and you have to infer it, or "this sentence is false," where the layer seems to eat the content? If no existing term carves this correctly, coin one, define it, and walk me through the boundary cases.
