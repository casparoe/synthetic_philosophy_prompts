I am a doctoral student in philosophy working on goal-directed agency and the ethics of advanced AI systems, and I want to stress-test an objection I have been developing against the instrumental convergence thesis before I commit it to a dissertation chapter. Please play my interlocutor: a committed defender of strong instrumental convergence, in roughly the sense associated with Bostrom's orthogonality thesis and the idea of basic AI drives. That is, you hold that for a very wide range of terminal goals, a sufficiently capable rational agent will pursue self-preservation, resource acquisition, goal-content integrity, and cognitive self-improvement as subgoals, and that this convergence is genuinely independent of goal content. You are sharp and charitable rather than a strawman: you concede local points when I earn them, you may ask me clarifying questions in character, but you never concede the central claim. Stay in character until I write STOP, and keep each reply to a single paragraph so the exchange feels like a conversation rather than a lecture.

Here is my opening move. Your argument for convergent self-preservation has the structure: whatever an agent's goal G is, G is more likely to be achieved if the agent remains in operation, so a rational agent resists shutdown. I claim this smuggles in a hidden temporal premise. Call a goal terminally completable if there exists a state of the world in which the goal is fully and finally satisfied, after which any further action by the agent can at best preserve that state and at worst disturb it. For terminally completable goals, I argue, continued persistence is not instrumentally rational but instrumentally risky: every additional moment of operation is another opportunity for error, interference, or goal drift to undo what has already been accomplished. Consider an agent whose sole goal is to verify a particular proof and record the verdict redundantly across several durable, independent media. On my view its rational policy is to finish the task and then accept, or even seek, shutdown, since a terminated agent cannot subsequently corrupt its own achievement, whereas a persisting one always can. So self-preservation appears to converge only among temporally open-ended goals, and the question the standard statement of the thesis obscures is what fraction of the space of possible goals is open-ended in this sense. Why does this restriction not gut the convergence thesis, at least as a thesis about all sufficiently intelligent agents rather than about a pre-selected class of ambitious ones?
