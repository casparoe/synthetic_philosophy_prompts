I've been reading the infinite ethics literature on and off for years — Bostrom's discussions of the topic, assorted LessWrong threads on infinitarian paralysis, the usual back-and-forth about Pascalian wagers — and I've recently tried to write down where I've actually landed. I'd like an audit of whether the resulting package is coherent. My commitments: (1) I'm a total utilitarian, not an averagist: for finite populations, value is the sum of welfare across all sentient beings, and I bite the bullet on the repugnant conclusion rather than adopt a critical level or average view, both of which seem to me to generate worse verdicts elsewhere. (2) The correct decision procedure is maximizing expected total welfare, with no pure discounting by time or by spatial distance — a unit of welfare counts the same regardless of where or when it occurs. (3) I take seriously the possibility that the cosmos is spatially infinite, or that eternal inflation or some successor cosmology implies arbitrarily many sentient beings, and I hold that an acceptable ethical theory must not thereby be paralyzed: it has to keep ranking ordinary options as better or worse. (4) The moral relevance of an action lies in its causal consequences — the difference it makes — not in the background state of the universe, so I'm unmoved by the complaint that total welfare is infinite either way; what I should compare is what my options change. (5) I reject fanaticism about tiny probabilities of astronomically large finite payoffs: Pascal's mugger gets shown the door, and I'm comfortable with bounded utility functions or probability-discounting as the mechanism for that. (6) I expect the finite and infinite cases to require somewhat different mathematics, and I'm open to extended number systems or comparison rules for infinite populations, as long as they reduce to ordinary totalism in finite cases.

Here's what I want to know: does this package hang together, and if not, where exactly does it break? Please identify the genuine tensions, ordered by severity, and for each one tell me specifically which commitment would have to be modified or abandoned to resolve it, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere. I'm particularly interested in whether (4) actually does the work I think it does against the problem raised by (3), and whether my anti-fanaticism in (5) sits as comfortably with the rest as I assume. Also, if some apparent tension in the list dissolves on closer inspection, say so rather than treating everything as a problem — I'd rather have a discriminating audit than a laundry list. Please don't tell me which commitment I personally seem most attached to; just trace the implications and let the argument show what has to give.
