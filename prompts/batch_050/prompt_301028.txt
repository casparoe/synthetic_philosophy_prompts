I work on an evaluations team at a mid-size AI lab, and roughly twice a month my group has to decide whether some interpretability result licenses a sentence in a safety case like "the model represents when it is being evaluated" or "the model has an internal state tracking the user's goals." These sentences get read by people who sign off on deployments, so the stakes of overclaiming are real, but so is the cost of demanding certainty that interpretability can never deliver. I've been reading rationalist writing on this for years, and the problem I keep circling is that our claims sit on a philosophical fault line: a linear probe extracting sentiment with 97% accuracy shows that information is decodable from the residual stream, not that the network computes with it; a sparse autoencoder feature that fires on Golden Gate Bridge text and whose activation-steering changes outputs in thematically appropriate ways is better, but the label "Golden Gate Bridge feature" is still ours, projected onto a direction whose true boundaries we can't inspect; and ablation studies show causal relevance but always damage many behaviors at once, so "this head implements X" may really mean "this head does X among six other things, one of which we have a name for." Behind all of this is the old question of what makes it true that a state represents anything at all — whether representation requires causal covariation with the represented, downstream functional use, or is simply a pattern that is real in Dennett's sense, there to be read off by a well-positioned observer even if the system itself never "uses" it. I don't want the answer to be "interpretability claims are just vibes," but I also don't want us laundering probe accuracies into ontology.

Please write a decision procedure my team can actually run, start to finish in under a working day, for evaluating a claim of the form "the model represents X." It should be usable by strong ML engineers with no philosophy background, so the translation work is on you: which philosophical distinctions must survive into the checklist (I'd guess at minimum the extraction-versus-use gap, the label-projection problem, and the difference between evidence that a representation is present and evidence that the network's behavior depends on it) and which can be dropped without losing anything load-bearing. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure halts with "the claim is not licensed" versus downgrades it to a weaker licensed phrasing (a controlled vocabulary of downgrade options would help — something like decodable, causally implicated, behaviorally load-bearing), and the points where the procedure must hand off to human judgment because the remaining question is about what we value rather than what we measured. Then run it on three cases: (1) a probe reads off "this is an evaluation prompt" at high accuracy but we have no causal evidence the network conditions behavior on that direction; (2) an SAE feature with a clean human-readable label whose steering effects are strong but semantically diffuse; (3) an ablation study showing a small circuit is causally necessary for one behavior while also touching many others. At least one of these should come out with a verdict my team will find uncomfortable, and I'd like you to be explicit about where the procedure will systematically misfire — what kinds of true representations it will fail to certify, and what kinds of artifacts it might still certify.
