So in my phil of logic reading group (we're doing a unit on self-reference and AI safety stuff) we keep hitting this analogy people use for the Löbian obstacle: a system trusting its own reasoning is like trusting a guy whose only credential is that he tells you he's honest. The con man says "you can trust me," and if that alone convinces you, you'll swallow whatever he says next. Same way, a formal system that just takes on board "if I prove it, it's true" will supposedly end up proving anything, and Löb's theorem is meant to be the formal proof of why that move is incoherent. But when I actually worked through the statement of the theorem it seemed... weirder then the con man story. It doesn't seem to say self-trust is impossible, it says something conditional, roughly: if the system can prove "if I prove P then P," then it could already prove P on its own, so the trust-principle never does any real work. And I'm pretty sure you CAN consistently add that reflection principle to a system as a new axiom -- you just get a stronger system, which then can't prove its own reflection principle, and so on up. So where exactly is the "obstacle"?

Can you audit the con-man analogy for me? I want: (1) the explicit mapping -- con man to what, "you can trust me" to the reflection schema, swallowing whatever he says next to what, formally; (2) where the mapping breaks, e.g. the con man is lying or at least unreliable, but the system isn't asserting anything false, it's just unable to certify itself from the inside -- is the failure mode really dishonesty/gullibility or something structurally different; (3) what the analogy smuggles in -- my suspicion is it makes Löb sound like a problem about bad motivation or naive trust when it's really about diagonalization and self-reference, but maybe I'm wrong.
