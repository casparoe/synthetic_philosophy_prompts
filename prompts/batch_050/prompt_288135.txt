My sister has spent the past year reading population ethics and has settled into a pattern of judgments I cannot quite systematize. I want to predict what she will say about a case I plan to put to her, and to understand what her commitments actually amount to. Her record, as I have it, is this. First, asked whether it would be an improvement to add two billion people to a world like ours, where the new people would live decent lives, clearly worth living but somewhat below the average quality of existing lives, with no effects on anyone already alive, she said it plainly would be an improvement, though a smaller one than if the added lives had been excellent. Second, offered a choice between two futures for humanity, one of twelve billion people with excellent lives and another of two trillion people whose lives are only just worth living, each day containing marginally more good than bad, she chose the smaller future without hesitation and said the larger one struck her as a loss rather than a bargain. Third, asked whether it would make the world better, worse, or neither to add a vast number of people whose lives are barely worth living in just this sense, again with no effects on anyone else, she said it would be neither better nor worse; pressed, she conceded she might mildly prefer that it not happen, but she could not tell whether that preference tracked anything real or was mere squeamishness.

The case I mean to put to her is a forced choice. One of two additions to our world must take place; there is no third option, and neither addition affects anyone now alive. Either one million people are added whose lives contain unrelenting suffering and are clearly not worth living, or ten billion people are added whose lives are barely worth living in exactly the sense of her earlier cases. Which will she choose, and which should she choose if she wants to be consistent with her own record? Please begin by explaining which standard views in population ethics her three verdicts already rule out, then identify the principles that remain consistent with all three, state what each of those principles predicts about the forced choice, and give me your best guess at her verdict together with your reasons. I would also like to know which of her earlier cases is doing the most work in your prediction, whether you think her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, and, if it does, what further case would most efficiently distinguish the surviving principles from one another.

Please answer in continuous prose, as though writing me a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
