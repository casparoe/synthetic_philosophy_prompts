Until recently I was a staff writer at a regional newspaper; six months ago I left to run an independent newsletter and podcast, and I have been turning over whether I escaped what Kierkegaard feared about the press or merely distilled it into a purer form. This question was sharpened for me by rereading his review of Two Ages, the section usually published in English as The Present Age. In that text, as I understand it, Kierkegaard argues that the daily press manufactures what he calls the public: an abstraction that lets everyone have an opinion on everything without anyone having to be someone in particular, and that this levels the distinction between the person who acts and the person who merely comments, so that courage and conviction are replaced by reflective chatter in which no one is ever committed and no one is ever responsible. (I am paraphrasing rather than quoting, since I am working from a translation and do not want to lean on any single rendering; please correct my summary if I have distorted him.)

Here is my question. Kierkegaard's target was an institution with editors, printing presses, and a small class of professional opinion-makers. What would he most plausibly say about the present arrangement, where there is no editor to scold, where the "press" is an engagement-optimizing algorithm, and where every reader is simultaneously a publisher, a critic, and a member of the crowd? I can see the case that this is leveling perfected — the abstraction of the public automated, opinion without responsibility at industrial scale. But I can also see the case that it is something he had no categories for: his critique assumed a distinction between those who address the crowd and those who merely belong to it, and that distinction has now collapsed. Which reading is better supported by his actual argument in The Present Age and related writings, and where does the analogy between the nineteenth-century feuilleton and the algorithmic feed genuinely break down?

I am asking you to reconstruct rather than to ventriloquize, so please be explicit about how far the evidence carries you: mark what is grounded in the texts, what is plausible extrapolation, and what is guesswork. And if the question as I have posed it rests on a confusion — a misreading of the passage, an anachronism smuggled into the framing, or a counterfactual that simply cannot be answered responsibly — I would rather be told that plainly than receive a polished answer to a badly formed question.
