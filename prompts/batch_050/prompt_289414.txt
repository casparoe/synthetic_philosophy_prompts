So I write mystery novels, and I've been obsessing over something since a philosophy-PhD friend explained counterpart theory to me over beers. In draft one of my new book my detective, Mara, never lies. In draft two she lies constantly. I kept saying "she'd never do that" and then catching myself, and my friend said, well, per David Lewis's modal metaphysics, draft-two Mara is a *counterpart* of draft-one Mara, not literally her. Fine. But then I worked out this argument and now I can't see where it breaks, and it breaks something I need as a novelist:

(1) "Mara could have been a liar" is true iff some possible world contains a counterpart of Mara who lies.
(2) What counts as a counterpart of Mara is fixed by a similarity relation, and which respects of similarity count is set by context and our interests, not by anything in Mara herself.
(3) For any property you pick, there's some similarity relation under which a thing with that property counts as Mara's counterpart — a teacup resembles her in being a finite spatiotemporal object made of atoms, and in a context where those are the respects that matter, the teacup qualifies.
(4) So "Mara could have been a teacup" comes out true. Worse: every modal claim about her comes out true, since *something* out there resembles her in whatever respects you like.
(5) A theory on which every modal claim about a character comes out true is useless to me — it can't underwrite the difference between "she'd never do that" and "she might."
(6) So counterpart theory proves too much. The only fix is to restrict which counterparts count by building in something essential to Mara — but then we've just reinstated transworld identity with essences, which is exactly what the theory was suppossed to avoid.

Act as a metaphysics professor who also happens to read a lot of genre fiction. Think carefully before you answer — maybe start by brainstorming a few candidate weak points before commiting to one. Then tell me: which step do you reject, why did it look plausible, and what does rejecting it actually cost? I'd most like to keep (1) and (5), since those are the ones doing real work for me. If the answer involves the Humphrey objection or Lewis just biting the bullet on context-dependence, spell out exactly how much of my "she'd never do that" talk survives.
