Molecular and organismal biology are saturated with normative and semantic vocabulary. Textbooks say that DNA encodes proteins, that transcription and translation machinery proofreads and corrects errors, that the immune system recognizes self and mistakes pollen or the body's own tissue for a threat, that a mutated enzyme is malfunctioning. Nobody believes ribosomes are conscious; the working assumption seems to be that a cell can get something wrong in roughly the same unembarrassed sense in which a photocopier can jam. I want to know what this practice presupposes philosophically.

Here is the puzzle that makes the question non-trivial. On a purely causal or correlational picture of information — a state carries information about whatever it reliably co-occurs with — there is no room for error: a molecule produced by the "wrong" cause simply carries information about that cause, since every causal chain is equally lawful. So the biologist's distinction between correct and incorrect functioning appears to need something more than bare covariance. The main candidate on offer is a teleological theory of content: a mechanism represents whatever it was selected to respond to (by natural selection, or by design, or by learning), and error is failure to perform that selected function. But there are rivals worth taking seriously: that the vocabulary is indispensable metaphor with no literal content; that error is grounded in the statistical norm of the current population rather than in selection history; that malfunction is fixed by designers' intentions for artifacts and only analogically extended to organisms.

Please do three things:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory of content and error that ordinary biological practice — encoding, proofreading, misrecognition, malfunction talk — appears to presuppose. Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits well with: could a fictionalist about function-talk, or a statistical-normal-function theorist, keep every part of the practice intact? Identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license it equally.
2. Check the practice's boundary conditions for consistency. Biologists withhold the normative vocabulary from some cases: no one says a crystal makes an error, or that water misrepresents the temperature by expanding, or that a rock malfunctions by failing to roll downhill — yet a broken clock does malfunction, and a bee's dance can misinform. What principle is doing the sorting, and does the same principle justify the usage uniformly across genetics, immunology, and neuroscience, where researchers also say the visual system misrepresents?
3. State what would actually have to change in biological writing and explanation if the teleological theory turned out to be untenable — whether anything empirically substantive would be lost, or only a façon de parler.

Please separate clearly what is settled (for instance, that bare correlational information cannot by itself underwrite talk of error) from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of biology (for instance, whether teleological theories succeed, or whether the vocabulary is literal), and do not present a contested position as the consensus.

Give the analysis in prose, then append a JSON object with exactly these fields: "implicit_theory" (string; your best reconstruction in two or three sentences); "strictly_presupposed" (array of strings; commitments the practice could not survive losing); "merely_compatible" (array of strings; theories that could license the whole practice); "ruled_out_if_literal" (array of strings; theories inconsistent with the practice on a literal reading of its vocabulary); "underdetermined_points" (array of strings; places where the practice does not decide between rivals); "cost_of_revision" (string; what would change if the teleological theory fails).
