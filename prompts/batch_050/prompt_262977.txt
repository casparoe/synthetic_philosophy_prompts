I write about science for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a feature on a philosophical dispute I keep colliding with in my reporting: whether the sciences carve nature at its joints. What strikes me is that every field I cover seems to have reached its own local verdict, while philosophers go on arguing the general question as if it were still open. Chemistry looks like the textbook success: classifying elements by atomic number unified a chaotic taxonomy and predicted undiscovered elements. Biology looks like the textbook failure: working biologists have produced dozens of competing species concepts and largely stopped trying to pick one. Psychiatry is openly contested: the US National Institute of Mental Health distanced itself from the diagnostic categories of the DSM on the grounds that they fail to track underlying mechanisms. Meanwhile the philosophical literature runs on as a single debate — realists about natural kinds, defenders of homeostatic property cluster kinds, and pluralists who think different classificatory schemes are legitimate for different purposes — with, so far as I can tell, no one changing sides.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the general debate has stalled because the phrase natural kind is being asked to do at least three jobs at once: to underwrite inductive inference, to mark which classifications are mind-independent rather than conventional, and to explain why some taxonomies support successful science. The realist, the cluster theorist, and the pluralist may each be optimizing for a different job, in which case the impasse is partly a disagreement about which question the label is supposed to answer.

Am I right about the structure of this disagreement? Diagnose it from what the positions actually claim: is it a substantive metaphysical dispute, a verbal dispute about the word natural, or several local questions — one per science — wrongly prosecuted under one heading? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a piece of empirical history, a formal criterion, or an argument that the general question should simply be retired.
