The exam question was: "Leibniz holds that the actual world is the best of all possible worlds. Explain what this thesis means, what considerations guide God's choice among possible worlds on Leibniz's picture, and how Leibniz responds to the objection that the existence of evil shows this world cannot be the best." Students had about 25 minutes. I'd like you to grade one student's answer out of 10 and, more importantly, give me specific comments I can adapt as written feedback — pointing to particular claims in the answer that are correct, that are incorrect, or that need qualification, rather than giving a general impression.

Here is the student's answer in full: "Leibniz's thesis is that God, being perfectly good, wise, and powerful, surveyed the infinity of possible worlds and chose to actualize the best one. Since God is omniscient, he could compare all these worlds, and since he is perfectly good, he could not have chosen a worse one over a better one — so the very existence of this world proves it is the best. This is why Leibniz famously holds that everything that happens is for the best: each event, however bad it seems, serves the best in its particular place in the order of things. God's choice is constrained only by logic: he cannot make contradictions true, but anything logically consistent is available to him. As for evil, Leibniz's response in the Theodicy is that evil is nothing real in itself — it is a privation, an absence of good, the way darkness is an absence of light. So strictly speaking God did not create evil at all, and the objection dissolves: the world contains no evil, only lesser degrees of perfection, which are unavoidable in any finite creation. A world with no such limitations would be God himself, and God cannot create another God. Hence this is the best of all possible worlds, and the complaint that a better world seems imaginable merely reflects our ignorance of what is actually possible."
