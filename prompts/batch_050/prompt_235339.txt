A colleague in my decision-theory reading group presented an argument last week whose conclusion seems to prove far too much, and neither of us has been able to locate the false step. I would like a diagnosis.

First, the setup, since not everyone knows the game. In the Pasadena game, a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads. If the first heads occurs on toss n, the player receives a payoff of (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n dollars — so +2 with probability 1/2, −2 with probability 1/4, +8/3 with probability 1/8, −4 with probability 1/16, and so on. The nth outcome contributes (−1)^(n+1) / n to the expectation, so the expected value is given by the alternating harmonic series: it converges, but only conditionally, and therefore — by the Riemann rearrangement theorem — it can be made to sum to any real number whatsoever under a suitable reordering of its terms. The standard moral is that the game has no expected value at all.

His argument runs as follows:

(1) Where an option's expected value is undefined, expected-value maximization delivers no verdict: it cannot rank that option against any alternative.

(2) The Pasadena game's expected value is undefined.

(3) For any ordinary decision, there is some nonzero probability that its consequences ramify without bound — every choice carries at least a minuscule chance of setting off chains of events with escalating positive and negative stakes whose probabilities decay geometrically (it might affect an unboundedly large future population, the actual physics of the universe might not bound achievable payoffs, and so on). That is, every real prospect contains a Pasadena-like component with some tiny probability.

(4) If a prospect decomposes into a bounded component plus a component whose expectation is undefined, then the expectation of the whole is undefined: an undefined term infects any sum it enters.

(5) So every option in every real decision has undefined expected value. (From 3 and 4.)

(6) So expected-value maximization delivers no verdicts about real decisions at all; it is not merely silent about pathological casino games but useless in general. (From 1 and 5.)

His conclusion: we should stop treating expected value as the backbone of rational choice. But this cannot be right. Expected value plainly does discriminate well among ordinary prospects — insurers, investors, and engineers rely on it daily to good effect — and a theory is not refuted by a game nobody is ever offered. So one of (1)–(4) has to go, or an inference has to be invalid. Yet each premise looks impeccable to me: (1) seems to state what an undefined expectation means, (2) is a theorem, (3) seems like a modest empirical concession (the probabilities involved may be astronomically small), and (4) seems like arithmetic.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? More specifically: if some premise or inference is doing hidden work — smuggling in a stronger assumption than it appears to — name it precisely and explain why it looked harmless. I would also like the alternatives priced: for each premise one might deny, say what one must then accept, whether that concerns bounded utilities, comparisons between options whose expectations are both undefined, the empirical claim in (3), or the question of whether the same argument generalizes to rival decision theories and thereby indicts them too. If I must keep as much as possible, I would most like to preserve (1) and the ordinary practice of expected-value reasoning; I am readiest to sacrifice (3), though I suspect the interesting fault lies elsewhere and I want to be told if I am wrong about that.

Finally, please indicate how confident you are in your diagnosis overall, and single out the one point in your answer about which you are least sure.
