Construct an original thought experiment that sharpens the Meno problem: why should knowledge be more valuable than mere true belief if both can guide the same action to the same successful result?

The scenario must satisfy these conditions:

1. Use a realistic contemporary setting involving two ordinary agents. Do not reuse the familiar road-directions example or rely on science fiction, perfect predictors, divine assistance, or extraordinarily specialized knowledge.
2. At the first stage, both agents believe the same proposition \(p\), \(p\) is true, and both perform the same action successfully. One agent knows that \(p\); the other merely has a true belief that \(p\). Make this epistemic difference arise from how each belief was formed and integrated with reasons, not from a difference in intelligence, confidence, diligence, social standing, or general reliability.
3. Give the merely true believer one localized advantage: in the first stage, that agent should act slightly faster or otherwise achieve a marginally better immediate result because the knower pauses to audit the grounds of the belief. This should prevent the case from defining knowledge as superior in every particular use.
4. Introduce a pressure test: a plausible but misleading cue then makes it tempting to abandon \(p\), even though \(p\) remains true. The scenario must generate, rather than merely stipulate, why the knower’s belief is appropriately stable while the merely true believer’s is not. The knower’s persistence must not look like blind stubbornness.
5. Add a contrasting branch in which circumstances change and \(p\) becomes false. There, the knower should be able to recognize a genuine defeater and revise. Explain what distinguishes this genuine defeater from the earlier misleading cue.

After presenting the scenario, provide a compact counterfactual ledger comparing the two agents across four situations: ordinary conditions, urgency, misleading evidence while \(p\) remains true, and a genuine change that makes \(p\) false. Distinguish first-order success—whether the agent performs the right action on a particular occasion—from counterfactual stability—whether the belief continues to guide action well across the relevant variations.

Then explain:

- exactly what the knower knows, how that agent knows it, and why the other agent’s corresponding belief falls short of knowledge;
- whether the case shows knowledge to be valuable in itself, instrumentally, or only as a sign of some further valuable state;
- why the answer is not merely that the knower is more reliable generally, since the case forbids giving the knower that broader advantage;
- which feature of the construction is doing the philosophical work, and whether the apparent advantage of knowledge disappears if that feature is removed;
- how someone defending the practical parity of knowledge and true belief would attack the case.

End with a final line in exactly this form: \answer{<one sentence naming the feature that makes knowledge more valuable than mere true belief in the constructed case>}
