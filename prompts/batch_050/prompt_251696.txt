I write literary fiction for a living, so my profession more or less presupposes that people have beliefs, desires, and motives, and that citing them genuinely explains what people do. For years I've belonged to a reading group of novelists and cognitive scientists that keeps circling one question without moving: is that presupposition true? The scientists lean eliminativist. Folk psychology, they say, is a rough pre-scientific theory of behavior, and a mature neuroscience will no more find beliefs in the brain than chemistry found phlogiston; the useful fictions will be retired. The writers, often without knowing whose view they're channeling, take Dennett's line: a belief is real the way a pattern is real. If you cannot compress the behavior of a chess engine or a spouse without adopting the intentional stance rather than the physical or design stance, then the attribution is as true as anything science delivers. My own suspicion is that the two camps are not disagreeing about minds at all but about what must hold for something to count as real, with one side demanding a mechanism and the other demanding only incompressible predictive purchase. Please diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicate it: is it an empirical bet on the future of neuroscience, a verbal dispute over the word real, or two questions, one ontological and one about the indispensability of a stance, being traded under a single name? And given your diagnosis, what would progress actually look like: a finding from neuroscience, a formal account of pattern-realism that both sides could test, or a decision to split the question in two?
