I'm helping organize a workshop on solar radiation management, and the two factions I've invited have spent every previous session talking past each other. On one side are the deployment-preparedness researchers, who argue that because stratospheric aerosol injection is cheap, technically within reach, and possibly the only lever that could cut global temperatures within a decade, we have an obligation to research it seriously now and to build the governance and monitoring infrastructure before a panicked state does it alone. On the other side are the non-use camp, who argue that even researching deployment creates constituencies and lock-in, that the prospect of a cheap thermostat already saps political will for decarbonization (the moral hazard worry), that a terminated program would unleash rapid warming ecosystems and agriculture cannot track (the termination shock worry), and that no legitimate global decision procedure exists for a technology whose rainfall and monsoon effects would fall unevenly on billions of unconsenting people. Both camps, as far as I can tell, accept that aerosols would do nothing about ocean acidification, that the engineering is easier than the politics, and that unilateral deployment by a single mid-sized state is physically plausible.

Draft the joint statement these two camps would produce if forced into an adversarial collaboration rather than another panel discussion. Specifically: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims, worded precisely enough that neither side would demand an asterisk -- don't pad this with banalities neither camp ever contested, but do include agreements each side might find uncomfortable; (2) a table or list of the residual disagreements, each stated neutrally enough that both camps would accept the wording as a fair description of what they disagree about, with an explicit note where a supposed disagreement turns out on inspection to be verbal rather than substantive; (3) the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp, even if not all the way -- and for each crux, at least one concrete piece of evidence, observable development, or real-world case that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance (for example, what each would expect to see in public-opinion data, treaty behavior, or the trajectory of mitigation effort if their view is correct); and (4) at least one disagreement each camp concedes is not empirically resolvable on any realistic timescale, with an explanation of how that camp proposes to act under that unresolved uncertainty.

The document should read like something both factions could actually sign, not like a stealth victory for one of them. Where a formulation would be slanted, say so and offer the neutral version. Aim for specificity throughout: name mechanisms, institutions, and timescales rather than gesturing at values in the abstract.
