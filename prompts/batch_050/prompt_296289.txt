I manage facilities for a mid-sized school district, which means I oversee our pest-control contracts and sign off on treatment decisions roughly once a month -- everything from ants in cafeterias and wasps near playgrounds to the occasional rodent problem in older buildings. For years our approach was purely pragmatic: whatever is cheapest, fastest, and keeps parents from complaining. But a recent conversation with a teacher on our sustainability committee, plus some reading I did afterward, convinced me that there's a genuine ethics literature on pest control that we simply never engaged with -- questions about whether insects and other "pests" have morally relevant experiences, whether suffering matters proportionally to the number of individuals affected, whether killing is different from excluding or repelling, and whether we owe creatures different treatment depending on how sentient they plausibly are. I don't want to become an amateur philosopher, but I also don't want to keep making these calls by reflex.

What I'd like is a decision procedure my staff and I can actually run when a pest problem is reported, one that operationalizes whatever the ethics of pest control genuinely establishes. It should be a sequence of questions we ask in order: things like whether the target species is a plausible candidate for morally relevant sentience, whether non-lethal methods (exclusion, habitat modification, repellents) have been seriously considered rather than merely gestured at, how to weigh a method that kills few vertebrates slowly against one that kills many invertebrates quickly, and how to factor in the genuine stakes on our side -- disease risk, allergic students, food safety regulations we can't waive. Please specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates in a clear verdict, and where it stops and hands the decision back to a human because the remaining question is one of values or genuine uncertainty rather than something a checklist can settle. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and remember that my staff are building engineers, not ethicists -- the procedure has to be runnable by someone whose entire moral toolkit is common sense plus this document.

Then run the procedure on three real cases from my files: (1) an ant infestation in an elementary school kitchen, where the current contract specifies broadcast insecticide; (2) mice in the walls of a 1920s-era building, where our vendor's default is glue boards -- cheap, effective, but the animals die slowly over hours; and (3) a wasp nest six feet from a playground entrance, flagged by a parent whose child has a documented venom allergy. At least one of these should come out in a way I won't like, because if the procedure never costs us money or convenience, it isn't really an ethical procedure. Finally, tell me plainly what this procedure cannot do: which decisions it will misfire on, and what I should do about the cases -- like a public-health-mandated treatment ordered by the county -- where the ethics is clear but my authority is not.
