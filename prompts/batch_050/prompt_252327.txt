I work as a software engineer and our team does a monthly "lunch and learn" where someone explains something semi-technical for about 40 minutes. I picked constructive math / intuitionistic logic, partly because half the team has poked at Haskell or a proof assistant and keeps hitting walls where Coq won't let them do the thing they want. Audience: 8-10 engineers, strong on programming, zero formal logic, no philosophy background. Same for me honestly, I've just been reading about this for a few weeks and I'm not confident I'd structure it right.

Don't give me the explanation itself — help me plan how to run the session. Specifically:

1. The order the ideas have to come in. My instinct is to start with "a proof is a construction / a piece of evidence" before touching which inference rules survive, because otherwise dropping the law of excluded middle just looks arbitrary and spiteful. But I'm not sure whether the meaning of the connectives (what counts as a proof of A∨B, of ¬A, of ∃x.P(x)) has to be rebuilt before or after that, or whether that's too abstract for a warmup. Lay out the dependency structure: what has to already be in their heads before each next step will make sense, and what breaks if I get the order wrong.

2. One carrying example. I'm leaning toward the classic "there exist irrationals a and b with a^b rational" proof that case-splits on whether √2^√2 is rational, since it makes excluded middle visibly do work and the nonconstructiveness is easy to feel. Is that the right one for this crowd, and when exactly do I spring it — before or after they know what's being given up? If you'd pick something else for programmers, say what properties the example needs to have.

3. The misconception map. The ones I expect, roughly in the order they'll surface: (a) "constructivists think excluded middle is false" rather than not provable in general, (b) "proof by contradiction is banned entirely" when really negation-intro is fine and it's only ¬¬elimination that's out, (c) "so constructive math is just math a computer can run, it's all Curry-Howard" — the team will overfit to this one hard because it's the only hook they have, and (d) "constructive math must be a tiny crippled version of real math." Where in the sequence do I head each one off, and is there one I should let them sit with for a while because correcting it too early breaks something downstream?

4. A check at the end: one exercise that shows it landed. I was thinking of handing them a short classical proof and having them find the exact step that fails constructively — tell me what makes a good specimen for that and what wrong answers would reveal.

5. What to explicitly skip at this level and say so out loud. I'm guessing Brouwer's actual philosophy, Kripke models, choice sequences, the fan theorem. Confirm or adjust that list.

Also they'll definately ask "ok but IS the law of excluded middle actually true or not?" — tell me where in the plan I should have a defensible non-answer ready so it doesn't eat the last fifteen minutes in a platonism argument nobody wanted.
