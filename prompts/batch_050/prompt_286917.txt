Last Saturday at our monthly poker night, two friends of mine got into an argument that ate the entire back half of the evening, and they've now both separately texted me asking me to settle it, which I honestly can't. Setup: after the game, Maya proposed a standing side bet, open to anyone in the group, forever. Flip a fair coin. Heads, your stake grows by 50%. Tails, you lose 40% of it. You can play as many rounds as you want, winnings compund, and you can walk away whenever you like. Dev's position: declining is just irrational. Every flip has positive expected value (0.5 times +50% plus 0.5 times -40% is +5% of your stake), the coin is fair, and the law of large numbers says the more you play the closer your result tracks the expectation -- so more rounds is strictly better, and refusing is leaving money on the table. Maya's position: if you actually simulate a few thousand people playing a few hundred rounds, almost all of them end up basically broke. The average outcome looks amazing, but the average is carried by a tiny number of absurdly lucky trajectories, while the typical trajectory bleeds about 5% per round, and that gets worse the longer you play, not better.

Dev said that's risk aversion in a costume. If losing hurts you more than winning feels good, fine, that's a utility function, own it -- but the arithmetic favors playing and no amount of simulation changes arithmetic. Maya said it has nothing to do with feelings about money: you personally only ever get to live one trajectory, you don't get to average yourself across parallel universes, so the number that matters for you is the growth rate along a single path through time, not the average across a crowd at a single moment. She mentioned some paper she'd read about "ergodicity economics" making exactly this point, though she couldn't remember the author. Dev came back with two moves. First, the fix for Maya's problem is trivial and classical: don't stake your whole roll, bet a fixed fraction like the Kelly people figured out in the 1950s, the single-trajectory growth rate goes positive, problem solved -- so the ergodicity stuff is just expected utility theory with new branding. Second, the ensemble average isn't even a fiction: if a pension fund ran this bet across a million members and pooled the proceeds, everyone really would get the average, so "you only live one trajectory" is a fact about bet sizing and pooling arrangements, not a deep fact about probability. Maya answered that both moves concede her actual point: "resize the bet using a growth-rate criterion" is admitting that raw expected value is the wrong thing to maximize, and pooling changes the bet itself, so it's no defense of the claim that you should take the bet as offered. Dev accused her of moving the goalposts.

So settle this for us, move by move. Who's right about the expected value claim? Is Dev using the law of large numbers correctly -- I have a feeling he's not, but I can't articulate why. Is Maya's typical-trajectory point really independent of utility-function talk, or is Dev right that it smuggles it in? Do the Kelly and pooling responses answer her or concede the game? And who's more right overall? Also tell me if there's something both of them are missing -- I've got a nagging sense that the "walk away whenever" clause matters and neither of them touched it, and I want to know if that's a real loose thread or nothing.
