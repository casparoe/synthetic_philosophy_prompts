Draft the joint statement that a sober documentary realist about history and a narrativist sceptic could both sign after a year-long adversarial collaboration. The realist holds, roughly in Ranke's spirit, that the historian's job is to recover how things actually were and that evidence can discipline interpretation well enough to make some accounts of the past simply more accurate than others. The sceptic, working from the line of thought associated with Carr and Hayden White, holds that facts do not select themselves, that every past event can be emplotted into rival narratives of equal fidelity to the record, and that claims to historical objectivity mistake the conventions of one community of inquirers for contact with the past itself.

The document should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered list of at least six agreed claims. These must be substantive rather than ceremonial: candidates to consider include claims about what the surviving record does and does not fix, about the illegitimacy of fabricating evidence, about the existence of anachronism as a genuine error, and about whether some historical statements are simply true regardless of narrative framing. Where the two camps would agree to a claim for different reasons, state both reasons rather than papering over the difference.

2. A disagreements table giving the residual points of conflict, each formulated twice: once in wording the realist would accept as a fair statement of the dispute, once in wording the narrativist would accept. Disagreements to consider include whether underdetermination of historical narrative by evidence differs in kind from the underdetermination of scientific theory by data; whether the structure found in a narrated past is discovered in the events or imposed by the telling; and whether the convergence of independent historians on an account is evidence of its accuracy or merely of shared training.

3. A cruxes section identifying the two or three sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one side to revise. For each crux, specify a concrete case or test both parties accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. At least one crux must be operationalized through a shared historiographical exercise of your design: pick one well-documented episode whose interpretation has shifted dramatically across generations (state which episode and why you chose it), and have each side write down what it predicts the exercise will reveal about the source of that shift.

4. A verdict on which parts of the dispute, if any, turned out to be verbal. Do not hedge here: if some disagreement dissolves under careful phrasing, name it and show the dissolution; if none does, say so and explain why the disagreement is robust.

Finally, append a short editor's note in your own voice committing to a definite position: state which side you judge to have conceded more ground in the collaboration, which single crux would most change the debate if resolved, and why. Take a stand and defend it; do not present the options and leave the choice to the reader.
