I'm drafting an essay for an undergrad philosophy blog and want comments before I submit it. It's about socially constructed kinds — money, race, gender — and my whole angle is that lumping them together under "socially constructed" hides that they're built in structurally different ways. Draft below, then what I want comments on.

**Three ways to build a kind**

Philosophers love to say that race, gender, and money are all socially constructed, but this bundles together things that work quite differently.

Start with money. A twenty-dollar bill is just paper, but it counts as money because we collectively accept that it does. Searle's picture: X counts as Y in context C, where the counting is all that matters. The paper doesn't have to change at all; only the collective acceptance does. And crucially, if everyone tomorrow stopped accepting dollars — all at once, completely — the dollar would simply stop being money. Nothing physical would have happened. Money is a pure acceptance-kind.

Race is different. Hacking's looping effect is the standard picture here: we classify people, the classification changes how the classified behave and how they're treated, and the changed behavior feeds back into and reshapes the classification. But here's my main claim: the looping picture underdescribes race. Racial categories in the US were maintained for a century less by what anyone believed than by infrastructure — the census form, redlining maps and segregated housing finance, school district lines, anti-miscegenation statutes, segregated blood banks. Call this an enforcement architecture. The architecture does two things neither acceptance nor looping does: (1) it makes the kind sticky — it persists even when beliefs shift, because the forms and the maps and the ledgers are still there sorting people; and (2) it classifies you whether or not you accept the classification, and whether or not the clerk processing the form does either. Nobody has to be currently accepting anything. The machinery does the accepting for you.

Gender, I argue, is mostly a hybrid of acceptance and looping but has a comparatively thin enforcement architecture nowadays (it used to have a heavy one: coverture, legal bars on property and professions, etc.), and I claim this is exactly why gender is the most actively contested of the three right now — there's less machinery holding it in place, so the contest over the acceptance and the looping actually matters.

The payoff of the taxonomy: the three kinds differ in what it would take to dissolve them. Money: stop accepting it, done. Race: changing attitudes isn't enough — you have to dismantle the architecture, which is why "colorblindness" as a shift in belief doesn't touch the kind itself. Gender: somewhere in between, depending on how thick you think its remaining architecture is.
