I'm preparing a graduate seminar on supertasks and the metaphysics of infinity, and I'm assembling a set of cases that between them display the different ways a supertask can generate philosophical trouble. Here is what I have so far, with the puzzle each one is meant to exhibit. (1) Thomson's lamp, toggled at ½, ¾, ⅞, and so on: the trouble is that the final state appears underdetermined by the completed sequence, so the case probes whether the description of an infinite process fixes what happens after it. (2) Zeno's dichotomy, taken as a sequence of genuinely distinct subtasks rather than mere spatial parts: the trouble is whether completing infinitely many tasks in finite time is coherent at all, before any question about end-states arises. (3) The Ross–Littlewood urn, ten balls added and the lowest-numbered one removed at each step: the trouble is a clash between two seemingly compulsory ways of taking a limit, since the urn is provably empty at noon although the count of balls grows without bound. (4) Black's transfer machine, shuttling a marble between two trays at accelerating intervals: the trouble is that the marble has no continuous path to any final position, which pressures the assumption that a completed supertask must leave the world in some determinate state at all.

What I want from you is one more supertask to round out the set — one whose philosophical bite is of a structurally different kind from the four above. A few constraints on what counts as different. It must not be a cosmetic variation on a listed case (no second lamps, no urn with different ball quotas), and it must not simply hybridise two of them. More substantively, its difficulty should not reduce to either of the two template puzzles my list already covers: the indeterminacy of a limit state, or the divergence of rival ways of computing a limit. I have a suspicion that there is room for a supertask whose problem is of another order altogether — for instance, one where the worry is not what the world is like at the end but whether the scenario describes a possible world in the first place, or one where the puzzle concerns causation across the infinite sequence rather than limits — but I don't want to prescribe the answer, and I'd rather you constructed or recovered the best case than force my hint into shape.

For whatever you propose, please do three things. First, state the supertask precisely enough that a seminar could argue about it without repairing it. Second, say exactly where its structure departs from (1)–(4), and why it is not a disguised instance of either template puzzle. Third, give me your honest verdict on whether it genuinely earns its place, including the strongest objection that it collapses into a case already on my list, and how you would respond to that objection. If, on reflection, you think my suspicion is wrong and every coherent supertask really does reduce to the limit-state puzzle or the rival-limits puzzle, then I'd rather hear that argued plainly than receive a contrived fourth case dressed up as a new one.
