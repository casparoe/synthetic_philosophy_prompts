I'm TAing an undergrad seminar on probability and paradox (juniors, about 14 students, one 75-minute block) and anthropic reasoning is up next. I want an in-class game that makes them feel the difference between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption before I ever write those names on the board. No lecture up front, they should just play.

The thing I want them to feel in their gut: the facilitator secretly flips a coin that decides whether a small or a large group of observers gets "created" this round, and students who reason "I should treat myself as a random sample from whoever actually exists" (SSA-ish) will bet differently from students who reason "I'm more likely to find myself existing in the world with more observers in it" (SIA-ish). I want a payoff structure where, over repeated rounds, one of those strategies visibly racks up more points — and then I want to hit them with the presumptuous philosopher problem: the winning strategy, applied to actual cosmology, apparently tells you the universe is almost certainly enormous before anyone looks through a telescope. That's an insane thing to conclude from an armchair and I want them to feel how insane it is at the exact moment they realize it's what won them the game.

So design it. Concrete rules, materials (I have index cards, envelopes, dice, and a bag of wrapped caramels for stakes), how many rounds fit in maybe 40 minutes, how to keep each student from knowing which "world" they're in while still letting them place bets, and a scoring scheme that doesn't just reward getting lucky on a single flip. Then a debrief script that maps specific moments — how people bet, who won, the reveal that the coin was fair — onto SSA, SIA, and the presumptuous philosopher, including the reference class question when it inevitably comes up (do the students who didn't get a card count as observers or not?).

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — students comparing notes mid-round, someone just counting heads in the room to figure out which world it is, one clever student conditioning on the fact that they were handed a card at all — and how to handle each. And be honest about what the game can't reproduce: in the real anthropic cases you only get one run and there's no facilitator who knows the answer, so tell me which parts of the debate I have to just lecture on afterwords.
