In discussions of self-reference, a distinction is often drawn between cleanly stratified hierarchies — where each level governs the one below and is untouched by it — and tangled hierarchies, where the levels loop back on themselves so that what is supposedly "above" ends up being modified by what is supposedly "below." A tangled hierarchy becomes especially interesting when a system is designed around a level that is assumed to be inviolate: a rule, layer, or authority that stands outside the loop and cannot be altered from within. The classic illustration of the tangled case is Escher's Drawing Hands; classic illustrations of inviolate levels include the hardware of a computer relative to the software it runs.

4. It is realistic — a setting that plausibly exists or has existed, not a thought experiment.
