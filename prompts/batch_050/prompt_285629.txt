Alright, so here's my situation. I'm a retired condensed matter physicist, and over the last year or so I've been reading my way into moral philosophy, mostly through the effective altruism literature because it appeals to the same part of my brain that liked optimization problems. Lately I've been chewing on the Sam Bankman-Fried / FTX affair, since he was the most famous living advertisement for "earning to give" right up until the exchange collapsed in late 2022 and he ended up convicted of fraud with a very long prison sentence. I've seen a million hot takes saying this proves EA is rotten or proves nothing at all, and I find both boring. What I want is an actual case analysis, using the standard critiques of earning to give as the lens: the replaceability worry (if he hadn't taken the job someone else would have, so marginal harm is small), the moral licensing / ends-justify-means worry, and the worry that the earning-to-give frame systematically underweights the risk that you'll do damage in the earning part.

Walk me through it in both directions. First, what did the EA framework, taken on its own terms, say Bankman-Fried was facing: what were his options, how should an expected-value-maximizing altruist have thought about running a crypto exchange with dodgy risk controls, and was the mistake (by EA's own lights) one of moral theory or just a bad expected value calculation that ignored tail risk to both customer funds and the movement's reputation? Second, which features of the episode do the standard critiques actually explain, and which do they miss or get wrong -- for instance, does the replaceability objection predict what happened, or does it fail because the harm wasn't really in the earning but in the fraud, which nobody's version of EA ever endorsed? And third, what does the episode show about the critiques themselves: is it a confirmation of the licensing worry, an anomaly for it, or a case none of the standard objections anticipated because it's really about one guy being dishonest rather than a structural problem with earning to give? If you think the whole episode is actually a poor test of any of these views -- say, because a theory of what to do with your career can't be evaluated by whether its most famous adherent turned out to be a criminal -- make that argument, but make it properly rather than as a dismissal. And be honest about where each framework just doesn't have anything to say rather than forcing it to pronounce.
