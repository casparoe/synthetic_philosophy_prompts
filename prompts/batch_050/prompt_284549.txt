For years I have watched the same argument replay among readers of Plato's Laws, and it never advances. The question is what to make of the drinking legislation of Books I and II, where the Athenian Stranger, addressing two Dorians whose cities forbid the symposium, defends regulated drinking as an instrument of moral education: wine, he argues, relaxes shame and inflates confidence, so a drinking party conducted under a sober president is a safe place to practice resisting disgraceful conduct and to observe what a man's soul is really like — a kind of assay of character, indeed the only vice that can be tested without danger, since (as he points out by way of an imaginary fear-inducing potion) no comparably safe test exists for courage. The entire defense rests on a psychology stated early in Book I. Cleinias volunteers the commonplace, which the Athenian at once adopts as his governing premise: "there is a victory and defeat—the first and best of victories, the lowest and worst of defeats—which each man gains or sustains at the hands, not of another, but of himself; this shows that there is a war against ourselves going on within every one of us" (Laws, Book I, trans. Jowett). Victory over self is the point of law; the symposium is its training ground.

The readers divide into three camps, and the camps are old. The first takes the argument at face value: the Laws corrects the Republic, whose guardians must never get drunk, and even the Republic's healthy first city already feasts on moderate wine; the symposium is a rational institution given the self-mastery psychology, and the elaborate hedges — age limits on drinking, the sober master of the revels, wine administered to the old to make them pliable for the choruses — are the marks of a serious design, not of irony. The second camp reads the praise as concessive or dialectical: the Athenian is softening up a Cretan and a Spartan, the assay argument proves too little (Socrates in the Symposium drinks all night and is unaffected, so wine tests nothing in the virtuous, and in everyone else merely displays what their friends already knew), and the very premise quoted above is the tell — the Protagoras had treated being overcome by pleasure as a confused name for ignorance, so legislation built on inner war is second-best politics for second-best souls, not Plato's account of virtue. The third camp dissolves the dispute into biography: the aging Plato abandoned Socratic intellectualism, embraced habituation, and meant exactly what the text says — no tension to explain, only development.

My working hypothesis, offered strictly as a hypothesis, is that the debate has stalled because it is three disagreements wearing one costume: a philological one about what the Athenian proposes, a hermeneutic one about whether he speaks for Plato at all, and a philosophical one about whether character can be trained or tested by deliberately lowering one's guard — and that the third does the real work, since readers seem to divide on the drinking passages according to their prior view of whether victory over self is Plato's mature psychology or a rhetorical fossil. No amount of re-reading the same lines settles that.

What I want is a diagnosis, not another verdict on Plato. Is this dispute verbal, substantive, or both, and where exactly does the joint run? Do the camps differ about what counts as data — the Republic's ban, its first city, the Symposium's ending, the fear-potion analogy — or about standards for detecting Platonic irony, or about the akrasia question underneath? Use the quoted passage as the test case: say how each camp must read it, and whose reading your diagnosis favors. Then state what would count as progress — a distinction, a methodological constraint applied uniformly across the Laws, comparative evidence about Greek sympotic practice, a change of question — or argue that no resolution should be expected and explain why. Please commit: I want a definite diagnosis with reasons, including, where the evidence underdetermines, a statement of which way you would bet and why, rather than a balanced menu from which I am left to choose.
