I'm a retired biochemist who's been going to a philosophy reading group at the local library for about a year now (my wife says it's cheaper than buying a boat). Last month we got onto consciousness, and I keep chewing over a phrase one of the regulars threw out to end an argument: "there's no Cartesian theatre" — Dennett's phrase, I gather, though nobody at the group had the book to hand.

Context: we were discussing that colour phi experiment, where two dots flash one after another in different spots, the second a different colour, and people report seeing one dot move across and change colour halfway. I asked what seemed to me the obvious question: which actually happened? Did the person genuinely see a white dot travel partway and then have their memory doctored afterwards (we were calling this the "Orwellian" option, rewriting history after the fact), or did the brain cook up the whole moving-and-changing story before any of it was experienced, so the white dot never crossed the screen in experience at all (the "Stalinesque" option, show trial staged in advance)? Or possibly I have those two labels backwards, it's definately the kind of thing I'd do.

Anyway, the regular said that once you accept there's no Cartesian theatre, my question dissolves — there's simply no fact of the matter about which description is right, and that's the whole point of the slogan. That felt like a cheat. When I ran a lab, "the question dissolves" was what people said when they didn't have the answer.

So here's what I want. What are the different precise claims that slogan could actually be making? I can gesture at a few candidates myself: something trivial like "the skull doesn't literally contain a little cinema screen" (surely nobody ever believed that); something about there being no single place in the brain where it all comes together; something stronger, that there's no single moment at which an experience happens; and maybe something very strong, that there are no facts about experience beyond what the subject would say or report. Lay the readings out as a list, from platitude to radical, and for each one say whether it's obviously true, genuinely contentious, or actually false, and roughly why.

Then the part I actually care about: which of those readings does my group-mate's move need? I.e. which ones, if true, would actually entail that there's no fact of the matter between the Stalinesque and Orwellian stories? My suspicion is that the slogan is obviously true only on the weak readings and does the argumentative work only on the strong reading, and that he's surfing the gap between the two — but maybe a middling reading is both defensible and sufficient, in which case I owe him an apology at the next meeting.

One more thing, from the scientist's side. People in the group kept appealing to the fact that neural processing takes time (hundreds of milliseconds and so on) as if that supported the strong reading. Does it? Or is there a bad inference lurking in going from "processing takes time and is spread out" to "there's no fact about when the experience occurred"? And if the honest upshot is just "be careful, the order people report things needn't match the order they experienced them," that's something every experimentalist already knows from reaction-time data, and it wouldn't dissolve my question at all. So tell me straight: is Stalinesque-vs-Orwellian a real question that happens to be empirically hard, or is it genuinely confused?
