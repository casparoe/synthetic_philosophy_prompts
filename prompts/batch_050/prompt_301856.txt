So a while back I got obsessed with the unilateralist's curse -- the idea that when a bunch of people can each individually trigger some irreversible action (releasing a finding, firing the missile, publishing the leak), and each has their own noisy private estimate of whether it's net good, the action ends up getting taken basically whenever the MOST optimistic person's estimate clears the bar. Since the max of a bunch of noisy estimates is biased upward, the group systematically over-triggers, even if every individual is unbiased. Fine so far. But then I was arguing with some friends after our reading group and I pushed this line of reasoning that seemed to follow, and now I think it proves way too much and I can't find the weak joint. You should answer as a professional philosopher with real expertise in decision theory and epistemic collective action stuff -- please think carefully and actually engage with the logic step by step rather than gesturing at it.

Here's the argument. (1) In any situation where several agents can each unilaterally do X, and any one of them doing X settles it for everyone, the effective decision-maker is whoever has the highest estimate of X's value. (2) The highest estimate in a group is, on average, an overestimate of X's true value, so such actions are systematically over-chosen, and a rational agent in that position should discount her own estimate before acting. (3) But notice: whenever you personally decide to do something that nobody else is doing -- start a weird company, blow the whistle, pursue a research direction the field has ignored, be the first to intervene in an emergency -- your decision to act is *selected* precisely because your estimate of its value exceeds everyone else's, since everyone with a lower estimate is by definition abstaining. So you ARE the most optimistic member of the relevant population, and (1)-(2) apply to you even though you're deciding alone. (4) So rationality requires you to treat other people's inaction as evidence against your judgment and heavily discount it. (5) Conclusion: acting on your private conviction when the crowd is abstaining is almost always irrational -- first movers, whistleblowers, and contrarians are mostly just the unlucky winners of an epistemic lottery, and the rational default is near-total deference to whatever everyone else is already doing or not doing. Step (5) strikes me as insane -- it would condemn basically every instance of individual initiative ever -- but I genuinely can't say which earlier step to reject. I'd *like* to keep the core curse insight (I think (1) and (2) are basically right in the classic cases) and I definitely want to keep the claim that whistleblowing and contrarian research can be rational, but I want to know what I'm paying for that.
