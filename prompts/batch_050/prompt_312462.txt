For the final exam of an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of physics course, write one essay question (45 minutes, 30 marks) on quantum suicide and quantum immortality. The question should target the epistemological crux rather than invite a summary of the many-worlds interpretation: would surviving repeated trials of a quantum suicide experiment give the experimenter -- though not any bystander -- evidence for many-worlds over collapse interpretations, and does anything deserving the name immortality follow from iterated survival on that picture? After the question, provide a model answer of roughly 500-600 words and a marking scheme out of 30. The marking scheme should name the specific moves a top answer must make: explaining precisely why the experimenter's and the bystander's evidential situations diverge even though the interpretations make identical third-person predictions; stating what survival probability each interpretation assigns the experimenter; and identifying the contentious premises the immortality inference needs, including assumptions about personal identity across branching, about how to weight branches, and about the fact that death is gradual rather than binary, so that branches containing a maimed or progressively incapacitated experimenter undermine the clean always-a-surviving-branch reasoning. It should also name the mistakes that should cost marks: claiming the experiment proves many-worlds; conflating the reasoning with generic anthropic-selection arguments; and treating immortality as a finite, testable consequence rather than an asymptotic claim about vanishing branch weight that no sequence of experiences could ever confirm. The question should be unanswerable by reciting what the Everett interpretation says: it should separate students who understand the self-locating epistemology from those who have merely memorized the labels. Write in American English.
