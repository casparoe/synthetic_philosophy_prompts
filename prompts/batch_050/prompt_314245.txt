I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I keep circling back to markets. Years ago I fell for the no-trade theorems -- Milgrom and Stokey, the Grossman-Stiglitz tension, the whole line of results suggesting that if everyone's rational and it's common knowledge, then purely speculative trading between informed and uninformed parties shouldn't really happen, since anyone willing to trade with you is telling you something by being willing. So I've adopted, maybe too confidently, the position that the enormous trading volume we actually see in financial markets is mostly noise, churn, overconfidence, and zero-sum transfers dressed up as price discovery, and that an ideally rational market would be a lot quieter and thinner than the ones we have. My editor thinks this is a cute seminar point I've turned into a personality. I want to stress-test it before I write anything.

So: what would count against this view? I'm asking for a map of its exposure, not a defense. Which observations or formal results would genuinely threaten it -- e.g., if it turned out that most volume is driven by liquidity needs and portfolio rebalancing rather than disagreement, does that actually vindicate me or does it rescue markets from the theorems in a way that undercuts my "it's mostly noise" gloss? What does the position predict that friendlier views of speculation (markets as Hayekian information aggregators, noise traders as the lubricant that makes information revelation possible) don't, and vice versa? I'd like the candidate defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd be, from "would force me to abandon the view" down to "awkward but survivable," and I'd like you to note which ones defenders of the no-trade spirit themselves would concede as real threats versus which ones only critics press. Also: have people like me quietly qualified the position into unfalsifiability -- every burst of volume gets explained as irrationality, every quiet stretch as the theory working -- and if so, is that actually a problem for a theorem-based view, which arguably never promised to fit the data in the first place? I personally find the persistent-excess-volume observation the most persuasive objection, so treat that one with special care, but don't let it crowd out defeaters I haven't thought of.
