I was recently appointed to the ethics committee of a regional health authority that writes allocation and triage policy -- organ distribution, ventilator protocols, that sort of thing. In preparing for this I read Scanlon's contractualism, which several committee members cite approvingly, and I drafted an argument to test my understanding of it. The argument now seems to me to prove that contractualism, on its most natural reading, rules out nearly everything my committee does. Since I take that to be a reductio, something must be wrong with it, but I cannot locate the error. Here it is.

(1) On the contractualist picture, an act is wrong just in case it would be forbidden by principles that no one, suitably motivated, could reasonably reject; and whether someone can reasonably reject a principle is assessed one person at a time, by comparing that individual's complaint against the complaints of other individuals. Complaints are not summed across persons -- this is the individualist restriction, and it is what the view uses to explain why one person's serious harm is not outweighed by any number of trivial harms to others, as in Scanlon's own case of the worker trapped in the transmitter room while millions await a broadcast.

(2) Consider the standard forced rescue: I can save one stranger from drowning, or five other strangers, but not all six. The one has a complaint -- death -- that is exactly as serious as the complaint of each of the five, taken individually. No one on either side has a stronger complaint than anyone on the other.

(3) Given the individualist restriction, there is therefore no contractualist justification for saving the five rather than the one; at most a coin flip is permitted.

(4) Now vary the harms. Suppose the choice is between preventing one death and preventing five cases of permanent paralysis -- or five thousand, or five million. Each paralyzed person's complaint is strictly weaker than the dying person's complaint, and aggregation is barred, so the principle that saves the greater number of paralyzed people is reasonably rejectable by the one, however large the number grows.

(5) Finally, generalize beyond rescue. Nearly every policy my committee considers -- indeed nearly every public policy whatsoever -- imposes on some identifiable class of persons a burden larger than the benefit received by any single beneficiary: siting a hospital, reallocating organs by medical urgency rather than waiting time, running ambulances at speed through residential streets, funding any of this through taxation. In each case some individual bears a cost that, assessed pairwise, exceeds what any one other person gains, and can therefore reasonably reject the authorizing principle.

(6) So contractualism forbids saving the greater number when complaints are equal, forbids saving the many from lesser harms in every case, and forbids virtually all cost-imposing public policy. That is absurd -- the view proves too much.

(7) The individualist restriction must therefore be abandoned and complaints pooled. But then contractualism has become a species of aggregation of just the kind it was constructed to avoid.

I would strongly prefer to keep the transmitter-room verdict -- I think it is the view's deepest insight -- and I would also prefer to keep (2), since the pairwise description of the rescue case seems like a neutral statement of fact. I am willing to give up a great deal else. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the fix is one of the standard moves -- tie-breaking among equal complaints, assessing policies ex ante rather than from the standpoint of identified losers, or admitting some restricted role for numbers -- please explain precisely which step it repairs, why the original step seemed innocent, and what the repair forces one to concede elsewhere.
