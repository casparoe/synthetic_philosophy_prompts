Historians and archaeologists disagree about whether "collapse" names a genuine kind of event or merely a dramatic relabeling of ordinary change, and I want a designed test case for a philosophy-of-history seminar that isolates what is actually at stake. Consider two rival accounts. On the rapid-complexity-loss account, a society collapses when it undergoes, within roughly a generation, a substantial simplification of its political, economic, and informational organization: fewer levels of hierarchy, less occupational specialization, less large-scale coordination and record-keeping, regardless of what happens to its population or culture. On the continuity account, a society collapses only when the relevant collective — a people bound by shared language, practices, and self-identification — ceases to persist, so that the abandonment or transformation of its institutions counts as collapse only if the collective itself dissolves or scatters.

Construct a pair of fictional societies, each described in no more than a paragraph, on which the two accounts deliver opposing verdicts twice over: one society that the rapid-complexity-loss account classifies as collapsed and the continuity account classifies as not collapsed, and one society on which the classifications are reversed. Constraints: neither society may be a thinly veiled copy of a real case (no Classic Maya city-states, no Rapa Nui, no Western Roman Empire, no Bronze Age palace system); both must be plausible pre-industrial agrarian societies with realistic demography and technology; and no supernatural or science-fictional devices. The verdicts must be generated by the descriptions meeting the accounts' criteria, not stipulated in the descriptions themselves.

After the two descriptions, demonstrate that the construction works: for each society, state the verdict each account delivers and identify which features of the description force that verdict. Then isolate the single variable — the fate of the collective as opposed to the fate of its institutions — on which the whole exercise turns, and assess whether the pair of cases shows that this variable is the real source of ordinary scholarly disputes about collapse, or merely restates the disagreement without advancing it. Conclude by explaining how a defender of each account could resist the classification unfavorable to them: what legitimate redescription or supplementary criterion would allow each side to classify both societies as collapses, or both as non-collapses, without simply begging the question against the rival account?
