In my seminar on the ethics of historical judgment, a classmate defended keeping Columbus's statues up by saying he was simply a man of his time, and the phrase ended the discussion without anyone being able to say what exactly it claimed, only that it sounded exculpatory. I have since heard the same line deployed for Jefferson's slaveholding and Churchill's views on empire. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that "a man of his time" might be expressing — that his beliefs were statistically typical of his milieu, that he could not reasonably have been expected to know better, that moral norms bind only relative to one's era, that judging the dead by our standards is unfair or pointless, or something else — and assess each as trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false? I would also like to know which readings actually do exculpatory work and which are merely descriptive, and which would have to hold for the slogan to settle the question my classmate used it to settle. One complication I want addressed directly: in each of these cases there were contemporaries who saw clearly what was wrong — abolitionists, critics of conquest and empire — so please say which readings of the slogan survive that fact and which collapse in the face of it.
