I'm a software engineer at a mid-sized AI company, and I help organize an internal speaker series where outside academics come talk to our engineering staff. Next month's guest is a philosopher who defends the view that large language model outputs are best classified as bullshit in Harry Frankfurt's technical sense from his essay On Bullshit -- produced with indifference to whether they're true -- rather than as lies or as honest errors. I have no formal philosophy training, but I've read the Frankfurt essay carefully, and I work on these systems daily, so I want the Q&A to go past the level of "the machine doesn't care about truth" that our guest presumably rehearses in every talk.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for me. For each question, give me: the question itself as I'd ask it aloud, the reply you'd expect from someone defending this view, and a follow-up keyed to that expected reply. Order the questions so pressure builds over the conversation rather than front-loading the hardest material, and end with a question about what evidence could count against the view. No generic warm-up questions. I'd like the set to cover the following ground -- feel free to merge, split, or reorder these as the sequencing demands:

1. The intentionality problem. Frankfurt's bullshitter is defined by an attitude -- indifference to truth. An LLM arguably has no attitudes at all, so is calling its outputs bullshit anything more than a metaphor? The expected reply is some functional characterization: indifference attributed to the generative process rather than to a mind. The follow-up should press on Frankfurt's Fourth of July orator case, where the bullshitter seems defined not merely by failing to track truth but by knowing the truth-constrained game and declining to play it -- a system that never could play that game seems different in kind from one that opts out.

2. Bullshit versus honest error. Engineers call these failures hallucinations, which frames them as errors: the system aims at correct output and misses, like an unreliable clock, and we don't call clocks bullshitters. The expected reply is that the training objective rewards plausible-sounding text rather than accuracy, so the indifference is baked into the design. The follow-up should ask where the agency then lives -- if the indifference sits in the lab's design choices rather than the artifact, is the model the bullshitter, or is the company, and does calling the outputs bullshit mislocate the blame?

3. The confabulation comparison. Patients with certain brain injuries confabulate -- producing elaborate false claims without intent to deceive -- and the clinical literature generally treats this as honest error from a broken self-monitoring mechanism, not bullshit. Why is the LLM case different? The follow-up should raise mechanistic interpretability: suppose probes show a model internally represents something like confidence and its output flatly contradicts that representation. Does the case then upgrade from bullshit toward lying, or does lying require further ingredients -- an assertion, a self -- that no probe result could supply?

4. Scope of the soft reading. I've seen a distinction drawn between hard bullshit, which requires an intent to deceive the audience about the speaker's agenda, and soft bullshit, which requires only indifference to truth. On the soft reading the category looks very broad: autocomplete, weather forecasters, horoscope writers. I want a question that asks where the line falls, with a follow-up for each branch -- if the guest bites the bullet and says autocomplete bullshits too, what normative work is left for the term, and if he narrows the category, what feature do LLMs have that the other cases lack?

5. Whether the outputs are assertions at all. Lying, and arguably bullshit, are speech-act categories. If the model never asserts, maybe its outputs can't be bullshit either. The expected reply is that users take the outputs as assertions, so they function as such. The follow-up should push the consequence: if the status depends on audience uptake, then the same generated text might be bullshit inside a chatbot interface but not inside a clearly labeled random-text demo -- which suggests the bullshit happens at the interface, not in the model.

6. Calibration and abstention. Current models are fine-tuned to hedge, decline, and sometimes say they don't know. Frankfurt's bullshitter is marked by indifference; a system trained to track its own reliability looks like it's acquiring something on the concern-for-truth side of the ledger. Is there a threshold of calibration or abstention behavior at which the charge lapses, or does any residual confident error keep the whole output stream in the bullshit category?

7. Audience complicity. Frankfurt's cases involve an audience whose impression the speaker manages. But many real deployments involve users who half-suspect the output is fabricated and don't much care -- the person generating filler marketing copy is not a truth-expecting victim. Does bullshit require an audience that expects truth, or can it proceed with a complicit one?

8. Closing evidence question. Frankfurt suggests bullshit is a greater enemy of truth than lying because the liar at least tracks truth in order to conceal it. I want a final question asking what empirical result would change the guest's mind -- for instance, if near-future models become well-calibrated, reliably cite real sources, and abstain appropriately, does the view predict the category simply disappears, and if the category tracks capability trends that closely, in what sense is it a philosophical thesis rather than an engineering status report?

Two last requests: flag any question in your draft that rests on a misreading of Frankfurt rather than a genuine pressure point, and note anywhere I might be importing an engineering assumption (about training objectives, interpretability, or calibration) that a philosopher would want to contest before answering.
