I'm a novelist working on a book that jumps between the Late Bronze Age and a fictional near-future society unravelling, and my writing partner has basically told me the whole premise is incoherent. Her argument, as near as I can reconstruct it from the pub:

(1) A society has "collapsed" only if it has ceased to exist -- collapse has to be a genuine ending, otherwise the word is doing no work.
(2) For every textbook collapse -- the Maya, the Western Roman Empire, the Hittites, Easter Island -- the people went on living there or nearby, their descendants carried on speaking related languages and farming related land, and chunks of the culture survived in altered form.
(3) If the population and its way of life continue, even in drastically altered form, then nothing has ceased to exist; it has merely changed.
(4) So none of the textbook cases were collapses. They were transformations.
(5) But the argument doesn't rely on anything special about those cases, so it generalises: no society has ever collapsed, and "collapse" is a label historians stick on change they find dramatic.

I obviously can't accept (5), partly because it guts my novel and partly because, come on, *something* big happened in the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BC. But each premise looks reasonable when I stare at it. Which one do I attack, why does it look plausible when it's the weak link, and what would it cost to reject each of the others instead? I'm especially reluctant to give up (2), since the continuity she describes seems to be just a fact about the archaeological record. Bonus points if you can tell me whether the argument, if sound, would also prove that felled trees don't die but merely "change", because I want to know if it proves too much in general or just too much for me.
