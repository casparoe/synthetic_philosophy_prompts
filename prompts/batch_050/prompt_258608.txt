My working position is that the verisimilitude problem doesn't need to be solved, and I want to know what would count against it. Quick backstory in case it matters: Popper wanted a formal notion of "closer to the truth" so he could say science makes progress even though its best theories keep turning out false, then Miller and Tichý showed his account collapses (roughly: on his definition all false theories come out about equally far from the truth), and the repair attempts since then keep getting accused of being language-dependent or gerrymandered to deliver the verdicts we already wanted. My take is: fine, let the project die. We don't need a metric of truthlikeness. We can just assess theories on predictive accuracy, explanatory scope, unification, whatever, and talk of "progress toward the truth" is a loose summary of that, not a real quantity we're tracking. The realist can keep her miracles argument and drop the odometer.

So: what would have to be true for me to be wrong? I want the exposure map. Are there cases where science genuinely needs a truthlikeness judgment that predictive accuracy plus the usual virtues can't deliver? Does the intuitively rock-solid ordering (Newton closer to the truth than Aristotle, oxygen chemistry closer than phlogiston) actually force a formal notion, or can I explain those verdicts away as retrospective success-talk? One thing that bugs me: in nested statistical models where the truth is inside the family, closeness seems to become a real, well-defined quantity (KL divergence and friends). Is my position secretly betting that mature science is never in that nice situation, and if that's the bet, what would losing it look like? Also tell me what defenders of the deflationary view would themselves concede as damaging, not just what Popperians throw at it, and whether I've qualified the view into something unfalsifiable -- and if so whether that's a defect for a view that is basically a "we don't need this" claim.
