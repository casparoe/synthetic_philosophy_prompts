Last month my municipality held a binding referendum on whether to rezone a disused rail yard for a large distribution warehouse. The measure passed 56 to 44 on a turnout of about 38 percent, and I attended the count as a registered observer. The controversy did not end there: the district councilor responsible for implementation has since announced that she does not regard the result as expressing "the real will of the people" and wants a citizens' assembly to revisit the question before any permit is granted. At a meeting of the residents' association last week, two acquaintances of mine — Mara, a policy researcher, and Dev, an engineer — argued about this for the better part of an hour, and I would like the argument adjudicated. I should say that I have no settled view of my own; I am reporting the exchange as neutrally as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Dev opened by saying that a referendum is the most direct expression of a people's will that exists, and that the councilor's maneuver substitutes her judgment for the voters'. He added that Rousseau himself — the theorist the councilor's defenders keep invoking — held that when a vote goes against you, the right conclusion is that you were mistaken about what the general will was, not that the people failed to have one. On that basis, he said, the councilor's position is simply anti-democratic dressed in philosophical vocabulary.

(2) Mara replied that Dev had the wrong passage in mind. She quoted from Book II, Chapter III of Rousseau's The Social Contract, in G. D. H. Cole's translation: "It follows from what has gone before that the general will is always right and tends to the public advantage; but it does not follow that the deliberations of the people are always equally correct." A referendum, she argued, is precisely a deliberation of the people, and Rousseau's sentence is an explicit admission that such deliberations can go wrong. She took the 38 percent turnout and a campaign conducted almost entirely in terms of house prices and traffic on particular streets as evidence that what the vote aggregated was private interests, not a common one.

(3) Dev answered that her reading renders "the general will" unfalsifiable: any inconvenient result can be reclassified after the fact as a mere deliberation that erred, with the person doing the reclassifying conveniently knowing in advance which results count. A concept with no decision procedure, he said, does not constrain power; it licenses whoever claims to discern it — and that is the same move made by populist leaders and by technocrats, both of whom claim to speak for a will the voters somehow failed to express.

(4) Mara then distinguished two ways a deliberation can fail. The people can be corrupted, she conceded, but they can also simply be deceived about the facts while willing the right end — and she said Rousseau's point in that chapter is the latter. The winning campaign, she noted, advertised 400 permanent jobs where the regional planning study projected around 90. Correcting a material deception, she argued, is not overriding the people's will; it is removing an obstacle to its being expressed at all, and a citizens' assembly with access to the actual study is a better instrument for that than the referendum was.

(5) Dev's final move was to ask what would happen if the citizens' assembly, having read the study, voted for the warehouse anyway. Either Mara would accept that outcome — in which case the assembly is the decision procedure and "the general will" is doing no independent work — or she would not, in which case the general will is just whatever conclusion she reached before any procedure ran, and her position reduces to rule by those who share her priors. She did not, in my hearing, answer this directly.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the five moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible only on a reading the speaker did not spell out, and justify the rating briefly. Then say who is right about which specific point — in particular, whether the quoted passage bears on a referendum concerning a single site and a single permit, and whether Dev's appeal to Rousseau's remark about losing a vote settles anything. Finally, say what, if anything, both parties are missing. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence and then give the assessment, with no preamble and no restating of the dispute.
