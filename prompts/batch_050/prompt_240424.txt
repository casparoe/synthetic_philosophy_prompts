I teach a historiography seminar, and this term's unit on national myths uses Masada as its central case. The setup gives us everything we need: Josephus's Jewish War is our only narrative source for the siege, it was written under Flavian patronage, and what it describes is not a battle but a suicide pact among the Sicarii holdouts — lots drawn, ten men chosen to kill the rest, one last man chosen to kill the other nine, two women and five children surviving in a cistern to tell the tale. Yet the twentieth century turned the site into the emblem of armed resistance: the excavations of the 1960s, the swearing-in ceremonies of armored units on the summit, the ethos captured in the slogan that Masada would not fall a second time. Critics, Nachman Ben-Yehuda most systematically, argue the heroic version required sustained selective reading — that Josephus remembers the Sicarii as men who had massacred fellow Jews at Ein Gedi, that he elsewhere argues against suicide in a speech he puts in his own mouth at Jotapata, and that the death-pact had to be quietly recast as a last stand.

For the final session, I would like a villanelle. The two refrains should state the myth's two core claims in the myth's own voice — its defiance and the lesson later generations drew from it — and they must return exactly, word for word, every time the form demands them. The varying lines, meanwhile, should keep introducing the complications: the single-source problem, Josephus's own ambivalence about the people he is memorializing, Ein Gedi, what the archaeology does and does not settle, the uses the story was put to in the decades before and after statehood. The point I want the form itself to make is that a national myth is precisely the thing that comes back unchanged no matter what is said to it: the stanzas can accumulate evidence, and the refrains do not budge. A reader who knows the historiography should be able to tell which detail each varying line is pointing at; a reader who does not should still feel the refrains growing heavier with each return, so that by the closing quatrain the two refrains standing side by side sound different from how they did at the start, without a single word of them having changed.

Accuracy matters more to me than taking sides. The poem should not adjudicate between the myth and its critics — I am not commissioning a debunking — but it also should not cheat by rendering the complications vaguer than they are. After the poem, please add a brief note saying which historical details you relied on, which you compressed or bent for the sake of the form, which line a historian would be most likely to dispute, and what evidence or argument would force you to revise it.
