I am preparing the Q&A segment of a philosophy podcast whose guest is the author of a new book defending what she calls plain expressivism: moral sentences express states of approval and disapproval rather than describe moral facts, and that, she insists, is the whole story. In an earlier interview she dismissed the Frege–Geach problem in a single sentence: moral reasoning works because states of mind can clash just as propositions can, so validity never required propositions in the first place. Draft a sequence of five or six questions that makes this dismissal progressively more expensive to repeat. The sequence should begin by pressing her on the negation case — the difference between disapproving of not-lying and merely failing to disapprove of lying — then move to embedding in conditionals, where a claim such as "if insulting one's colleagues is wrong, getting an intern to insult them is wrong" seems assertible by someone who disapproves of nothing at all; it should then force her to say whether her account of validity equivocates on the moral sentence between its unembedded and embedded occurrences. Include at least one question that anticipates her inconsistency-of-attitudes reply and supplies the follow-up asking what the inconsistency consists in when no proposition is endorsed. Close the sequence with a question about what theoretical or experimental result, if any, she would count as refuting plain expressivism. For each question, note briefly the reply she is most likely to give and why the follow-up blocks it.
