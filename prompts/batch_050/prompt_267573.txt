In Martin Shubik's dollar auction, a dollar bill is auctioned under the rule that the runner-up must also pay his bid; the predictable result is that two bidders escalate past a dollar, each rationally bidding a little more to avoid crystallizing a loss, until both have paid far more than the prize is worth. Read Christopher Nolan's The Prestige as a dramatization of this game. Map Angier and Borden's escalating rivalry onto the auction's structure: what counts as a bid (sabotage, injury, the sacrificed marriage, the double life, the nightly drowning), and at what point has each man unambiguously paid more than the prestige he is bidding for could be worth? Then press on where the film complicates the game-theoretic reading. The dollar auction assumes bidders who could walk away and simply don't; the film gives its magicians obsession, grief, and revenge as motives -- do these passions undermine the rational-entrapment analysis, or do they function as commitment devices that make the escalation even more inescapable? Consider too how the film's two revelations scramble the accounting: Borden's costs are divided between two people sharing one life, while Angier pays with himself in the most literal sense available, so in neither case is there a single bidder cleanly weighing bid against prize. Close by saying what, if anything, the film adds to the theory of escalation of commitment that the formal model cannot: for instance, whether a prize that is partly constitutive of one's identity can ever be assigned a value against which bidding looks irrational. I am preparing a seminar session on the rationality of escalation and want a reading faithful to both the film and the game theory, not a plot summary in game-theoretic vocabulary.
