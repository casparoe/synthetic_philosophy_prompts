**Exam question**

Moore’s paradox concerns sentences such as:

(O) It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining.

(C) It is raining, but I believe that it is not raining.

Explain why these sentences can describe genuinely possible situations while still being deeply defective for the person concerned to assert or believe. Your answer should distinguish semantic inconsistency from pragmatic or doxastic defect, compare the omissive form (O) with the commissive form (C), and explain why shifting to a third-person or past-tense formulation changes the result.

**Student’s answer**

(O) can be true: it may be raining even though I do not believe that it is. Thus, (O) is not a contradiction in the ordinary semantic sense. Its problem arises only once someone asserts it. The first conjunct presents the speaker as believing that it is raining, whereas the second explicitly denies that the speaker has that belief. A fully rational speaker therefore cannot believe (O), since believing the first conjunct would give the speaker the very belief that the second conjunct says is absent.

(C) is the commissive version: it combines p with the speaker’s belief that not-p. It has the same basic structure as (O), except that instead of reporting the absence of a belief, it reports a belief whose content conflicts with p. Unlike (O), (C) can be true only if the speaker’s belief is false.

The third-person sentence “It is raining, but Alex does not believe that it is raining” is not paradoxical, because Alex’s mental state need not match the speaker’s. Similarly, “It was raining yesterday, but I did not believe that it was raining yesterday” is entirely coherent, since I can now know what I failed to believe then.
