I'm a PhD student in economics working between the history of economic thought and computational economics, and I run a reading group on the socialist calculation debate. Two members keep talking past each other: one is a committed Austrian in the Mises-Hayek tradition who regards the impossibility of rational economic calculation under socialism as essentially settled, and the other is a modern market socialist, with some sympathy for recent proposals for algorithmic or digital planning, who thinks Lange's trial-and-error response was basically adequate and that whatever force the original challenge once had has been eroded by modern computing, large-scale optimization, and the internal logistics of firms like Amazon and Walmart, which already plan resource allocation at the scale of a small national economy. Their discussions generate heat but no record, so I'd like to give them a document to react to.

Please draft the joint statement these two could plausibly sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. It should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both can accept — I suspect these include the distinction between Mises's original calculation argument (without market prices for the means of production there is no common denominator for comparing the costs of alternative production plans) and Hayek's later knowledge argument (much economically relevant knowledge is local, tacit, and cannot be centralized even in principle), the observation that Lange's scheme answers the first argument more directly than the second, and the point that solving a static general-equilibrium system and running an actual economy are different tasks, since the former says little about discovery, incentives, or entrepreneurship; (2) a table of the remaining disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording of its own position — candidates include whether tacit knowledge can increasingly be elicited, inferred, or simply bypassed by sensor data and machine learning, whether the binding constraint on planning is epistemic or instead a matter of incentives and political economy (soft budget constraints, who chooses the objective function), and whether the existence of large planned economies inside firms shows that planning scales or shows precisely the opposite, since firms operate within a surrounding price system, use internal shadow prices derived from it, and can fail and be dissolved; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, a concrete empirical case, historical episode, or thought experiment both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance (for instance, what each would predict about the limits of algorithmic pricing and assortment decisions in large retailers, or about the performance of wartime planning episodes relative to peacetime markets).

One more requirement: both members have conceded in their better moments that part of their dispute may be verbal — over what "calculation" and "knowledge" mean, or over whether the debate concerns feasibility in principle or comparative institutional performance. End the document with a short section identifying which parts of the disagreement, if any, dissolve under careful definition and which survive, with reasoning. Format: the numbered agreed-claims section first, then the disagreements table, then cruxes with predictions, then the verbal-dispute section. Aim for 1,500–2,000 words, and keep the tone like an actual joint statement rather than a debate transcript.
