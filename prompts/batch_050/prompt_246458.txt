I am putting together the syllabus for a graduate seminar on the ethics of war, and for the unit on the moral equality of combatants I want to screen Das Boot rather than assign a third journal article. The standard framing in the literature has the traditional view holding that soldiers on both sides of a war, just or unjust, are moral equals -- neither the German submariner nor the British sailor fighting him is to be blamed for fighting, and each is a legitimate target of the other -- while revisionists deny this, arguing that a combatant who fights for an unjust cause lacks justification for the killing he does and is therefore not the moral equal of his adversary. The 1981 film seems to me to lean heavily on the traditional picture: it spends two and a half hours making the crew of a German U-boat comprehensible, frightened, professionally skilled, and mostly indifferent or hostile to the regime they serve, while the British remain faceless shapes in convoys and depth charges overhead. But I keep going back and forth on whether the film is actually committed to moral equality, or whether it is making a different point -- that soldiers are instruments of their states, or that shared mortal peril generates a solidarity that overrides the justice of causes -- and whether the captain's open contempt for the Nazi leadership is doing philosophical work the revisionist would approve of.

For a two-hour post-screening discussion, I would like a reading of the film through this debate. Which scenes most strongly dramatize the traditional thesis, and does the film ever undercut it -- for instance, by suggesting the crew's innocence lies in coercion or ignorance rather than in the permissibility of fighting as such? Where would a revisionist object to the reading, and is there anything in the film that resists the revisionist objection -- some way the film insists on the symmetry between the men in the boat and the men dropping the charges? Please be faithful in both directions: I do not want the film flattened into an illustration of Walzer, nor do I want the revisionist position distorted to make the film's sympathy legible. If the honest conclusion is that the film is philosophically muddled or deliberately ambivalent on the question, say so and locate the muddle in specific sequences. Finally, tell me what, if anything, the film contributes to the debate that the argumentative literature does not -- what the seminar would lose if we dropped the screening and just read the papers. Keep the whole response under 800 words.
