I'm a novelist, about twenty chapters into a book where one of the main characters is a lapsed Hume scholar who spends the second act making a case to his sister that he should be allowed to end his life. I built his argument out of Hume's essay Of Suicide, and the problem is it works too well: I can't find the flaw, and if I can't, neither can the sister, and act three collapses. Here it is:

(1) A suicide does no wrong to society when the person's continued life contributes nothing, or when they're a net burden on others; stepping out of life can even do others a small good.
(2) It violates no duty to God or the order of nature, since nothing in the universe is disturbed by it, and a human life matters no more to the cosmos than an oyster's does.
(3) It violates no duty to oneself when life holds more misery than happiness with no realistic prospect of reversal, since nobody owes it to themselves to keep hold of a thing that only harms them.
(4) Whether the condition in (3) actually holds is something only the person living that life is in a position to judge, so their sincere, settled judgment settles it.
(5) So whenever someone sincerely and stably judges their life a net burden with no realistic prospect of reversal, their suicide is permissible.
(6) Forcibly preventing a permissible act — locked wards, holds, confiscating the means — is unjust.
(7) So society may never justly interfere with a competent adult's sincerely chosen suicide, whatever the source of the misery, including treatable depression.

The sister's counter in my draft is basically that (7) proves the argument broken, because most people who survive attempts are glad they survived, and depression distorts exactly the judgment (4) defers to. But my character replies that paternalism always talks like that, and I genuinly can't tell who gets the better of it. Which premise should the sister attack, and what does attacking it cost her? I'd rather keep (1)–(3) if possible, since I want suicide to come out permissible in some cases — but tell me if that's too cheap a win.

Could you answer in four sections, roughly in this order: The step that breaks (one short paragraph naming it), Why it looks airtight (a paragraph or two), What each fix costs (a few sentences each on dropping (4), dropping (6), and any alternative you like better), and How it lands for the novel (a short bit on which repair gives me the most honest ending).
