I'm a retired organic chemist (forty years in catalysis, now mostly reading and annoying my wife) and a few months ago I joined a philosophy reading group at the local library. We recently got onto Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument, and two members have been at each other's throats about it ever since. One of them holds what I'd call the hard-line view: physics is causally closed, every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause, so unless a mental property just IS a physical property, it can't cause anything -- my back pain doesn't cause my wincing, the firing of some neurons does. The other accepts causal closure but insists mental properties are genuine causes anyway, on grounds I mostly associate with talk of "difference-making" and the idea that the right level of causal description is the one that's proportional to the effect -- she keeps saying exclusion would prove too much. I've suggested they stop arguing and instead do one of those "adversarial collaboration" writeups, and they told me to draft it. So: please draft the joint statement these two could actually sign. I'd like a numbered list of claims both sides accept (about closure, about what would count as problematic overdetermination, about what the exclusion argument does and doesn't assume), then the residual disagreements each phrased so that the person holding that view would accept the wording of their own position -- a table is fine -- then the cruxes, and for each crux a case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction about it written down in advance. If some piece of their dispute turns out to be merely verbal, say which piece and why.

One thing I keep bringing up, and I'd like it built into the document rather than treated as my eccentric aside: in my old line of work we'd happily say that the temperature of a reactor caused the runaway reaction, or that the pressure burst the vessel, even though at bottom it's all molecules bumping into things. If Kim's argument goes through, does it also show that temperature and pressure are causally inert, excluded by molecular-level goings-on? The hard-liner in our group bites that bullet (or says reduction saves it, since temperature just is mean kinetic energy); the difference-making person says this is exactly the reductio of the argument. Have both of them commit, in the joint statement, to what their view implies for the special-science case -- chemistry, biology -- and treat "does the argument generalize to all higher-level properties?" as a candidate crux to be ruled in or out.

Two requests about how to write this. First, don't just split the difference or paper things over -- if the two positions turn out to share so little common ground that a genuine joint statement can't be written, tell me that plainly instead of faking a consensus document. Second, if my chemistry analogy is confused or rests on a misunderstanding of what the exlusion principle actually says, push back on it directly rather than politely working around it. I'm used to being wrong in front of colleagues; I'd rather be told than humored.
