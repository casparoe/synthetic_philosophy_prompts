I retired four years ago after a career in experimental physics, and I have lately been spending my mornings with philosophy books, which I find both stimulating and frustrating in roughly equal measure. The occasion for my question is a practical decision I now face. I have been diagnosed with a condition that, left to its ordinary course, gives me perhaps two years. There is a treatment available that my oncologist estimates would extend this to perhaps five, but it is arduous: months of hospital visits, considerable nausea and fatigue, and long stretches in which I would be too unwell to read, walk, or see my grandchildren. The alternative is to decline treatment and arrange matters so that my remaining time is comfortable and my own.

When I discussed this with my brother-in-law, a man I respect, he made an argument that has stayed with me and that I cannot quite answer. Whatever happens, he said, there will come a time when I am dead; but once I am dead, there is no one left to whom anything bad is happening, since being dead is not an experience I will undergo or suffer through. Death, on his reasoning, can never be bad for the person who dies, because badness requires someone there to receive it, and the dead receive nothing. Therefore, he concluded, the extra three years cannot count as a gain to me in any sense that should weigh against three years of misery, and I should decline the treatment with a clear conscience. I find his reasoning disturbingly hard to fault step by step, and yet I cannot bring myself to accept the conclusion. It seems to me that if I decline, I am deprived of something real: mornings, conversations, the sight of my granddaughter growing up. A thing can be taken from me, surely, even if I will not be around afterward to miss it.

So here is what I would like. Tell me, first, whether my brother-in-law's argument is sound or where precisely it goes wrong; and second, give me an actual recommendation about the treatment, taking a definite position rather than surveying the possibilities and leaving me to choose. I am not asking you to weigh my medical probabilities for me — I have stated them as best I can — but to tell me how the philosophical question bears on the practical one, and to commit to an answer. If you believe the deprivation I would suffer by declining is a genuine harm that can justify real present misery, say so and say why. If you believe my brother-in-law is right and my reluctance is a confusion, say that instead, and explain why the feeling of impending loss is misleading. I would rather be argued out of my instinct than be left with a tasteful arrangement of both views.
