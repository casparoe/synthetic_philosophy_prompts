A friend ran this one on me at the pub and it's been bugging me ever since. (1) If two things you do produce the same belief in your listener, with the same intention and the same downstream consequences, they're morally on a par. (2) A straight-up lie and a carefully crafted true-but-misleading sentence can be built to produce exactly the same false belief, with the same intention and the same consequences -- compare "I didn't take the last biscuit" with "someone must have moved the plate," said by the biscuit thief. (3) So lying is morally no worse than misleading. (4) But the lying/misleading line is just the general distinction between what you outright say and what you merely get the hearer to infer, applied to deception. (5) So if the manner of producing a belief made no moral difference there, it makes no difference generally: inducing the same false belief by doctoring a photo, planting a fake diary entry, or (in principle) slipping someone a belief-causing pill is on a par with just saying it. (6) That's obviously absurd, so the lying/misleading distinction must be morally empty. Now I can't swallow (6) -- pretty much everyone feels that being lied to is a special kind of violation, and the whole casuist tradition of equivocation and mental reservation only makes sense if there's a real difference. But each premise looks fine to me on its own, and (5) in particular feels like the same move as (3), just zoomed out. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I have to pick, I'd rather give up (1) than admit the forged diary is morally just like telling her, but I want to know what I'm paying for that.
