So I got this properly weird email last week and I can't work out what the sensible move is. Bit of background: I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized company, and among other things I own our backup setup. If it ever fails in anger, it's probably my neck.

The email was from one of those prediction services — I'll skip the name, but they've got a public track record where they post hashes of predictions months ahead and reveal them later, and as far as anyone can tell they've never been wrong. Hundreds of predictions, people have genuinely tried to catch them out. So when they say "our model predicts X" it's not nothing.

Anyway, the email says, roughly: our model has predicted whether your backup system has a hidden flaw that will cause a total, unrecoverable failure within the next 12 months. We want £2,000. And here's the bit to read carefully: we only send this email when the model has predicted one of two specific combinations — either (a) your backups will fail AND you won't pay, or (b) your backups are fine AND you will pay. If the model predicted any other pairing, you simply never heard from us.

So assuming their model holds up, sitting here with this email in my inbox, one of those two is true. Either the backups are doomed and I don't pay, or they're fine and I do.

My flatmate (an economist, annoyingly) reckons it's obvious I shouldn't pay: whether the backups fail is already fixed, it's a fact about my code and hardware as they stand right now, and wiring someone two grand can't retroactively change any of that. Paying is just buying a comforting story.

But here's what's bugging me. Say I refuse. Then the only way I could've recieved this email at all is if the model predicted failure — so refusing is basically equivalent to finding out I'm sat on a ticking timebomb. Whereas if I pay, the email means I'm fine. It's as if the money buys the good outcome, even though I know perfectly well it causes nothing. My flatmate says that's exactly the confusion — you can't purchase a healthy backup system with a bank transfer, the pattern is just the predictor being clever about who it writes to. Fair enough. But I can't help noticing that everyone who takes his advice ends up in the branch where the house burns down.

(And yes, before anyone suggests it, I did think of the cheeky third option: don't pay, treat the email itself as a tip-off that the backups are broken, and go fix them. But the model predicts my behaviour too, so presumably if I were the type to pull that move it would've been factored in, and then... honestly my head starts spinning round about there.)

Do I pay the £2k or not? And more than just the verdict — whose reasoning is actually going wrong here, mine or my flatmate's, and at exactly which step? I keep going back and forth and I'd love to see the whole thing laid out properly.
