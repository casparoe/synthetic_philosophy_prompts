I am a PhD student working on early modern theories of mind, and I keep running into the same characterization of Descartes on animals, most recently in a survey chapter of an anthology on animal ethics that a colleague recommended to me:

"Descartes held that animals are automata: intricate machines with no minds whatsoever. Because he identified the mind with an immaterial thinking soul, and denied such souls to animals, he concluded that animals feel nothing at all — a dog's cries when injured are no more an expression of pain than the chimes of a struck clock are an expression of distress. His chief argument was that animals cannot use language, which he took as conclusive proof that they lack mentality. From this it follows that our treatment of animals is a matter of moral indifference, and the Cartesians who performed vivisections in the following decades were merely drawing out the consistent practical consequences of his position."

Something about this strikes me as too tidy. I have read Part V of the Discourse on Method and some of the correspondence with More, and my recollection is that the language argument is aimed at rational thought rather than sensation, and that commentators disagree sharply over whether Descartes denied animals feeling or only denied them reason. But the author is a respected historian, and I may be overcorrecting on the basis of revisionist secondary literature.

Please audit this summary as exactly five numbered items, with no introduction or conclusion, each item one or two sentences: first, what in it is historically accurate; second, what is a standard simplification that a defender of the traditional reading could still stand behind; third, which specific claim goes beyond what Descartes actually committed himself to, and what more careful position his texts support; fourth, where the genuine scholarly fault line lies (i.e., which question the summary settles by fiat that specialists treat as open); fifth, whether the closing inference — moral indifference as Descartes' own consequence — survives once the earlier corrections are made, or whether it rests on the very conflation the corrections expose.
