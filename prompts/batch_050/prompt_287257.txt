I'm a second-year PhD student in linguistics and I'm TAing a grad semantics seminar this term, and we got into a huge argument after class about presupposition accommodation that I keep turning over in my head. I have pretty firm gut verdicts on a bunch of cases but I can't tell whether there's one principle behind them or whether I'm just being inconsistent. Here they are. (1) I say to a colleague who's never met my family, "I can't stay late, I have to pick up my sister from the airport" — totally fine, they just quietly add that I have a sister. (2) Same colleague, same setup: "I can't stay late, the King of France is visiting my department" — totally broken, no accommodation, just confusion. (3) A job interviewer asks me, "So, have you stopped missing deadlines?" — I object, even though they could in principle just accommodate that I used to miss deadlines; there's something aggressive about it that case (1) lacks, and I can't articulate what. (4) "Even my thesis advisor failed the qual exam" said to a new student — fine, and oddly it works even if the student had no idea my advisor took the qual; it's like the surprise is doing work. (5) "John didn't take the exam because he dropped the course" — here I feel the presupposition that there was an exam scheduled for him just evaporates, no problem, no accommodation needed, which seems to clash with how I've been thinking about (3) where the presupposition seems to stick. (6) "I need to stop by the store to get food for my cat" — fine. (7) "I need to stop by the store to get food for my pet wallaby" — noticeably weirder than (6), even though structurally identical, and my officemate insists it's exactly as fine as (6) and I'm wrong.

So what I want to know: is there a principle (or a small family of principles, like a Lewis-style rule of accommodation plus some constraint) that captures these verdicts, or close to all of them? I'm especially stuck on what distinguishes (1)/(6) from (3) and (7), since in each case the addressee learns something new about me and just has to go along with it — it can't just be "new information can't be presupposed," because (1) is new information too. My hunch is something about the accommodated content needing to be uncontroversial or unsurprising or "quiet," but that feels vague to the point of unfalsifiable, and I'm not sure it handles (5), where the presupposition doesn't get accommodated at all and that's fine. Bonus question: what should a good principle predict for a case I didn't list — say, a doctor opening with "Your test results came back positive for the condition we discussed," when in fact we never discussed any condition? Is that smooth accommodation, malpractice, or both? And be honest with me about which of my verdicts the best principle forces me to give up — I suspect (4) or (7) is the odd one out but I can't decide which.
