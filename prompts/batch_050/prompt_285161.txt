So I'm a freelance journalist, mostly covering science and ideas for a couple of general-interest mags, and I'm working up an essay I want to pitch about George Eliot's 1855 Westminster Review piece "Evangelical Teaching: Dr. Cumming" and what it reveals about her complicated post-Christian religiosity. Before I send it to my editor I want a serious round of comments, and here's how I'd like you to approach it: you're a scholar of Victorian literature and intellectual history who also happens to have a good ear for magazine prose. Think carefully about the interpretive questions before you start writing — maybe brainstorm the strongest objections to my reading first and then work through them — and when there are live disputes about how to read Eliot, take a side and defend it. I specifically don't want a balanced menu of possible positions; I want to know what you actually think is right and why, because I can generate the hedge-y version myself.

Here's the sketch of the essay (this is my own notes, not the finished thing, so don't grade the prose — though do tell me if the structure sags anywhere). Working title: "The Prophet of Crown Court." Roughly 1,800 words planned, not counting the block quote below, which is the hinge of the whole piece:

"Dr. Cumming’s mind is evidently not of the pietistic order. There is not the slightest leaning toward mysticism in his Christianity–no indication of religious raptures, of delight in God, of spiritual communion with the Father. He is most at home in the forensic view of Justification, and dwells on salvation as a scheme rather than as an experience."

My three moves:

1. I read this passage as Eliot attacking Cumming not for believing the wrong things but for feeling the wrong things — or rather for not feeling much at all. Doctrine, prophecy, polemics: all present. Devotion: absent. So her implicit test for genuine religion is affective and sympathetic rather than credal, and Cumming fails it on his own terms, as it were from the inside.

2. Bigger claim: this is why Eliot's humanism after her loss of faith (she'd translated Strauss and Feuerbach just before this) wasn't really secular in the modern sense. She kept the whole evangelical architecture — moral seriousness, the hunger for something worthy of devotion — and just swapped the object from God to suffering humanity. The Cumming essay only makes sense, I argue, if sympathy with human suffering is already functioning as something quasi-sacred for her, since that's the standard she's measuring him against.

3. Present-day hook: her diagnosis of the preacher type — prophecy-mongering as entertainment, straw opponents refuted from an unanswerable pulpit, polemic dressed up as piety — maps uncannily onto a certain kind of today's media commentator. I name no names.

What I need from you, in order of how much I care:

(a) Take a definite position on move 1. Is "scheme rather than experience" doing the work I say it is, or am I overreading one sentence? If I'm overreading, tell me plainly and say what the passage actually supports.

(b) Move 2 is where my editor will push back hardest. Is the "she relocated rather than abandoned religion" reading actually defensible, or does it flatten the real distance between Eliot and her evangelical past? Commit to a verdict, with reasons. If you think I've got it backwards — that the essay shows she was already fully outside religion judging it coldly — argue that instead, but argue *something*.

(c) Does move 3 cheapen the piece? Be honest. If you think the contemporary parallel works, tell me what the sharpest version of it is; if you think it's a gimmick that would embarass me, say so.

Also flag any factual or historical errors in the sketch — I've been known to be sloppy about dates and translation details. One more thing: don't just sand down the essay into something safer. If the interesting version is the risky one, I'd rather know that.
