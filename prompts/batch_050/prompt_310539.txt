I'm a hobbyist with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been chewing on Molyneux's problem -- the question Locke discussed about whether someone blind from birth, who knows a cube and a sphere only by touch, would immediately recognize which is which by sight alone if their vision were restored as an adult. Please act as a careful philosopher of perception, and take a moment to brainstorm before settling into your answer. I don't want the textbook summary; I want help thinking through what the question is actually asking.

Here's where I've gotten stuck on my own. It seems to me there are really several different questions hiding inside the famous one, and people talk past each other because they never say which they mean:

1. A narrow behavioral question: would the person point to the right object in a forced-choice test on day one?
2. A question about concepts: does the person have a single, shared concept of, say, "cube" that lives independently of any one sense, or two separate concepts that need to be linked?
3. A question about representation format: is what touch delivers convertible into whatever format vision uses, the way you might translate between two languages, or are the two formats simply incommensurable?

My own suspicion -- which you should feel free to dismantle -- is that the narrow behavioral question is almost a trap. Brand-new vision is presumably blurry, overwhelming, and lacking in depth; someone might fail the cube/sphere test for boring reasons (their visual system isn't online yet) that tell us nothing about concepts, or pass it via a cheap trick (counting corners by eye versus by hand) that also tells us nothing deep. So my guess is that the philosophically interesting version of the problem has to be formulated more carefully than "would they get it right immediately."

Please explain:

- Whether my three-way split is the right way to carve up the problem, or whether the standard treatment cuts it differently, and what I've missed.
- What the interesting version of the question becomes once we set aside the day-one recognition test. For instance: is the real issue about space (whether "the space I reach into" and "the space I see" are automatically experienced as one space) rather than about shapes at all?
- What kinds of evidence -- from restored-sight cases, from cross-modal skill transfer in ordinary people, or from thought experiments alone -- could in principle settle each of the sub-questions, and which sub-questions (if any) no experiment could ever settle.

One important request: please clearly separate what is broadly agreed among people who work on this from what is still genuinely contested, and flag whenever a position you describe is one live option among several rather than the consensus. If part of my framing rests on a contested assumption, say so explicitly rather than quietly adopting it.
