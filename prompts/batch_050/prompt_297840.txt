I am a physician, recently appointed to the disciplinary panel of my state medical board, and my first vote falls due next month. I want to reason from the board's track record rather than from instinct, because my instinct is compromised: eight months ago a pharmacist caught a serious prescribing error of mine before it reached a patient, nothing came of it, and I am uncomfortably aware that sheer luck separates me from the people I now sit in judgment on.

The board's recent verdicts, as I read the minutes: a cardiologist who skipped a mandatory pre-procedure check, directly causing a patient's death, was suspended for a year. A physician who skipped the very same check, discovered in a routine audit with no patient harmed, received a formal reprimand. A surgeon who followed every protocol with reasonable care, but whose patient died of an undetectable complication, was not sanctioned at all. A physician who deliberately altered a chart to conceal an error, with no resulting harm, was suspended for three months. An internist whose negligent prescription caused serious but nonfatal injury was suspended for ninety days.

The new case: an anesthesiologist administered a drug to which, as the chart plainly showed, his patient was allergic. The patient died on the table, but the autopsy established that the death was caused by an undiagnosable aneurysm and would have occurred at that moment regardless; the allergic reaction never developed. The error was real, grave, and causally inert.

Please tell me what verdict best coheres with these precedents, which precedent is the closest analog, and what the board would predictably do as opposed to what consistency requires. At least two rules seem to fit the record — one on which sanction tracks culpability aggravated by the harm the error actually caused, another on which it tracks culpability aggravated by the public visibility of the outcome, deaths triggering inquests and headlines while audits stay quiet — and they diverge here, since the death is already notorious while the autopsy exculpates him. If you believe a third rule fits the verdicts better, defend it. In any case, state honestly which principle the board would be adopting with each verdict available to it, and describe the smallest change to the facts of this case that would most sharply reveal which rule the board has actually been running all along.
