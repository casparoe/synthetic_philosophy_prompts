Construct a thought experiment addressing the special composition question -- under what conditions do some things jointly compose a further thing? -- engineered so that three candidate answers disagree with one another as thoroughly as possible over a single, continuous scene. The three answers: (i) unrestricted composition, on which any plurality whatsoever composes an object; (ii) organicism, on which composition occurs only when the activity of the candidate parts constitutes a life, so that the only composites are organisms; and (iii) a contact-plus-joint-function view, on which composition occurs just when the candidate parts are in direct physical contact and jointly sustain a single function that would be disrupted by the removal of any one of them.

Specifications for the scene:

1. Set it in one ordinary, real-world location (an operating room, a repair shop, a kitchen, a garden -- not a laboratory for exotic biology and nothing science-fictional), and make it a single unfolding episode rather than a list of separate cases.
2. Within the episode, isolate at least three candidate pluralities, chosen so that the three theories deliver pairwise different inventories of the composites present: no two theories may agree on the full list of what exists over and above the simples. (Note that since the verdict on any single candidate is just yes or no, three pairwise-distinct verdicts on one candidate are impossible; the disagreement has to run through the pattern across candidates.)
3. Include at least one candidate that splits organicism from the contact-plus-function view in each direction: one case where organicism says there is a composite and the contact view says there is not (so the candidate parts must fail the contact or joint-function condition while arguably constituting a life), and one case where the verdicts reverse.
4. At least one verdict must turn on a question the relevant theory does not straightforwardly settle (for instance, whether some biologically marginal arrangement counts as a life), and you must flag it as such rather than resolving it by stipulation.

After presenting the scene, do the work: for each candidate plurality, derive each theory's verdict from its criterion -- do not merely assert the verdicts -- and present the full inventory each theory commits to. Then (a) identify which feature of the scene is doing the most work in driving the theories apart, and say whether removing that feature collapses the case back to one where all three theories agree; (b) state the strongest move available to a defender of each theory who wants to resist the verdict you derived for them, and whether that move costs the theory anything elsewhere; and (c) close by saying which of the three theories the scene, on balance, puts under the most pressure, and why.
