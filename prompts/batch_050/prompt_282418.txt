Two friends of mine got into a long argument last weekend that I've been unable to settle, and I'd like it adjudicated properly. Some background: I have no academic training in biology or philosophy, but I read a lot of popular science, and I'd mentioned to them the familiar claim that DNA is a code or blueprint for the organism. This set them off.

The first friend, Marcus, a software developer, said DNA is a code in the strictest sense, not a metaphor. His argument: the mapping from three-letter codons to amino acids is not dictated by chemical necessity, since there is nothing about the chemistry of, say, the codon GGG that forces it to correspond to glycine rather than something else — the correspondence is implemented by adaptor molecules, and it actually differs slightly in a few organisms, which proves the assignment could have been otherwise. An arbitrary, changeable mapping between symbols and what they stand for is exactly what a code is. He added that biologists can store a digital photograph as DNA and read it back, which you could not do with a mere chemical.

The second friend, Lena, replied that a code requires conventions fixed by users who mean things by their symbols, and there are no users or meanings inside a cell — only chemistry following physical law. She said calling the mapping "arbitrary" just means it was settled by evolutionary accident rather than by chemistry, and accident is not convention. She also argued that the "information" in DNA is nothing more than reliable correlation: smoke carries information about fire in the same thin sense, and nobody calls smoke a message. If DNA is a code merely because you can compute with it or store files in it, she said, then the word has been watered down until the interesting claim — that genes contain instructions or meanings — gets smuggled in without argument.

Marcus then made what he called his strongest point: mutations are literally errors, and cells contain proofreading machinery that distinguishes correct from incorrect copies. Pure chemistry, he said, cannot be wrong about anything — a reaction just happens. The fact that we can even talk about copying mistakes shows there is a norm built into the system, and norms of correctness are the hallmark of a code. He also noted that the same stretch of DNA would produce nothing without the cell's reading machinery, which is exactly how codes work: the cipher is useless without the key.

Lena answered that the normativity comes from function, not from meaning. A damaged hammer is a bad hammer, but it is not a false statement; natural selection explains why some molecular states count as malfunctions without any semantics entering the picture. She added that if the cell's machinery is the "key," then the metaphor cuts against Marcus, because the same sequence is read differently depending on context — through splicing, chemical modification of the DNA, and so on — so the sequence by itself does not determine any message, the way a real text determines what it says regardless of who is holding it.

Neither would concede anything, and I stayed out of it. Please go through their exchange round by round and tell me who is right about each specific point: the arbitrariness of the codon mapping, whether information without a sender amounts to a code, whether mutations being "errors" requires the code framing, and whether context-dependent reading undermines it. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both of them are missing.
