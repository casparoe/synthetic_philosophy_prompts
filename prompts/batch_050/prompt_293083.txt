I'm drafting a decision-tree worksheet for our university's animal-research ethics committee. When a new protocol arrives, members currently just announce a verdict ("vertebrates only," "all animals get protections," "depends on the data") and talk past each other, so I want an instrument that sorts them by what they actually believe about where consciousness runs in the animal kingdom. The tree should start from one question and branch via concrete diagnostic questions, each phrased so a member can answer without knowing any theory of mind -- e.g., cases like an octopus that trades food to avoid an anesthetic, bees whose learned-association behavior is fully explained by a simple algorithm, a decapitated insect that still mates, or a colony that acts coordinated with no single locus of control. Each branch point must separate positions the committee actually encounters, not textbook categories. The leaves must include at minimum: (a) consciousness requires a cortex-like substrate; (b) any creature meeting a fixed list of functional markers counts, however implemented; (c) consciousness is graded and widespread, so protections should scale with estimated degree; (d) uncertainty itself warrants protection only above some evidential threshold; (e) no nonhuman animal is conscious in the morally relevant sense. Build the tree from the logical space first: state the questions, the answer combinations they permit, locate the five positions in that space, and explicitly mark any unoccupied combination with a one-line diagnosis of whether it's empty because it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of the five leaves the tree cannot reliably distinguish in practice, and say what extra question would be needed to split them. Keep the whole thing usable on a single printed page.
