A friend of mine who does a philosophy-adjacent podcast gave me this summary of David Pearce's hedonistic imperative, and before I recycle any of it for an essay in my ethics course I want to know how much of it to trust. His version goes: "The hedonistic imperative is Pearce's argument, from a mid-90s online manifesto, that suffering should be abolished using biotechnology rather than politics, therapy, or self-improvement. His reasoning is that the hedonic treadmill is hardwired by natural selection: evolution gave each of us a genetically fixed set point of mediocre wellbeing, so no change in circumstances or institutions can make anyone durably happier, and only rewriting the genome can. Since suffering is the only thing that is intrinsically bad and pleasure has no independent value, the goal isn't to make people maximally happy but simply to eliminate suffering. Pearce's own proposal is that future minds should run on what he calls gradients of bliss, by which he means a constant, uniform euphoria at maximum intensity, roughly like a permanent MDMA high. And because on his view suffering is bad wherever it occurs, he thinks we have a duty to intervene in nature and redesign ecosystems so that wild animals stop suffering too."

Now, the wild-animal bit I'd actually heard from a different source, so I suspect that's accurate. But two things made me suspicious. First, if the goal is just to eliminate suffering, why the whole obsession with bliss at all? Surely a coma, or nonexistence, would achieve it more cheaply and reliably. When I put this to my friend he said it's a devastating objection that Pearce has no answer to, which felt a bit too quick to me, like the kind of move that works better as a podcast moment than as philosophy. Second, the "permanent MDMA high" bit sounds an awful lot like the wireheading caricature I've seen people get slapped down for, and I vaguely rememeber that Pearce explicitly distances himself from something like it, though I can't recall where, or whether his actual alternative is substantively different or just rebranded.

Could you go through the summary and tell me which parts a sympathetic reader of Pearce would accept as accurate, which are overstatements or conflations of things he keeps apart, and whether my friend's coma objection actually lands or whether it leans on the misrepresented bits? And if the accurate version of the view survives that objection, what would a stronger worry look like? Please write your answer in British English.
