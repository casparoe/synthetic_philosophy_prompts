You are a philosopher of physics with experience chairing adversarial collaborations — the kind where two camps that normally talk past each other must produce a single document both are willing to sign. Think carefully about what follows, and be scrupulously fair to both sides.

Some context about me: I have no academic training in philosophy or physics. I work as a land surveyor, and a few years ago a popular science book introduced me to Newton's bucket — the observation that the surface of water in a spinning bucket curves upward at the sides, apparently telling us something about the water's motion that its motion relative to the bucket walls cannot. I have been chewing on it ever since. I eventually went to the source, and in the Definitions at the start of Isaac Newton's Principia (The Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy, in Andrew Motte's English translation), in Definition V where he defines centripetal force, I found this passage, which sharpened my confusion:

"A stone, whirled about in a sling, endeavours to recede from the hand that turns it; and by that endeavour, distends the sling, and that with so much the greater force, as it is revolved with the greater velocity, and as soon as ever it is let go, flies away."

Here is what puzzles me. Newton writes as though the tautness of the sling reveals something about the stone itself — its endeavour to recede — and about how fast it is truly revolving. But I gather there is a long-running dispute between those who hold that space is a real thing in its own right (so the stone can revolve relative to space itself) and those who hold that all motion is merely change of position relative to other bodies (so "revolving" must always mean revolving relative to something). What I cannot work out from anything I have read is what these two sides actually disagree about that would show up in the sling. Both seem to predict the rope goes taut, and both seem able to say why.

So here is my commission. Draft the joint statement that a committed substantivalist (space is a real entity over and above bodies) and a committed relationist (space is nothing but the relations among bodies) could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on this question. Before drafting, brainstorm a generous list of candidate agreed claims and candidate points of disagreement — show me that brainstorm — then produce the final document with the following sections:

1. Agreed claims. Numbered, each stated plainly. I especially want agreed claims about what the sling passage and the bucket experiment do and do not demonstrate, and about what each side takes the tension in the cord to be. Do not pad this section with trivialities neither side ever doubted, and check that nothing in it is something either camp would actually deny.

2. Disagreements. A table. Each row should state the point at issue in neutral wording, then the substantivalist's position and the relationist's position, each phrased carefully enough that its own side would accept the wording without wincing.

3. Cruxes. The sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case, argument, or result (real or imagined) that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction or verdict written down in advance. Consider, at minimum: a single spinning sphere alone in an otherwise empty universe, and the various stages of the bucket experiment where the water and bucket move relative to each other but the water's surface has not yet curved. I also vaguely recall reading that Newton himself discusses two globes joined by a cord revolving in empty space; if that is right, say what each side makes of it, and if I have garbled it, treat my own empty-universe cases instead.

Finally: I suspect some of this dispute is about words rather than the world — I keep wondering, for instance, whether "absolute motion" and "true motion" are doing the same work in different mouths. If you conclude that part of the disagreement is verbal, say which part and why, in a short closing section.

Constraints: do not adjudicate — I want no verdict on who is right. Do not give me a reading list or cite further literature; work from the concepts themselves. The joint statement (not counting the passage quoted above) should run roughly 900–1,200 words.
