I teach a second-year policy seminar at a university, mixed econ/poli-sci/soc students, about 22 of them, 80-minute session. Next week we're on parental leave policy and I want an in-class game or simulation that makes them feel why "just give both parents the same legal right to leave" doesn't fix anything on its own -- how you can have a formally gender-neutral leave policy and still end up in an equilibrium where mothers take a year, fathers take two weeks, employers quietly price that in when they hire and promote 28-year-old women, and then the wage gap that creates makes it *rational* for the next couple to have the mother stay home again. The loop is the point. I want them to land in that self-reinforcing cycle themselves by making individually sensible choices, not to hear about it from me.

Design the activity: roles (I'm guessing some students play employers making hiring/promotion calls, some play couples deciding who takes leave, but surprise me if there's a better split), the actual rules, what info each side does and doesn't get to see, a payoff scheme that works without real money, and how many rounds we can fit in 80 minutes including debrief. The debrief should map what happened in the room onto the real mechanisms -- statistical discrimination, the motherhood penalty, why "use it or lose it" earmarked leave for the second parent changes the incentives rather than just being symbolic. Don't lecture them up front; they shouldnt hear terms like "statistical discrimination" or "equilibrium" until after the game has done its work.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (e.g. a student couple who just refuses to optimize, or an employer who promotes everyone out of niceness and breaks the incentive structure) and how to handle it. And be honest about which part of the real phenomenon the game can't reproduce and I have to just tell them about afterwards.
