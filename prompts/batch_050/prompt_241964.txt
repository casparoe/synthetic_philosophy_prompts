Please explain, at an intuitive rather than fully technical level, Gödel's argument that his rotating cosmological solutions to Einstein's field equations show time to be something less than fully objective. I'd like the explanation to cover three things in order. First, how closed timelike curves arise in these solutions — what it is about the rotation of matter that lets a worldline loop back into its own past. Second, why Gödel thought that a spacetime permitting such curves cannot support an objective lapse of time; please connect this to the idea that objective time requires a global, observer-independent ordering of events into earlier and later, which is precisely what these models lack. Third, the further and more controversial step in which he moves from "some physically possible worlds have no objective time" to "time is not objective in our actual world either," on the grounds that the difference between such worlds and ours is one of degree rather than kind. This last step is where I suspect the argument is weakest, so please scrutinize it carefully. Then address the following wrinkle, which I haven't seen discussed: imagine a spacetime that contains closed timelike curves, but only inside a region permanently sealed off behind an event horizon — a pocket that no observer in the main region can ever enter, observe, or be influenced by. Would Gödel's conclusion about the ideality of time still hold for the main region? Whatever your answer, use it to diagnose whether his argument ultimately rests on the actual physics of the spacetime we inhabit or on the bare mathematical possibility of such worlds. Present your entire response as exactly five numbered points, each one to three sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
