I’m a novelist working on a piece of literary sci-fi and I’d like a short story of about 2,000–2,500 words built around the difference between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, plus the global-workspace idea that a mental content becomes conscious when it’s made broadly available for report, reasoning, memory, and action.

The setup I have in mind is a near-future neurological treatment with two independently adjustable systems. One controls whether information can be “broadcast” across the person’s mind—spoken about, remembered, used in decisions, and so on. The other supposedly controls whether the information has any felt quality at all. Maybe a character injured in an accident can still flinch from pain, answer questions about it, and plan around it while sincerely insisting there is no pain *feeling* involved; another character undergoes something with an intensely felt quality that never becomes accessible enough to report or integrate into memory. Feel free to change the medical details as long as the two directions of separation remain clear.

The story should be about people first and the philosophy second. I’d rather see the distinction create a concrete personal, legal, or ethical problem than have characters sit around defining terms. Maybe the treatment is used during surgery, testimony, grief counseling, criminal sentencing, or end-of-life care. The plot should force someone to decide whether the inward feel, the globally available information, or the connection between the two is what matters morally. Please avoid making the story resolve neatly by revealing that one system was “obviously” the real seat of consciousness all along; part of the unease should come from the fact that first-person evidence and third-person evidence pull apart.

A couple of guardrails: don’t make this a routine “is the AI conscious?” story, don’t use a dream or simulation twist, and don’t have a neuroscientist character dump the whole theory into dialogue. Some philosophical vocabulary is fine where a character would naturally use it, but the central tension should be dramatized.

After the story, add a short author’s note, maybe 150–200 words, explaining where you took liberties with access/phenomenal consciousness or leaned on a contested interpretation of global workspace theory. Also say what philosophical argument or empirical discovery would most likely change the story’s framing—for example, what result would convince you that the two systems couldn’t really be separated in the way the fiction assumes.
