So I got into it with a friend of a friend at a dinner party last weekend and it's still bugging me. I was making basically Singer's drowning child case -- if you'd wade into a shallow pond to save a kid at the cost of ruining your shoes, you should also donate to cheap life-saving stuff abroad, since the cost is comparably trivial. And this guy shut me down with "well, charity begins at home." When I pushed him he said something about how we owe more to our own community and how people far away aren't our responsibilty the way our neighbors are.

Here's the thing though. In "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972), Singer writes:

"It makes no moral difference whether the person I can help is a neighbor's child ten yards away from me or a Bengali whose name I shall never know, ten thousand miles away. ... The moral point of view requires us to look beyond the interests of our own society. Previously, ... this may hardly have been feasible, but it is quite feasible now."

So Singer seems to have seen the "charity begins at home" move coming fifty years in advance. But I want to know whether he actually answered it, because I'm not sure he did.

What I'd like from you: unpack what "charity begins at home" could precisely mean. I suspect it conflates a bunch of different claims -- maybe an empirical claim about where our money does the most good, maybe a claim that special obligations to people near us outweigh general duties of rescue, maybe just a claim about what comes first in the order of learning to be decent, maybe something about what we can know or verify. Lay the readings out as a menu. For each one, say whether it's a platitude nobody disputes, a substantive but defensible claim, or just false -- and crucially, for each one, say whether it survives the Singer passage. Does the passage already refute that reading, does it concede part of it, or does it just talk past it? Note Singer's own move about feasibility: is "we can help faraway strangers now" actually responsive to the special-obligations reading, or only to the practical one?

Then tell me which reading my dinner party opponent most likely needed for his comeback to work, and whether "charity begins at home" on that reading even touches the drowning child analogy or just changes the subject.

At the end, tell me which reading you find strongest and most threatening to Singer's argument, and be honest about where your analysis is most likely to go wrong -- e.g., what consideration or argument would convince you that the distance passage is doing less work than you claimed.
