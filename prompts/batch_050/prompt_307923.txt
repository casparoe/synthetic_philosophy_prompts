I'm preparing a seminar on the ethics of infinite populations and want to find the principle, if any, behind my verdicts on the following cases. Represent each world as an infinite sequence of lifetime welfare levels, one entry per person. (1) The world (1, 1, 1, ...) is worse than the world (2, 2, 2, ...) — this much seems clear. (2) Raising the welfare of any finite number of people in an infinite world, while leaving everyone else untouched, makes the world better; the improvement is real even if it would be invisible to any measure based on totals or long-run averages. (3) The alternating worlds (2, 1, 2, 1, ...) and (1, 2, 1, 2, ...) are equally good — intuitively, each is just a relabeling of the other, and neither population is better off. (4) Discounting welfare geometrically by a person's position in the sequence, so that the sums converge and comparisons become tractable, is unacceptable; a world's value cannot depend on how its inhabitants happen to be ordered. (5) Adding infinitely many people with barely positive lives (say, welfare 0.1 each) to the world (1, 1, 1, ...) makes things worse, even though each added life is worth living. Is there a coherent principle that captures all five verdicts? If not, state the strongest candidates precisely, identify which verdicts each candidate must reject, and say which verdict you think I should surrender. I particularly suspect that (2) and (3) are in tension, and I want that confirmed or dissolved. Whatever principle you favor, also report its verdict on a case I haven't settled: the world (1, 2, 3, ...), where welfare rises without bound along the sequence, compared with the world (3, 3, 3, ...). Please write your response in American English.
