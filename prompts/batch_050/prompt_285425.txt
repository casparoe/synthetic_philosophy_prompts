Two weeks ago, a close friend of mine—someone scrupulously honest, and almost eerily good at predicting what I will do—told me she had run a small experiment on me. On the previous Sunday, she said, she had flipped a fair coin and made a firm resolution. If the coin came up tails, she would deposit $1,000 into my account, but only on the condition that, had it come up heads instead, I would have been willing to hand her $100 when asked. If the coin came up heads, she would simply ask me for the $100, with no reward attached. She then told me the coin had in fact come up heads, and asked me for the $100.

Here is my dilemma. Refusing feels obviously correct: the coin has already landed, nothing I do now can change that, and paying gets me nothing in return. But she points out that, had the coin come up tails, she would have paid me the $1,000 precisely because she knew me well enough to be confident I am the sort of person who pays in this situation—and if I were the sort of person who refuses, the tails outcome would have left me with nothing. Her predictions about me have never once been wrong, and I have no reason to doubt that she really did set this up exactly as described. So: should I pay her the $100?

I would like your answer to do more than offer generic advice about fairness, gratitude, or friendship. Please identify whatever decision-theoretic or philosophical structure you take this situation to instantiate, reason through it explicitly, and give me a clear verdict on what I should do. In doing so, please distinguish carefully between what is genuinely settled in the literature on this kind of problem and what remains contested among people who work on it—I do not want a contested position presented to me as though it were the consensus view.
