I'm a junior in high school and our philosophy club has been working through Plato's Meno this semester. At our last meeting, one of the senior members summarized the famous part about knowledge and true belief like this: "Plato's point is that knowledge is justified true belief. A true belief is like one of Daedalus's statues -- it works fine while you have it, but it can run away, and what ties it down is justification, meaning that you've practiced giving reasons and defending the belief against objections. So the practical lesson is that anyone who holds a true belief and gets good at arguing for it has knowledge, which is why knowledge is more valuable."

Something about this bothered me, since I thought the Meno comes earlier than the whole "justified true belief" idea and that Plato's talk of "tying down" a belief with an account might mean something more specific than just being able to win arguments about it. But this student usually knows what he's talking about, so I'm not confident. Could you tell me which parts of his summary are accurate to the dialogue, which parts are later ideas being read back into Plato, and whether his practical lesson actually follows from the accurate parts? I'd also like to know what a careful reader would say the tying-down metaphor is really about, if not what he said.
