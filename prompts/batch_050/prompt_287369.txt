I've been rereading the old Aumann/agreement-theory threads on LessWrong and I want to stress-test the common prior assumption against a real episode: the subprime mortgage market in 2005–2008. Take the rough facts as common knowledge — a handful of outsiders (Burry at Scion, the Cornwall Capital guys, Paulson) read the loan-level data, concluded the mortgage bonds were badly mispriced, and bought huge amounts of CDS protection, while big banks and AIG happily sold it, and most of the market (rating agencies, the Fed, most institutional investors) treated a *national* decline in house prices as basically off the table. Both sides knew the other side of the trade existed; the shorts were hardly a secret to the desks selling them protection.

So do the mapping carefully. Who exactly are the agents here, what is the prior supposed to be common *over* (models of the housing market? default correlations?), and what does Aumann's agreement theorem say should happen the moment Burry's willingness to keep paying premiums for protection becomes known to the desk on the other side — should the desk update, should Burry update, and what did each actually do? Then take the no-trade angle: with common priors and common knowledge of rationality, none of these trades should clear, yet they did, at scale. Walk through the candidate escapes and say which one you think is doing the real work: (a) priors genuinely weren't common, and "they were running different models" is just what differing priors look like in the wild; (b) rationality wasn't common knowledge — bonus cycles, agency problems, executives who knew they'd look stupid sitting out a boom even if they suspected it was one; (c) the trade wasn't pure belief arbitrage at all, since CDS can function as insurance and a bank laying off exposure has gains from trade that don't require disagreement. Where do the observed behaviors fit — Goldman eventually flipping net short, AIG never hedging, Burry's own investors trying to pull their money out the whole time?

Then run it back at the theory. Does 2008 show the common prior assumption is just false for real markets, or that the assumption held and the anomaly lives in incentives and career risk instead? And is there anything in the episode that could even *distinguish* "different priors" from "same priors, but nobody updated on each other's trades" — because if not, I worry the common prior assumption is unfalsifiable in practice and the whole Aumann frame is doing less than LW treats it as doing. Last thing: does the episode vindicate or complicate the standard rationalist advice that when you disagree with the market you should ask what you know that it doesn't?
