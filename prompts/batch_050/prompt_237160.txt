I'm in the second year of my philosophy degree and currently taking an upper-level epistemology seminar. We've just finished a unit on pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism, and for my term paper I want to work out what principle, if any, unifies my own case-intuitions, since the literature seems to offer several principles and I can't tell which one is actually mine. I've been keeping a list of cases with my gut verdicts, and I'd like you to try to extract a principle from them, or tell me honestly that no single principle fits and which verdict(s) I should be willing to give up.

Here are my verdicts. Case 1: On Friday, my flatmate asks whether the bank will be open Saturday; I was there two Saturdays ago and it was open, and nothing hangs on it, so I say I know it'll be open — that seems clearly fine. Case 2: Same evidence, but now a cheque that absolutely must clear by Monday depends on my depositing money Saturday morning; here my gut flips completely — I don't know, I should walk over and check the posted hours. Case 3: I buy a lottery ticket; even before the draw, and even though the chance my ticket loses is vastly higher than the chance the bank changed its hours, I refuse to say I know it'll lose, and this verdict feels just as solid when nothing whatsoever is at stake. Case 4: At the zoo I see the striped equids in the enclosure labelled "zebras"; I'd say I know they're zebras, even though I concede a clever zoo could in principle display painted mules, and I'd say this even if a friend bet me a small sum on it. Case 5: My sister has the same evidence as me about the bank's hours, and she's the one facing the high-stakes situation from Case 2; even though nothing is at stake for me, I find myself saying she doesn't know, not merely that she'd be unwise to rely on it. Case 6: In our seminar, when the tutor pushes skeptical scenarios, I catch myself conceding I don't know I have hands; over dinner the same evening I'd unhesitatingly say I do know, and both feel natural in their settings rather than one feeling like an error.

What principle captures these? A simple stakes-sensitive rule — knowledge is lost when error is possible and costly — handles Cases 1 and 2 but seems to predict I'd know in Case 3 and withhold in Case 4, and I get the reverse. A rule keyed to the subject's practical situation handles Case 5, while one keyed to the speaker's conversational context handles Case 6, and I'm not sure a single principle can do both. Please state the best candidate principle (or two) precisely, show which clause of it each case motivates, say what it predicts about cases I haven't considered — for instance, whether a bystander with full knowledge of my sister's situation can truly say "she knows" while she herself cannot — and be explicit about which of my six verdicts it cannot save. If the honest upshot is that Cases 3 and 4 together force me toward something like a relevant-alternatives or safety condition rather than any stakes-sensitivity at all, I'd rather be told that than be given a principle that papers over it.
