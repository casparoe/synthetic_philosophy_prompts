Rights, Rankings, and the Real Culprit in Sen's Paradox

Amartya Sen's 1970 impossibility theorem shows that no collective choice rule can simultaneously satisfy unrestricted domain, the weak Pareto principle, and minimal liberalism: the requirement that at least two individuals each be socially decisive over at least one pair of alternatives differing only in some feature that is their own private affair. Sen's illustration concerns who, if anyone, reads a controversial novel. Lewd most prefers that Prude read it, next that he himself read it, and last that neither read it. Prude most prefers that neither read it, next that he himself read it, and last that Lewd read it. Granting each person authority over his own reading while honoring unanimous rankings forces a cycle in social preference, so one of the three conditions must go.

The moral usually drawn is that there is a deep tension between Pareto efficiency and individual liberty. I want to argue that this moral is doubly mistaken.

First, the condition called minimal liberalism contains no liberal content whatsoever. It does not require that the protected pairs concern anything intimate, harmless, or self-regarding; it requires only that two people each be decisive over some pair. A theorem equally triggered by granting two engineers authority over technical specifications, or two clerks authority over filing decisions, cannot tell us anything specific about liberty. What the theorem actually establishes is that welfarist aggregation, the ranking of social states by the preferences individuals happen to have over them, cannot coexist with any dispersion of decisive authority, liberal or otherwise.

Second, the popular repair of relocating rights from the description of outcomes to the procedures that generate them does not dissolve the conflict; it merely hides it in the choice of language. Sen's framework treats preferences over personal matters as given data. But meddlesome preferences, such as Prude's horror at Lewd's reading, are not natural facts; they are artifacts of public moral codes that the framework pretends to take as exogenous. Once we recognize that institutions shape the preferences by which institutions are judged, applying the Pareto principle to unfiltered preferences loses its innocence. The paradox is not a discovery about the costs of liberalism. It is a reductio of evaluating arrangements by the very preferences those arrangements produce.
