Consider two games whose payoffs are determined by tossing a fair coin repeatedly until it first lands heads, with the payment depending on the number n of the toss on which this happens, an event with probability 2^-n. In the St. Petersburg game, the player receives 2^n dollars; the expected payoff is then a sum of terms each equal to 1, which diverges, and the classical puzzle is that apparently no finite entry fee is too large, although nobody sensible would pay one. In a second game, sometimes called the Pasadena game, the player instead receives (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n dollars, so that the successive possible payoffs are 2, −2, 8/3, −4, 32/5, and so on. Each term of the expected payoff is then (−1)^(n+1) / n, yielding the alternating harmonic series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., which in its natural order converges to ln 2. But this series is not absolutely convergent, and so, by the Riemann rearrangement theorem, its terms can be reordered to sum to any real number whatsoever; on the standard mathematical definition, the expected payoff of the game is therefore undefined rather than infinite.
