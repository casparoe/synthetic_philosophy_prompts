I'm drafting a short book chapter on Galileo's hermeneutics -- basically my attempt to extract from the Letter to Christina a principled position on scripture-and-science conflicts, one I could also defend as a live view and not just attribute historically. Before I commit to it in print, I want a consistency audit of the package. Here's what I'm committed to:

1. The two-books thesis: God authors both scripture and nature, truths can't conflict, so any apparent collision between a reading of scripture and a finding of natural philosophy is a defect in the reader, not in either book.
2. The demonstration rule (which I take to be Galileo's own): a demonstrated conclusion in natural philosophy obliges us to reinterpret the apparently contrary scriptural passage; but where the science is merely probable, the literal reading stays in charge.
3. The accommodation point: passages like the sun standing still in Joshua never asserted an astronomical thesis in the first place -- the text speaks according to appearances for pastoral ends, so strictly there is no astronomical content for science to collide with.
4. Bellarmine's 1615 challenge -- produce a genuine demonstration of the earth's motion and we'll happily revisit the exegesis -- was intellectually fair given rule 2, not obstructionism dressed up as openness.
5. Galileo was nonetheless epistemically entitled, by the time of the Letter and then the Dialogue, to read Joshua through heliocentric lenses rather than leaving the literal reading in charge.

My worry is that someone in my department is going to tell me these don't hang together, and I'd rather hear it from you first. So: go through the set and tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which pairs only look like they do. For each real tension, say how severe it is, and be specific about what exactly has to give -- don't just gesture at "there's a worry here." And for the pairs that merely appear to conflict, show me the move that dissolves them, because I want to have that ready.

Two things I ask in particular. First, when you assess commitment 5 against 2 and 4, actually engage with what Galileo's evidence could and couldn't establish -- I want to know whether the physics he had available to him meets the very standard that 2 and 4 impose, and if it doesn't, which of the three I'm forced to drop or weaken. Second, tell me whether 2 and 3 are really compatible or whether holding the accommodation point quietly drains the demonstration rule of any work to do -- I've gone back and forth on this and keep talking myself out of seeing a problem.

End with a verdict: the single least costly revision that gets me a coherent package, ranked against the runner-up revision, with honest costs attached to each. Order everything by severity rather than treating all the tensions as equally bad.
