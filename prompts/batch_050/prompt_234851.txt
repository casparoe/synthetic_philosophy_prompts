Write a dialogue between two philosophers arguing about what norm governs assertion. One defends the view that you should assert only what you know (the knowledge norm); the other defends the view that you should assert only what you have good enough justification to believe (a justification norm). Neither is a strawman -- both should be sharp, and they should respond to what the other actually says rather than rehearsing canned speeches.

Have them work through these cases (they can bring up their own too):

1. A detective has overwhelming evidence that the suspect did it -- fingerprints, motive, footage -- and tells the press the suspect is guilty. The evidence was all genuinely excellent, but the suspect is in fact innocent (it's a freak coincidence). Did the detective assert properly?

2. A teacher who privately doubts evolution nonetheless tells her class that evolution is well-supported by the evidence, because she thinks her job is to convey what the science says, not what she believes. Was her assertion ok, even though she didn't believe (let alone know) it?

3. Someone holding a ticket in a fair 10,000-ticket lottery tells their friend, before the draw, "your ticket didn't win" -- purely on the odds. The friend's ticket in fact lost. Knowledge-norm person: obviously this assertion was bad. But how is it different from case 1, where the justification-norm person wants to say the assertion was fine? Both sides should feel some pressure here.

4. A chess engine outputs "the best move is Nf3" based on a calculation that has a bug but happens to land on the right move anyway (a Gettier-ish case). If a human had asserted that on the same basis, would it have been proper?

At some point they should clash over whether "you shouldn't have said that" and "what you said was false" are the same criticism or different ones, and over what happens when your evidence gets defeated later -- does the justification-norm person owe us an account of retraction, and does the knowledge-norm person owe us an account of why assertions based on misleading evidence feel wrong even when lucky?

End the dialogue with them either reaching a genuine crux (a single case or principle where they clearly can't agree and both understand why) or one of them conceding a real point -- not both of them just agreeing to disagree.
