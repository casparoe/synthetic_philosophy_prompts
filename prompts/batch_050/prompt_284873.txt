I'm in the middle of a business degree and we're doing a module on managerial decision-making. One of the set readings is a chapter from a popular book on "thinking like an economist", and it summarises the sunk cost fallacy like this: 'Economists have shown that money and effort you've already spent should never influence your decisions, because what's spent is gone either way. So anyone who stays in a bad film because they paid for the ticket, finishes a degree they hate because they've already done two years, or keeps funding a failing project because of what's been invested is simply reasoning incorrectly. Notice this also means that finishing what you start, keeping your commitments, and not being a quitter are just the sunk cost fallacy dressed up as virtues — grit is a bias with good PR. The rational person treats every decision as if they were encountering the situation fresh, with no history.'

Something about this rubs me the wrong way, but I can't quite locate the problem. The first part sounds like standard economics, and our lecturer would probably endorse it. But the jump to "finishing what you start is just the fallacy in disguise" feels too quick — surely there are cases where sticking with something *because* of what you've invested is sensible, or where treating yourself as having "no history" is exactly the wrong move (like if abandoning projects teaches you you're the kind of person who abandons projects, or if other people are watching whether you follow through). Also, is it even right that economists "showed" this, as if it's an empirical result?

Could you go through the summary and tell me which parts a careful economist or decision theorist would accept, which parts overstate or distort the actual claim, and whether the bit about grit and commitment actually follows from the fallacy being real? I'd like to be able to push back in the seminar without just relying on vibes, so the more precisely you can separate the solid core from the rhetorical flourish the better.
