So I'm a retired plasma physicist who has spent the last two years falling down the philosophy-of-history rabbit hole, and I need a stress test. Please act as a professional philosopher of social science who also knows their ancient political thought -- not a cheerleader, someone who grades hard. Think carefully and brainstorm a long list of candidate defeaters before you pick and structure your answer; I'd rather you show me the ones you discarded too.

Here's my position. I've become convinced that Polybius' anacyclosis (the cycle of constitutions in Book VI of the Histories) is a genuinely scientific theory of political change, in roughly the way a phenomenological law in physics is: not deduced from first principles, but a real, risky, checkable pattern claim, and far better than the just-so story its modern reputation suggests. The passage that converted me is this one from Book VI, chapter 9 (Shuckburgh translation):

"This is the regular cycle of constitutional revolutions, and the natural order in which constitutions change, are transformed, and return again to their original stage. If a man have a clear grasp of these principles he may perhaps make a mistake as to the dates at which this or that will happen to a particular constitution; but he will rarely be entirely mistaken as to the stage of growth or decay at which it has arrived, or as to the point at which it will undergo some revolutionary change."

What I want from you: what would count AGAINST my reading? Not generic "ancient theories are primitive" grumbling, but specifics. In particular:

1. Take the passage seriously. Polybius explicitly gives up on dates but claims near-infallibility on direction and stage. In my old field, a theory that predicts the shape of the curve but not the timescale is still falsifiable (the shape can be wrong). Is that true here, or has he quietly built himself an unfalsifiable scheme where any observed transition can be redescribed as a stage of the cycle? What would a transition have to look like to count as a counterexample rather than as a "stage"?
2. Which actual historical sequences (ancient or modern -- feel free to use modern states, since he says the law is natural and general) would a committed Polybian concede as damaging, and which would they absorb with relabeling? Be honest about whether the absorbing move is legitimate or a cheat.
3. What does the theory predict that its rivals don't -- say, against a linear-progress view, against a random-walk view of regime change, against Ibn Khaldun-style dynastic cycles that don't claim a fixed order of constitutional forms?
4. Where does my physicist's analogy break down? I suspect the "stage attribution" part (saying where a state IS in the cycle) is doing sneaky work, but I can't quite pin it.
5. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which of them a fair-minded defender of Polybius would themselves accept as real tests versus dismiss as category errors.

If your honest conclusion is that the theory is so insulated that nothing could count against it, say so and tell me whether that's fatal to my position or survivable. Dont be polite about it.
