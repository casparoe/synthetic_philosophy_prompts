After retiring from a career as a marine biologist, I have been trying to determine what I think makes something an organism rather than merely an organized collection of organisms. I am not looking for a survey of positions in the literature, and I do not want biological individuality defined so broadly that every population, ecosystem, or unit of selection automatically counts. I want a principled account of organismhood.

Here are my present judgments:

1. A Portuguese man o’ war is one organism. Its specialized zooids develop from a single founding event, are genetically uniform, perform different functions, and generally cannot survive independently.

2. A reef-coral colony is one organism, even though it is composed of many clonal polyps and a detached fragment may become a viable colony. The polyps are physically continuous, coordinate growth, and ordinarily participate in a shared colony-level life history.

3. A honeybee colony is also an organism, despite the fact that each bee remains an organism. The reproductive division of labor, cooperative brood care, colony-level temperature regulation, and dependence of most members on the colony seem sufficient to me.

4. A eukaryotic cell containing mitochondria is plainly one organism, regardless of the symbiotic origin of those mitochondria. Present functional and reproductive integration matters more to me than the fact that the partners were once separate lineages.

5. A lichen is not one organism, although it is a highly integrated symbiosis. The fungal and photosynthetic partners do not consistently share the same reproductive fate: the fungus can disperse and acquire a new partner, while the photosynthetic partner may enter other associations. I would call the fungus an organism and the lichen a stable holobiont.

6. A human being together with the gut microbiome is not one organism. Microbes affect digestion, immunity, and development, but the association is too compositionally variable and its members retain too many independent reproductive trajectories.

7. A clonal aspen grove connected by roots is not one organism merely because the stems are genetically identical and may exchange resources. The individual trunks seem to me substantially autonomous, and the grove lacks the functional subordination found in a coral colony.

8. When separate cellular slime-mold amoebae aggregate into a migrating slug and then form a fruiting body in which some cells die as stalk cells, I am inclined to call the slug a temporary organism, though not the original free-living population. This judgment persists even if the aggregate contains more than one genotype.

I recognize that some of these judgments are difficult to reconcile. I count a temporary, genetically mixed slime-mold aggregate as organismal while denying that status to a long-lived lichen. I count a fragmentable coral colony as one organism while denying that status to a genetically uniform aspen grove. I also treat a honeybee colony as an organism without denying that its members are organisms. A simple appeal to genetic identity, physical continuity, permanence, cooperation, or common evolutionary origin does not seem to capture the pattern.

My tentative principle is this: an organism is a bounded, self-maintaining system whose components cooperate in a system-level life cycle and whose opportunities for independent persistence or reproduction are sufficiently constrained or subordinated to the organization of the whole. I am dissatisfied with the vague phrase sufficiently constrained or subordinated, and I am unsure whether a system-level life cycle is necessary or merely strong evidence for the other conditions.

Please formulate the best precise principle, or small family of candidate principles, behind my judgments. Explain which case motivates each clause. If no plausible set of necessary and sufficient conditions preserves all eight judgments, say whether a weighted cluster account would be more honest. In that event, identify which of my judgments would have to be abandoned if I wanted a simpler theory, and tell me which abandonment you think would be least costly.

Please also apply your proposed principle to three further cases: a lichen lineage whose propagules reliably contain both partners; two genetically distinct coral colonies that fuse, exchange resources, and develop partly shared reproductive structures; and an engineered microbial consortium whose members cannot reproduce alone and are dispersed only in capsules containing every required lineage. Finally, distinguish your conclusion about organismhood from the separate question of whether these systems can be units of selection.
