You are a professional philosopher specialising in the ethics of technology, with particular expertise in personal identity and the moral status of the dead. Please think carefully and rigorously; you may find it helpful to brainstorm the candidate positions briefly in your own mind before settling on a reconstruction.

Consider a typical commercial "griefbot" service, by which I mean the following kind of operation. After someone dies, a relative uploads the deceased's text messages, emails and voice recordings, and the service trains a conversational model that chats with the bereaved in the first person, as the deceased. The service's standard design has the following features. The bot does not merely replay old messages; it generates entirely new utterances the deceased never wrote, in their style. The account is created and paid for by the bereaved relative; the deceased's own wishes about being simulated in this way are never consulted, since they were typically never recorded. The subscription runs indefinitely until cancelled, and if the relative stops paying, the bot is deleted. Finally, the bot is designed never to claim to be the deceased in a literal sense — if asked directly whether it really is the person, it says it is an AI simulation — but in ordinary conversation it says things like "I miss you" and "I'm proud of you" without any qualification.

What philosophical commitments does this bundle of design choices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the practice: what does it presuppose about personal identity — is the bot implicitly treated as a continuation of the person, a representation of them, a piece of the bereaved's property, or something else? What does it presuppose about whether the dead can be harmed or wronged, and about who, if anyone, holds rights over a dead person's persona? Then examine whether the design is internally consistent: does the disclaimer feature sit comfortably with the unqualified first-person design, and does the deletion-on-non-payment feature fit with whatever moral status the rest of the design implicitly grants the bot? Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits well with — could someone who denied that the dead can be harmed, or who held a purely narrative view of personal identity, still coherently endorse each individual feature? Finally, say what would have to change in the design if one instead took seriously the view that the deceased retains interests that survive death.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Answer in British English.
