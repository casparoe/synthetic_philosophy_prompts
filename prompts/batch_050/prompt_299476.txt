I have no background in philosophy or economics; I got into this because of a standing argument with my brother-in-law, who insists over every family dinner that gold is the only "real" money because its worth doesn't depend on anyone's say-so. While hunting for ammunition I stumbled onto George Berkeley's *The Querist*, a peculiar little book of numbered questions about money and Irish poverty, first published in the 1730s and later revised by the author. One query has been rattling around in my head for weeks:

> Whether the terms crown, livre, pound sterling, etc., are not to be considered as exponents or denominations of such proportion? And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?

That is Query 25 of the revised collected edition, and what scandalizes me (and would scandalize my brother-in-law) is that Berkeley lumps gold, silver, and paper together as equally tickets. After some more reading I learned that this is the same Berkeley who held that things exist only insofar as they are perceived and that there is no mind-independent "matter" underlying them. It struck me that my brother-in-law's position is, by those lights, roughly the cardinal error: he believes in value that sits inside the metal itself, independent of any perceiving or crediting mind — monetary matter, so to speak.

Here is what I'd like written: a satirical sermon of about 600–700 words (the quoted query does not count toward that), in the voice of an unnamed eighteenth-century Church of Ireland bishop preaching to a congregation of Dublin goldsmiths, bankers, and hard-money men. The bishop takes the query above as his sermon text, set as an epigraph, and preaches against the heresy of "metallism" — the superstition that value resides intrinsically in precious metal — diagnosing it as the very same error as believing in matter: the gold-worshipper has mistaken the ticket for the power it commands and reveres the counter as though it were the game. The humor should come from pushing Berkeley's actual views (immaterialism, the attack on abstract ideas, money as a ticket whose whole being lies in circulation and credit) one step further than he took them himself, never from invented silliness, so that a reader who knows the philosophy recognizes the satire as fair while a reader who knows only the gold debate can still follow it.

Keep the bishop deadpan and genuinely devout throughout — no winking, no anachronisms, no modern references. Give the sermon real period structure: an exposition of the text, two or three numbered heads or "uses," and a peroration. Let the phrase "gold, silver, and paper" do serious exegetical work, since it is the part of the text the congregation least wants to hear. The target is the doctrine of intrinsic monetary value and the rhetoric of sound money, not any real person, living or dead. Close with a collection-plate appeal that lands the final joke.
