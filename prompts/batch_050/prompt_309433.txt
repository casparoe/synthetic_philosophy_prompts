So I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on has an actuary protagonist who slowly becomes obsessed with probability (it makes sense in context, trust me). I've written down the worldview I'm giving her, which is honestly also my own worldview, and before I bake it into 300 pages I want to know if it actually hangs together. If she's internally inconsistent I'd rather that be a deliberate character choice than me not noticing.

Act as a philosopher who works on probability and formal epistemology. Think carefully, and feel free to brainstorm the possible tension-points to yourself before committing to an answer. Here are her commitments re: the reference class problem:

1. Probability claims about single events are only meaningful relative to a reference class. "There's a 30% chance Mara gets the disease" doesn't say anything until you specify: 30% of *which group* gets it.
2. What grounds the probabilities is actual frequencies in the world — how often the thing really happens in the group. No mysterious propensities baked into objects, and not just degrees of belief either.
3. There is no "correct" reference class for a given case, only more or less useful ones. Choosing a class is a pragmatic decision, not a discovery.
4. When you actually have to decide something (price a policy, deliver a prognosis) you should use the narrowest reference class you have decent data on. More specific = more informative.
5. A single-case probability claim can be flat-out wrong. If Mara announces her chance is 80% based on vibes when the relevant frequency is 30%, she's mistaken — it's not that she's "right relative to some other class she had in mind."
6. It's legitimate for her to charge two customers different premiums based on a trait neither of them chose (the neighborhood they live in, say), so long as the claim frequencies really do differ between the groups.

Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do? I'm fairly sure at least one pair that seems contradictory is actually fine on inspection, and I'd rather you discriminate than flag everything. Rank the real tensions by how severe they are, and for each one say what the cheapest fix is — i.e. what she'd have to drop or qualify, and what that costs her overall picture. Dont tell me which commitment you think *I* should abandon; just map the costs and let me decide.

Format: give me a table with these columns — Tension (which numbered commitments), Real or apparent, Severity (high/medium/low), Why they clash (one or two sentences), Cheapest fix + what it costs. Then below the table, one short paragraph naming the single revision that would remove the most tension overall.
