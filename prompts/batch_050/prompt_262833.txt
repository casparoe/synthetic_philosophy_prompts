I just rewatched The Invention of Lying (the Ricky Gervais film where nobody can lie until his character Mark stumbles into it) and I want to write a long essay reading it through Augustine on lying — On Lying and Against Lying, the eightfold ranking of lies, the intent-to-deceive definition, the refusal to permit lies even to save a life. Help me draft the reading.

Things I want it to do:

- Take the film's big lies — the afterlife story Mark invents at his mother's deathbed and then for the whole world (the Man in the Sky business), the bank teller lie, the assorted comforting lies he tells strangers — and sort them into Augustine's ranking of the eight kinds of lie. Where does each land, and does Augustine's ordering track the film's own moral instincts or cut right across them? The bit I'm most curious about: Augustine puts lies about religious teaching at the worst end, which means the film's centrepiece is, by Augustines lights, the single most culpable lie in the movie — while the film plays it as his most compassionate act. Does the film have any actual reply to that, or just the satire?
- Augustine defines a lie as a falsehood told with intent to deceive, which is why jokes don't count as lies for him. But the film's premise seems to collapse lying, fiction and religion into one thing — in the no-lie world there's also no novels, no acting, no faith. Is that conflation doing real philosophical work or is it just the set-up for the jokes? Does Augustine's definition dissolve the premise entirely (you could have films in that world, you'd just label them as made up)?
- In the no-lie world everyone also blurts out every cruel thought they have. But not-lying isn't the same as saying everything you think, and Augustine never equates them. Is the film cheating by running honesty and total disclosure together, and does that matter for the argument or just for the comedy?
- The one lie Mark conspicuously refuses: he won't lie to Anna to win her. The film's implicit ethics is otherwise all about consequences — good lies comfort, bad lies take advantage — but that refusal is doing something else. Does the film even notice the tension, and how would Augustine read that scene, given he'd condemn the comforting lies and (I think) oddly approve of the refusal?
- Where the reading has to be honest in both directions: what does the film get right about lying that a summary of Augustine wouldn't predict, and where does it flatten him into a strawman of religious uplift?

Finish with a paragraph on what the film adds to the debate that the treatises themselves don't give you. No need for a plot recap — assume the reader knows the film. Around 1,500 words, and in British English please.
