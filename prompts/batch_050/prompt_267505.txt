Two of my office mates got into a surprisingly heated argument at a going-away party, and it has stuck with me because I genuinely cannot settle it. The occasion was the departure of Dana, a former elite gymnast who left the sport after a spinal injury and has since become a sought-after coach; she can no longer perform even a basic tumbling pass, but her athletes win medals, partly because she can watch one attempt at a vault and tell the gymnast exactly which fraction-of-a-second adjustment will fix it. Mira argued that Dana no longer knows how to vault, however much she knows about vaulting: knowing how to do something, she said, just is being able to do it, and Dana has plainly lost the ability, so what she retains is a huge stock of factual knowledge plus the distinct skill of coaching. Theo replied that this gets the case backwards: Dana obviously knows how to vault -- that is precisely what her coaching success demonstrates -- and it would be bizarre to say that the knowledge evaporated the moment the injury occurred, as if a pianist who breaks a wrist instantly stops knowing how to play the sonata she could perform yesterday. Mira answered that Theo's view has its own absurd consequence: if knowing-how is a matter of possessing and deploying the right information, then a biomechanics graduate student who has memorized everything about vaulting but never touched a springboard knows how to vault, which nobody believes. Theo said he was happy to bite part of that bullet -- the student lacks something, but what he lacks is not knowledge, it is the capacity to act on it, whereas Dana's knowledge is still live because it is exercised in the only way left to her, through instruction -- and Mira retorted that he had thereby smuggled ability back in through the side door, so his position either collapses into hers or floats free of anything observable.

They turned to me to referee and I declined, mostly because each reply seemed to expose a real cost in the other's position without ever landing a knockout.
