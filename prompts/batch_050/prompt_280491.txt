In a popular book on decision theory I came across this summary of the absent-minded driver problem:

'A driver is on a highway with two consecutive exits he cannot tell apart, and he cannot remember whether he has already passed the first. Exiting at the first exit gets him a modest payoff, driving past both gets him nothing, and exiting at the second gets him the best payoff. Whatever plan was optimal when he set out will look wrong to him when he reaches an exit, since at that moment he will always have reason to deviate from it. The example thus shows that standard game theory breaks down for agents with imperfect memory: a perfectly rational agent can be forced by memory loss into acting irrationally against his own best interests. Philosophers have also noted that the problem is structurally identical to the Sleeping Beauty case, and most take it to settle that debate in favor of the halfer position.'

I suspect the author is overstating things but cannot pinpoint where. Which parts of this summary would a careful game theorist or philosopher accept as accurate, which are conflations or overstatements — for instance, about what the driver's epistemic situation actually is, about whether deviation from the ex ante optimal plan really is rational at the moment of decision, and about what the example is generally taken to show — and do the two closing conclusions, that rationality can be self-defeating here and that the Sleeping Beauty debate is thereby settled, survive once the summary is corrected?
