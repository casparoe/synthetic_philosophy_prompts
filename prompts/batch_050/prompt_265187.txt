I retired from a chemistry department four years ago and have spent the time since reading philosophy the way I used to read journals, mostly out in the garden. Which is relevant, because the slogan I want taken apart came up at my allotment society last month. We've got a waiting list for plots, and a newer member, Dieter, spent one autumn clearing a bramble-choked corner nobody had touched in fifteen years, built raised beds, put in a little shed. Then the commitee voted to reassign all the plots by lottery, to be fair to the people on the list, and Dieter said, more or less: I mixed my labour with that land, so it's mine. Someone at the meeting told him this goes back to Locke, and someone else said Locke only lets you do that when there's enough and as good left for everyone else, which there plainly isn't, since the list is twenty names long. It got quite heated and I realised I had no idea what the slogan was actually claiming in the first place.

So: take "I mixed my labour with it, so it's mine" and pull apart the distinct precise claims it could be making. Off the top of my head I can gesture at a few candidates -- that working on something creates a kind of moral desert, that being first on the scene is what does it, that improving a thing gives you title to the improvement (but why to the whole thing?), that labour is just a public way of marking your claim, and some murkier idea about your labour literally becoming part of the object so the object becomes yours by extension -- but I'm sure there are cleaner carvings and ones I've missed. For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or just false, and say which readings would actually support Dieter's case against the lottery, since that's the work the slogan was doing. Then do the same dissection on the proviso: what are the live ways of making "enough and as good left for others" precise (equal shares? nobody left worse off than they'd otherwise have been?), and does the proviso sink the labour story for a crowded garden or only restrict it. Finish by telling me which reading Dieter most likely meant, given how people actually talk at allotment meetings.

One more thing, since I'm new to this field and can't yet smell when a philosopher is being overconfident: please say how confident you are in each of your verdicts as you go, and at the end single out the one point in your answer you're least sure of and why.
