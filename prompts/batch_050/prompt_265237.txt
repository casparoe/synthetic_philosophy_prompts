I'm a PhD student in economics (macro concentration, but I've been taking a history of economic thought seminar this term) and I've run into something in Hume that seems flat-out contradictory, and I want to settle it before my qualifying paper proposal is due.

From what I understand, Hume's essays Of Money and Of the Balance of Trade basically give us the quantity theory: the amount of money in a country doesn't matter for real wealth, prices just adjust proportionally, and any trade imbalance self-corrects because gold flows out, domestic prices fall, exports get competitive, and the money comes back. So money is neutral -- it's just a veil, a unit of account, and mercantilists who obsessed over hoarding specie were confused about what wealth even is. Fine, textbook stuff.

But here's my problem: in the same essays Hume apparently also says that a *gradually increasing* money supply is good for a country, that it stimulates industry and commerce, keeps people employed and enterprising, and that nations with growing money supplies are more prosperous than ones where money is stagnant or shrinking. My roommate (who's in the history dept) says Hume even thought this was one of the main engines of European economic growth. But if money is neutral -- if it's just a veil over the real economy -- then how can printing more of it (or minting more of it, whatever) make anyone actually richer? Isn't Hume just contradicting himself? It seems like he refuted the mercantilists with the neutrality argument and then turned around and endorsed a mercantilist-flavored policy of money growth.

So my question: was Hume simply inconsistent here, or is there some way to reconcile these two positions? And if he's inconsistent, which half should modern economists keep -- the neutrality result or the money-growth-is-stimulative claim? I'm tempted to write my proposal around "Hume's unresolved tension" but I want to make sure I'm not missing something obvious before I pitch it to my advisor.
