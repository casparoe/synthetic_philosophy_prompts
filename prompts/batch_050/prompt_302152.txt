I'm a high school junior, and our biology class spent a unit on ant colonies, which led me down a rabbit hole. In Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary thought experiment, the individual ants are simple and mindless, but the colony as a whole is a conscious, conversational mind -- the ants play the role that neurons play in a brain. I find this surprisingly compelling, but my biology teacher says the analogy breaks down because neurons are physically wired together in one skull while ants just bump into each other and leave pheromones, so any "colony mind" is at best a loose metaphor. I'm trying to figure out who's right, and I want your actual position rather than a survey of the debate.

Here is the proposition, stated as precisely as I can manage: an established colony of, say, 200,000 Argentine ants possesses genuine mental states -- beliefs about its environment and desires regarding food, defense, and reproduction -- of the same general kind as the mental states of an individual mammal, though presumably stranger and dimmer in content. By "genuine" I mean not metaphorical or merely useful-to-ascribe, but states the colony really has, over and above the behavioral dispositions of its parts.

Please give me your credence in this proposition as a single number, and decompose the reasoning. The sub-questions I think it depends on are: (1) whether substrate-independence is true -- whether mindedness depends only on functional organization, not on the material or on being physically contiguous in one skull; (2) whether an actual ant colony, as opposed to Hofstadter's idealized one, exhibits enough integration, flexibility, and information processing to satisfy whatever the correct theory of mind demands; (3) whether the fact that colony behavior is fully explainable by the local rules of individual ants "screens off" a colony-level mind, or whether that's no more threatening than the fact that my behavior is fully explainable by my neurons; and (4) whether mindedness comes in degrees at all, in which case the real question might be where colonies fall on a spectrum rather than whether they qualify. Assign a rough probability to each of these (and add any sub-question I've missed), show how they combine into your final number, and identify which term the final number is most sensitive to. Then tell me: given your decomposition, where does my disagreement with my teacher most likely live, and what empirical discovery or argument would move your credence the most in either direction?
