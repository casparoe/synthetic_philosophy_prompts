I am a high school student preparing for a seminar discussion on whether museums should return cultural objects to their countries of origin, and I want to test my own instincts by laying out my verdicts on a series of cases and asking what principle, if any, unifies them.

Here are my cases and verdicts. First, the Benin Bronzes, seized by British troops in a punitive raid in 1897 and sold off to museums: these should clearly be returned, since they were taken by force from a kingdom that still has cultural descendants today. Second, the Parthenon Marbles, removed by Lord Elgin in the early 1800s with permission from the Ottoman authorities who then ruled Athens, though many Greeks dispute whether that permission was legitimate: I still think these should go back, but this feels noticeably harder than the Benin case, and I am less certain of my verdict. Third, an artifact purchased legally and in good faith by a museum decades ago from a local dealer, where it later turned out the dealer had smuggled it out: I think this should also be returned, even though the museum itself did nothing wrong. Fourth, an object from an ancient civilization with no identifiable living descendants and no modern state with a special claim: I do not think it must be returned, though I cannot say exactly why that absence matters. Fifth, a case where returning an object would plausibly lead to its destruction or neglect because the claimant country lacks secure facilities: here I lean against immediate return, but I feel uneasy, because this reasoning sounds uncomfortably like a justification historical colonizers themselves would have offered. Sixth, human remains taken for scientific study in the colonial era: I think these must be returned regardless of any of the considerations above, and I am more confident in this verdict than in any other.

My questions are these. Is there a single principle — concerning wrongful acquisition, ongoing connection to a living culture, or something else — that captures all six verdicts, or do I need more than one? If my verdicts cannot all be reconciled without an unacceptably complicated principle, which one or two should I give up, and why? Please also state what your proposed principle implies for a case I have not listed, such as objects acquired as war reparations or objects voluntarily sold by a community that later regretted the sale. Finally, I would like you to be explicit about your confidence: which of your conclusions is most fragile, and what argument or consideration would most plausibly lead you to a different verdict?
