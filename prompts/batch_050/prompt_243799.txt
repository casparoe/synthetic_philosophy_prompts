Here's my half-baked idea. Everything I've read about satisficing — picking "good enough" instead of the best — treats it as a kind of compromise: ideally you'd maximize, but you're short on time or brainpower, so you settle. But it seems to me that in most real decisions (who to marry, what career to go into, where to live) you can't even list all the options. The options aren't sitting there in a menu, you generate them by searching, and there's no point where you know you've seen them all. So "pick the best option" isn't just hard, it's not even a well-defined instruction. If that's right then satisficing isn't a sad fallback for limited minds — it's the only decision policy that makes sense for open-ended problems, even for a perfectly rational agent with unlimited time. And the interesting question becomes what makes a stopping rule rational when there's no "best" to measure it against, rather than how close satisficers get to maximizers.
