So I just spent like three hours arguing with my friend Marcus about when it's okay to sell things and we got nowhere, so I want to settle it by figuring out what principle my gut is actually using. Here are my verdicts, roughly in the order they came up in the argument:

- A rich guy scalping Taylor Swift tickets for triple face value: fine. Annoying, but fine.
- A hardware store jacking up generator prices 5x right before a hurricane hits: absolutely not fine, even if that's "just what the market will bear."
- Paying someone $50 to stand in line overnight so I get concert tickets: fine.
- Someone selling one of their kidneys for $30k to pay off medical debt: not fine, and I felt strongly about this, but I couldnt articulate why it's different from the next one.
- A woman being paid $9k to donate eggs (uncomfortable procedure, small health risks): fine, normal even.
- Paying a stranger $200 for their Saturday brunch reservation at some impossible-to-book restaurant: fine, but somehow gross in a way the concert ticket scalping isn't.
- Buying someone's vote for $100: not fine, obviously, and I think it should stay illegal.
- A dying woman selling her life insurance policy to investors who pay out more than the surrender value (she gets money now, they collect when she dies): fine, good even, she should have that option.
- A startup paying homeless people to carry wifi hotspots around Austin at SXSW: not fine, and this is the one Marcus hammered me on, because I couldn't explain how it differs from paying the guy to stand in line.

So my problem is obvious. The scalping/gouging pair and the hotspot/line-stander pair both look like I should have to take the same side on both halves, and I don't. Marcus said my real principle is just "it's fine when rich people do it to each other" which I refuse to accept but also can't fully rule out.

What I want: a principle (or two candidate principles) stated precisely enough that I can see which clause does the work in each case, an honest accounting of which of my verdicts it can't save, and what it predicts for a case we didn't argue about: airlines selling passengers the right to board earlier and skip the general boarding scrum.
