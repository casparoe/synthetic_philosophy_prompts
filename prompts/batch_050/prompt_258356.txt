I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-audience magazine and host a public salon attached to it: typically 14 to 20 attendees, mixed backgrounds, no philosophical training, one 75-minute session. For an upcoming evening on the ethics of minds, I want an activity that lets participants feel for themselves both the pull and the trouble of the idea that sentience may come in degrees, and that moral status might shade accordingly across a spectrum running from humans through insects down to a thermostat. I do not want to lecture: whatever the attendees are meant to see, they should run into it themselves, through what the activity makes them do, before anyone names it.

Design the activity. It should include: (a) a concrete setup with roles, materials, and rules, built around a staged decision with real structure -- for instance, allocating a strictly limited "protection budget" across a series of entities presented at first without identifying labels, so that attendees commit to a ranking of "entity 7" before learning what it is; (b) at least one designed reversal, in which information revealed mid-game forces some participants to defend a ranking they no longer endorse, or to revise it in front of the group and explain what changed; (c) a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the session onto the underlying philosophical commitments -- the line-drawing problem, the temptation to switch criteria mid-spectrum (capacity to suffer, behavioral flexibility, integration of information, similarity to us), and what it costs to hold any one of those criteria consistently all the way down to the thermostat; and (d) a candid account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- attendees guessing the trick early, refusing to rank at all, anchoring on cuteness or familiarity -- with a handling for each failure mode, plus an honest statement of which feature of the graded-sentience idea this format cannot reproduce and the facilitator must simply supply.

The whole thing must be runnable with index cards, sticky notes, and a whiteboard, and the rules must be explainable in under five minutes. End with a final line beginning "Bottom line:" that states, in one sentence, what a participant who engages honestly should come away seeing.
