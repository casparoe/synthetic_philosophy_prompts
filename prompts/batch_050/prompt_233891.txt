I am a novelist working on a counterfactual novel in which industrialization begins in the Yangzi Delta rather than in Lancashire, and in sketching the premise I talked myself into an argument that, if sound, would sink not just my book but most of the historiography it draws on. I want a careful diagnosis, and I'd like you to answer as a philosopher of history who also knows the Great Divergence literature, reasoning step by step rather than gesturing. The argument: (1) A "why P rather than Q?" question is well-posed only when the contrast Q picks out a reasonably determinate alternative state of affairs. (2) In "why did sustained industrial growth begin in Britain rather than in China?", the contrast is radically indeterminate: there are indefinitely many distinct ways China might have industrialized first (earlier coal exploitation near the lower Yangzi, a different fiscal-military trajectory, a surviving Zheng He-era maritime sector), and what explains Britain's lead relative to one of these alternatives may be irrelevant or even backwards relative to another. (3) So the famous question is really indefinitely many different questions sharing one interrogative sentence. (4) An interrogative that is indefinitely many questions is not one well-posed question. (5) Therefore the Great Divergence question — the Needham question in modern dress — is ill-posed. (6) But the same reasoning applies to virtually any historical why-question, since "why did Rome fall rather than endure?" or "why did the Revolution happen in France rather than elsewhere?" face exactly the same multiplicity of contrasts. (7) So no large-scale historical why-question is well-posed, and causal explanation in history collapses. Step (7) is absurd, so something has gone wrong; but each premise felt innocent when I wrote it. Identify exactly which step you reject, explain why it looked plausible, and tell me what rejecting it costs — including, if possible, what this means for whether a novelist may legitimately treat "why Britain and not China?" as a question with an answer. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the argument.
