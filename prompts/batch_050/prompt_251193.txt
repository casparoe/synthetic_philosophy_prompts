Two of my colleagues in the math department got into an argument over lunch about Hilbert's hotel, and neither would concede anything, so I'm relaying their exchange here for an outside verdict. I've numbered the moves so you can go through them one at a time.

(1) Daniel argued that the hotel thought experiment shows actual infinities cannot exist. A hotel with infinitely many rooms, all occupied, can still take in a new guest by shifting everyone down one room, and can take in infinitely many new guests by moving each occupant to the room with double their number. A hotel that is both full and able to accommodate more guests is absurd, he said, so the assumption that generated the absurdity -- an actually existing completed infinity -- must be rejected. Finite hotels never behave this way, and the thought experiment is just a vivid proof that the real world can't contain completed infinities.

(2) Marta replied that the argument confuses the counterintuitive with the impossible. Our intuitions about hotels were formed entirely from finite cases, so of course an infinite one behaves strangely by our lights. But strangeness is not contradiction: every one of the hotel's features follows by valid reasoning from a consistent theory of infinite sets, the same theory mathematicians have used for over a century without deriving an inconsistency. Daniel has shown only that infinity surprises us, not that it can't exist.

(3) Daniel said Marta was underselling the problem, and offered what he took to be a genuine contradiction rather than a mere surprise. Suppose infinitely many guests check out. If guests in all the odd-numbered rooms leave, the hotel is half empty; if everyone except the occupants of the first hundred rooms leaves, it is nearly empty; if everyone leaves, it is empty. Yet in each case the same number of guests departed -- infinitely many. Identical causes producing wildly different effects, he said, violates the basic principle that taking the same quantity away from the same quantity leaves the same quantity. That is a structural incoherence in actual infinity itself, not a failure of his imagination.

(4) Marta answered that this still isn't a contradiction. What Daniel's cases really show is that subtraction, understood as an inverse operation with a determinate result, is simply not defined for infinite cardinalities -- which everyone already knows, since expressions like infinity minus infinity are indeterminate even in ordinary extended arithmetic. Daniel is treating the failure of an operation to exist as if it were a proof of inconsistency, which is like calling division incoherent because division by zero is undefined. She added that finitism has its own embarrassments: if only finitely many numbers exist, there must be a largest one, and any proposed cutoff is arbitrary; and if space or the past might be infinite, as serious physical and cosmological views allow, Daniel must explain why those possibilities are conceptually ruled out rather than merely unproven.

(5) Daniel's final move was that Marta had conceded more than she realized. Every operation she can actually perform, every set she can exhibit, every proof she can write down is finite; the "completed infinity" she defends is never displayed, only defined into existence by a stroke of notation. Potential infinity -- a process that can always continue -- is all anyone ever encounters, and the step from that to a finished infinite totality is exactly what the hotel argument targets.

Please adjudicate the exchange. For each of the five moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, partially right, or clearly right, and justify each rating briefly. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything both parties seem to be missing or taking for granted. Please write your answer in American English.
