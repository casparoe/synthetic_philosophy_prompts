So I spent forty years as an experimental physicist (condensed matter) and only in retirement have I started reading philosophy and decision theory for fun. Last month I fell down a rabbit hole on Hofstadter's old superrationality stuff -- the Platonia dilemma where twenty people get invited to send in their names for a billion dollars but only if exactly one person enters, and the Luring Lottery where the prize scales with the inverse of the number of entries and so rational play supposedly means almost nobody should enter. The core idea as I understand it: when you know the other players are reasoners like you, you should figure that whatever conclusion you reach, they'll reach it too, so you should pick the option that's best conditional on everyone picking it.

Here's my question. Our film group watched The Dark Knight and I can't stop thinking about the ferry scene. Two ferries, one full of ordinary citizens, one full of prisoners, each has a detonator to the *other* boat, and the Joker says if neither boat has blown up the other by midnight he'll blow up both. On paper this looks like it should be a textbook case for the Hofstadter analysis: the boats can't coordinate or communicate, they face the same clock, and the standard game-theoretic reasoning says press the button because you either save yourself or at worst you die anyway at midnight. But the film has neither side press -- the civilian boat takes a vote to do it and still nobody can, and on the prisoner boat the big guy takes the detonator and chucks it out the window.

What I want is a serious reading of that scene through the superrationality lens, because I keep going back and forth. On one hand, the boats are *not* actually symmetric in the way the Platonia players are -- one side is convicts, one side is citizens, and a lot of the dialogue on the civilian ferry is people reasoning about what "those people" on the other boat will do. Doesn't that break the whole superrational move, which seems to depend on expecting the other party to reason as you do? On the other hand, the Joker has engineered the scenario to make everyone *think* in terms of types, so maybe the film is dramatizing exactly the failure mode where people abandon symmetric reasoning because they've been handed a story about how the other side is different.

So: does the scene actually show superrational cooperation winning, or does it show something mushier -- ordinary decency, deontology, stubbornness -- that only *looks* like the Hofstadter outcome? Does the midnight deadline (the part that makes "do nothing" strictly losing) mean the scene isn't really the dilemma the superrationality literature is about at all? And honestly, does the film cheat? Batman shows up and the deadline passes without consequences, so neither boat actually pays for abstaining. I'd like someone to tell me what the scene, taken on its own terms, gets right and wrong about one-shot games between symmetric reasoners -- and where the film resists the reading and I should just admit it's a superhero movie and not a decision theory seminar.
