Ok so I run a small board game club and we play a lot of Hex. For anyone rusty: two players take turns placing stones on a rhombus of hexagons, each trying to connect their two opposite sides; draws are impossible, and there's a classic argument (usually credited to Nash) that the first player must have a winning strategy, since an extra stone on the board can never hurt you — if the second player had a winning strategy, the first player could make some arbitrary opening move and then "steal" that strategy, treating the extra stone as irrelevant. Notably, the argument proves a winning strategy exists without telling you what it actually is.

Here's what I keep chewing on: at our club we use the swap rule — after the very first move, the second player may switch colors and take over the first player's position. The standard justification is that this neutralizes the first-player advantage: the opener should place a stone just strong enough that they're happy to keep it but not so strong the opponent swaps.

My question: what does adopting the swap rule actually presuppose about the strategy-stealing argument and about what kind of "advantage" matters in a game? Does the rule only make sense if you treat a non-constructive existence proof as settling something real about fairness, or would a purely practical notion of balance (say, measured win rates among human players of different strengths) license the exact same rule — meaning the practice is underdetermined between those views? If someone published a constructive winning strategy tomorrow, would the swap rule become pointless, or even more important? And does the rule smuggle in the monotonicity premise (an extra stone never hurts)? In games where that fails — where an extra piece can hurt you, zugzwang-style — would an analogous swap rule lose its justification, or does it stand on different ground entirely?
