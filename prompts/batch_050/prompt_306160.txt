After forty years in experimental fluid dynamics, I’ve retired and somehow become the volunteer interviewer for our local philosophy circle. Next week’s guest is an epistemic game theorist defending a view she calls public justifiability. The rough idea is that ordinary common knowledge of rationality only says that everyone is rational, everyone knows this, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so forth. Her stronger view says that a choice is admissible only if the player could publicly defend it as instrumentally rational, publicly defend the beliefs used in that defense, and continue doing so recursivly. The test also has to apply at decision points that her theory predicts will never actually be reached.

Draft nine interview questions that go beyond asking her to repeat that definition. Each entry should contain the main question, the answer she is most likely to give in two or three sentences, and then a sharper follow-up. Across the set, please cover at least the following:

- what public justifiability adds to common knowledge of rationality, beyond merely making the hierarchy of knowledge especially strict;
- a finite centipede game, including whether her view really does force the first player to end the game immediately;
- how players should reason after witnessing a supposedly inadmissible deviation, without simply saying that the impossible has happened;
- whether the view smuggles in forward induction while presenting itself as something more basic;
- whether it makes too many actions inadmissible by treating every unexpected move as evidence of irrationality rather than caution, confusion, or an unusual preference;
- the difference between a public announcement that creates a shared reason and one that merely creates shared information;
- bounded agents who cannot actually survey an unlimited chain of public defenses;
- what sort of experimental result would put real pressure on the view, rather than just showing that ordinary people are noisy.

Coming from the lab, I keep hearing her view as a stability condition: announce the intended line of play, perturb it with a hypothetical deviation, and see whether the justification settles back down. Include one question that tests whether she accepts that analogy or thinks it mistakes a static standard of justification for a dynamic process.

Also include one question built around a very small two-player game of your own invention, with simple numerical payoffs, rather than relying entirely on centipede or another stock example. Make the questions probing but courteous; I want friction, not a gotcha session.
