I've followed the decision-theory corners of the rationalist internet for years, and the debate over Kavka's toxin puzzle still goes in circles. For reference: an eccentric billionaire offers you a fortune if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon — the toxin will make you miserably ill for a day but won't kill you, and whether you actually drink it is irrelevant to the payout, which depends only on the sincerity of your midnight intention. One camp insists a rational agent can win the money by simply resolving at midnight to drink, even while fully foreseeing that come afternoon she'll have no reason to follow through. The other camp insists you cannot rationally intend to do what you already know you'll have no reason to do, so the prize is unreachable by any rational agent.

Write the joint statement that these two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Specifically, I want:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — including what the puzzle actually tests, where both sides agree the naive "just decide and collect" framing goes wrong, and whatever propositions about the relationship between rational intention and reasons for action both camps can endorse without hedging.

2. A disagreements table stating the residual disputes, each row phrased so that the camp whose view it describes would accept the wording as accurate and complete — no slanted formulations.

3. A cruxes section: for each crux, the case, variant, or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. As candidate test-beds, address at least: a variant where the payout is determined by a brain scan at midnight that detects the intention directly; a variant where the toxin is replaced by something merely unpleasant rather than harmful, like drinking a glass of vinegar; and the structural parallels with Ulysses binding himself against the sirens and with nuclear deterrence, where an intention to do something arguably irrational at execution time seems both formable and effective. Wherever a side claims a variant changes the verdict, write down its prediction and its reasoning.

Throughout, separate disagreements that are genuine from disagreements about words — for instance, whether "intending" requires believing you will actually do the thing, or merely being set on doing it — and mark any dispute that dissolves under disambiguation as dissolved rather than resolved.

Begin with the joint statement itself, with no preface or commentary.
