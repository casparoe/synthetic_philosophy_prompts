I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in applied ethics (about 16 students, one 80-minute session), and I want to build a class around something that happened on our own campus last spring: the dining services office, with much fanfare, replaced most of its beef dishes with chicken, issued a press release about reducing its "animal footprint," and received a sustainability award from a student group. When I brought this up in seminar, every single student nodded along -- the intuition that white meat is the kinder choice is apparently bedrock. I would like an in-class activity that dislodges this intuition by making the students run into the numbers themselves, before I say anything about them. Some seeding for your design: a slaughtered steer yields on the order of two hundred kilograms of meat, several hundred thousand calories, whereas a broiler chicken yields a kilogram or two, a few thousand calories -- so holding calories fixed, switching from beef to chicken means killing well over a hundred times as many animals. And the plant-based option is not a free pass either: plowing, harvesting machinery, and pest control in intensive crop production kill large numbers of small wild animals (field mice and the like) per hectare, to the point that some published estimates suggest pasture-raised beef may cause fewer total animal deaths per calorie than grains do. Students should not hear these figures from me up front.

Please design the activity accordingly. I imagine something like a staged deliberation -- the class acts as a dining committee given a fixed calorie budget and a mandate to minimize harm, equipped with yield cards, per-hectare harvest data, and whatever props or handouts you think necessary -- but I am open to a different structure if you can make it land harder. What matters is that the rules and materials force the group to construct the comparison themselves: they should choose the "compassionate" option, tally the consequences, and only then notice that their preferred policy multiplies the death count, and that even the vegan corner of the menu is soaked in field mortality they had never pictured. Specify the rules, roles, materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline, including how to keep the session to 80 minutes when the arguments inevitably break out.

Finally, give me a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the activity onto the conceptual distinctions I want on the board afterward: deaths per animal versus deaths per calorie, intentional slaughter versus incidental field deaths and whether that difference does moral work, differences in sentience and welfare across species, and the replaceability wrinkle (the farmed animals would not exist absent demand, the field mice would). Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- I suspect students will either dismiss wild-animal deaths as accidental and therefore irrelevant, or retreat to per-life quality-of-suffering arguments to rescue the chicken switch -- and how to handle it. And be frank about which features of the real ethical problem the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in lecture: for instance, the deep uncertainty in field-mortality estimates, and the possibility that wild animals' lives are net-negative in a way farmed animals' are not.
