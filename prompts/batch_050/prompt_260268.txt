Here's the question I set: "State the four conditions of Nozick's truth-tracking account of knowledge. Then explain how the account responds to the brain-in-a-vat skeptical argument, and what the view implies about the closure principle for knowledge. Be explicit about which condition does the work in each step."

And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Nozick's account replaces the traditional justification condition with two counterfactual conditions. S knows that p iff: (1) p is true; (2) S believes that p; (3) if p were not true, S would not believe that p (the sensitivity condition); and (4) if p were true, S would believe that p (the adherence condition). Conditions (3) and (4) are subjunctive conditionals, evaluated with the method S actually used to form the belief held fixed.

The BIV argument says: you don't know that you're not a brain in a vat, and if you don't know that, you don't know that you have hands. Nozick's reply is that the belief 'I am not a BIV' fails the adherence condition: nothing in your experience would reveal the difference between the two scenarios, so the belief doesn't vary with the truth in the way the account requires. Ordinary beliefs like 'I have hands' pass all four conditions, so we do know them, and the skeptic's mistake is assuming that knowing the ordinary proposition requires first knowing the denial of the skeptical hypothesis.

This is also where the view's treatment of closure comes in. Nozick preserves closure: because the method is held fixed when the counterfactuals are evaluated, knowing p and knowing that p entails q is enough to know q, provided you deduce q using the same method. So the account blocks the skeptical argument without giving up the principle that knowledge is closed under known entailment, which Nozick treats as a desideratum any adequate theory of knowledge has to meet."
