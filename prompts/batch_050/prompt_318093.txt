Here's the pattern, in three pieces:

1. The ranking is (as far as I can tell) built on asking people to rate their life on a ladder from best possible to worst possible. That's not "how do you feel," it's "how does your life compare to the best life you can picture." So the thing being measured is relative to an internal benchmark.

2. When a country genuinely improves stuff — incomes, healthcare, safety — the score often barely moves, because the benchmark moves too. People's picture of the "best possible life" inflates as their surroundings improve. My grandmother on a 1960s salary would rate her life differently than the same material conditions would score today. So real progress can be invisible in the metric.

3. Meanwhile, a government that wants to climb the ranking has two routes: actually improve lives, or work on the answering behavior — national pride campaigns, framing, expectations management. The second one moves the number without moving anyone's Tuesday afternoon. And unlike, say, GDP, there's no external check, because the metric IS the self-report.

So the loop is: the measure depends on a reference point, the reference point drifts with the very conditions the measure is supposed to track, and once the measure becomes a policy target, you can game it by moving the reference point directly instead of moving the underlying conditions.

Now, I know about Goodhart's law ("when a measure becomes a target..."), and this feels related but not identical. Goodhart is about the correlation breaking under optimization pressure — but here there's this extra ingredient where the measurement scale itself is anchored to a moving internal standard, so the measure can fail to track the underlying thing even with zero gaming, purely because people recalibrate. It also feels adjacent to the "hedonic treadmill" idea, but that's about actual felt happiness resetting, whereas I'm talking about the *report* resetting even if daily felt experience genuinely improved.
