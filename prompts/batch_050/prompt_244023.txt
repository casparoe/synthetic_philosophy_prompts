**The Missing Encoder: A Physicist's Grumble about the Gödelian Analogy**

Hofstadter's big idea, as I understand it, is that self-awareness arises the same way Gödel's self-referential sentence arises: a system rich enough to talk about things can be made to talk about itself, and once the loop closes, something qualitatively new pops out. Formal systems get their strange loop via Gödel numbering; brains get theirs via symbols that represent the brain's own activity. The analogy is beautiful. I want to believe it. But there's a step that bothers me, and I haven't seen it addressed in the chapters I read (maybe it's in there and I missed it).

In Gödel's construction, there is an encoder. The mapping between symbols and numbers, the trick by which a string of symbols comes to also mean something about arithmetic, is imposed from outside. Gödel stands outside the system and assigns the numbering. The system itself does not choose to be about itself; we read it that way because we hold the codebook. A string in Principia Mathematica is, considered purely internally, just a string. The self-reference lives in the interpretation, and the interpreter is us.

Now: who holds the codebook for the brain? If my self-model is a Gödel sentence, what plays the role of the Gödel numbering? Presumably the answer is that the brain encodes itself, that no external interpreter is needed because the neural symbols do causal work inside the system regardless of how any outsider reads them. Fair enough. But then the analogy has quietly shifted ground. The Gödel sentence's power comes precisely from the fact that its meaning is fixed by an external scheme while its truth eludes the system internally. If the brain's self-reference needs no external scheme, then whatever the brain is doing, it is not doing the Gödel trick. It might be doing something better -- something more like what I would call auto-calibration, the way a feedback amplifier senses its own output without needing anyone to interpret the signal. But auto-calibrating amplifiers are not conscious, as far as anyone knows, so the analogy stops explaining the thing it was brought in to explain.

Here's another way to put my worry, in physicist's terms. Gödel's construction is like a measurement where the measuring apparatus is outside the system. Self-awareness is supposed to be a measurement where the apparatus is inside. These are famously different situations -- ask anyone who has thought about quantum measurement -- and it is not obvious that results from the first transfer to the second. Hofstadter's strange loop seems to me to straddle the two cases without acknowledging the seam.

I want to be clear about what I am not saying. I am not saying the brain cannot model itself; of course it can. I am not saying formal self-reference is impossible without an external encoder; a quine manages it, in a way, though even a quine needs a language to be quined in. I am saying that the specific route by which Gödel's sentence achieves self-reference -- external encoding plus diagonalization -- may be the wrong template for minds, and that the analogy's appeal comes from glossing over who does the encoding.

If that is right, then the interesting question for a theory of consciousness is not "how does the loop close?" but "what does a self-encoding system buy you that a merely self-measuring one does not?" And I suspect the answer will have more to do with the encoder -- with whatever plays the Gödel-numbering role internally -- than with the loop itself.
