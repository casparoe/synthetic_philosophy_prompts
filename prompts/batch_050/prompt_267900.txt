I work on the data team at a fitness-app company, and last week two of my colleagues, Dana and Marcus, got into a dispute during an experiment review that I later rehashed at length with a friend over the weekend; the two of us ended up on opposite sides and could not settle it, so I would like a neutral adjudication. We had run a randomized experiment on a new onboarding flow: a thousand new users saw the new flow, a thousand saw the old, and thirty-day retention came out to 50% under the new flow versus 39% under the old. Dana's position (1) was that the new flow wins and we should ship it, end of story. Marcus then pulled up the same data split by whether the user had completed a workout in their first 24 hours (2): among first-day exercisers, retention was 70% under the new flow versus 75% under the old; among everyone else, 20% versus 30%. The old flow, he argued, beats the new one within both groups, so the aggregate advantage is an artifact: the new flow merely pushed far more people into the first-day-exerciser group (600 of 1000 versus 200 of 1000), and a pattern like that in the numbers is a warning sign, not a reason to ship. Dana replied (3) that Marcus was conditioning on a downstream consequence of the treatment — the extra first-day workouts are precisely the mechanism by which the new flow works — so splitting on that variable compares different populations in each row and discards exactly the effect we care about; in a randomized experiment the overall comparison is the unbiased one. Marcus countered (4) that even granting the mechanism story, the within-group rows show the new flow retaining strictly fewer users conditional on the same early behavior, meaning at best it wins by relocating people across groups rather than making anyone more likely to stay, and at worst it is actively harmful to the users it fails to activate; he wanted the launch blocked pending an analysis split by pre-experiment activity levels, which he conceded were balanced across arms given the random assignment.

Please adjudicate the dispute as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: items 1 through 4 should rate each numbered move above from clearly mistaken to clearly correct with a brief justification, and item 5 should state who is more right overall and what, if anything, both colleagues are missing.
