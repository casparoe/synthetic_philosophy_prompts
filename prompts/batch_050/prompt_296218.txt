A friend of mine who did a philosophy minor summarized the debate between counterfactual and interventionist theories of causation for me, and I want to know how much of it to trust before I cite it in something I'm writing. Here's what he said:

"The old counterfactual theory, associated with David Lewis, says that A causes B just means if A hadn't happened, B wouldn't have happened. This theory was basically killed by preemption cases -- like when two assassins both shoot at the target and the first one's bullet gets there first. Since the target would have died anyway from the second bullet, the first shot doesn't count as a cause on the counterfactual definition, which is obviously wrong, so the theory is refuted. Interventionism, developed by James Woodward, fixes this by defining causation in terms of manipulations: A causes B if intervening on A (while holding everything else fixed) would change B. The catch people usually raise is that an intervention requires a human agent who could in principle wiggle A, so the theory can't say anything about causes no one could ever manipulate, like events in the early universe or inside black holes. Also, interventionists admit their definition is circular, since 'intervention' is itself a causal notion, which is generally taken to be a fatal problem for a theory that was supposed to analyze causation. The upshot is that interventionism is really a theory for scientists doing experiments, not a metaphysical account of what causation is, whereas the counterfactual theory, whatever its flaws, at least tried to tell us what causation consists in."

How fair is this? I'm inclined to think it's roughly right since it matches the vibe of what I've read elsewhere, but a couple of claims feel too confident. Go through it claim by claim and tell me: what a specialist in the metaphysics of causation would accept, what they'd say is a subtle distortion (and of what -- is something being conflated or overstated?), and what's just wrong. In particular I'm suspicious of the 'refuted' framing, the bit about interventions needing humans, and the 'fatal circularity' point, but I don't know enough to say why. And does my friend's final upshot -- interventionism as a mere methodology for experimentalists rather than a theory of causation -- actually follow from anything true in the summary, or is he smuggling in an assumption?
