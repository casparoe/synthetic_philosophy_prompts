My roommate is in a decision theory reading group and came home last week with an argument he's very pleased with, built off the Death in Damascus case. Quick version of the case in case it's fuzzier for you than for him: Death makes an appointment with you and writes the location down in advance, Death is an extremely good predictor of what you'll do, you see Death in Damascus looking surprised to see you (or you hear the appointment exists, versions differ), and you can either stay in Damascus tonight or flee to Aleppo. Here's his argument. (1) Death writes down the appointment location before you decide anything, so at the moment of decision the location is fixed. (2) Since Death predicts you near-perfectly, whichever option you pick, the appointment turns out to be where you end up: stay and it's Damascus, flee and it's Aleppo. (3) A choice where the bad outcome happens no matter which option you take is a choice that makes no difference to whether the bad outcome happens. (4) Deliberation is only worth doing when your choice can make a difference to the outcome. (5) So in Death in Damascus, deliberating is a waste of time, and you might as well flip a coin or just stay put. (6) But nothing in steps 1 through 5 required Death to be literally perfect, just very good, and ordinary life is full of very good predictors: actuaries, hiring algorithms, your spouse, recommendation engines that know what you'll click. (7) So whenever you're being predicted well enough, deliberation is pointless and you might as well not bother. That conclusion is obviously nuts, but I genuinely cant find the step to kill. My instinct is something goes wrong around (3), where "no matter which option you take" seems to slide between two different things, but when I push on it I keep ending up at the boring answer "well, causally, the book is already written, so your choice really doesn't change where Death is waiting" and then the argument looks fine again. Which step do you actually reject, and what does it cost to reject it? If I have to give something up I'd much rather sacrifice (6) than admit that being predictable makes thinking useless, but I want to know whether the flaw is really there or earlier. Also relevant, maybe: my roommate claims this argument shows Death in Damascus is a dumber problem than Newcomb, because at least Newcomb has a real choice in it, and I'd love ammunition against that.

Please write your answer in American English, and just write it as continuous prose like an email back to me -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. I'd rather you walk through the argument step by step in sentences than hand me an outline.
