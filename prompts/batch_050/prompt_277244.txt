I teach a second-year ethics seminar, 18 students, one 75-minute slot near the end of our unit on contractualism. We've read the material where Scanlon builds morality around principles nobody could reasonably reject, and the class thinks they get it, but when we hit the aggregation problem they just nod along -- they can state that contractualism compares complaints one person at a time and that this has trouble with saving the many, but nobody feels why that's a problem rather than a feature. Design me an in-class activity that fixes this.

What I want: some kind of structured game or staged decision where students are holding complaints of different severities (secret cards? assigned roles? your call) and the group has to actually run the contractualist procedure -- propose a principle, let anyone object, compare objections -- on cases I feed them. Start them on easy distributive cases where the pairwise-complaint rule works fine and feels obviously right, then land them in a case of the form: one person dies under principle A, versus a huge number of people each suffer something trivial under principle B. The pairwise rule will tell them to save the one from death no matter how many headaches are on the other side, and I want at least some of them to balk at their own verdict in real time. Then ideally one more case that pulls the other way, where the aggregate intuition feels like the obviously crazy one, so they can't settle into a simple "numbers do/don't count" resolution.

Give me the full setup: the card/role mechanics, the exact rule for when a complaint "beats" another complaint (this is the crux -- how do you operationalize reasonable rejectability with 18 undergrads in a room without it collapsing into whoever argues loudest?), the sequence of cases with the numbers tuned so the trap actually springs, and a rough timeline. Then the debrief: which moments in the game map onto the individualist restriction, which onto the question of whether trivial complaints are even relevant when a serious one is on the table, and where Taurek-style vs Kamm-style positions would want to change the rules they just played by. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing: students start inflating their trivial complaints to win, and someone tries to bargain across rounds) and how to handle it. And be honest about what the game can't reproduce -- my worry is that the activity measures how much students *feel* their assigned burden, whereas the theory is about reasons, and I don't want them walking away thinking contractualism is just a complaint-loudness contest.
