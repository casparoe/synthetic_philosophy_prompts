I’m a high school senior in an intro philosophy elective and we hit Moore’s paradox this week: sentences like It is raining, but I do not believe it. Our teacher also made us read the Advertisement to Hume’s A Treatise of Human Nature, where Hume snarks: "Nothing is more usual and more natural for those, who pretend to discover anything new to the world in philosophy and the sciences, than to insinuate the praises of their own systems, by decrying all those, which have been advanced before them." I keep wondering if Moore’s paradox is a real discovery about belief/assertion, or if philosophers are doing exactly the Hume thing—acting like a weird sentence breaks common sense so their “new” explanation looks deep. That Hume quote should actually matter in the dialogue, not just sit at the top.

Act like a careful philosophy tutor who is good at making both sides smart, then write a dialogue (max 700 words; the Hume quote doesn’t count) between two equally competent classmates, Noor and Eli. Noor argues the paradox is mostly pragmatic/speech-act-y: the sentence can be true, but sincerely asserting it normally communicates or presents belief, so it becomes unassertable rather than contradictory. Eli argues the problem is more first-person and doxastic: in the normal case judging that it is raining just is believing it is, so the speaker is trying to report a perspective they cannot coherently occupy. Brainstorm the cruxes privately, then give only the dialogue. Make them answer each other’s actual points, test cases like third-person versions (It is raining but Sam does not believe it), past-tense self-reports, lying/acting, whispering to yourself, and I believe it is raining, but it is not. Have each one, at least once, use Hume’s warning to accuse the other of inflating a verbal point into a “discovery,” and end with them saying precisely whether the disagreement is substantive, partly verbal, or both—no strawmen, no citations, definately no bibliography.
