Three months ago I became the director of a mid-sized philanthropic foundation, and I inherited a strategic fight. Two of our senior advisors — one a security studies scholar, one an economic historian — disagree about which historical episode should anchor our grantmaking on transformative AI. The first insists the closest analogy is nuclear weapons: a small number of actors, a technology whose control turned on state-level agreements, verification regimes, and the physical bottleneck of fissile material. The second insists the closest analogy is the printing press: a technology whose transformative effects came precisely from its unstoppable diffusion, and whose governance lesson is that adaptation and institutional redesign, not control, did all the real work. Each considers the other's analogy not merely wrong but actively dangerous as a guide to funding, and their disputes have started stalling our grant cycle.

I want to commission from them an adversarial collaboration, and I need a model of the joint statement it should produce if conducted in good faith. Draft that statement. A few requirements:

Both advisors have already agreed, as ground rules, that a historical analogy can be doing three different jobs — a forecasting job (predicting how the technology will develop and spread), a governance job (indicating what control or adaptation regimes are feasible), and a mobilizing job (framing the issue for policymakers and the public) — and that an analogy can succeed at one job while failing at another. The statement must keep these separate throughout, and neither side may score points by winning on one job while losing on another.

The statement should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered list of claims both advisors can sign — about what actually happened in the nuclear and printing press episodes, about which features of those episodes do and do not carry over to AI, and about what a historical analogy can and cannot establish in principle. These must be substantive enough that each side gives something up, not banalities like 'history is complicated'.

2. A disagreements section in which each residual dispute is stated once, in neutral wording, followed by each side's position phrased the way that side would phrase it. I expect at least these disputes to appear: whether the success of nuclear nonproliferation was owed mainly to the physical bottleneck of fissile material (which AI lacks) or mainly to the geopolitical structure of the Cold War (which might be approximated); whether the printing press case shows that diffusion-driven transformation defeats control efforts in general, or only that it defeated the particular control efforts then attempted; and whether choosing an analogy is itself a policy act, given that the analogy chosen shapes what institutions get built.

3. For each dispute, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one advisor — and, for each crux, the historical evidence, case, or consideration both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction or reading recorded in advance.

4. A closing section stating which parts of their disagreement, if any, turned out to be verbal, and what each advisor would have to concede for the other's funding priorities to follow.

Write the statement in American English, in the formal register of a document both advisors could actually sign, and do not adjudicate between them — my board's decision depends on seeing the disagreement made exact, not on having it settled for us.
