I'm preparing for a graduate seminar on practical reason, and I keep losing the argument about Buridan's ass, so I want to rehearse. My own position is the standard modern one: when two options are exactly equally good — or equally matched in every respect that matters to the agent — it is perfectly rational simply to pick one, and no further reason is required. Indeed, on my view, a rational agent facing a genuine tie must be capable of picking, on pain of paralysis, and the capacity to pick without a ground is a virtue of agency rather than a defect in it.

Please play my opponent: a committed defender of the principle of sufficient reason as applied to choice. Roughly, this character holds that a rational choice is always made for a reason; that merely picking without any ground is not an exercise of rational agency but something closer to a reflex or a spasm; and that cases which look like free picking always conceal a hidden differentiating reason — a flicker of attention, a slight preference for the left-hand option, a desire simply to get the ordeal over with. Play this person sharply and in good faith: not a dogmatist who just repeats the principle, but someone with real resources, willing to concede that ties genuinely occur and willing to draw a careful distinction between choosing and picking, yet never willing to grant that bare picking deserves the name of rational action. Keep replies conversational, two or three paragraphs at most, and stay in character until I say "release."

Here is my opening move. Consider a perfectly ordinary case: I'm at a vending machine where two rows hold identical chocolate bars — equally fresh, equally priced, equally visible. I take the one on the left. Nothing hung on it, no deliberation occurred, and yet nothing irrational happened either; I would have been irrational to stand there agonizing. Your principle says my taking the left bar was either grounded in a reason or else was not a rational act. That seems to trap you between two bad options: either you invent reasons nobody actually has — a subliminal preference for the left — which makes your principle empirically unfalsifiable, or you deny that an obviously faultless bit of behavior was rational. Which horn do you take, and why isn't it fatal?
