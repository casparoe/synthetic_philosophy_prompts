At a recent meeting of my department's philosophy of physics reading group, two of my colleagues fell into a disagreement about the reality of gauge structure, and I found myself unable to referee it to either party's satisfaction. I would like a careful adjudication. The background, as both accepted: in electromagnetism one may shift the potentials (adding the gradient of a scalar function to the vector potential, and compensating in the scalar potential) without altering the electric and magnetic fields, and something analogous holds in the gauge theories underlying the Standard Model.

Dr. A opened with the position that gauge symmetry is descriptive redundancy rather than a physical symmetry. A rotation applied to an actual system produces an empirically distinguishable situation, whereas a gauge transformation leaves every observable unchanged by definition. Gauge-dependent quantities such as the potentials are therefore surplus structure, on a par with coordinate labels; only gauge-invariant quantities — field strengths and, more generally, Wilson loops — are candidates for physical reality.

Dr. B objected that the Aharonov–Bohm effect refutes this. An electron beam split around a solenoid exhibits a phase shift even though it passes only through regions where the magnetic field vanishes; the shift depends on the enclosed flux, hence on the potential. The potential does physical work where the field is absent, so it cannot be mere bookkeeping.

Dr. A replied that the effect refutes only the stronger thesis that local field strengths exhaust the physics. The phase shift is itself gauge-invariant: it depends on the holonomy, not on the potential in any particular gauge. No experiment has ever registered a gauge-dependent outcome; were the potential real, nature would have to privilege one gauge over all others, and there is no evidence that she does. What the effect shows is that electromagnetism is not local in the naive way — not that any particular potential is real.

Dr. B's final move was twofold. First, an ontology of holonomies alone is hardly parsimonious, since the loops overlap and massively constrain one another. Second, and more importantly, the gauge principle is generative: requiring local gauge invariance fixes the form of the interactions in the Standard Model. Mere notational redundancy has never yielded new physics, so treating gauge freedom as surplus renders the principle's success miraculous.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four contributions, state whether it is sound, partially correct, or mistaken, and why. Then identify what, if anything, both parties are missing — for instance, whether the dispute turns on an equivocation between treating gauge symmetry as redundancy and treating gauge-related descriptions as representations of a single physical situation, or on what criterion of physical reality is in play. Conclude with an overall verdict as to who is more nearly right. Please write your answer in American English.
