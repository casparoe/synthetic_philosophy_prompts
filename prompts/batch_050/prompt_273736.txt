I help run the interview segment for a small podcast that grew out of our local rationalist reading group -- most of our listeners came up through LessWrong and the usual blogs, so they know their way around Solomonoff induction, Kolmogorov complexity, and Martin-Löf randomness, and they have zero patience for a guest being allowed to gesture at "incompressibility" without being pinned down. Next month we're interviewing a philosopher of probability (a composite rather than a named figure) who defends a position I find genuinely interesting but under-defended: that a single, actually existing finite sequence -- this particular string of coin-flip outcomes, say -- can be objectively random in itself, with randomness explicated as incompressibility relative to some fixed universal Turing machine, rather than randomness belonging only to the generating process (as the frequentists have it) or only to infinite sequences (as in Martin-Löf's framework). Act as an expert philosopher of probability and mathematics who has pre-interviewed many guests on this cluster of views. Think carefully about where the position is actually load-bearing, and begin by brainstorming, in a few bullet points, the half-dozen pressure points in the literature and in the intuitive pre-theoretic notion that this view must address.

Then draft eight to ten interview questions I can use, in an order that builds pressure progressively rather than jumping around. The questions should go well beyond having the guest restate the incompressibility account. In particular, I want the set to include: at least one question on the machine-dependence problem -- the invariance theorem buys you only an additive constant, which is cold comfort for short strings, so in what sense can a fifty-bit string be objectively anything; at least one on uncomputability -- if Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable, does it follow that a string's randomness is in principle unknowable, and is that a bullet to bite or a reductio; at least one on the all-heads case -- twenty consecutive heads looks maximally patterned yet has the same probability as any other outcome of a fair process, so what exactly does the view say about that string, and does the answer conflate randomness of the sequence with randomness of its source; and at least one on whether the whole framework quietly collapses into subjective Bayesian talk, since choosing a universal machine looks uncomfortably like choosing a prior. For each question, give me the answer the guest is most likely to give, and then a follow-up specifically engineered to make the standard reply -- some version of "that's just the price of having an objective notion" -- harder to lean on. Close the set with one question about what result or argument would make the guest abandon the view, since our listeners trust guests who can name their cruxes. Avoid anything generic about the guest's career or motivations; every question should turn on the content of the position.
