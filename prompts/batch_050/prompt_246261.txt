Construct an experiment that would behaviorally discriminate between three of Dennett's kinds of creatures -- the Skinnerian, the Popperian, and the Gregorian -- using a single novel problem-solving environment. The constraints: the setup must be realistic enough to run with actual animals or humans (no hypothetical brain scanners or stipulated inner lives, since the whole difficulty is that these kinds are defined by cognitive machinery rather than by behavior, and the discrimination must therefore be engineered); the environment must be novel to the subjects, so that no creature can succeed by Darwinian means alone, i.e., by relying on instincts shaped by selection; and a single trial structure must suffice, so that differences emerge in the pattern of attempts rather than only in aggregate learning curves. Then demonstrate that the specification is met: state, for each of the three kinds, the signature behavior the environment is designed to elicit and the mechanism that produces it -- reinforcement-driven trial and error, hypothesis-testing via internal simulation, or exploitation of culturally transmitted tools and representations. Identify which feature of your environment does the discriminating work and what happens to each prediction if that feature is removed. Finally, give the strongest objection from someone who holds that the Skinnerian-Popperian boundary is not behaviorally detectable at all -- that anything your Popperian creature does can be redescribed as covert reinforcement learning -- and say how, if at all, your design blocks that redescription.
