I teach a second-year undergraduate course in formal philosophy, and we're spending two weeks on social choice theory. Next week we get to Arrow's impossibility theorem, and in past years I've found that students can state the conditions — unrestricted domain, Pareto efficiency, independence of irrelevant alternatives, non-dictatorship — and even follow the proof sketch, but treat the whole thing as a curiosity about voting trivia rather than a result with real bite. They nod along and then, ten minutes later, happily propose "just use ranked ballots" as though that obviously settles things. So for the first session on the topic, I want to run an in-class activity with about 24 students and 75 minutes that makes them collide with the theorem themselves before I ever state it. The idea is that they should feel the impossibility: I want them to design, propose, and defend collective decision procedures in small groups, and then watch those procedures break — majority cycles, agenda manipulation, the winner flipping when an irrelevant option is removed, someone quietly becoming a dictator — under conditions they themselves agreed were reasonable. Ideally every group ends the session having been hoist by at least one of Arrow's conditions without knowing the names for what happened to them.

Please design the full activity: the concrete decision problem the class faces (it should feel real enough that they care about the outcome — not an abstract A/B/C ranking exercise, but something with genuine stakes within the classroom, like allocating something scarce or choosing something they'll actually have to live with for the rest of the term), the rules and phases of the game, what materials I need, how the groups and any asymmetric roles (agenda-setters, tie-breakers, people who get to eliminate options) are assigned, and a payoff or incentive structure that doesn't involve real money but makes students want their preferred outcome to win rather than just observe. Crucially, I want the failure to be engineered into the structure rather than narrated by me: the activity should be set up so that whichever procedure a group converges on, the next phase forces a choice profile or a rule change that exposes one of the Arrow conditions being violated. That probably means you need to design the candidate ranking profiles or choice situations carefully, so give me the actual profiles and explain why each one triggers the failure it's meant to trigger. Then give me a debrief plan: how to map what happened in each phase onto Arrow's conditions and the theorem, how long to leave the class sitting in the confusion before naming it, and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (students colluding, someone spotting the trick early, the stakes falling flat, a group accidentally inventing a dictatorship and being fine with it) and how to handle each failure mode. Also be frank about which aspect of the theorem a classroom game like this cannot reproduce — I suspect the full generality of unrestricted domain is hard to dramatize — and tell me what I'll need to supply in lecture afterwards.

I don't want a menu of possible activities to choose between; give me one design, committed, with definite choices about the decision problem, the payoff scheme, and the sequence of phases, and defend those choices — if you think a role-play beats a scoring game here, or that the stakes should be fabricated rather than real, say so and say why. Where the design leans on a claim about what Arrow's theorem does or doesn't establish (for instance, whether it applies to deliberation and not just aggregation), flag it rather than smoothing it over.
