I'm a TA grading intro modern philosophy exams and I'm drowning in them, so I want a second opinion on one. The exam question was:

"Hume gives two definitions of 'cause' in Section VII of the Enquiry. State both definitions, explain why they seem to pull apart rather than say the same thing, and then explain what the so-called 'New Hume' interpretation says about Hume's view of necessary connection in objects."

Here's the student's answer:

"Hume's first definition: a cause is an object followed by another, where all objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second. This is the regularity or constant conjunction definition, including temporal priority and contiguity. His second definition: a cause is an object followed by another, such that the appearance of the first conveys the mind to the idea of the second. This second definition is stated in terms of what happens in the observer's mind rather than in the objects. Hume intends these as two ways of saying the same thing, since both ultimately reduce causation to regular succession; the difference between them is merely verbal, the first describing the regularity and the second describing it as experienced by us.

As for necessary connection, Hume argues that since no single instance of cause and effect gives us an impression of any power or tie between them, the idea of necessary connection must come from an internal impression, namely the feeling of the mind's determination to pass from the cause to the effect after many repetitions. Because this impression is internal, Hume concludes that necessity is something we project onto the world and that there is no necessary connection in the objects themselves.

The New Hume interpretation, associated with Galen Strawson and others, holds that Hume was in fact a realist about causal power: there really is a necessary connection in nature, but we can have no knowledge of its nature. On this reading Hume's skepticism is epistemological rather than ontological. Opponents object that this sits badly with Hume's own account of where the idea of necessity comes from."
