I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about owning ideas, so I'm going to report my gut verdicts on a run of cases and ask you what principle, if any, they're verdicts *of*. Please don't just sort them by what current law says -- I want to know whether there's a coherent normative view here, and if there isn't, which of my verdicts I should give up.

1. Two inventors, unknown to each other, each spend years developing the same water-purification process. One files a patent application a month before the other. The second inventor -- who copied nothing and stole nothing -- is now barred from using or selling the fruits of her own work. My verdict: the system is defensible, maybe even necessary, but the individual outcome is genuinely unjust to her, not merely unlucky. I want both halves of that kept.

2. A restaurant across the street copies a rival's signature dish, down to the plating, and advertises it openly. My verdict: completely fine. That's competition, not theft.

3. A producer lifts a four-second drum break, unaltered and recognizable, from a 1970s recording, loops it as the backbone of a hit single, and never asks or pays. My verdict: not fine; he owes the original musician something.

4. At a work meeting, a colleague presents an idea I floated to him over coffee last week as his own, and gets credit for it. No law is broken and I still have the idea. My verdict: clearly wrong.

5. A novelist openly reworks the plot of an old folktale, changes the names and setting, and publishes it as her novel. My verdict: fine.

6. A company holds the patent on a drug and prices it far out of reach in the country where most people who need it live. My verdict: enforcing the patent there is wrong, but I don't think the company thereby forfeits the patent everywhere else, and I don't think an ordinary infringer in a rich country gets a free pass.

7. I mention an app idea to an acquaintance at a party; she builds it over the next year and makes a fortune. My verdict: she did nothing wrong, though I'd think her a bit shabby. Ideas mentioned casually at parties are up for grabs.

The pair I can't reconcile myself is (2) and (3): in both, someone takes a piece of another's creative work wholesale, without permission, for commercial gain -- and my verdicts split. I also notice that (4) is the case I feel most strongly about even though no property in any recognizable sense changed hands, which sits oddly with (1), where I accept that a real property right blocks an innocent person.

What I'd like from you: a principle -- or, if needed, a small family of principles, e.g. one for ownership and one for credit -- stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself, with the case or cases motivating each clause. Then its verdicts on two cases I haven't listed: (a) a fashion house sells a stitch-for-stitch knockoff of an independent designer's runway piece, which the law mostly permits; (b) a comedian repeats, word for word and without attribution, a joke she heard at an open mic. Finally, if no principle can cover all seven verdicts without gerrymandering, tell me plainly which verdict you'd sacrifice and why, rather than bending the principle to save it.

Please end your response with a last line of the form \answer{...} containing a one-sentence statement of the winning principle, or, if you conclude no principle survives, the case whose verdict should be overturned.
