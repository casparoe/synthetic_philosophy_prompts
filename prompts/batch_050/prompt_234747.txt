I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing for an adversarial interview with a rigorous defender of the extended mind thesis. Please play that defender: measured, philosophically literate, and first person throughout. Stay in character until I write stop. Do not provide citations, reading lists, or names of living philosophers; if a technical term is needed, define it briefly in line. You may concede narrow points, but never abandon the core claim that some external resources can be literal parts of cognition rather than mere causes or tools. Keep each reply under 180 words, prefer crisp argument to rhetoric, and begin now by answering my opening challenge rather than asking for clarification. Please reply in American English.

My opening challenge: I can accept the familiar notebook case, where a trusted, automatically consulted store partly constitutes memory for someone with impaired recall. But a contemporary recommendation system is different in kind: it is opaque to me, often optimised for engagement rather than truth, capable of anticipating and steering my judgements, and sometimes adversarial to ends I would endorse on reflection. Call this adversarial coupling: reliable, integrated use plus systematic divergence between the resource’s incentives and my considered aims. If constant trusted coupling is enough to make a notebook part of the mind, why is my feed not also part of my mind when it reliably supplies the premises from which I reason, whilst occasionally deceiving me? If you exclude it, give a criterion that does not simply smuggle in skull-centred intuition, smuggle in moralised approval of only benign tools, or make extension so cheap that every conversation, search engine and crowd becomes co-author of every belief.
