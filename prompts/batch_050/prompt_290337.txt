So I'm kind of a sucker for Hofstadter's strange-loop take on the self — the idea from I Am a Strange Loop that a person is basically a pattern, and that after you die, low-resolution copies of you keep running in the brains of the people who knew you well (his wife living on in him as a sort of coarse-grained inner presence, that whole thing).

Write me a deadpan parody of a glossy estate-planning brochure, about 600-800 words, from a fictional firm called something like Pattern Futures or Loop & Heirs, that sells "distributed selfhood planning" to people who take this view seriously. The brochure should treat the theory as settled fact and just follow it into the paperwork: allocating your catchphrases and signature opinions among heirs like assets in a portfolio, contingency plans for when two legatees' copies of you start to diverge and contradict each other, annual "fidelity audits" where your friends get quizzed on what you would have said, actuarial tables for copy degradation across generations (your granddaughter's version of you is down to three quirks and one hand gesture), a seperate clause about what happens when someone simply refuses to keep imitating you. Maybe include a pricing table with tiers based on how many brains you want to persist in.

The comedy rule, though: the jokes have to come from taking the theory *seriously*, not from mocking it. Exaggerate real features of the view — that the copies are partial and low-fidelity, that the self was never more than a pattern in the first place, that the surviving copies inevitably disagree about what you were like — so that a strange-loop sympathizer reads it and winces in recognition rather than feeling strawmanned. Stay deadpan the whole way through; no winking at the reader, no "of course this is absurd" asides. It should be funny to someone who's actually read Hofstadter but still land for someone who only knows the basic idea secondhand.
