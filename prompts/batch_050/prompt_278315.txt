You're a poet who's also read thier fair share of philosophy of mind, and I want to close a seminar on personal identity with something special. Write me a villanelle — five tercets and a quatrain, the standard two refrains — in the voice of Dennett in his brain-in-a-vat story (brain removed, alive in a vat, steering the body by radio link; later a duplicate brain gets switched in and he can't tell the difference). Here's the constraint: one refrain should locate the self where the brain is, the other should locate it where the body's point of view is, so the way villanelle refrains keep trading places does the actual philosophical work — his answer to "where am I?" keeps flip-flopping as the story goes. Use the middle stanzas for the moments that make the flip-flopping happen (staring at his own brain floating in the tank, the switchover to the duplicate that changes nothing from the inside). In the closing quatrain the refrains finally meet, and the meeting has to land the real moral: not that one answer wins, but that the question was badly put. Someone who knows the essay should be able to tell which refrain is which without being told.

Take your time — sketch the stanza plan before committing to lines. Format: the villanelle first, then a section titled "Why I built it this way" (roughly 120 words) saying which refrain is which and what the final quatrain is doing.
