Historians who argue about the role of non-human forces — epidemics, climate shifts, volcanic eruptions — in human affairs have been stuck in roughly the same exchange for decades. One side, running from McNeill's Plagues and Peoples through Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel to recent work on the volcanic winter of 536, assigns microbes and climate real causal weight in explaining things like the fall of Rome or the conquest of the Americas. The other side replies that this is environmental determinism in new dress: it flattens politics, dissolves human responsibility, and conveniently lets conquerors off the hook by making smallpox the true conquistador. The first side answers that treating humans as the only causal agents in history is a humanist prejudice that ignores everything we know about ecology and disease. And so it goes. My hypothesis is that the dispute is not really about historical facts but about two things that keep getting conflated: what 'cause' means at different scales of explanation, and what 'agency' can intelligibly mean when predicated of a virus or a volcano. Is that the right diagnosis — a partly verbal dispute masking a genuine methodological one — or is there a substantive empirical disagreement underneath, for instance about how much of the variance in outcomes microbes actually explain? Diagnose the impasse using what each side actually claims, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a sharper distinction between causal contribution and moral responsibility, better comparative methods, a reframed question, or an argument that no progress should be expected.
