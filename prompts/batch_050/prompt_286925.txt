I'm designing a classroom instrument for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in moral philosophy, and I'd like you to build it: a "do you actually believe in standing to blame?" sorting quiz. The seminar's underlying question is whether the notion of standing—the idea that a person can be unentitled to blame someone even for a genuine wrong—picks out anything our case judgments really support, or whether it collapses under pressure. So the quiz must sort respondents by their verdicts on cases, not by which label they like, and it must be written so that someone with no background in the literature can complete it.

Requirements:

Ten questions, each a short concrete vignette (two to four sentences) followed by two or three answer options. No question may mention standing, use "hypocrisy" as a term of art, or name any position. Each answer option should state a verdict plus a one-sentence rationale in plain language, so that respondents are choosing the reasoning they endorse, not just the bottom line.

Across the ten vignettes, include at least: (i) one case of straightforward hypocrisy, where the blamer currently does the same thing they condemn; (ii) one case of a reformed wrongdoer condemning the very wrong they used to commit; (iii) one case where the blamer helped cause or encouraged the wrongdoing without being a hypocrite in the ordinary sense; (iv) one case where the wrong is arguably none of the blamer's business; (v) one case where the blamer condemns the same conduct far more harshly in one target than in another; and (vi) one case engineered to pull apart two hypotheses about why hypocrisy might undermine standing: that the hypocrite fails to apply their own professed standard to themselves, versus that the hypocrite claims a superior moral status over someone who is their equal. Vignettes should involve ordinary settings—family, workplace, local politics—not courtrooms or trolleys.

Then provide a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto at least these positions: the no-standing view (no one ever lacks the entitlement to blame; "who are you to talk?" is always a deflection rather than a real objection); the self-application or commitment account of hypocrisy; the equality-of-status account of hypocrisy; a complicity-based restriction on standing; a meddling-based restriction; and a reform-sensitive view on which past-but-genuinely-repudiated wrongdoing never costs standing. The key should also report one summary dimension the literature does not usually package: how many independent standing-restricting conditions the respondent's pattern commits them to, since the seminar's question is whether the concept survives at all.

Finally: flag any two positions your quiz cannot separate by case verdicts alone, and state what non-verdict question—about the point of blaming, or about what "who are you to talk?" literally claims—would be needed to separate them. Note any answer combination your key treats as unstable or self-undermining, and diagnose why. Write the whole instrument in American English, suitable for printing as a two-page handout.
