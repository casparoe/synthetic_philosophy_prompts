Having retired a few years ago from forty years in experimental fluid dynamics, I have been making my way through moral philosophy, and I recently joined a reading group on population ethics where a position called lexical threshold negative utilitarianism came up. As it was explained to me, the view holds that below some threshold of intensity, suffering can be traded off against pleasure and against lesser suffering in the ordinary aggregative way, but that suffering above the threshold — torture being the standard example — occupies a lexically prior tier, such that no quantity of mild suffering, however vast, and on some versions no quantity of pleasure, can compensate for a single instance of it. The motivation is to deny that for every case of torture there is some number of pinpricks or mild headaches whose prevention would matter more, which defenders of the view regard as a reductio of unconstrained aggregation.

At the group, one member defended the position with an analogy I have since encountered again in an essay on the subject, and it was plainly doing argumentative work. The moral ordering of outcomes, he said, works like the ordering of words in a dictionary. In a dictionary, every word beginning with a precedes every word beginning with b, no matter how long the words are; no lengthening of a b-word can move it ahead of an a-word. Likewise, the intensity of suffering fixes the tier, while the number of instances and their duration merely lengthen the word, so no accumulation of below-threshold suffering can outrank a single case of above-threshold suffering. He then deployed the analogy against the charge that the lexical view is arbitrary or unprincipled: dictionary order is a perfectly well-defined, consistent, and utterly familiar ordering, he argued, so the view is coherent, and its threshold is no more mysterious than the alphabet we all use without anxiety. Most of the group found this reassuring. I came home less sure, and I would like the analogy audited properly.

Concretely, I would be grateful for the following. First, an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the letter, of the word's length, of the ordering relation, and — this seems to me a gap — where pleasure is supposed to enter, since the analogy as stated orders sufferings among themselves but appears to say nothing about whether pleasure can offset suffering, which is half of the view. Second, the disanalogies, sorted into those that are superficial and those that bear on the conclusion: I have in mind that letters are discrete and crisply bounded while the intensity of pain presumably varies continuously, so that the alphabet gives the view sharp tier-boundaries the phenomenon itself may not supply; that the alphabet's boundaries are fixed by convention whereas the threshold must be stipulated or discovered, with a sorites problem lurking wherever it is drawn; and that a dictionary is an artifact designed for retrieval, so nothing follows from its structure about what matters. Third, a verdict on the dialectical situation: does the consistency of dictionary order actually answer the arbitrariness objection, or does it merely show the view can be stated without contradiction — which the serious critics presumably never doubted — so that the analogy's apparent force comes from the weakness of the objection it was aimed at rather than from any real mapping? Finally, if the dictionary fails, I would like to know what the better analogy is. My own instinct, from a career spent around asymptotic expansions, is that the truer picture is a lower-order term that dominates a series regardless of the coefficients multiplying higher-order terms; but I can already see one vice in that comparison, since higher-order terms in a convergent expansion still contribute something to the sum, whereas the lexical theorist wants mild suffering to contribute nothing at all against the dominant tier. Whether that vice is fatal, and whether the underlying claim about torture and pinpricks survives once all analogies are set aside, is what I most want to understand.
