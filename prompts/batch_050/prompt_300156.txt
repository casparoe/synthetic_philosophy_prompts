Now that I have retired from laboratory work, I spend my evenings reading the philosophy of biology, and I have built an argument whose conclusion I find intolerable yet whose premises I cannot fault. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

(1) Every proposed criterion of life — metabolism, homeostasis, reproduction, the capacity for Darwinian evolution — is either failed by some clear case of the living (a mule, a sterile worker ant, a spore lying dormant for decades) or satisfied by a clear case of the non-living (a candle flame, which consumes fuel, maintains its form, grows, and can divide; a growing crystal; a self-replicating RNA molecule in solution).

(2) Combining the criteria into a disjunctive checklist does not help: either the checklist still admits the flame, or it is trimmed until it merely restates the catalogue of known terrestrial organisms, which is description masquerading as definition.

(3) A concept that admits no principled criterion, and whose borderline cases — viruses, prions, minimal synthetic cells — have no determinate resolution, is not a natural kind but a folk category; so whether a virus is alive is not a factual question, only a conventional one.

(4) Therefore "life" is a folk category.

(5) But the same form of argument applies to every central concept of biology: "species" founders on ring species, asexual lineages, and widespread hybridization; "gene" on overlapping reading frames and alternative splicing; "organism" on colonial siphonophores and slime molds.

(6) Hence these too are folk categories, and biology's ontology dissolves.

(7) Biology's ontology does not dissolve; its kinds support prediction and explanation as well as any in the sciences I practiced for forty years.

(8) So at least one of (1) through (3) must be false.

I am most reluctant to surrender (1), which seems to me a plain inventory of facts, or (7). Which step should I reject, why did it look innocent, and what will rejecting it cost me?
