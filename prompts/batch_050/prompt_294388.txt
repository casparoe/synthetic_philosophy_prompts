Our firm advises clients who submit profit-sharing disputes to a private arbitrator with a long track record, and we are trying to anticipate how she will rule in an upcoming case. In each dispute she has handled, two parties can adopt one of several collaboration structures, and by blending structures they can realize any combination of expected annual profits along the straight-line segments connecting the listed outcomes. If the parties fail to agree, each is left with its standalone profit (the fallback). Assume each party's utility is simply its own profit. The arbitrator imposes a binding award consisting of one profit figure per party. Her four most recent awards, with the first figure always being party A's profit and the second party B's:

Dispute 1. Fallback: (2, 2). Feasible outcomes: the segment from (12, 2) to (2, 12). Award: (7, 7).

Dispute 2. Fallback: (0, 0). Feasible outcomes: the segment from (20, 0) to (0, 10). Award: (10, 5).

Dispute 3. Fallback: (4, 2). Feasible outcomes: the segment from (12, 2) to (4, 8). Award: (8, 5).

Dispute 4. Fallback: (2, 6). Feasible outcomes: the segment from (14, 6) to (2, 14). Award: (8, 10).

We are now preparing for a fifth dispute. The fallback is (0, 0). Two deal architectures are on the table: the first realizes the segment from (0, 24) to (24, 20), the second the segment from (24, 20) to (48, 0). Blending across architectures is permitted, so every point on this two-segment frontier is attainable.

Please provide the following:

1. Your prediction of the arbitrator's award in the fifth dispute.

2. An identification of the distinct decision rules that are each consistent with all four prior awards. Candidates you should consider include maximizing the product of the parties' gains over their fallback (the Nash bargaining solution), equalizing the fraction of its maximum feasible gain that each party realizes (the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution), and any simpler rule the record also fits, such as a geometric midpoint rule. State precisely what each surviving rule recommends in the fifth dispute, showing the calculations, and note which intuitively plausible rules (for instance, equal gains over the fallback, or maximization of the parties' combined profit) are already excluded by the record.

3. Your best single guess as to where the award will land, together with your reasoning: which rule is a professional arbitrator most plausibly running, and does the fact that the earlier disputes all had straight-line frontiers whereas this one has a kink matter for how confident we should be?

4. An honest statement of whether the record underdetermines the outcome. If it does, identify the kind of hypothetical past dispute whose inclusion in the record would have discriminated between the live candidate rules, and state what principle the arbitrator would be committing herself to going forward under each possible award in the new dispute.
