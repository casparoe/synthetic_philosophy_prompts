Last night my friend and I argued for nearly three hours about Parfit's hitchhiker and could not settle a single point. Her claim was that the case only bites because the stranger can read dispositions: strip out the near-perfect lie detection, she said, and the puzzle about rational irrationality dissolves into an ordinary problem of trust under uncertainty. My claim was that the puzzle survives perfectly mundane conditions, because the real engine is the temporal structure — a benefit conferred now in reliance on a promise whose fulfillment is later costly, unobserved, and unenforced — and not any exotic psychology on the part of the rescuer. I want to settle the argument by construction, so I need a case built to order, and I should be explicit about what counts as cheating: the verdicts must fall out of the structure of the scenario, not be stipulated into it.

Build me a scenario with exactly the hitchhiker's structure, transposed into ordinary modern life: no desert, no dying traveler, no mind-reading stranger, no precommitment device available to either party, and a length of no more than a paragraph. Engineer it so that two kinds of chooser come apart — the sophisticated chooser, who at each moment does whatever is rational at that moment given what has already happened, and the resolute chooser, who carries out the plan it was rational to adopt ex ante even when the later step is no longer rational to take — and so that the resolute chooser is predictably better off than the sophisticated one as a direct result. Then run the check: identify the decision points in the case, state what each chooser does at each one and why, name the single feature of the scenario that does the work, say what happens to the divergence if that feature is removed, and give the strongest reply available to the defender of sophisticated choice who wants to stop my case from counting.

Present your entire answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
