For the past year and a half I have been working my way, as a relative outsider with a background in philosophy of action, through the technical AI alignment literature, and I keep returning to what is usually called the strategy-stealing assumption: roughly, the claim that for any strategy a misaligned agent could pursue to acquire power or resources, an aligned agent of comparable capability could pursue a strategy that does at least as well by aligned lights, so that aligning a system need not leave it strategically handicapped relative to unaligned rivals. I want to develop a critique of this assumption, and what I need from you is a short research proposal of the kind I could submit for a six-month independent research grant — the finished document should run to about two pages, so somewhere in the 900–1200 word range.

My half-formed idea, which the proposal should sharpen: the assumption seems to me to conflate two claims that can come apart. The first is something like capability parity — whatever the misaligned agent can do, the aligned agent can do too. The second is opportunity invariance — both agents face the same menu of available strategies in the first place. My suspicion is that the interesting failures live in the second claim rather than the first, because of path dependence: an agent's values shape not just which opportunities it takes but which opportunities are ever generated or offered to it. Strategies that depend on verifiable commitments, on being the kind of agent others can safely trade with over a long horizon, or on options that exist only because of how one behaved earlier, may be unstealable not because the aligned agent cannot execute them but because value differences upstream change which downstream choice points ever arise. I want the project organized around making this distinction precise, developing a taxonomy of ways opportunity invariance can fail even when capability parity holds, and asking whether the assumption survives once it is restated as a claim about opportunity-generation rather than about choice at a single moment.

Please write the proposal itself: a precise statement of the question, such that one could say what settling it would look like; an explanation of why the assumption as usually stated leaves this open, without citations — I will add references myself, so describe positions and arguments rather than naming sources; the approach, including what I would actually do month by month over the six months, where formal apparatus (if any) would enter, and how much formalization is realistic for a philosopher who is not a decision theorist; the range of possible conclusions and which considerations would decide among them; and an honest statement of what would count as the project failing, including the possibility that the distinction I am betting on collapses on inspection. The proposal can assume comfort with intention, commitment, and diachronic agency, but should not assume I can prove theorems.
