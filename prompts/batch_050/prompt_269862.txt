I write a science-and-ideas column for a magazine and I'm doing a long peice on machine translation, and my editor wants a sidebar where I nail my colors to the mast on whether translation software "understands." Before I file, I want someone to check that my positions actually hang together, because I suspect at least one of them is freeloading.

For context, here's a bit of Douglas Hofstadter's essay "The Shallowness of Google Translate" (The Atlantic, 2018) that I keep coming back to:

"When one hears that Google bought a company called DeepMind whose products have 'deep neural networks' enhanced by 'deep learning,' one cannot help taking the word deep to mean 'profound,' and thus 'powerful,' 'insightful,' 'wise.' And yet, the meaning of deep in this context comes simply from the fact that these neural networks have more layers (12, say) than older networks, which might have only two or three. But does that sort of depth imply that whatever such a network does must be profound? Hardly. This is verbal spinmeistery."

My commitments, as drafted for the sidebar:

(a) Hofstadter is right in that passage, and I want to say the same about the hype around large language models today.
(b) I'm a functionalist about understanding: to understand a sentence just is to have the right web of dispositions for using and responding to it, and there is no extra "inner glow" beyond that.
(c) Google Translate doesn't understand the sentences it translates, and Hofstadter's examples of its failures are exactly what show this.
(d) Searle's Chinese Room argument succeeds: merely executing a program, however good the output, is never by itself sufficient for understanding.
(e) Meaning is use: what a word means is exhausted by its role in speakers' practice.
(f) Some future translation system that reliably handled all of Hofstadter's hard cases -- pronouns, nuance, literary style -- would thereby understand.

Which of these pull against each other, and how badly? Rank the tensions by severity, say which apparent ones dissolve on inspection, and for each real one tell me the cheapest revision and what it costs. Also: does endorsing the passage in (a) actually commit me to anything about what deep networks can't do, or have I read more into Hofstadter than he said?
