So I'm in a political philosophy seminar and we just wrapped the unit on automation and the future of work, and one thing kept bugging me: every reading we did just assumed we already know what makes work valuable (or not) and then argued about whether UBI or a jobs guarantee or whatever patches the hole. For my final paper I want to back up and get the spec first. What would a theory of the value of work actually have to deliver before you could trust it to say anything about a post-work world?

Give me the desiderata list. For each one, give the case or intuition behind it. Stuff I'd want on there, off the top of my head:

- It has to explain why replacing the income doesn't replace the work. Lottery winners and early retirees often do worse, not better, and unemployed people's wellbeing drops way beyond what the lost paycheck predicts. A theory that treats work as pure disutility we endure for money can't touch that.
- It has to tell drudgery apart from meaningful work without going all the way to either extreme -- if all work is automatically ennobling it just blesses terrible jobs, if all wage labor is automatically toil it can't explain people who love what they do.
- It has to handle unpaid work. Care work and housework look like work in every relevant sense but automation debates act like they don't exist.
- It has to leave room for the person who is genuinely better off not working. A theory on which work is simply necessary for human flourishing seems empirically false for some people, and suspiciously convenient for employers.
- It has to say something about why accomplishments have to be real -- why building an actual table or actually helping a patient matters in a way that the experience-machine version or an elaborate video game apparently doesn't. Or it has to bite the bullet and say it doesn't matter.
- It has to handle the gap between felt meaning and actual contribution: the job that feels deeply meaningful but accomplishes nothing, and the grim assembly-line job that feeds thousands. Whose perspective does the theory privilege, the worker's or the world's?
- And it has to actually be usable: it must give some grip on whether full automation is liberation or loss, not just describe work's value in terms that go silent exactly when the robots show up.

Mark which of these you think are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp. What I most want: flag any subset of these that can't be jointly satisfied -- like, does the drudgery/meaning distinction force a choice between the worker's perspective and actual contribution? Is the "some people are better off not working" requirement in tension with explaining why retirees deteriorate? If the list itself turns out to be inconsistent, that's basically a result and I'd love that.

Then, briefly, score three generic contenders against the list: the self-objectification/perfectionist view (work is how we make our mark on the world), the social contribution view, and the grumpy preference view (work is just a cost we pay for stuff and any meaning is icing). Keep it short -- the criteria are where the real thinking is.

Please write the answer in American English.
