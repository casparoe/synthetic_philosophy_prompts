I am a PhD student in philosophy of science, and I serve as a teaching assistant on a third-year undergraduate module in philosophy of biology. Next term I am leading a 75-minute seminar of about 18 students on the debate between adaptationism and the spandrels critique associated with Gould and Lewontin, and I want a classroom activity rather than anything lecture-like.

The target I want the students to feel in their own performance, before anyone names it, is this: a fluent functional explanation of a trait feels exactly as compelling from the inside whether the trait is a genuine adaptation, an incidental byproduct of something else that was selected, a developmental or architectural constraint, or a product of drift, and so the ease with which a story comes to mind is no evidence for the story. I also want them to trip over the subtler distinction between a trait's being useful and its having been selected because of that use, since most treatments they will have read blur the two.

The design constraints are these. The students must generate the adaptationist explanations themselves, in small groups, working from a careful description of a system whose actual causal history is withheld; the facilitator reveals that history only afterwards. Please do not use a real biological example, since half the class will recognise the chin or the blood-spot cases from their reading: instead specify a fictional or non-biological system, such as the floor plan of an old building, a legacy software system accreted over decades, or an organism on another planet, whichever you judge easiest to make rigorous, and specify its hidden history completely, identifying which features are, within the fiction, genuine adaptations, which are byproducts, which are constraints, and which are drift. The activity needs explicit rules, a timeline for the 75 minutes, a division of labour within groups, and a scoring scheme or small stakes that reward plausible storytelling, so that the groups are genuinely set up to be fooled rather than playing along knowingly. Construct the reveal so that at least one feature nearly every group explained adaptively turns out to be a byproduct or constraint, and at least one feature the groups ignored or dismissed as noise turns out to be the actual historical target of selection, with an earlier byproduct later co-opted for a new use.

The debrief must do real work: map the moments of the activity onto the components of the critique, meaning spandrels, constraints, drift and exaptation, together with the distinction between selection of a trait and selection for a property, and then give the adaptationist's best replies, so that the students see what evidence would actually discriminate between these histories, such as complexity of design or comparative data across lineages. The activity should not simply straw-man adaptationism.
