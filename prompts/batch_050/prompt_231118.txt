I've read the rationalist blogs and forums for years, including the alignment writing that grew out of them, and last week two members of my reading group got into a serious argument about AI safety via debate — the proposal whereby two AI systems argue opposite sides of a question before a human judge, so that a weak-but-reliable judge can oversee systems much smarter than itself. I stayed out of it, I genuinely can't tell who came out ahead, and I'd like you to referee. Both accept the basic motivation (we'll need to supervise systems whose reasoning we cannot check directly); they disagree about whether the debate format itself favours truth. Here's the exchange as best I remember it:

(1) Anna argued that debate has a structural asymmetry favouring honesty. The honest debater's claims are all mutually supporting, so any of them can withstand cross-examination; the dishonest debater must hold an entire edifice consistent with a false conclusion. Recursive cross-examination — zooming in on whichever sub-claim the honest side challenges — eventually forces the liar either to contradict themselves or to retreat to a leaf claim the judge can directly check and find false.

(2) Ben replied that this proves both too much and too little. Too much, because the same logic would imply that adversarial legal systems convict only the guilty, since barristers cross-examine too. Too little, because the recursion only bites if the judge can evaluate the leaf claims, and the whole point of scalable oversight is that the leaves are beyond the judge. A dishonest debater can always retreat to a sub-claim whose falsity only a specialist could spot. The honest side then needs the judge to rule correctly at every branch point, while the liar needs the judge to slip only once — so the asymmetry Anna invoked actually runs the other way.

(3) Anna conceded the leaves must be judge-checkable but called that a design constraint, not a refutation: restrict debate to questions that decompose into verifiable leaves, the way arithmetic does, and train debaters to supply such decompositions. She added that the early experiments with human judges on reading-comprehension-style tasks found judges given two adversarial debaters reached more accurate answers than judges given a single advisor, which suggests the asymmetry is empirically real, not just a formal hope.

(4) Ben said the experiments show almost the opposite of what Anna needs: those are tasks where a careful judge could in principle verify everything, so they test exactly the cases where her design constraint already holds and say nothing about the questions oversight is actually for — novel research directions, macrostrategy, code with no test suite. There, debate rewards whatever the judge finds persuasive, and persuasiveness tracks truth only when the judge's priors are good, which is precisely what fails out of distribution. His parting shot: debate isn't a truth-seeking machine, it's an amplifier of the judge's existing epistemics — for better and for worse — and in the cases that matter it's worse.

(5) Anna's final move: she said the amplifier framing cuts against Ben too. Every oversight scheme — consultancy, amplification, task decomposition — bottoms out in human judgement somewhere, so unless he has an alternative that doesn't, his objection is really an objection to scalable oversight as such, not to debate; and debate at least adds an adversary whose entire job is surfacing what the judge would otherwise miss. Ben said maybe it is such an objection, and that debate's real function might be to delay our noticing that human judgement stops being load-bearing past some capability threshold.
