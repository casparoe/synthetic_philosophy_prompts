I run a reading group on tech ethics and next month we're doing machine responsibility, and honestly every paper we read seems to just declare that there's a "responsibility gap" when an autonomous drone or trading algorithm causes harm and nobody fits the bill, then either panics about it or shrugs and says the gap is fine actually. What I want before the session is a proper specification: if someone claims to have an account of moral and legal responsibility that handles autonomous systems, what does that account actually have to do to count as adequate? Write me the requirements list, with the case or intuition motivating each one. It obviously has to say something about the classic control condition (can you be responsible for outcomes of a system whose specific behavior you couldn't predict or steer?), but also the many-hands problem where the harm emerges from interactions between a manufacturer, a programmer, a data supplier, and an operator, and each one can point at the others. It should handle learning systems that change after deployment, near-automation where a human nominally supervises but realistically just rubber-stamps, and cases where the system does something nobody was negligent about at all -- does the theory allow responsibility without fault, and if not, does it just accept the gap?

For each requirement, say briefly why it's a genuine desideratum versus a prejudice of one camp (the retributivists want an answerable agent no matter what, the consequentialists mostly want incentives pointed at the right people, and the liability lawyers want someone with deep pockets -- these pull in different directions). And here's the part I care most about: flag whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. Like, if a theory must respect the intuition that punishing the innocent is wrong AND must guarantee someone is always on the hook for machine-caused harm AND must refuse to treat the system itself as a responsible agent, can all three hold at once? Tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking out the standard proposals (strict manufacturer liability, holding the operator responsible, treating the AI as an agent, just accepting gaps). Skip the detailed scoring of each proposal against the list -- I'll do that live with the group -- but a quick note on which requirement kills which proposal is welcome. A table for the requirements is fine.
