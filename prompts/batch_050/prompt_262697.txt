I'm a machine learning engineer who moonlights in my department's evolution reading group, and at last week's session someone dismissed Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique as obvious to anyone who trains models: of course not everything a network does is because the loss function wanted it. The critique, as I understand it, holds that not every trait of an organism exists because natural selection favored it; that some traits are unavoidable byproducts of building something else, the way the tapering triangular spaces appear automatically when you set a dome on rounded arches, and can then be put to use without having been built for that use; and that developmental constraint, historical contingency, and drift are genuine rivals to selection as explanations, so that inferring a selective story from the mere existence of a trait is a just-so story. Translate this into the terms of my field: objective functions and what they do and don't reach, architectural priors and inductive bias, behaviors that emerge as byproducts of scale or of the optimizer rather than of the objective, the path dependence of a training run's history, and the random seed as a source of variation nobody selected.
