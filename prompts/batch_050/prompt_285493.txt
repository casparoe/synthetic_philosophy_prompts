I serve on the release-review committee at a mid-sized AI lab, and we are currently deadlocked over whether to publish the weights of our next frontier model or offer API access only. At our last meeting the debate collapsed into a duel of analogies, and I would like you to audit both before our next session.

One colleague argued that model weights are like cryptographic software: the security community learned decades ago that openness beats secrecy, because published systems get scrutinized, attacked, and hardened, while closed ones fail silently; so releasing weights is the safety-positive choice. Another colleague countered that weights are like weapons designs or pathogen synthesis protocols: publishing working code for a compiler is nothing like publishing the means to build something destructive, and some artifacts are dangerous in proportion to how cheaply they can be copied; so weights should stay closed.

Please give me: (1) the correspondence table for each analogy, stating explicitly what plays the role of the software, the adversarial reviewers, the vulnerability disclosures, and the patch cycle in the first, and what plays the role of the design, the fissile material, and the would-be proliferator in the second; (2) the points where each mapping breaks, distinguishing disanalogies that matter for our release decision from ones that are merely cosmetic (I am particularly unsure whether AI weights are more like a tool, a weapon, or a recipe, since each analogy presupposes a different answer); (3) what each analogy quietly smuggles in about offense-defense balance and about whether misuse or under-scrutiny is the bigger risk; (4) a verdict on whether either colleague's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and a better analogy if you have one.

After the analysis, give me a structured summary as a table with one row per analogy and these columns: analogy; source domain; conclusion it was used to support; strongest correspondence; most damaging disanalogy; verdict (survives / survives in weakened form / collapses); and one-sentence justification.
