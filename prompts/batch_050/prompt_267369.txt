Our school's philosophy club runs a series where a different member leads each session, and I've drawn Lakatos's account of scientific research programmes for next Wednesday. We have forty-five minutes. In the previous two weeks the club covered Popper's falsificationism and Kuhn's paradigms, so everyone will arrive with those fresh in mind and, I suspect, with the lazy assumption that Lakatos is just a compromise stitched between the two. Don't explain research programmes to me; plan how I should explain them to fellow students who are sharp but haven't read a page of him.

What I need from you, specifically:

The sequence. Which components have to be introduced in which order, and why each depends on the one before it — I'm thinking of the hard core, the protective belt, the positive and negative heuristics, and the progressive-versus-degenerating distinction, but tell me if that's the wrong decomposition for this audience or the wrong order.

The carrying example. Commit to one example that will carry the entire session, and say why you chose it over the obvious alternatives — if you think the standard candidates (Newtonian mechanics accumulating anomalies, astrology as the degenerating case) are wrong for a room of high schoolers, say so and pick something better rather than giving me a menu to choose from myself.

The misconception map. Which misunderstandings this audience will predictably form, in the order they'll form them, and exactly where in my sequence I should head each one off. I'm guessing the big one is that 'degenerating' just means 'falsified by the evidence,' and that the earlier Popper session will make this worse — but you tell me what the others are.

The check. One question I can pose at the end whose answer would show the idea actually landed, plus what a wrong answer to it would sound like.

The omissions. What I should deliberately leave out at this level, with a sentence on why leaving it out is a feature rather than a gap.

Take definite positions throughout — one example, one sequence, one check — and give reasons for the choices rather than surveying options. Keep the whole plan under 500 words.
