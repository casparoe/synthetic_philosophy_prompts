I'm a graduate student in cognitive science who recently read Gödel, Escher, Bach, and I've been arguing with a friend about a particular idea from it: the notion of a tangled hierarchy and the inviolate level. The classic illustration is Escher's lithograph Drawing Hands, in which two hands emerge from a flat piece of paper and each appears to be drawing the other into existence. The hierarchy of levels (the drawing hand is "above" the hand being drawn) seems to loop back on itself, creating the paradoxical tangle. But of course, the tangle is only apparent: there is a hidden, inviolate level above both hands, namely Escher himself, whose actual hand drew both depicted hands and was not itself drawn by anything in the picture. My friend claims that this structure generalizes — that every apparent strange loop or self-referential tangle we encounter in logic, mathematics, or the mind must similarly bottom out in (or top out in) some inviolate level that sits outside the loop and explains it. I disagree: I think genuine, ungrounded strange loops can exist, and I suspect consciousness or the self might be exactly such a case — a tangle with no hidden Escher behind it.

Write a dialogue between two characters who take these opposing positions. One character defends the view that every tangled hierarchy is like Drawing Hands in requiring an inviolate level — some stable, untouched stratum from which the loop's apparent self-generation actually flows — and should marshal considerations such as how self-referential sentences get their truth conditions only relative to a metalanguage, how a computer program's self-modification is always governed by unmodified underlying hardware or interpreter rules, and how seeming paradoxes dissolve once the hidden level is made explicit. The other character defends the possibility of authentic strange loops without any inviolate level, and should press on points such as whether the supposed inviolate level of the brain's symbols (neurons, chemistry, physics) is really doing the same kind of work Escher's hand does, whether the inviolate level is always merely pushed back rather than eliminated, and whether the demand for an inviolate level is itself a substantive thesis or just a prejudice about how explanation must work.

Both characters should be equally sharp and well-informed; neither should be a caricature. They should engage with each other's actual arguments rather than delivering parallel monologues — each new turn should pick up something specific the other just said. Feel free to have them invent fresh examples beyond the ones mentioned above (for instance, from set theory, law, fiction, or neuroscience), and by the end they should have at least clarified where exactly their disagreement lies, even if neither concedes.
