I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I recently came across the philosopher Harry Frankfurt's distinction between lying and "bullshit." As I understand it: the liar knows (or believes) the truth and deliberately conceals or contradicts it, so the liar is in a sense deeply engaged with the truth. The bullshitter, by contrast, is indifferent to whether what they say is true or false — they speak to achieve some effect (impressing an audience, filling silence, advancing a goal) without tracking truth at all. A third category is plain honest error: getting things wrong while genuinely aiming at the truth.

I'd like help applying this three-way distinction — lying, bullshit, and honest error — to a phenomenon I deal with daily: LLM "hallucination." Concretely, I mean cases where a language model confidently produces a fabricated API method name, a nonexistent court case, or a plausible-sounding but wrong historical claim, with no internal flag of uncertainty.

Please compare the three categories as candidate descriptions of this behavior, and then defend a verdict on which fits best. Along the way, please address the points where I'm genuinely stuck:

1. Both lying and Frankfurt-style bullshit seem to require mental states — knowledge of the truth, or an attitude of indifference toward it. Does an LLM have the relevant states? If not, does that push hallucination into the "error" category by default, or does it instead show that Frankfurt's categories simply don't transfer to systems without beliefs and intentions — in which case the whole taxonomy might need rework rather than a verdict within it?

2. Does the answer change depending on which stage of the system we look at? A base model trained to continue text plausibly seems truth-indifferent almost by design, whereas a fine-tuned model optimized for helpfulness and user approval might be doing something else again — perhaps something closer to a person telling an audience what it wants to hear. Is that difference philosophically significant, or a distraction?

3. Error is usually excused because the erring agent aimed at truth and missed. Does it make sense to say an LLM "aims at truth" at all, and does the answer matter for how we should morally or professionally evaluate hallucination — e.g., whether vendors describing their models as "reliable" are committing the bullshit rather than the model?

Please be explicit about how confident you are in your verdict, and state what would change your mind: which consideration or argument — about machine intentionality, about the structure of Frankfurt's distinction, or about how these models actually work — would flip your classification, and where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
