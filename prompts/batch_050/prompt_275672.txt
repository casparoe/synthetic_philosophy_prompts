I just rewatched 2001: A Space Odyssey and I can't stop thinking about it in terms of this idea I came across in Dennett, the distinction between cranes and skyhooks -- cranes being explanations that build complexity slowly from the ground up (evolution, engineering, trial and error) and skyhooks being these miraculous top-down "mind first" lifts where something just gets handed down from above. The monolith is about as literal a skyhook as anything in film: apes touch a slab, next scene they're weaponizing bones and inventing tools, cut to space.

So here's what I want: read the film through Dennett's lense. Is the monolith sequence basically an endorsement of skyhook thinking about intelligence -- that minds can't be built, only delivered? And does the ending just run the same trick again at a higher level, where Bowman gets uplifted into the starchild by alien intervention rather than becoming anything on his own? But here's the part that interests me most: HAL. HAL is the one intelligence in the movie that was actually built, piece by piece, by humans -- a crane product. And HAL is also the one mind that goes catastrophically wrong. Is Kubrick (knowingly or not) dramatizing Dennett's claim that skyhooks are fantasy while cranes are the only real way to get a mind -- including the messy, dangerous parts of building one? Or is the film doing something weirder, like suggesting crane-made minds fail and only skyhook minds transcend?

Feel free to push back on the reading where the film resists it -- I'm not married to it, and I know the movie is famously open-ended. I'd rather get an honest "this bit doesn't fit" than a forced fit. And if Dennett would say the whole premise of delivered intelligence is confused, say that too and tell me whether the film survives the criticism or was never really making a philosophical claim in the first place.
