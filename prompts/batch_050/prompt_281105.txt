Alright, here's one I've been mulling over. I spent forty years as an experimental condensed matter physicist — wrote more grant proposals than I care to remember and sat on funding panels for a good chunk of that time — and now in retirement I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole, lately Parfit and the population ethics literature that came after him. This "repugnant conclusion" business, where a world of wonderful lives supposedly ranks below some vast world of lives barely worth living so long as you add enough people. My old panelist brain keeps translating it back into the genre I know best: somewhere out there is a PI who takes total utilitarianism seriously enough to write it up as an actual grant application, and the thought of reviewing it is equal parts hilarious and horrifying.

So: write me a parody research grant proposal, around 650–750 words, formatted like the real thing (Specific Aims, Significance, Innovation, Approach, maybe a Budget Justification at the end), in which an unnervingly sincere research team proposes to *empirically locate the exact welfare level at which the repugnant conclusion kicks in*. Their methodology should follow the logic of total utilitarianism with complete fidelity and complete moral blindness: pilot studies that keep adding people at marginally lower welfare to check whether the total really keeps going up, a sub-aim responding to anticipated reviewer concerns about the mere addition paradox, a contingency plan in case the data end up favoring the sadistic conclusion, careful operational definitions of "barely worth living" (pilot-tested on graduate students, presumably), and a line item for compensating the lives created in the control arm. The humor should come from the actual philosophy — the non-identity problem making informed consent a conceptual impossibility, the awkward fact that you can't run the counterfactual where these people were never born, critical-level views dismissed in one paragraph as a rival framework that's "underpowered" — not from making the scientists stupid or cartoonish. Deadpan throughout, no winking at the reader; it should read like a genuinely fundable proposal from a world that has gone very slightly wrong. Someone who knows the population ethics literature should find the satire fair, and someone who doesn't should still sense that something is deeply, quietly off by the time they reach the budget.
