I'm on the steering committee of an interdisciplinary reading group where a social psychologist and a normative ethicist have been arguing for two semesters about moral foundations theory -- the research program that analyzes political moralities in terms of a handful of intuitive foundations (care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, liberty) and reports that self-described liberals lean heavily on a few of them while conservatives distribute their concern more evenly across all. The psychologist keeps saying these findings "should embarrass the left" or at least "correct" liberal moral self-understanding; the ethicist keeps replying that no descriptive finding about which intuitions people have can bear on which intuitions are right, and that the whole exchange is the naturalistic fallacy with extra steps. They've agreed to stop arguing and produce a joint statement instead, and I've been deputized to draft it.

Write that draft. It should be the document two serious versions of these people could both sign: a numbered section of agreed claims, covering what the empirical research does and does not show, what "moral foundation" even means, and which popularized conclusions (on both sides) neither can endorse; then a disagreements table stating each residual dispute in wording its own side would accept -- I suspect one is whether the distribution of foundations across the political spectrum is evidence about the foundations' normative standing or only about their social function, and another is whether "conservatives use more foundations" is even an intelligible sense of moral breadth rather than an artifact of how the questionnaire individuates moral concerns; then the cruxes, the sub-questions whose resolution would force at least one side to update. For each crux, give the empirical result, thought experiment, or conceptual argument both parties would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If part of their long-running fight turns out, on inspection, to be verbal -- for instance, if "X debunks Y" means different things to the psychologist and the ethicist -- say so explicitly in the statement, identify which part, and redraft it as a question both sides agree is substantive.
