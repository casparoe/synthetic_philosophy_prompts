I'm in a grad reading group on the metaphysics of chance and we keep going around in circles on the Principal Principle -- roughly Lewis's idea that, absent any weird "inadmissible" information, your rational credence in an event conditional on its objective chance being x should just be x. Everytime we get to this part the same fight breaks out, so for next week's session I want to stage it as a written dialogue, and I'd like you to write it for me.

The two characters: Alex thinks the Principal Principle is basically constitutive of what chance is. Chances are whatever magnitudes play the constrain-rational-credence role, so deference to chance isn't some mysterious extra norm bridging mind and world; it's close to analytic, and asking for a further justification of it is like asking why you ought to believe what's true. Sam thinks this is far too quick. Chance is a metaphysical feature of the world (frequencies, propensities, Humean patterns, pick your poison), credences are states of a subject, and a principle connecting the two is a substantive normative claim that could in principle fail and so needs a genuine justification -- one she insists nobody has ever actually given. Please make both of them genuinely sharp. I don't want Sam written as someone who just doesn't get the functionalist move, or Alex as someone who repeats "it's analytic" louder each time.

To stop the dialogue from staying at slogan level, have them tangle with two specific pressure points. First, the crystal ball case: suppose a perfectly reliable oracle tells you today how the coin will land next Tuesday. Intuitively you shouldn't defer to the 50/50 chance anymore -- but Alex will want to say the oracle's testimony is "inadmissible," and Sam should press hard on whether "admissible" is just a label for "information that would make the principle come out wrong," which would make the principle circular or unfalsifiable. Second, the undermining problem, the one Lewis himself dubbed the big bad bug: a chance distribution can assign positive probability to futures in which that very distribution would no longer hold, which seems to make full deference to the chances of a complete history impossible to square with itself. Alex will have to restrict the principle somehow (Lewis's own response was to weaken what you conditionalize on) and Sam gets to demand a non-ad-hoc reason for the restriction rather than a patch. Aim for maybe 700-900 words, real back-and-forth where each reply engages the last point, and don't let either side win -- if anything, have them end by jointly locating what the actual crux of the dispute is.
