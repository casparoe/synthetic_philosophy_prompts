My PhD is in philosophy of cognitive science, and my supervisor and I keep circling the question of when an interpretability result licenses saying that a network *represents* something, rather than merely carrying information a probe can extract. Her verdicts on cases so far: a single LSTM unit whose activation tracks review sentiment and which, when clamped high or low, flips the sentiment of generated text — a genuine representation of sentiment. A linear probe that decodes verb tense from a language model's hidden states, where ablating the decoding direction leaves the model's behaviour entirely unchanged — not a representation, just an exploitable correlate. A probe that recovers syntactic structure about as well from a randomly initialised network as from a trained one — not a representation. In a small transformer trained on Othello move sequences, a direction that decodes board state and whose manipulation changes the model's move predictions in the semantically expected way — a representation. A sparse autoencoder feature that fires on mentions of a particular landmark and, when amplified, makes the model talk about that landmark unprompted — a representation.

Our new case: a direction that decodes whether a prompt is a request for medical advice; ablating it reliably degrades the model's refusal behaviour on such prompts, but amplifying it on neutral text does not make the model treat that text as a medical request — it merely adds noise. Predict her verdict, and say which rule she is implicitly running: a purely causal criterion (decodable plus behaviourally efficacious), a stronger manipulationist criterion (interventions must change behaviour in semantically expected ways, not just degrade it), or something else. Note which other rules fit her earlier verdicts equally well, and describe the further case that would best tell them apart. Please reply in British English.
