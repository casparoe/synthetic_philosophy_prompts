I've been teaching a decision theory unit in my intro philosophy course, and a student asked me after class whether the value-of-information stuff we covered has any real teeth, and I ended up rambling about the Columbia disaster -- but afterwards I felt like I botched the analysis. Here's the episode as I understand it, and please correct me if I get something materially wrong: during the launch of STS-107 in January 2003, a chunk of foam broke off the external tank and hit the orbiter's wing about 80 seconds in. Over the following days, engineers on the debris assessment team got worried and pushed for outside imagery of the wing -- spy satellite or telescope photos -- to check how bad the damage was. Management effectively let those requests die. Part of the reported reasoning was roughly: even if we find damage, there's nothing we can do about it up there, so the pictures wouldn't change anything. The crew came home on February 1st and the orbiter broke up on reentry. Later investigation concluded a rescue or repair attempt, while desperate, wasn't obviously impossible.

Here's what I want: analyze this episode through Good's theorem on the value of information -- the result that a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer, offered free information before choosing an action, should always weakly prefer to take it, because information can only help if it might change what you'd do, and if it can't change anything it's worth zero, never less. Do the mapping carefully: what exactly was the decision problem as NASA management had framed it, what actions were actually in their choice set (including ones they never seriously costed), and was the imagery decision-relevant under the real choice set rather than the assumed one?
