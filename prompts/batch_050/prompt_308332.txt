I'm a PhD student in legal philosophy, and I co-run a graduate reading group on transitional justice. Our sessions on Nuremberg and Tokyo keep dissolving into the same stalemate: half the room treats nullum crimen sine lege as an absolute bar to retroactive prosecution, half treats it as obviously inapplicable to people who organized aggressive war and mass murder, and nobody can state in advance what an adequate theory of the legality principle would even have to accomplish. For the next session I want to circulate a specification rather than another position paper. The passage at issue is from the section "The Law of the Charter" in the 1946 judgment of the International Military Tribunal, where the Tribunal responds to the retroactivity objection:

"In the first place, it is to be observed that the maxim nullum crimen sine lege is not a limitation of sovereignty, but is in general a principle of justice. To assert that it is unjust to punish those who in defiance of treaties and assurances have attacked neighbouring states without warning is obviously untrue, for in such circumstances the attacker must know that he is doing wrong, and so far from it being unjust to punish him, it would be unjust if his wrong were allowed to go unpunished."

Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of when criminal punishment without prior enacted law is permissible must deliver. I want the cases it has to get right, with the reasoning behind each — the post-war German grudge-informer prosecutions under retroactive statutes, the convictions of East German border guards after reunification, a head of state whose own decrees immunized everything he ordered, and, on the other side, at least one prosecution that an adequate theory must condemn as victor's justice rather than launder. I want the distinctions it cannot collapse: conduct already criminal under the defendant's own domestic law versus criminal only under contested international norms; retroactive creation of an offense versus retroactive removal of an immunity or defense; the immorality of an act versus its illegality; the wrongness of convicting versus the wrongness of punishing. And I want the constraints it must respect — for instance, it should explain why fair warning matters even for defendants who, as the Tribunal puts it, must have known they were doing wrong; it should not vindicate Nuremberg at the price of licensing any future victor's tribunal, nor condemn it at the price of condemning every subsequent atrocity prosecution; and it should say something about the tu quoque problem, given that the Charter applied only to the defeated. For each desideratum, give the motivating case or consideration and mark whether you take it to be genuinely non-negotiable or merely the prejudice of one camp.

Two further asks. First, use the quoted passage as a test case: the Tribunal's move is to demote the maxim from a limitation of sovereignty to a defeasible principle of justice and to substitute foreknowledge of wrongdoing for prior enactment — say which items on your list this move satisfies and which it sacrifices, since I suspect the whole debate turns on whether those sacrifices are among the non-negotiable desiderata. Second, flag any subset of your desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify the single requirement that knocks out the most live positions. Then, briefly and only at the end, score the main contenders against the list: the Tribunal's own foreseeability-of-wrong rationale, strict legality absolutism of the kind animating Judge Pal's dissent at Tokyo, the Radbruch-formula approach on which sufficiently extreme injustice is no law at all, and the view that core international crimes were already binding through general principles of law recognized by civilized nations. Keep the scorecard short — the desiderata and their tensions are the deliverable, and I will run the scoring exercise with the group myself.
