I am a novelist at work on a book set in a regional hospital that is cut off from the outside world for several days by a catastrophic flood, and the moral spine of the story is a single allocation decision made by a small ethics committee under time pressure. I do not want a generic dilemma in which one character is clearly right and another clearly monstrous; the novel only works if three decent, competent people can examine the same scarce resource and honestly reach three different conclusions. I would like you to construct the case that generates their disagreement.

Please take on the role of a philosopher specializing in the ethics of health-resource allocation — fluent in cost-effectiveness analysis, regulatory cost-benefit analysis, and the triage literature — who occasionally consults for fiction writers. Think carefully, and begin with a short brainstorm: list three or four candidate bottlenecks (a single rescue helicopter, a ventilator, a finite drug supply, one surgical team, and so on), noting in a sentence what kind of divergence each could plausibly support, before settling on the one that best meets the specifications below.

The specifications. The scenario must be engineered so that, confronted with one allocation of one resource, three standard frameworks deliver three different verdicts, each favoring a different use:

1. A cost-per-QALY protocol of the sort a health technology assessment body would apply must favor one option.
2. A cost-benefit analysis built on the value of a statistical life — willingness to pay for small reductions in mortality risk, aggregated across a population — must favor a second option.
3. The rule of rescue, the standing imperative to save identifiable lives in front of us regardless of what the statistics recommend, must favor a third.

Constraints: the setting must be contemporary and realistic, with no science-fictional devices and no prognostic certainty (the committee may have defensible probabilities, never guarantees); the scenario must be describable in one page; and the verdicts must be generated by the frameworks rather than stipulated — a case that merely asserts each framework says what I need is useless, because some of my readers will check.

The deliverables. (a) The scenario itself. (b) For each framework, the verdict and the reasoning that produces it, worked out in enough detail that I can put the argument into a character's mouth. (c) The structural feature of the case that makes the three frameworks peel apart — I suspect it involves identified versus statistical lives, or the fact that remaining life-years count for nearly everything in one framework and almost nothing in another, but show me rather than taking my word for it. (d) What happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse into an ordinary triage problem on which the frameworks agree, and if not, what else is doing the work? (e) For each framework, one move a sophisticated defender would make to resist the verdict I have assigned it, since each character's opponents need ammunition too.

The purpose fixes what counts as cheating: this is for fiction, so each committee member must be able to defend their position in dialogue without sounding foolish, and no reader trained in health economics or bioethics should be able to object that the case was rigged by fiat. If the specifications cannot be jointly satisfied, tell me which one to relax rather than quietly bending it.
