I'm a couple years into a PhD in evolutionary theory and my reading group has now spent basically a whole semester going in circles on the units of selection debate, and I want a diagnosis, not another survey. Here's my sense of the terrain: on one side the gene's-eye people (Dawkins, and more technically folks like Grafen) who say only replicators can be units of selection in any deep sense, and that organism- or group-level talk is at best useful bookkeeping shorthand. On the other side the multilevel selection people (Sober & Wilson and co.) who say groups can be genuine vehicles of selection in their own right, and that the Price equation lets you decompose selection at whatever level you like. And then there's this third camp that keeps popping up and annoying everyone by claiming the whole thing is a pseudo-debate, since kin selection, inclusive fitness, and multilevel selection are supposedly just equivalent accounting conventions -- same math, same predictions, pick whichever ledger you find intuitive.

My hypothesis is that the disagreement isn't really about the math at all (nobody seems to dispute the equations) but about what a "unit of selection" is supposed to *do* in a theory: is it the level at which fitness differences are *caused*, the level at which adaptations *manifest*, or just whichever level gives the most compact *description*? Like, the gene's-eye folks seem to be answering "who benefits," the MLS folks are answering "where does selection act," and the equivalence camp thinks both questions are confused. If that's right, is this a verbal dispute wearing causal clothing, or is there a real causal question buried in there that one side is actually correct about?

What I want: a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, grounded in what each side actually claims rather than a recap of their positions. And tell me what would count as settling it, if anything -- a formal result? some empirical case (does the eusociality fight from a few years back actually bear on it? the major transitions stuff?), a conceptual distinction everyone should adopt? Or should I just tell my reading group the impasse is permanent and we should move on with our lives. Also, if my hypothesis up there is wrong, say so plainly and explain why.
