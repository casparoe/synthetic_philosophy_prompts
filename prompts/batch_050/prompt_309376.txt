So I'm in this philosophy of mind reading group and last week we got completely stuck on the digger wasp. You probably know the case: Sphex paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the entrance of her burrow, goes in to check things out, comes back up and pulls the cricket in. But if some meddling experimenter nudges the cricket a few inches away while she's inside, she drags it back to the threshold and then goes in to check *again*, from scratch, and she'll do this dozens of times without ever just skipping the check. Hofstadter called this sphexishness, and Dennett used it to poke at whether we're really so different. Anyway, the group split into two camps: the people who think our habits and routines are just bigger, fancier loops of the same kind, and the people (me, honestly) who think flexible, context-sensitive response is a difference in kind, not just degree. One friend said free will is "having enough nested loops that you can't see the edges anymore," which I found annoying and haven't stopped thinking about.

Write me a pantoum from the wasp's point of view that enacts this debate rather than just describing it. I want the form to do the philosophical work: a pantoum is built out of recycled lines (the 2nd and 4th lines of each stanza come back as the 1st and 3rd of the next, and the poem ends on its own opening line), so the poem should literally *re-perform* the wasp's resetting behavior — each time the cricket gets moved, the same lines return, the way her routine restarts. Get the ethology right enough that someone who knows the example recognizes it (the paralyzed cricket, the threshold, the inspection trip below, the whole thing running again unchanged). But here's the part I care about: at least once, a repeated line should come back with a genuinely shifted meaning — same words, but the surrounding context makes it mean something new — so the poem stages in miniature the question of whether recontextualized repetition is an escape from sphexishness or just the loop with better PR. And the closing line, which has to be the opening line returning, should land differently the second time: I want to finish the poem unsure whether I've been reading about a wasp at all.

Maybe five or six stanzas, whatever works. Please don't have the wasp name-drop philosophers or say the word "sphexishness" — she shouldn't know she's an example. Oh and if it helps: I want to read this at our last session of the semester, so it should be good as a poem first and an argument second, not the other way round.
