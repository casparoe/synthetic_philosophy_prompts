I teach a small undergrad seminar on intergenerational ethics (12 students, 75 minutes, they're mostly second years who think discounting is just "an economics thing"). I want an activity that makes them feel the pull of pure time preference -- the idea that a benefit counts for less just because of *when* it lands, even with uncertainty and growth and everything else stripped out -- and then notice how slippery their own pull actually is.

Here's roughly the shape I'm imagining, but design it properly: I want the classic setup where you offer people money now vs. money later and then the same choice pushed into the future, and their preferences flip (patient when both options are far off, impatient when one is now). But most classroom versions of this stop at "look, you're inconsistent." I want to go a step further: after the flip has happened to them, I want a round where the delay is real but the stakes are something like cookies or choosing who does the dishes next week, and then a round where the "delay" is a *generation* -- like their own choice is mapped onto a policy tradeoff affecting people in 80 years -- so they have to confront whether the feeling they just had about Tuesday vs. Friday is the same kind of thing they're doing to people in 2105, and whether it's defensible there.

Please give me: concrete rules and materials for each round (nothing that needs real money -- points, snacks, small stakes are fine), exact numbers for the offers so the flip is actually triggered and I don't have to fiddle with it, a script for what I say between rounds that doesn't tip them off, and a debrief that (a) separates pure time preference from the respectable reasons to discount (inflation, risk you won't be around, compounding returns) -- the point being that their preference reversal was none of those, it was just "now feels different," (b) maps each round onto that distinction, and (c) lets them fight about whether "now feels different" could ever be a *reason* rather than just a fact about our psychology.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong: e.g. students who've read about this performing patience to look consistent, someone gaming the snack stakes, the whole thing collapsing if I run it right after lunch. And be honest about what the activity can't show -- my guess is you can make them *feel* present bias but you can't make a 75-minute session settle whether discounting over generations is the same phenomenon or something else entirely, so tell me where I have to just lecture that bit.
