I am a retired natural scientist who has come late to philosophy, and I want an audit rather than encouragement. After decades in a mature experimental field, I find myself holding the following package about inside view, outside view, and epistemic modesty. Please tell me whether it hangs together.

My commitments:

1. In a mature science with a stable expert consensus, an outsider should defer almost completely unless they have personally worked through the primary methods and data; disagreement from the armchair is nearly always overconfidence.

2. In my own former field, however, I still trust my inside view enough to dissent from a newer consensus if I have specific, checkable doubts about instruments, controls, or model assumptions, even if I am no longer positioned to publish the reply.

3. For long-range questions where base rates are poor, such as AI takeoff or abrupt Earth-system thresholds, I give more weight to detailed causal-mechanistic models than to reference-class forecasting, because the relevant reference class is easy to gerrymander after the fact.

4. I accept a modesty rule: when I discover that an honest, informed, and comparably competent person disagrees with me, I should move at least partway toward them, often about halfway in expectation, unless the dispute is mainly about values or the other party has a distorting financial or political incentive.

5. I also believe track records beat rationales: if one forecaster is visibly better calibrated than another, I should prefer the calibrated one even when their stated reasoning sounds less sophisticated.

6. I reject a pure view from nowhere. A measurement I made carefully myself can rationally count more for me than the same number reported secondhand, because I know which failures were excluded and which were not.

To keep myself honest I have invented a private discipline I call the two-ledger rule. The lab ledger records mechanism-level judgments: what I think is physically or computationally happening and why. The audit ledger records impersonal constraints: base rates, consensus strength, incentive gradients, calibration scores, and how often people with my confidence have been right. My rule is that the lab ledger may generate hypotheses and private wagers, but only the audit ledger may license public advice, policy recommendations, or claims that others ought to defer to me.

Please pressure-test this set using three cases. Case A: after I retire, my old field converges on a method I consider subtly biased; I have specific criticisms but no current lab access. Case B: on advanced AI timelines, a forecaster with an excellent calibration record gives a vague outside-view estimate, while a mechanistic modeler with no forecasting record gives an intricate account I find causally compelling. Case C: I personally obtain a striking result, fail to find my error, and then learn that three careful groups cannot replicate it, though I suspect they used a reagent batch my lab had already rejected.

I am not asking which side is fashionable. I want to know which commitments genuinely constrain one another, which only appear to, and what my own stated principles actually force me to say in A, B, and C. Please do not assume I already know which belief I would abandon.

Format your answer in exactly these sections, in this order:

- Charitable reconstruction: about 150 words; state the strongest coherent version of my view, including what the two-ledger rule is supposed to buy me.
- Tension map: about 250 words; classify pairwise tensions as severe, moderate, or merely apparent, and explain the mechanism of each rather than just naming the pair.
- Application to the cases: about 300 words; give my required verdict in A, B, and C under my own commitments, noting any case where the commitments underdetermine the answer.
- Cheapest repairs: about 220 words; propose up to three revisions, ranked by how much consistency they restore per unit of philosophical cost, and say what each revision sacrifices.
- Bottom line: about 80 words; state whether the package is coherent as written, and if not, name the single commitment whose modification would do the most work.
