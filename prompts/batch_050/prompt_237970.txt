As a novelist, I am inventing a post-civil-war tribunal that reduces or suspends punishment when a perpetrator’s confession is judged not merely complete but concordant: it must align with survivors’ testimony, name the command structure accurately, and include a credible account of why obedience felt necessary at the time. Critics say this turns remorse into a performance and rewards the best storytellers; defenders say it makes acknowledgment public and repairable. Compare this imagined practice, in general terms, with South African-style amnesty for disclosure, Argentine-style prosecution after truth findings, and Rwandan-style local confession and reintegration, without relying on fine historical details. What philosophical commitments would my tribunal presuppose about truth as correspondence versus narrative coherence, individual guilt within hierarchical violence, the moral difference between explanation and excuse, and whether reconciliation can legitimately be an aim of criminal justice? Which rival views does it quietly exclude, and where is it underdetermined enough to fit several theories? Please write in American English.
