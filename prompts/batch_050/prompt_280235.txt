Here's the exam question as the professor wrote it: "In recent animal ethics, some philosophers rely on what we will call the scale argument: even if we should be quite uncertain whether insects (or other small invertebrates) are sentient, the sheer number of insects affected by human activity is so large that the expected moral cost of harming them may rival or exceed the cost of harms to animals whose sentience is nearly certain. (a) Reconstruct this argument carefully, making its premises explicit. (b) State what you take to be the strongest objection to it. (c) Does the objection succeed? Defend your answer."

And here's the student's answer, verbatim: "The scale argument runs as follows. (1) For any creature, the expected moral cost of harming it equals the probability that it is sentient and thus capable of suffering, multiplied by the amount of suffering it would undergo if it is sentient. (2) Human activity affects a staggering number of insects — agriculture, pesticides, and increasingly insect farming involve trillions of individual animals. (3) Even a small probability of insect sentience, say 5%, multiplied by numbers this large yields an expected total of suffering comparable to factory farming of chickens or pigs. (4) Therefore we should give insect welfare substantial weight in our deliberations, potentially approaching the weight we give vertebrates. The strongest objection is that the probability of insect sentience is extremely low. Insects have tiny brains — a fruit fly has only around 100,000 neurons, versus billions in mammals — and lack most of the neural structures associated with pain in vertebrates. Since the probability term is so small, the expected value collapses and the argument fails. I think the objection succeeds. Sentience presumably requires some minimal neural complexity, insects plausibly fall below the threshold, and so the probability is close enough to zero that no finite number of insects can compensate. This is basically Singer's point in Animal Liberation, where he argues that the capacity for suffering tracks neural machinery and lets us draw a rough line in the animal kingdom. Note also that the scale argument assumes utilitarianism, since only on a utilitarian view do vast aggregates of tiny harms add up to something morally significant; a deontologist or virtue ethicist can simply reject premise (1)."
