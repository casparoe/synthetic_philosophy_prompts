I teach a survey course on classical Islamic philosophy to about fifteen undergraduates, most of whom have read Hume's treatment of causation but find al-Ghazālī's occasionalism either quaint or indistinguishable from it. We have one 80-minute session. I want a structured in-class activity or game that makes participants feel the force of the occasionalist's central observation from the inside: that what we call a cause is only ever a constant conjunction we have grown accustomed to, and that no amount of observation of the fire touching the cotton discloses a power in the fire itself. The rules should be arranged so that students first confidently extract "causal laws" from repeated sequences, then discover -- ideally with some genuine surprise and a little frustration -- that the regularities they relied on were sustained by a hidden will that could revise them at any moment. I am imagining something where the instructor or a designated participant effectively occupies the position of the divine agent, but I am open to other mechanisms; what matters is that the students encounter the gap between observed regularity and necessary connection before anyone names it, rather than having it lectured at them afterward.

Please give me the full rules and any materials needed, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the activity onto al-Ghazālī's argument in the seventeenth discussion of the Incoherence of the Philosophers -- including his concession that people can still rely on regularities in practice, since the game should not end in pure skepticism about induction. The debrief should also help students distinguish occasionalism from Hume's position: Hume naturalizes the expectation as custom, whereas al-Ghazālī relocates the connection in God's will, and I want the activity's structure to give us a hook for that difference rather than collapsing it. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (for instance, students gaming the setup, or the debrief sliding into a generic lesson about falsification), and be honest about which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce -- I suspect the theological content itself resists simulation, and I would rather have that limitation stated plainly than papered over.
