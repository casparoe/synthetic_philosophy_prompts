I run a small philosophy club at my high school (about 14 students, and we meet for an hour after class), and for our next session I want to tackle a problem I keep circling back to: how anyone can figure out what actually exists when part of the relevant information is in principle out of reach. We've been calling it the "ontology identification" problem -- if two people only ever have access to incomplete or aggregated observations of the world, they may each build a different picture of what the world fundamentally contains, and both pictures can fit every observation either of them will ever make. The members are comfortable with simple games but have no formal background, so the concept has to emerge from what they experience rather than from a lecture. I'd also like them to feel a second, subtler point: that settling on one of these rival pictures isn't just a neutral bookkeeping choice, because once you act on it, your chosen ontology quietly shapes what you predict, what you bother to check, and what you can get wrong.

Please design an in-class game or simulation that makes participants feel both parts of this. The basic shape I have in mind is that the room interacts with some hidden system whose true structure only I (as facilitator) know, and players receive only lossy or partial readings of it -- but I'm open to a better mechanism if you have one. I need concrete rules, a complete list of materials, and a payoff scheme that doesn't use real money (points, tokens, or privileges are fine). The game should be structured so that at least two genuinely different theories about what the hidden system contains can flourish among the players at the same time, and so that players are pushed to make choices whose outcomes depend on which theory they've privately committed to. Please also include a timeline for the 60-minute session.

Finally, give me a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the game onto the philosophical idea -- including the moment where players discover that the other team's picture of reality was different from theirs yet just as well-supported -- plus an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (confusion, collusion, someone guessing the trick) and how to handle each, and a frank note on which feature of the real epistemic problem the activity cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply through discussion instead.
