I have no formal training in philosophy, but a long-running argument with a friend has forced me to think hard about what ethicists apparently call the nonidentity problem, and I would like you to produce a document the two of us should have written a year ago.

Here is the setup. My friend holds what I gather is called a person-affecting view: an action can only be wrong if it is bad for someone -- for some actual or future person who is worse off than they would otherwise have been. I hold the opposing, impersonal view: when we choose between futures, what matters is how good the lives in them are, and a choice can be wrong simply because it brings about worse lives, even if no one who exists in the worse future would have existed in the better one. Our standard battleground is a case of our own devising: a country choosing between two energy policies. Policy A is cheap and dirty; two centuries from now, whoever is alive will lead somewhat diminished lives. Policy B is expensive and clean; the transition changes economic conditions enough that substantially different people are conceived, and those people live well. I say A is wrong. My friend says A harms no one, since the diminished people would never have existed under B, and that my view, taken seriously, drags me toward demanding that we maximize total welfare even at the cost of grotesque conclusions about population size.

Draft the joint statement we could both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. I want:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims -- about the cases, about what any adequate view must be able to say, and about what each side is not committed to (for instance, I do not think my friend is indifferent to the distant future, and he does not think I am committed to every consequence of total utilitarianism). These must be substantive, not peace treaty boilerplate.

2. A disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated in neutral wording that each of us would accept as a fair description of our own position. I suspect at least one disagreement is really about the word harm -- whether it requires a comparison with how the same person would otherwise have fared, or whether a life sufficiently bad can be a harm without any such comparison. If you conclude that part of our dispute is verbal rather than moral, identify exactly which part and show that the remaining disagreement survives the disambiguation.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of us. Candidates I want you to assess include whether causing someone to exist with a life worth living can ever wrong that person; whether the impersonal view can be held without accepting the repugnant conclusion; and whether the person-affecting view can handle cases involving a fixed set of future people (where it and my view agree) in a way that does not make its treatment of the nonidentity cases look ad hoc.

4. For each crux, a concrete case, argument, or theoretical result that we would both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance -- what my friend expects that case or result to show, and what I expect it to show.

The standard I care about is that both of us could put our names to the final text without feeling misrepresented. Where you cannot find a formulation we would both accept, say so and explain why the disagreement resists neutral statement -- that itself would be informative.
