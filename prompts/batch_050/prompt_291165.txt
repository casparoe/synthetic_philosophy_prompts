I teach a seminar on twentieth-century American philosophy, and each year I close our unit on neopragmatism by circulating a piece of satire, because I find students understand a position best once they have seen it lovingly mocked from the inside. This year I would like you to write the piece for me: a commencement address, roughly 650 words, delivered by an invented figure — a devoted disciple of Rorty-style antirepresentationalism, say a beloved emeritus professor of comparative literature — addressing a graduating class. The speaker must be a thoroughgoing liberal ironist in the relevant sense: he holds his own vocabulary lightly, he offers the graduates no foundations, no Truth with a capital T, nothing the universe ratifies, only redescriptions, solidarities, and the hope that suffering diminishes. The comedy should arise from his taking this completely seriously as a genre that traditionally promises graduates timeless wisdom. Every sentence must be one he can utter without betraying the view: he may not once claim that his outlook is true, only that it is his, and perhaps that it is catching. The irony should stay deadpan throughout — no winking, no aside admitting the joke — and yet a reader should be able to feel, without anything being stated, the standard worries pressing in: whether advice with no claim to truth can be advice at all, whether redescription is just argument in disguise, whether the speaker's evident decency smuggles in the universalism he disavows, and whether an ironist can sincerely celebrate anything.

A few further constraints, because past attempts have failed in predictable ways. First, the satire must exaggerate features the position actually has — the final-vocabulary talk, the preference for hope over knowledge, the frank admission of ethnocentrism, the relegation of philosophy to one literary genre among others — rather than inventing absurdities no pragmatist holds; a specialist should recognize that the author understands what is being parodied and judge the parody fair. Second, the speaker is a fictional proponent and the target is the style of discourse, not any real philosopher. Third, the address should have a genuine emotional arc, so that by the end a reader has felt, rather than been told, the real question of whether irony and solidarity can bear the weight a commencement address is supposed to bear.

Before writing, please work through a brief brainstorm: list the specific doctrines and rhetorical habits you intend to send up, and for each, note how it will be exaggerated without being distorted. Then produce the address itself, with a title. Finally, after the address, give me a structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "piece_title" (a string); "targeted_features" (an array of objects, each with "feature" — the genuine element of the view — and "exaggeration" — how the address inflates it while remaining faithful); "objections_landed" (an array of strings naming the criticisms the satire makes felt without ever stating them); and "consistency_check" (a string explaining why the speaker never once asserts the truth of his own position, citing the lines that most risk doing so and why they survive). Approach this as a professional philosopher with a good ear for academic ceremony, and take care over the prose: this will be read aloud to twenty sleepy undergraduates, and it needs to land.
