In my graduate seminar on methodology, I put together the following argument against reflective equilibrium and now suspect it proves too much, since its conclusion would discredit most of moral and political philosophy. (1) A method of justification is legitimate only if competent application of it can lead someone from beliefs they ought to abandon to beliefs they ought to hold; a method that merely polishes whatever it is fed is not a source of justification at all. (2) Reflective equilibrium takes our considered judgments as input and revises judgments and principles against each other until they cohere. (3) Coherence is a purely internal relation among beliefs: it constrains form, not content, so for any consistent body of convictions, however false or vicious, there exists some equilibrium state that preserves most of them. (4) Therefore reflective equilibrium, competently applied, will typically leave systematically distorted starting points substantially intact — think of someone raised to find it obvious that some people are born to serve others. (5) So reflective equilibrium fails the test in (1). (6) Therefore it is not a legitimate method, and the large parts of philosophy that rely on it are without a legitimate method. Where exactly does this go wrong, if it does? Identify the weakest step, explain why it seemed plausible, and say what rejecting it costs. Take a definite position rather than laying out the menu of replies. I would rather weaken (1) than abandon (5), but tell me honestly which premise is cheapest to surrender and what, if anything, the argument still establishes about the limits of the method.
