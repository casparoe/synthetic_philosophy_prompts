There's this puzzle about fiction and imagination I keep going back and forth on and I'd like you to explain it properly, not just give me the textbook summary.

Consider two stories with the same plot skeleton.

Story A: a realistic story set in a poor farming village. An elderly widow can no longer work, food is scarce, and the village council decides to turn her out into the winter cold to die. It's narrated plainly, no fantasy elements. The story ends with the narrator flatly stating that everyone agreed she had been treated justly, and that they were right about this. Readers reliably balk at that last move. They followed the story fine up to there, but something jams at the final sentence -- they refuse to go along with it even as make-believe.

Story B: same basic plot, but now the world is thickened up. In this world there's a genuine god, established early in the fiction, who has arranged things so that the old are reincarnated into new, healthy bodies, and being turned out promptly is actually what secures them a good rebirth. Everyone in the fiction knows this, and it's true in the fiction. Now the "she was treated justly, and rightly so" ending goes down fine, no balk at all.

Here's what I want explained. The balk seems selective in a weird way. We happily imagine all sorts of impossible stuff -- wizards, time travel, talking animals -- so it can't be about mere impossibility. And it can't simply be about the moral claim being false, because in story B the same core claim (turning her out was right) is accepted once the world is built up around it. So what exactly is being resisted in story A?

People float a few diagnoses and I want you to walk me through them:
(1) We're literally failing to imagine it, full stop. If moral truths are necessary truths, then "and they were right" is like being asked to imagine a round square -- there's no coherent content to imagine.
(2) We could imagine it but refuse, because imagining it properly means taking up the evaluative perspective of the fiction, and we balk at doing that even temporarily.
(3) The resistance isn't to the content at all but to the narrator's authority. A bare "and it was right" is the narrator ordering you to feel a certain way, whereas story B earns its moral conclusion by letting you work it out from the world's structure yourself.

In your answer: (a) make clear why each of the three has real pull, (b) explain why story B is a serious problem for diagnosis (1) in particular, and (c) say something about what the balk in A reveals about how fiction smuggles moral content past us in the first place. I'm less interested in which philosopher said what and more in getting the conceptual machinery clear.
