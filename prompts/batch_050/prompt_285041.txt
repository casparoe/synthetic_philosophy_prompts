At a recent meeting of our department's cognitive science journal club, a colleague closed a disagreement about whether patients with anosognosia for hemiplegia — who sincerely report moving a paralyzed limb — genuinely have veridical perceptual experience by remarking that, on the predictive processing picture, perception is a "controlled hallucination" anyway, so there is no interesting difference to explain. Since then I have noticed the same slogan doing quite different work in different mouths: sometimes it appears to be the modest point that prior expectations shape perceptual experience; sometimes the much stronger claim that the brain constructs, rather than receives, its model of the world; and sometimes something approaching the view that we are never in direct perceptual contact with external reality at all.

I would like the slogan treated as a piece of language in need of disambiguation. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, ordered from the near-trivial to the genuinely radical. For each thesis, make clear what it asserts about the respective contributions of top-down prediction and bottom-up sensory signal, what it implies about veridicality and perceptual contact with the world, and how it relates to the standard evidence cited for predictive processing (binocular rivalry, perceptual illusions, and the like). Include at least one reading grounded specifically in the free-energy formulation — the claim that neural dynamics collectively minimize a bound on surprise — and say whether that reading adds anything to the others or merely redescribes them.

For each thesis on the menu, then give three verdicts: (i) whether it is a platitude no serious theory of perception would deny, a substantive empirical hypothesis, or a claim that is arguably false or incoherent; (ii) whether the predictive processing literature actually supports it, or whether the evidence underdetermines it; and (iii) whether it would license my colleague's move — that is, whether it entails that there is no theoretically interesting difference between ordinary perception and the anosognosiac's confabulation.

Finally, address the qualifier "controlled": state what work that word does on each reading, and whether any single thesis earns both words of the slogan at once. Conclude by saying which reading you believe competent users of the phrase most often intend, and whether the slogan's rhetorical effectiveness depends on sliding between a weak reading that is defensible and a strong reading that is not.
