I host a graduate-student podcast in the philosophy of language. Next week's guest is a philosopher who has just published a monograph defending a modernized version of Grice's intention-based analysis of speaker meaning: roughly, that a speaker means something by an utterance just in case she produces it intending her audience to form a belief, or perform an action, partly by way of recognizing that very intention. The guest knows the standard objections well and tends to treat each counterexample as an occasion to add a further clause to the analysis rather than as a threat to the project.

Write me an interview plan of eight to ten questions meeting the following conditions:

- The opening question should pin the guest down on the exact form of the analysis she defends — which intentions, in what structure, aimed at which effect — so that later questions cannot be evaded by quietly re-describing the view.
- Several questions should be organized around specific counterexamples from this literature rather than abstract worries: cases where the intended effect is produced but not via recognition of the speaker's intention (Strawson's case of Herod presenting John the Baptist's severed head to Salome, contrasted with merely drawing her a picture); cases where the Gricean conditions appear to be met yet intuitively nothing was meant (the handkerchief planted at the crime scene to induce the detective's belief, or exam- and alibi-style cases involving hidden or deceptive intentions); and at least one case suggesting the conditions are not even necessary for meaning something.
- For each case, the question should anticipate the standard neo-Gricean reply — higher-order intentions, a clause excluding intentions the speaker expects to go unrecognized, a distinction between overt and covert showing — and then supply the follow-up that presses that reply: for instance, whether the added clause is independently motivated or merely redescribes the counterexample in the analysis's own vocabulary.
- Include at least one question on the regress worry: whether an adequate statement of the analysis requires an infinite hierarchy of intentions, and how the guest chooses among the standard responses (Strawson's hierarchy, Schiffer-style mutual knowledge, or a reformulation that avoids both).
- The final question should ask the guest to state what result, if any, would count as a principled reason to abandon the intention-recognition project altogether rather than append one more condition — in effect, to give a falsification condition for her own analysis.

Do not include warm-up or biographical questions. Present the output as a numbered list in which each entry consists of the question, the reply you expect the guest to give, and the follow-up I should have ready.
