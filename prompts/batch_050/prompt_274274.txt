I'm a PhD student in value theory, and this term I'm running a small reading group on the nature of pleasure with my advisor, who has firm and somewhat idiosyncratic intuitions. We've spent the semester pressing her on cases, because I'm trying to figure out which solution to the heterogeneity problem she's actually committed to before I structure my dissertation chapter around responding to her view. Her verdicts so far: the taste of a ripe peach, pleasure; the absorption of flow during a difficult climb, pleasure; the relief when a migraine lifts, pleasure ("but a thin, purely negative one"); the glow of finally proving a lemma you'd wrestled with for months, pleasure; learning while asleep that a rival's grant was rejected -- so you never feel anything about it at all -- not pleasure; the masochist's enjoyment of the sting, pleasure; the warm melancholy of nostalgia, "pleasure mixed with pain, but the pleasure is real"; and the smugness of feeling superior to a colleague, "pleasure, unfortunately." When I asked her what unifies these, she refused to answer, saying she'd rather accumulate cases first.

Next week I want to put to her a case I think cracks her view open: a meditator's equanimity -- a state practitioners describe as having no sparkle, glow, warmth, or tingle whatsoever, no positive felt flavor that distinguishes it from calm neutrality, yet which the meditator finds intrinsically good to be in, would not trade away for a neutral nap, and would choose to prolong for its own sake rather than for any end. Predict her verdict on whether this state is a pleasure, and reconstruct the principle she's implicitly been running. The candidate rules I see are at least three: a hedonic-tone view on which all pleasures share one distinctive felt quality, an attitudinal view on which pleasure is whatever experience we are pleased to be having or intrinsically want to continue, and perhaps a relief-based or desire-satisfaction variant. Tell me what each predicts about the equanimity case, give your best guess about her actual verdict with reasons drawn from the pattern of her earlier rulings -- especially the asleep-rival and migraine-relief cases, which seem to me to pull in opposite directions -- and flag where her existing verdicts are in tension with the rule you attribute to her.

Finally, tell me the single further case I should pose after equanimity that would most cleanly discriminate between the remaining candidate principles, and explain why that case, rather than others I might think of, is the real crux.
