I am preparing a seventy-five-minute seminar for a mixed group of public-policy students and clinicians who understand basic utilitarian reasoning but have not previously studied Scanlon. I want to teach one focused idea: why Scanlon’s contractualism makes the aggregation of harms difficult, and how a view organized around individual complaints can nevertheless sometimes justify saving the greater number.

Plan how I should teach this rather than simply explaining the theory to me. Build the session around one invented rescue case presented in three stages: a boat can reach only one of two groups before a storm; first, one person will die unless rescued while five people elsewhere will die unless rescued; second, one person will die while five will suffer severe, permanent, but nonfatal disabilities; third, one person will die while fifty will experience intense but fully temporary pain. Keep probabilities certain and remove special obligations, prior wrongdoing, scarcity of rescue effort, and other avoidable complications. The same case should carry the whole session, with the staged variations doing the conceptual work.

Explain the order in which the components must be introduced and what each depends on: justifiability to each person, reasonable rejection rather than actual consent, the individual standpoint, generic reasons for complaint, comparison of the strongest relevant complaints, and only then the question whether smaller complaints can be aggregated. I am especially interested in how to prevent the class from hearing individual complaint as either personal veto or strongest single interest wins.

Use a board device called a complaint ledger. For each stage, record who is burdened, what happens to them, the severity and reversibility of the burden, the strongest generic complaint available to that individual, and whether another person’s comparable complaint can reinforce that complaint, merely break a tie, or cannot properly be counted. Explain where this device helps and where it risks covertly reintroducing utilitarian summation.

Map the misconceptions the students are likely to form in the order they are likely to arise. These should include the ideas that contractualism is hypothetical unanimous consent; that reasonable rejection means rationally self-interested refusal; that any seriously burdened person has a veto; that numbers never matter on Scanlon’s view; that all harms can simply be totaled; and that a tie requires an arbitrary coin flip. For each misconception, identify exactly where in the lesson I should head it off and what question or board entry would do so.

Conclude the planned session with one transfer case of your own design, in which the right answer requires students to distinguish severity, comparability, and aggregation rather than merely count heads. The check should require them to identify the strongest individual complaint on each side and state what role, if any, the number of similarly situated people plays.

Structure the response under these headings, in this order and at roughly these lengths: Teaching Objective and Dependency Chain, 150 words; The Rescue Case and Its Three Stages, 250 words; Session Sequence, 350 words; Misconception Map, 300 words; The Complaint Ledger, 200 words; Check for Understanding, 150 words; What to Leave Out, 100 words. Do not provide a reading list or citations, and do not turn the lesson into a general survey of contractualism.
