I have been collecting objections to massive modularity — the thesis, associated with evolutionary psychology, that the mind consists largely or entirely of domain-specific computational modules, including its central, reasoning systems. I want a further objection, but so that you do not repeat what I already have, here is my list.

1. Fodor's abduction argument. Central cognition — belief fixation, scientific and everyday reasoning — is abductive: whether a hypothesis merits acceptance can depend on considerations drawn from anywhere in the believer's total system. Abductive inference cannot be carried out by informationally encapsulated modules, since encapsulation is precisely the restriction of the information a mechanism may consult. Massive modularity therefore either inherits the frame problem in its most severe form or concedes that central processing is not modular, which surrenders the thesis.

2. The plasticity objection. Cortex is strikingly uniform in its fine structure, and development shows substantial cross-domain plasticity: tissue that would normally serve one sensory modality can come to serve another when its inputs are rerouted early in life. This suggests a relatively general-purpose learning device that acquires specialized competences, rather than a large suite of pre-specified special-purpose devices.

3. The evolutionary novelty objection. Humans reason competently about chess, calculus, insurance contracts, and microprocessors — domains with no evolutionary history in which selection could have fashioned a dedicated module. Facility in genuinely novel domains points to domain-general machinery that takes whatever content it is fed.

4. The proliferation problem. If every distinct competence requires its own module — cheater detection, face recognition, folk physics, folk biology, mindreading, and so on — the required number of modules becomes enormous, and the picture loses its explanatory economy: positing a module for X threatens to redescribe the competence rather than explain it.

5. The granularity problem. There appears to be no principled answer to how finely modules are to be carved. Is there one module for social exchange, or separate ones for threats, promises, and fair division? Without an individuation criterion, the thesis is empirically slippery, since any recalcitrant result can be absorbed by positing another module at a finer grain.

What I am after is an objection that is not on this list and not a variant of anything on it: not a rewording, not a special case (for instance, naming one more evolutionarily novel domain), and not a mere conjunction of two entries. It should target the thesis as stated — that central cognition, not only perception, is massively modular. If the best available further objection turns out on inspection to be a dressed-up version of one of mine, say so plainly rather than forcing novelty. After presenting the objection, assess it: whether it is any good, how it relates to the five above, and how a defender of massive modularity would most plausibly reply.
