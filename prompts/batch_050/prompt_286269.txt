I run a graduate working group on philosophy of biology. For six months our discussions of function have split along a now-familiar line: two members defend the selected-effects (etiological) account, on which a trait's function is whatever it was historically selected for; two defend a causal-role account, on which a trait's function is the contribution it in fact makes to some capacity of the containing system. Every session ends the same way: the etiological side wheels out malfunction (a diseased heart still has the function of pumping blood, and the causal-role theorist allegedly cannot say so), and the causal-role side wheels out novelty (a structure doing useful work on its first occurrence, or in a lineage propagated without selection, allegedly has a function the etiological theorist must deny). Both sides agree the exchange is stuck but disagree about why.

I want you to draft the joint statement this group should produce as an adversarial collaboration, so we can workshop it at our next meeting. Structure it as follows:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of claims all four members can sign — about what functions-talk is for in biology, about what any adequate account must explain, and about which stock examples (the heart, vestigial organs, exaptations like feathers, traits fixed by drift) are data rather than theory. These must be substantive, not vacuous: a claim like "functions matter in biology" does not count.

2. Disagreements, exactly stated. Each residual disagreement phrased in neutral language that both camps would accept as a fair statement of their own position. In particular, distinguish the disagreement about malfunction from the disagreement about novel or selection-free function, and check whether there is a third disagreement about what the analysis of "function" is even supposed to deliver (a definition, a regimentation for science, an account of normativity) that has been masquerading as the first two.

3. Cruxes and tests. For each genuine disagreement, identify the crux: the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side. Then, for each crux, describe a concrete case or finding both sides agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts the right verdict on that case is. To force the issue, the statement must include this stipulated case at minimum: a bacterial lineage maintained for thousands of generations in a chemostat under deliberately random sampling, so that no trait has been under selection, in which a particular gene product nonetheless reliably detoxifies the medium and thereby keeps the population alive. Do the etiological theorists deny this gene product has that function? Do the causal-role theorists accept that saying so strips "function" of its normativity? Record each side's committed answer and what it would take for them to concede the case is decided against them.

The whole document should run about 800–1,000 words, be written in the first-person plural as if signed by all four members, and avoid adjudicating who is right — its job is to make the remaining disagreement precise enough that our next meeting is about evidence and cases rather than restatements of Wright and Cummins.
