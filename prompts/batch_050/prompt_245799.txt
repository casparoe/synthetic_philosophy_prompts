So I host a small podcast on medicine and philosophy, pretty low-key, and next week my guest is a neurologist-philosopher who's written a bunch defending the whole-brain criterion of death. She thinks the standard tests are basically fine, that death is a biological event and not a social construction, and she pushes back hard on both the higher-brain people (who'd count persistent vegetative patients as dead) and the people who say brain-dead patients are plainly still alive and we should just admit organ procurement is killing them.

I've interviewed defenders of brain death before and the conversation always stalls at the same place, so I want to do better this time. Draft me a set of interview questions, in order, that actually press on the weak points. Specifically I want:

1. At least one question about the chronic survival cases -- people diagnosed brain dead by accepted criteria whose bodies, with support, kept going for months or years, maintained temperature, fought infections, in one case gestated a pregnancy. She'll say the brain-dead body is just a ventilated corpse doing things on reflex. I need a follow-up for that reply.

2. At least one on the "integrative unity" move -- the idea that the brain is what integrates the organism into a whole, so lose the brain, lose the organism. The critics point out that plenty of integration (blood pressure regulation, wound healing, homeostasis) clearly survives brain death. I want a question that forces her to say which functions count as "integrating the organism as a whole" without her just defining the term so the answer comes out in her favor.

3. One question on controlled donation after circulatory death, since she's also defended that practice: if five minutes of pulselessness is enough to declare someone dead, but the same person could in principle be resuscitated with CPR during those five minutes, is she using "irreversible" to mean "won't be reversed because we've decided not to try"? And if so, doesn't that make death depend on our intentions, which is exactly the "death is a social construction" view she rejects?

4. A question about why she resists the higher-brain view -- is it a principled objection or just that the implications (burying people who breathe on their own) are unpalatable?

5. And to close, one question about what finding would make her give up the whole-brain criterion -- an actual falsifiability question, not a soft one.

For each question, give me the answer she's most likely to give (I've read her stuff, she'll defend integration as the criterion and say permanence is enough for circulatory death) plus a follow-up keyed to that answer. No softballs, no "what got you interested in this" questions please. Also keep in mind shes quick and will dodge hypotheticals by saying they're empirically impossible, so where a question rests on a hypothetical, phrase it so that dodge is harder to pull off.
