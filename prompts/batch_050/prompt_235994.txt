You are a philosopher of mathematics and physics with particular expertise in infinite processes and the literature on supertasks. Before answering, think carefully through the dispute below; you may find it useful to first sketch, as scratchwork, the strongest defensible version of each party's position before rendering any verdicts.

Last week, after a department colloquium on supertasks, I listened to two graduate students, Mara and Jon, argue about Thomson's lamp for nearly an hour. Neither convinced the other, and I've been asked by both to find a referee. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

Setup both accepted: a lamp is off at time 0. It is switched on at t = 1/2, off at t = 3/4, on at t = 7/8, and so on — one switch at each time 1 − 1/2^n, infinitely many switches, all completed before t = 1. Question: at t = 1, is the lamp on or off?

(1) Mara opened by saying the question has no answer, and that this is the whole moral of the puzzle. The description of the supertask fixes the lamp's state at every time strictly before t = 1 and simply does not constrain t = 1; both "on at 1" and "off at 1" are logically consistent completions of the story. She compared it to the alternating sum 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + ..., which has no limit: asking about the lamp at t = 1 is like asking whether that series sums to an even or an odd number — the question misfires.

(2) Jon replied that the series analogy proves too much. Mathematicians do assign that alternating sum a value, namely 1/2 (it is the Cesàro sum, and it is the value forced by continuing the geometric series formula 1/(1 + x) to x = 1). So "no limit" is a fact about one particular notion of convergence, not a deep fact about anything. A physical lamp at t = 1 is determinately on or off, so the supertask description is not merely silent but physically incomplete — and his diagnosis is that the scenario is physically impossible, since no mechanism can perform infinitely many switches in finite time, in which case the question never arises.

(3) Mara answered that he was conflating two different claims. The Cesàro value is a convention — useful, but it says nothing about what the partial sums actually do, and the lamp's state is analogous to a partial sum (the lamp genuinely is on or off at each stage), not to an assigned sum. And the physical-impossibility move, she said, is a retreat: the logical point stands regardless of engineering, since even granting the supertask, no contradiction follows from either final state — so the final state is underdetermined, not impossible, and declaring the scenario physically impossible just changes the subject.

(4) Jon's counter: her "underdetermination" verdict papers over distinctions that matter. Consider a ball bouncing on a hard floor, each bounce taking half as long as the one before — infinitely many bounces before t = 1, and everyone agrees the ball is at rest on the floor at t = 1, the end state being fixed by the continuity of the motion. So "infinitely many events with no last event, therefore no fact about the limit" is false as a general principle; continuity assumptions can and do determine end states. The lamp, he said, differs only in that no continuous mechanism was stipulated. If we idealize the switch as a pure toggle with no path between positions, the scenario is inconsistent as a description of a physical lamp; if we allow a continuous switching mechanism, the state at t = 1 is fixed by the limit of the mechanism's position. Either way, "no answer" is wrong: the answer is either "impossible scenario" or "determined by the mechanism."

(5) Mara's final response: the bouncing ball is not a counterexample to her, it is an illustration of her. The conclusion there comes entirely from an added physical assumption (continuity of motion), which is exactly her claim — the bare supertask description does not determine the outcome; extra assumptions do. And the toggle idealization is precisely the case where no continuous limit exists, so Jon's "determined by the mechanism" has no content in the only version of the puzzle anyone cares about. Jon said something I couldn't follow about whether a lamp switched infinitely many times would even count as the same lamp, and then they had to leave.

Please adjudicate. For each of moves (1) through (5), say whether it is clearly correct, clearly mistaken, or defensible but incomplete, with a brief justification that engages the specific argument made — drawing, where relevant, on the standard distinctions in this area (logical consistency versus causal or physical determination, limit versus assigned sum, what a description entails versus what it leaves open). Then give an overall verdict: who is closer to right, on which specific points, and what, if anything, are both of them missing? I want a point-by-point refereeing of the arguments as actually made, not a general overview of Thomson's lamp.
