A colleague and I have produced the following argument, and we are embarrassed by it: the conclusion strikes us as clearly false, yet we cannot locate the error. We would like a diagnosis.

Let a life's quality be fixed entirely by what it contains, and compare outcomes solely with respect to the lives lived in them. Consider two outcomes. Alpha contains a single person who lives for one hundred and twenty years at the highest quality of life a human being could enjoy: deep love, significant achievement, aesthetic and intellectual rapture, unbroken health. Omega contains ten trillion people, each living for centuries at the lowest quality that still makes a life worth living: every day is just barely preferable to not existing at all, containing modest, repetitive pleasures and nothing that would be missed.

Now consider a sequence of outcomes S_0, S_1, …, S_n, where S_0 is Alpha and S_n is Omega, constructed so that each S_{i+1} differs from S_i only in containing somewhat more people, each of whose lives is negligibly worse or negligibly shorter than the lives in S_i — a difference, at each step, smaller than the difference between an excellent day and a merely very good one. (There is evidently room for such a sequence; the distance between Alpha and Omega is vast and continuous in both dimensions.)

The argument:

(1) Transitivity. If outcome X is at least as good as outcome Y, and Y is at least as good as outcome Z, then X is at least as good as Z.

(2) The spectrum step. For each i, S_{i+1} is at least as good as S_i. Rationale: at each step the per-person loss is negligible — below the threshold at which anyone could notice or rationally care — while the number of worthwhile lives increases substantially, and an enormous gain in the number of good lives surely compensates for a negligible loss in their quality.

(3) The comparative judgment. Alpha is strictly better than Omega: one life of genuine flourishing is a better thing for the universe to contain than any number of lives whose contents barely repay the trouble of being lived.

From (1) and (2), Omega is at least as good as Alpha. Together with (3) this is a contradiction; so if the reasoning stands, (3) must be surrendered, and we must accept that a boundless crowd of barely-worth-living lives outranks a life of bliss. We cannot accept this.

If forced to choose, I would rather abandon (2) than (1) or (3): giving up transitivity feels like giving up the idea that outcomes can be ranked at all, and (3) seems closer to a datum than to a premise. But I do not want my preferences to do the argumentative work, and I am prepared to be told that they are exactly what has to go.

Please do the following. First, identify the premise or step that should be rejected, and state precisely what rejecting it commits one to. Second, explain why that premise looked innocent — what makes the argument seductive rather than obviously broken. Third, price the leading alternatives: denying transitivity (as on views where betterness is essentially comparative, so that whether one outcome beats another can depend on which other outcomes are in the comparison); holding that some spectrum step must fail even though we cannot say which, on a sorites-style diagnosis where betterness is vague or indeterminate; holding that the per-person losses, though tiny at each step, are real and accumulate, so that the sequence was never improving in the first place; and denying that every adjacent pair is comparable at all, so the chain breaks without any failure of transitivity. Fourth, say whether the argument can be rebuilt in a form that escapes your diagnosis — for instance, with some steps strengthened from weak to strict betterness — or whether every such repair merely relocates the problem.
