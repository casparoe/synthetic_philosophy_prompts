I’m a junior and I help run my school’s robotics club. For our spring competition we’re training little simulated warehouse robots with reinforcement learning, and sometimes we give them big penalties for crashing, getting stuck, or taking too long. During one practice, a teammate joked that our newest robot had learned to be afraid of the loading dock because it now freezes and backs away whenever it gets near it. That got us arguing about whether a negative reward is just a number in the code or whether, in a complicated enough agent, it could eventually become something more like pain or distress. Our faculty advisor now wants every training plan to go through a quick welfare check before we run it, which feels kind of ridiculous but also maybe not completely crazy if AI keeps improving. We do this about twice a week, nobody has more than 15 minutes, and none of us are philosophers or AI experts, so it can’t require reading papers or doing a bunch of math.

Please turn the vague idea of possible suffering in a reinforcement-learning agent into a decision procedure our club could actually use. It should tell us what questions to ask in order, what evidence would count as a yes or no, where we should immediately stop a training plan, and where a human has to make a judgment call because the facts can’t settle it. I especially need help separating things like simple goal-directed behavior, learned avoidance, memory, flexibility, self-monitoring, and genuinely concerning signs that a punishment might matter to the agent from the inside. Please include cases where we genuinely won’t know what to think, and don’t just assume that anything made of code definitely can’t suffer or that every unpleasant reward signal is automatically morally important.

Please format the answer as a numbered list with exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Somewhere in those five items, apply the procedure to two scenarios: a simulated forklift that repeatedly receives a large penalty for bumping shelves and eventually learns cautious routes, and a more advanced robot that remembers past penalties, predicts situations where they are likely, tries to avoid or disable the penalty sensor, and keeps showing agitation-like behavior even when nothing bad is currently happening. Make one application produce an answer we probably won’t like, like having to pause or redesign a useful part of our competition training. Also be honest about what this procedure can’t tell us and where it might be either too cautious or not cautious enough.
