I'm writing an essay for a small philosophy newsletter a friend of mine runs, and I'd like comments before I submit it. It's about why promises bind. The standard answers I know of are roughly: promises bind because breaking them frustrates expectations the promisor deliberately created; because promising is a social practice and breaking a promise is a kind of cheating within that practice; or because promising communicates an assurance. My essay tries a different tack, which I half-believe and half-suspect is confused. Here it is:

"When I promise to pick you up from the airport, I don't merely cause you to expect me, and I don't merely say words that a social convention assigns a meaning to. I hand you something: a standing permission to complain about me if I don't show up. Before the promise, if I failed to appear, you could be disappointed but your complaint would be idle. After the promise, your complaint lands. Promising is the deliberate transfer of a complaint-right, and that's what the obligation consists in. Obligation here just IS someone else holding a legitimate grievance against you, the way owning a house just is others being excluded from it.

"This explains some things the expectation view fumbles. If you never come to rely on my promise (you cancel the flight, forget I offered), the expectation view says the obligation evaporates, but it doesn't feel like it does -- you could still rightly be annoyed I never showed, you just wouldn't be. It also explains why coerced promises don't bind: the transfer of the complaint-right has to be voluntary to be legitimate, the same way a 'gift' handed over at knifepoint isn't a gift.

"One consequence I want to defend: promises to the dead do bind, weakly, because the complaint-right doesn't need a living holder who will actually exercise it, only someone who would have been entitled to exercise it. And self-promises don't bind at all, which is correct: you can't transfer a right to yourself that you already have, so 'I promise myself I'll quit smoking' is strictly empty, though it can be a useful bit of theater."

Two things I want comments on, specifically. First, section 3 (the dead and self-promises) is where my friends push back hardest -- is the move from "a right needs a possible exerciser" to "a right doesn't need an actual exerciser" doing legitimate work, or am I just helping myself to whatever answer sounds good? Second, does the whole view secretly collapse into the social-practice account, since the very idea of a "complaint-right" that others recognize seems to presuppose a standing convention? If so, tell me straight rather than letting me find out from the newsletter's comment section. General comments on whether this is even interesting are welcome too, but those two points are my priority.
