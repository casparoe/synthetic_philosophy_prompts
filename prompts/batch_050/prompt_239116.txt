In a reading group on Gödel, Escher, Bach, I was pressed into defending the book's central move -- the claim that a self is what you get when a symbol system becomes powerful enough to loop back and represent itself, what Hofstadter calls a strange loop -- and in trying to make the claim precise I produced an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept. Here it is.

(1) For a system to have a self -- an "I" -- is for the system to contain a symbol that refers to the system itself. Selfhood just is self-reference achieved at the symbolic level, and it comes in degrees depending on how rich the self-representation is.

(2) Gödel's construction shows how to build, inside any formal system expressive enough to encode arithmetic, a sentence that talks about itself -- one that says, in effect, of itself, that it has a certain property.

(3) So any such system contains self-reference, and contains it entirely on its own resources: the self-referential sentence is a theorem or well-formed formula of the system, not an annotation added from outside.

(4) Peano arithmetic is such a system.

(5) Therefore Peano arithmetic contains a symbol referring to itself, and therefore -- by (1) -- has a self, albeit a very thin one.

(6) But Peano arithmetic is a list of axioms. It does not have a self, not even a thin one. The same goes, a fortiori, for a three-line quine program that prints its own source code, and for the sentence "this sentence contains five words."

(7) So something above is wrong.

The claims I most want to keep are (1) -- because without it, the book's Gödelian analogy seems to shrink from an explanation of consciousness into a suggestive metaphor -- and (2), which is a theorem and not negotiable. That puts the pressure on (3), (5), and (6), and I genuinely cannot tell which one buckles. My suspicion is that the move from (2) to (3), or from (3) to (5), trades on an equivocation between a sentence in a system referring to itself and the system referring to itself -- and that the self-reference in Gödel's construction is something we read into the formalism via an interpretation we supply, rather than something the system does. But I can't make that precise without seeming to concede that no purely syntactic fact could ever amount to self-reference, which would gut (1) anyway.

Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please explain why the bad step looked good, and what a defender of the strange-loop model should say about quines and "this sentence"-type tokens if the model is to avoid counting them as minimal selves.
