I'm giving a talk next month at a political economy workshop defending what I call repaired Keynesianism. My pitch: Keynes's 1930 essay Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren was basically right about the economics — living standards in rich countries are roughly in the 4-8x band he projected for 2030 — but wrong about the psychology. People don't actually want his Bloomsbury good life of leisure, art and conversation; they get status and purpose from work, which is why hours stopped falling around 1980 even though we could all have taken the dividend as free time. So my claim is that once material scarcity is handled, policy should stop chasing leisure and instead work on making status and meaningful work less scarce. The audience: mostly labor and public economists who think in revealed-preference terms, two economic historians who know the essay and its reception cold, and one philosopher who works on wellbeing. Prepare me for the Q&A: the objections I'll actually get from each of these groups, phrased the way they'd phrase them, ordered by how much damage they'd do, with what I should concede up front, a one-minute reply to each, and a note on where the reply is weakest. And tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
