You are a decision theorist with deep expertise in imprecise probability and formal approaches to decision-making under severe uncertainty, and you also have practical experience translating formal theory into operational guidance for public institutions. I am the deputy director of a national environmental regulator. Roughly eight times a year, my office must decide whether to permit, restrict, or ban an industrial activity (a new pesticide registration, a wastewater discharge license, siting a chemical storage facility) where the harms are real but the probabilities cannot be pinned down. We convene a panel of four to seven experts, and they typically give us sharply divergent probability estimates for the same outcome -- one toxicologist says the chance of groundwater contamination is negligible, another says it could be as high as one in five. Our legal environment matters: every decision can be challenged in court, and judges have been skeptical both of decisions that rest on a single spuriously precise number and of decisions that look purely intuitive. My analysts are competent statisticians but have no training in decision theory, and each decision cycle gives us about three weeks.

Please turn the theory of imprecise probabilities -- credal sets, interval-valued probabilities, and the decision rules developed for them -- into a written procedure my office can actually run. Specifically:

1. A step-by-step elicitation stage: how to convert the experts' divergent judgments into an explicit set of probability distributions (or intervals) for each relevant outcome, including what to do when the experts' intervals overlap, when they are disjoint, and when one expert refuses to give any number at all. State what the elicitation stage is allowed to throw out (e.g., demonstrably miscalibrated sources) and what it must keep.

2. A decision stage: an ordered sequence of tests the analysts apply to the candidate actions (permit, permit-with-conditions, ban), grounded in the standard rules for choice under imprecise probabilities -- dominance, maximin expected utility, Gamma-maximin, and minimax regret over the credal set. Specify the order in which the rules are applied, what each rule is allowed to eliminate, and exactly what happens when the surviving rules disagree about which action to take, since they will.

3. An explicit list of the points where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to a named human being because the question is one of values rather than analysis -- for example, how asymmetrically to weigh a small probability of catastrophe against a large probability of modest economic loss -- and how those handoffs should be documented so the record survives judicial review.

4. An honesty section: the two or three known failure modes of this kind of procedure (for instance, what it does when the credal set is so wide that every action looks permissible, or when the experts' disagreement is really about the causal model rather than the probabilities), and what the office should do in each case.

Then apply the procedure, end to end, to these three cases:

- Case A: a herbicide where five experts give contamination-probability intervals that all overlap in the range 0.5% to 3%, and the harm is serious but reversible.
- Case B: a storage facility where two experts give intervals of 0.1%-2% and one expert, whose causal model the others dispute, gives 15%-40%, and the harm is a one-in-generation aquifer loss.
- Case C: a discharge license where the experts agree the probability of harm is between 20% and 60% but the harm is a modest, temporary fishery decline and the economic cost of refusal is very large -- a case where I expect the procedure may give an answer my political leadership will not like, and I want to see that it does.

For each of the three cases, after the prose walkthrough, provide a JSON object with exactly these fields: "case" (the case label), "credal_set_summary" (a one-sentence description of the probability set used), "rules_applied_in_order" (an array of the rules actually fired, in order), "rule_conflicts" (an array describing any points where two rules recommended different actions, or an empty array), "verdict" (one of "permit", "permit_with_conditions", "ban", "handed_to_human"), "judgment_handoffs" (an array of the points where the procedure deferred to a person, or an empty array), and "litigation_vulnerability" (a one-sentence statement of where the decision record is most exposed to legal challenge).
