The homework question was: "State Hempel's ravens paradox, identifying the two principles about confirmation that generate it. What was Hempel's own response to the paradox? Some philosophers respond by appeal to degrees of confirmation in a Bayesian sense: explain how that response goes, and say whether it dissolves the paradox or merely explains why the conclusion feels wrong. Finally, briefly: would the paradox arise in the same form for a statistical generalization like 'Most ravens are black'? Why or why not?"

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"The paradox is generated by two principles. The first is Nicod's criterion: a universal generalization of the form 'All Fs are G' is confirmed by its positive instances, i.e. by observing something that is both F and G, and disconfirmed by counterinstances. The second is the equivalence condition: whatever confirms a hypothesis also confirms any hypothesis logically equivalent to it. Now, 'All ravens are black' is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, 'All non-black things are non-ravens.' By Nicod's criterion, the contrapositive is confirmed by observing a non-black non-raven, such as a white shoe. By the equivalence condition, the white shoe therefore confirms 'All ravens are black.' But it seems absurd that what has been called indoor ornithology — examining shoes and pencils from your desk — could constitute evidence about raven coloration.

Hempel's own response was to reject the equivalence condition. Since the paradoxical inference goes through only because equivalent hypotheses are held to share their evidence, denying that principle blocks the move from the shoe to the ravens while leaving Nicod's criterion intact. Later writers, however, have generally kept both principles and treated the conclusion as sound.

The standard Bayesian response holds that both a black raven and a white shoe confirm the hypothesis, but to wildly different degrees. Because there are vastly more non-black things than ravens, the observation of a non-black non-raven raises the probability of 'All ravens are black' only by a minute amount, while a black raven raises it substantially. So the Bayesian conclusion is that Nicod's criterion is wrong: strictly speaking, instances do not confirm generalizations at all, and they only seem to because we overlook how small the confirmation can be in cases like the shoe. This dissolves the paradox rather than merely explaining away its counterintuitive appearance.

For 'Most ravens are black' the paradox does not get going in the same way, because contraposition fails for statistical generalizations: 'Most ravens are black' is not logically equivalent to 'Most non-black things are non-ravens' (the latter is presumably true on independent grounds, since most things are not ravens). So the equivalence condition cannot be applied, and the bearing of the white shoe on the original claim is unclear at best."
