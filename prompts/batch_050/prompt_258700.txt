I'm a high school senior taking a philosophy elective on rational choice, and in two weeks our class is holding a formal debate. I've been assigned the affirmative side of the motion that rational preferences must be transitive: that anyone who prefers A to B, B to C, and yet C to A is thereby being irrational, full stop. My teacher has warned me that the standard argument for this, the money pump, is more controversial than it looks in our textbook, and I want to rehearse against serious resistance rather than the easy version. So I'd like you to play my opponent: a sharp, committed philosopher who denies that transitivity is a requirement of rationality. Stay in character until I say "stop," keep each reply to a single paragraph so it feels like a real exchange, and be a worthy adversary rather than a strawman: you may concede small points, such as that being pumped for money is obviously bad or that most real people with cyclic preferences are merely confused, but you must never concede the main thesis that cyclic preferences are in themselves irrational.

To make your side fair, here's the position I expect you to hold and defend: the money pump argument cheats. It convicts a person of irrationality not for any single choice they make, since each individual trade is perfectly rational by their own lights at the moment they make it, but for the pattern of choices across time, and it's not obvious why a theory of rational preference should govern sequences rather than single decisions. You're also welcome to press on whatever details of the pump you find suspicious, such as whether the victim is allowed to see the whole sequence of offers coming, and to demand that I state the argument precisely before you answer it. If I make a genuinely good point, acknowledge it the way a confident debater would, then show me why it doesn't touch your core claim.

Here is my opening move, which your first reply should answer directly. Suppose you prefer a mountain bike to a phone, the phone to a concert ticket, and the ticket back to the bike, and that each preference is strong enough that you'd pay a dollar to swap the less preferred item for the more preferred one. I hand you the bike for free. You pay a dollar to trade it for the ticket, a dollar to trade the ticket for the phone, and a dollar to trade the phone back for the bike. You now hold exactly what you started with and you're three dollars poorer, and I can run the loop as many times as your wallet allows. A preference ordering that lets a stranger drain your bank account using nothing but your own choices is a defect in you, not in me. How do you respond?
