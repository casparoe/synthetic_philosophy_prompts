I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm drafting a piece on why human thinking seems so much more powerful than animal thinking. I wanted to lean on Daniel Dennett's framework of four kinds of minds, but I'm worried the only version of it I've actually read closely is a summary in another outlet, and I'd rather not launder someone else's mistakes into my copy. Their piece described it like this:

'Dennett divides minds into four species. Darwinian creatures, like bacteria and most animals, get their behavioral repertoire purely from natural selection: what works is hardwired into the genes, what doesn't dies with the organism. Skinnerian creatures can also learn within their own lifetime by trial and error, keeping whatever behavior gets rewarded. Popperian creatures go one better: they can simulate possible actions in their heads and discard the bad ones before acting, so that their hypotheses die instead of them. Finally, Gregorian creatures, which is just humans, can use cultural tools like language and writing to think with. Because these tools are passed down genetically, Dennett says each later kind replaced the earlier one: once a species reaches the Skinnerian stage it no longer relies on natural selection to shape behavior, and once it reaches the Popperian stage it stops learning from reward altogether.'

Parts of this sound right to me based on half-remembered college philosophy, but a couple of claims bug me. The genetic bit seems backwards -- I thought the point of the Gregorian stuff was that the tools spread culturally, not through genes. And the 'each stage replaces the last' framing sounds fishy too, since presumably we humans still learn from being rewarded, it's not like that machinery got switched off. But the piece was written by someone with a PhD so I don't want to just trust my gut.

Can you audit it for me? Tell me which parts Dennett would accept, which are plausible-sounding distortions, and which are flat-out wrong, and for each correction say roughly how confident you are in it. Also, since this is going in print and I really don't want a correction note from an angry philosopher, please flag whichever point of your audit you're least sure of, so I know where to be extra careful or just cut.
