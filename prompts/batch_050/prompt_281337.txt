The essay question, as my colleague set it, was:

In chapter V of the Second Treatise, Locke argues that labour can make private property of what was held in common, at least where there is "enough, and as good, left in common for others." Reconstruct his argument for why such acquisition is legitimate without the consent of others, explain the limits he places on legitimate accumulation, and assess whether the introduction of money removes those limits or merely transforms them.

The student's answer, in full, was:

Locke begins from two premises: that God gave the earth to mankind in common, and that every person has a property in his own person and in the labour of his body. Because my labour is unquestionably mine, when I remove something out of the state nature left it in and mix my labour with it, it becomes my property, provided enough and as good is left for others. The great strength of this account is that it explains how private property can arise without anyone's consent: had the agreement of all the commoners been required before anyone could appropriate, mankind would have starved amid the plenty God provided.

Locke does impose a limit on accumulation, which he expresses in two ways: nothing may be taken that will spoil in one's possession, and appropriation must leave enough and as good for others. These are best read as two formulations of a single constraint, since both come to the requirement that one's taking not prejudice anyone else. The invention of money dissolves this constraint. Because gold and silver do not spoil, a person may heap up as much of them as they please, and by tacitly consenting to the use of money, everyone has agreed in advance to the unequal holdings that result. The proviso therefore ceases to bind once a monetary economy is in place.

Consent in fact runs deeper in Locke's theory than is usually noticed. Just as money rests on tacit agreement, so does the labourer's original title: mixing labour generates property only because the community acquiesces in appropriation by labour as a practice. Property is thus conventional all the way down for Locke, though the conventions are tacit rather than express.

Locke supports this with a comparative argument: a day-labourer in England lives better than a king over the uncultivated lands of America, because enclosure and industry make land vastly more productive. Since commercial society improves everyone's condition, the requirement that enough and as good remain is in practice guaranteed wherever labour and money have done their work.
