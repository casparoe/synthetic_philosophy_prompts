I'm trying to work out what I think about moral testimony by going through cases, and I'd like to know what principle my verdicts add up to — or, failing that, which verdict I should abandon.

1. As a child I believed my parents that cruelty is wrong, years before I could see why for myself. Verdict: fine.
2. A historian whose judgment I trust tells me that a proposed boycott would backfire. I accept her empirical claim on her say-so. Verdict: fine.
3. A friend with excellent moral antennae tells me the toast I plan to give at a wedding would land as a dig at the groom's family. I can't see it, but I believe her and rewrite the toast. Verdict: fine.
4. The same friend tells me eating meat is wrong. I can't follow the arguments well enough to see it myself, but she is usually right, so I become a vegetarian and now hold the conviction on her authority. Verdict: this feels off, though I struggle to say why.
5. A colleague testifies that our hiring rubric disadvantages applicants from certain backgrounds; I don't fully grasp her reasoning, but I put the rubric on hold. Verdict: fine.
6. Someone I barely know tells me a mutual acquaintance deserves to be ostracized; I join in without learning the facts. Verdict: not fine.

Cases 3 and 4 are the pair I find hardest to keep apart: in both I defer to the same trusted friend on a moral matter I cannot see for myself, yet 3 seems fine and 4 does not. Is there a single principle that yields all six verdicts — perhaps one distinguishing belief from action, particular judgments from general principles, or matters where moral understanding is owed from those where it isn't? If no such principle exists, please say which verdict you would overturn and why. Finally, tell me what the best candidate principle predicts about a case I keep circling back to: a survivor tells me that a "harmless" family tradition is in fact a humiliation ritual, and even after hearing her out, I still cannot see it.
