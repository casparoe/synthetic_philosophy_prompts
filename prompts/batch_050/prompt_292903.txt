For several years I have kept a notebook of my intuitive verdicts about the Ship of Theseus and its variants, and I would now like to know what principle, if any, those verdicts collectively endorse. Here are the cases, with my judgments as they actually strike me rather than as I think they should.

1. The harbor master's gradual repairs. Over a century, every rotten plank, rope, and spar of Theseus's ship is replaced with a new one of the same material, one piece at a time, and the ship is never out of service for more than a day. Verdict: still Theseus's ship, throughout and at the end.
2. The collector's reconstruction. Every discarded original plank has been kept in a warehouse, and a collector with no connection to the ship's crew or owners eventually reassembles them into a ship-shaped whole. Verdict: not Theseus's ship, despite being made of exactly the original matter.
3. The museum relocation. Theseus's ship is carefully dismantled plank by plank, each piece numbered, shipped across the country, and reassembled by conservators. Verdict: still Theseus's ship. I note that this sits uneasily with my verdict in case 2, since both end with the original material reassembled into a ship after a period of disassembly.
4. The fire and the faithful rebuild. The ship burns to ashes, and shipwrights construct a plank-for-plank replica from the original drawings. Verdict: not the same ship, however perfect the replica.
5. The gradual conversion. Over decades of continuous use and modification, the ship is altered until not one part conforms to the original design, though the process was unbroken. Verdict: still Theseus's ship. I am less confident here than in case 1.
6. The plank exchange. Two ships moored in the same harbor swap planks one at a time over the years until each carries half the other's original material. Verdict: each ship remains itself; neither has become the other.
7. The long dry dock. The ship sits untouched in dry dock for three hundred years and is then sailed again. Verdict: obviously still Theseus's ship.
8. The mixed reassembly. The numbered planks from the museum relocation are reassembled together with an equal number of newly cut planks, interleaved. Verdict: this is the case I go back and forth on; I lean toward saying it is still the ship, but I cannot say why the proportion should matter.

What I want is not a survey of positions but a candidate principle — one, or a small set of rivals — stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing the work for which case. In particular, the principle must explain why case 2 and case 3 come apart even though both involve reassembly of original material after disassembly, and why case 1 and case 4 come apart even though both involve the total turnover of material. I would like to know which of my verdicts the simplest adequate principle forces me to abandon, and what it costs, in terms of additional complexity, to save that verdict instead. Finally, tell me what the resulting principle predicts for two cases I have not listed: a ship whose planks are gradually replaced by newly fabricated copies indistinguishable from the originals down to the grain, and a ship that is continuously crewed, renamed, rerigged, and repurposed as a floating restaurant for fifty years before being restored to working order. If the principle delivers what I consider the wrong answer to either of those, say so plainly rather than rescuing it with a qualification.
