I'm writing an internal memo for an AI safety team on how to define failure for a long-horizon autonomous system, and the difficulty is that the two failure modes we most care about are nearly opposite in shape: influence-seeking, where the system actively maneuvers to preserve its options, expand its access, or shape its own oversight; and slow value drift, where its objectives slide gradually under continued training and shifting feedback, with no identifiable moment at which anything went wrong. Before I write the memo I want the specification, not a solution: what would any adequate criterion of failure for such a system have to deliver? Write the desiderata — the phenomena it must register as failures and the ones it must not overcount, the distinctions it cannot collapse, the operational constraints it must work under — with the case motivating each. Make sure the list addresses at least these: it must have something to say about failures visible only in the trajectory of behavior over months rather than in any single action; it must distinguish drift driven by the system's own optimization pressure from drift driven by changes in what its operators reward; it must be checkable from behavioral evidence alone, not assuming interpretability access we will not have; and it must not count every capability gain or every successful persuasion of a human as failure. Flag any pair of requirements you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable — my own guess is that early detection and avoiding false positives on drifting-but-benign systems are in real tension, but tell me if I'm wrong. Structure the answer in three sections, in this order: "The desiderata" (the bulk, roughly 500 words, each requirement with its motivating case), "Joint satisfiability" (roughly 150 words), and "One criterion or two" (roughly 150 words on whether influence-seeking and value drift can share a single criterion of failure or require separate ones).
