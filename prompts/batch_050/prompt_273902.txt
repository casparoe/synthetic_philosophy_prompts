My roommate is taking a philosophy of mind class this semester and he's picked up the annoying habit of ending arguments with "the whole is more than the sum of its parts." Last week I said something like, maybe consciousness will turn out to be fully explainable in terms of neurons doing their thing, and he hit me with the slogan like it was checkmate. Then yesterday we were arguing about whether a football team's "team spirit" is anything over and above the players plus their coordination, and out it came again. It's driving me a bit nuts because it feels like it should mean something but I genuinely can't tell what.

He's also been name-dropping Dennett, the whole "cranes vs skyhooks" thing — cranes being slow, boring explanations built up level by level from the ground, skyhooks being mysterious top-down helpers that just float in and do the work for free. Though honestly I suspect he mostly just likes saying the word skyhook.

Here's what I actually want. Take that slogan and spell out the different precise claims it could be making, as a menu. I can gesture at some candidates: (1) a trivial reading where a whole is obviously more than a mere heap of parts because arrangement and organization matter — you can't build a car out of an unordered list; (2) a stronger reading about interaction effects, where the parts' joint behavior can't be predicted from studying each part seperately; (3) a really strong reading where wholes have causal powers that somehow float free of whatever the parts are doing; (4) maybe a purely epistemic version that's only about the limits of our predictive abilities. But I'm sure there are more, and I don't trust my own taxonomy.

For each reading: is it a platitude, a live substantive thesis, or actually false? And crucially — which readings, if any, would actually justify what my roommate was doing, i.e. treating the slogan as a reason to stop looking for reductive explanations? My suspicion is that the true readings are all compatible with "cranes all the way down," and the readings that would get him his checkmate are secretly demands for a skyhook. Tell me whether that suspicion holds up, and if it doesn't, what the best non-skyhook version of his move would be.

Please answer in British English — I'm trying to purge my American spelling from a paper I'm writing for a UK journal, so I'd like the modelled spelling. A list I can bring to our next argument would be ideal.
