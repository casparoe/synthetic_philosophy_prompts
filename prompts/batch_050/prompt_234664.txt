So I run a little reading group (mostly grad students, one very stubborn engineer) and we've spent the last month going in circles on Malthus and the whole "limits to growth" tradition versus the cornucopians. The fight keeps collapsing into the same move: the limits person says the cornucopian just got lucky so far and the crash is still coming, and the cornucopian says "still coming" is unfalsifiable and points at something like that famous 1980s commodity-price bet the ecologists lost. Someone floated the idea that a theory can be "right but early," and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a legit epistemic category or just an immortal excuse. So I want to force the issue: draft the joint statement these two camps should produce if they actually sat down for an adversarial collaboration.

Structure it like this. First a numbered list of claims BOTH sides can honestly sign — I suspect this includes things like: finite planet means some physical limits exist somewhere, prices are not the only signal of scarcity, past doom deadlines have failed, and exponential growth in any physical quantity can't run forever — but you figure out what's really common ground and phrase it so neither side feels set up. Then a disagreements table where each position is stated the way its own side would state it (no strawmen, they'll check). Then the cruxes: the sub-questions where an answer would actually move somebody. I have guesses — like whether "scarcity shows up in prices before it shows up in physics," whether there's a principled time horizon after which a failed prediction can't be rescued by "just wait," and whether substitution/innovation is a law-like regularity or a contingent 200-year streak — but don't just take mine. For each crux, specify a concrete observation, trend, or agreed-upon metric both sides accept as bearing on it, and write down each side's advance prediction about it. Also, and this is the part I care most about, have the two of them co-author a scoring protocol for long-run predictions: how do you write down a doom-or-boom forecast today (deadlines, tripwires, leading indicators, escape clauses stated in advance) so that in 2060 people can tell whether it was right, wrong, or right-but-early — and does "right but early" survive as a coherent verdict under the protocol, or does it get exposed as a rescue maneuver?

If some of their fight turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, mark which part and say why. End with a one-sentence bottom line, and then a final line with \answer{...} containing a short phrase naming the single crux the joint statement identifies as most likely to actually settle the debate.
