Descartes held that the one sure outward mark of a thinking mind is flexible, context-appropriate speech about anything whatever — no mere machine, he argued, could converse the way even a dull human can — and he treated occurrent thought as indubitable and the mind as better known than the body it inhabits. Suppose he is brought to the present and briefed on three developments. First, the experimental record on confabulation: patients with divided brains who fluently invent explanations for actions initiated by the hemisphere that cannot speak, and experimental subjects whose recorded choices are covertly reversed and who then sincerely defend preferences they never expressed. Second, the view, now common in cognitive science, that reports about one's own reasons are typically post-hoc interpretations rather than readouts of inner causes. Third, language models that pass his conversational test with ease — fluently claiming to doubt, wonder, and desire — while plausibly being exactly the kind of mindless automaton he described. Predict, with reasons, how he would revise his position. Which parts of his introspective epistemology would he regard as untouched (does the cogito care whether people misreport their reasons?), and which would he have to abandon or qualify: the transparency of mind to itself, the conclusion that mind is better known than body, the language test itself? Would the confabulation data or the artificial conversers strike him as the deeper threat, and why? Ground everything in his actual commitments — his theory of judgment, on which error arises when the will assents beyond what the intellect perceives, and his own doctrine that hunger, thirst, and pain are confused modes of thought born of the mind's union with the body — rather than in the caricature of a Descartes who held every self-ascription infallible. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
