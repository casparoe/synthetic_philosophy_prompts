I am the managing editor of a mid-sized popular history press, and my desk gets a steady flow of manuscript proposals that sit somewhere on the spectrum between heterodox scholarship and outright pseudohistory: hidden civilizations, re-dated chronologies, claims that the academic consensus is a cover-up. I currently decide these by instinct, which means my decisions are inconsistent and sometimes wrong in both directions -- I have rejected work that was merely unfashionable and passed along work that was sophisticatedly packaged nonsense. I would like a decision procedure my editorial assistants could run on any proposal in perhaps half an hour, before anything reaches me, grounded in whatever the philosophy of demarcation actually has to offer that survives translation into practice: falsifiability and its well-known limits, the difference between a research programme that is learning from its failures and one that is only absorbing them, the proper location of the burden of proof, and the diagnostic value of how an author treats anomalies and hostile evidence.

Write the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions, with, for each, what counts as a sufficient answer and what triggers an immediate stop with a rejection recommendation. It must handle the characteristic moves of pseudohistory: arguing from gaps in the mainstream account to a positive alternative, treating the absence of evidence as evidence of suppression, citing anomalies while ignoring the base rate of anomalies in any large historical record, and insulating the thesis so that no imaginable discovery could count against it. Mark explicitly the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and must hand the decision up to me, and say which steps are judgment calls dressed up as checklist items.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical proposals: (1) a manuscript arguing that a famous ancient monument could not have been built with the tools its builders possessed, resting its case on impressive-sounding precision measurements and the incompleteness of the orthodox construction account; (2) a manuscript claiming that a settlement is several centuries older than the accepted date, resting on newly excavated material and a published radiocarbon series, whose author describes exactly what result would make her withdraw the claim; and (3) a manuscript arguing that the standard chronology of an entire region is a fabrication, whose author answers every specific objection by widening the circle of who must be in on it. Finally, tell me honestly where this procedure will misfire -- including the kind of case where it will clear a proposal I will wish it had rejected, and the kind where it will reject one I will wish it had cleared.
