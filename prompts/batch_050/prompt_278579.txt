I serve on the planning committee for an annual commemoration in my family's country of origin, and this year our central dispute is whether to keep pressing the government of the country responsible for the historical wrong in question to issue a formal state apology. A widely shared newspaper column has hardened the skeptics among us. Its argument runs on an analogy: an apology, the columnist says, is essentially the outward expression of an inward turn -- the wrongdoer recognizes what they did, feels remorse, and resolves not to repeat it -- and since a state has no inner life, no conscience, and no feelings, demanding an apology from one is like demanding that a doll say it is sorry: you can move its lips, but nothing that makes an apology an apology is present. The people who committed the wrong are mostly dead; today's officials are merely reciting words on behalf of an entity that cannot mean them. Therefore, the column concludes, official apologies are at best hollow theater and at worst a cheap substitute for material redress, and we should stop asking for them.

I find the analogy rhetorically powerful but suspect it is doing more work than it can bear, and I would like a proper audit before our committee votes. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly: in a paradigm interpersonal apology, what plays the role of the wrongdoer, the remorse, the addressed victim, the expression, the credibility conditions, and the forward-looking commitment, and what in the state case is supposed to occupy each of those slots? Then identify where the mapping breaks -- it seems to me that a state, unlike a doll, is a continuing corporate agent that can act, bind itself, hold records, honor veterans, and teach a national curriculum, none of which requires it to feel anything -- and separate the disanalogies that matter for the columnist's conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. I am also interested in what the analogy quietly imports: whether assuming that feeling-remorse is the core of apologizing, rather than, say, acknowledging the wrong, accepting responsibility, or marking a norm as settled, already rigs the conclusion.

Finally, does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? If the interpersonal-apology model underdetermines what a state apology could be, please say what a better-governing analogy would be -- a verdict, a contract, a vow, a funeral rite, a correction of the historical record -- and what each alternative would imply about what our committee should actually be demanding and how we would know if we got the real thing rather than theater.
