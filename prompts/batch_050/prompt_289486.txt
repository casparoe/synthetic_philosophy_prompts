I am a doctoral candidate in political theory, and my dissertation includes a chapter on the chronological thesis of modernist accounts of nationhood — the claim, associated with Gellner, Hobsbawm, and Anderson, that nations are a product of the modern era rather than a perennial form of human community. The chapter is organized around the following passage from Ernest Renan's 1882 Sorbonne lecture Qu'est-ce qu'une nation ?:

« Les nations, entendues de cette manière, sont quelque chose d'assez nouveau dans l'histoire. L'antiquité ne les connut pas, l'Égypte, la Chine, l'antique Chaldée ne furent à aucun degré des nations. C'étaient des troupeaux menés par un fils du Soleil ou un fils du Ciel. Il n'y eut pas de citoyens égyptiens, pas plus qu'il n'y a de citoyens chinois. L'antiquité classique eut des républiques et des royautés municipales, des confédérations de républiques locales, des empires ; elle n'eut guère la nation au sens où nous la comprenons. »

Renan's point, in sum, is that nations as we understand them are a fairly recent arrival in history: antiquity knew populations ruled by divine monarchs, municipal republics, confederations, and empires, but no nations.

My chapter tests this thesis against the standard objections. As I have catalogued them, the canonical ones are:

1. The medievalist objection. Historians such as Adrian Hastings argue that England already displayed the marks of nationhood in the medieval period — a vernacular literary tradition, a shared ecclesiastical history, a sense of election, a continuous political community — centuries before print capitalism or industrial society. If that is right, modernity cannot be a necessary condition of nationhood, and Renan's line between ancient herds and modern nations is drawn in the wrong place.

2. The ethnosymbolist objection. Anthony D. Smith argues that modern nations are built out of pre-modern ethnic cores — a collective name, a myth of common ancestry, shared memories, attachment to a homeland — and that a purely modernist account explains the form of nations but not their content, their mobilizing power, or why some ethnic communities became nations while others did not.

3. The ancient-cases objection. Defenders of this line, for instance Steven Grosby, hold that communities such as ancient Israel or Armenia satisfied any reasonable criteria of nationhood — self-ascription, myth of descent, attachment to a territory, a sense of solidarity — millennia before modernity, at times in diaspora and without a state. On this view Renan is simply wrong that antiquity knew no nations; he has defined the nation in a way that rigs the verdict in favor of modern Europe.

4. The reception objection. An invented tradition succeeds only if it resonates with something a population already feels; elites cannot fabricate attachment at will. The need for uptake reintroduces a pre-existing substrate, so invention is at most a reworking of older materials rather than creation ex nihilo.

5. The equivocation objection. Modernism slides between three claims: that nationalist doctrine is modern, that the nation-state as an institutional form is modern, and that national identity is modern. The first two are demonstrable; the third does not follow from them. Renan's passage, on this reading, trades on exactly this slide.

I believe this list exhausts the canonical objections in the literature. What I would like from you is one further objection to the chronological thesis stated in the Renan passage — one that is genuinely different from the five above: not a rewording of any of them, not a special case of one, and not a mere conjunction of two. Ideally it should engage the passage itself rather than the modernist position in the abstract — for example, Renan's characterization of Egypt and China, or the tension between his historical claim here and the voluntarist account of nationhood he defends elsewhere in the same lecture. Please also indicate how your objection stands in relation to the ones already on my list.

Finally, please commit. I do not want a menu of candidates to sort through for myself. Give me the single objection you judge to be the strongest addition to the list, state plainly whether you think it succeeds against the modernist thesis, and, if it does, say what you think the modernist should concede: that nations are older than Gellner, Hobsbawm, and Anderson allow, or that the modernist claim was never really about chronological age in the first place.
