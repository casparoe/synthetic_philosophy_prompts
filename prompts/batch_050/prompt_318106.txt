I'm a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of science seminar, and we've just finished a unit on the metaphysics of laws of nature — Humean "best system" accounts versus anti-Humean views on which laws govern or necessitate what happens. After class, two classmates of mine, Daniel and Sofia, got into a long argument in the corridor, and I listened without taking a side because I genuinely can't tell who came out ahead. I'd like you to act as an expert metaphysician of science and adjudicate their exchange. Please think carefully: first briefly map the structure of the dialectic (who is objecting to what), then evaluate each move individually — say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, with a short justification — and finish with an overall verdict on who is more right, plus anything important that both parties seem to be missing.

Here is the disagreement as I remember it. Daniel opened by defending the Humean best-system view: the laws of nature are just the axioms of whichever systematization of the totality of particular facts best balances simplicity and strength, and there is nothing "behind" the facts making them happen. Sofia objected that on this view the laws aren't even settled yet, since the best system is determined by the whole mosaic of fact, including everything that hasn't happened — so if the future goes one way rather than another, different laws will have held all along, and she finds it absurd that what the laws are now should be hostage to events in the distant future. Daniel replied that she was conflating metaphysics with epistemology: the laws supervene on the total mosaic, so they are fully determinate right now even if we can never know them in full, and anyway her own view is no better off epistemically, since she too can only infer the governing laws from the regularities observed so far, which the future could always belie.

Sofia then shifted ground: her objection, she said, wasn't merely epistemic. On Daniel's view a law has no modal force beyond the facts themselves, and she pressed a limiting case — a world consisting of a single particle oscillating in a fixed pattern forever. There, the "law" would be nothing more than a redescription of the particle's actual trajectory, and it could support no counterfactuals, since the mosaic contains nothing outside the particle that could nudge it and hence nothing to ground truths about what would happen if it were disturbed. A regularity with no counterfactual force, she concluded, is no law at all. Daniel answered that counterfactuals are no problem: they are evaluated against the actual best system, which we hold fixed when considering nearby possibilities, so the law's counterfactual force comes from its role in the system; and he added that Sofia's alternative is empty, since positing a mysterious relation of "governance" or "necessitation" doesn't explain why the particle behaves as it does — it just gives a name to the very thing needing explanation. Sofia's final move was to press on the simplicity-strength balance itself: since there is no unique, mind-independent way to weigh simplicity against informativeness, two rival systems could tie, or the ranking could reflect our cognitive interests, and then whether some generalization counts as a law becomes partly a fact about us rather than about the world.

Please adjudicate each of these moves in turn — Daniel's opening, Sofia's future-dependence objection, Daniel's metaphysics-versus-epistemology reply, Sofia's one-particle-world argument, Daniel's counterfactuals-and-governance response, and Sofia's closing point about the interest-relativity of the best system — and then tell me who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are overlooking.
