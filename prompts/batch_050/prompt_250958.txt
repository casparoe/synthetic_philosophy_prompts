Now that I am retired from four decades in condensed matter physics, I have been working my way through moral philosophy the way I once worked through journals, and I keep returning to a problem that strikes me as embarrassingly physical. Suppose the universe is spatially infinite and contains infinitely many sentient beings, as several respectable cosmological models permit. Then for a total utilitarian, every action available to me issues in a history containing infinite total welfare, and the standard apparatus of summing utilities across outcomes collapses: infinity does not exceed infinity in the way the decision theory I learned requires. I have encountered the usual patches. One discounts welfare by spatial or temporal distance, which restores finite sums but seems to build in an arbitrary partiality toward the nearby. Another restricts attention to the causal consequences of one's own action, on the grounds that the infinite background is common to every option and cancels out; but over an unbounded future, at least in cosmologies without an event horizon sealing us off, the region one can influence grows without limit and the cancellation fails. A third, more technical suggestion is to compare outcomes by welfare per unit comoving volume, taking a limit over ever larger regions, the way we define intensive quantities in statistical mechanics; this is the approach I find most natural, yet a colleague in my reading group objects that the limit depends on the shape of the regions one chooses, so different choices of averaging sequence rank the same pair of outcomes differently, and there is no physical fact of the matter about which sequence is correct.

Here is the case I would like analyzed. Imagine a benevolent engineer with a device that, once activated, seeds uninhabited regions of the cosmos with happy beings at a fixed density forever. Leaving it idle likewise leaves infinite welfare in place, since the cosmos already contains infinitely many happy lives elsewhere. Activating it changes welfare per unit volume on some averaging sequences but not others, depending on how the seeded regions are distributed. Write an essay that works through the three proposals above as responses to this case, considering for each whether it yields a determinate verdict about what the engineer ought to do and whether that verdict can be defended against the arbitrariness objections. Then take a position: either defend one of the three as the least bad framework for infinite-universe consequentialism, or argue that the case shows total utilitarianism must be abandoned or restricted to finite domains, and explain what a finite-domain utilitarian should say about the engineer. I would like a genuine conclusion with reasons, not a survey that ends by noting the question is difficult.

One request about form: please write the essay as continuous prose, the way one would compose a long letter to a colleague. No headings, no numbered sections, no bullet points, no boldface, no tables — just paragraphs that carry the argument from beginning to end.
