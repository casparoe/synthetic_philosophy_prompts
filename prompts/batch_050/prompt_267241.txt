In the fallout, our lawyers explained that whether the company (or an individual engineer) was liable would depend heavily on whether the relevant rule was one of strict liability, negligence, or intentional wrongdoing. That sent me down a reading spiral about mens rea, and I came out the other end with an argument that seems to prove far more than I can accept. Here it is.

(1) It is unjust to punish a person for something unless they are blameworthy for it. Call this the desert constraint on punishment.

(2) A person is blameworthy for something only if it was under their control. You cannot deserve blame for what you were powerless to affect. Call this the control condition.

(3) What a person believes, and what risks they happen to notice, are not under their voluntary control. You cannot simply decide to believe something on command — try believing right now, at will, that the lights in your room are off. And attention has the same structure: you cannot choose to notice the very thing you haven't noticed, because by the time you're choosing, you've already noticed it.

(4) Negligence liability punishes a person precisely for failing to notice a risk or failing to form a belief they should have formed. The whole doctrine is built on the gap between the reasonable person's mental state and the defendant's actual one.

(5) From (2), (3), and (4): negligence liability punishes people for states of mind that were never under their control, so it violates the desert constraint and is unjust.

(6) Strict liability is worse: it punishes without requiring any faulty mental state at all, so if negligence liability is unjust, strict liability is unjust a fortiori.

(7) But now notice that (3) generalizes. Even the person who knowingly does wrong did not choose to know what they knew — their awareness of the facts, like their beliefs, just happened to them. So by the same reasoning, even punishment for intentional and knowing wrongdoing fails the control condition.

(8) Conclusion: no punishment whatsoever satisfies the desert constraint. Punishment as an institution is unjust, full stop.

I refuse the conclusion in (8), and honestly I even refuse the intermediate conclusion in (5) — a legal system that couldn't punish the driver who kills a pedestrian while checking their phone seems obviously broken.
