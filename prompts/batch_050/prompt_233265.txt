AI safety teams routinely audit large language models by reading the chain of thought a model produces before its final answer: reasoning that looks benign is treated as evidence that the answer was reached for acceptable reasons, and models are trained to "explain their reasoning" to users as a transparency measure. Yet many of the same practitioners acknowledge that a model's verbalized reasoning can diverge from whatever actually generated the answer, functioning more like a rationalization composed after the fact. My question is what this audit practice presupposes about self-knowledge. Does it treat the model as having something like privileged first-person access to its own cognitive processes, analogous to a person sincerely reporting why they acted? Reconstruct the implicit theory of self-report the practice relies on, say whether it remains coherent once you note that the explanatory report is produced by the very system it purports to describe, and identify what would have to change in AI evaluation if we instead classified verbalized reasoning as just another output to be distrusted. Finally, consider whether the human version of this practice -- asking people why they did something and trusting the answer -- presupposes anything different, and whether it is on firmer ground.
