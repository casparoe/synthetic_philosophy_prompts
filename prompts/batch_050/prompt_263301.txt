Ok so I'm taking a phil of mind seminar and we've been going through Dennett's stuff on qualia, specifically his two coffee-taster cases. Quick recap of how I understand them, in case the details matter: Chase is a professional coffee taster who comes to dislike the coffee and says the taste itself has changed for him over the years — like, the flavor quale is different now. Sanborn also came to dislike it, but he insists it tastes exactly the same to him as it always did, and figures his standards or his reactions must have drifted instead. Dennett uses the pair to argue there's no fact of the matter about whose qualia "really" changed, and from there that qualia as usually conceived are kind of a muddle.

Here's what I actually want help with: compare the two cases as arguments. It seems to me they're doing pretty different work — Chase's version attacks the idea that you have incorrigible access to your own qualia (maybe he's just misremembering what it used to taste like), while Sanborn's attacks the idea that qualia are cleanly seperable from our reactive attitudes and judgments about them. Is that the right way to split it, or am I forcing a distinction? And does one case get Dennett closer to eliminativism about qualia than the other, or do they only work as a pair? Finally, if a qualia realist just bites the bullet and says both tasters are simply wrong about their own experience — the quale changed (or didn't), full stop, and introspection is fallible — has anything in the cases actually ruled that out, or does Dennett need extra premises? Poke holes in my framing wherever it's off.
