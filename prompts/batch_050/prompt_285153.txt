Six months ago I was promoted to lead the analytics team at a regional logistics firm, and last month I ran the post-mortem on a routing product that failed badly at launch. In my write-up I kept reaching for group-agent language: "the team decided to cut user testing," "the department lost sight of the customer." The outside facilitator hired for the session pushed back on every instance — teams don't decide anything, she said; people decide, and until the report names the individuals who chose what, it explains nothing and holds no one responsible. We argued for an hour and convinced each other of nothing. The dispute has since followed me home, because my father-in-law, a longtime union member, keeps saying his union "sold him out" on the new contract, and I find that phrasing objectionable in a way I can't fully articulate given what I defended at work. The facilitator eventually sent me a passage from the first chapter of Max Weber's Economy and Society (1922) that she said settled the matter:

"in sociological work these collectivities must be treated as solely the resultants and modes of organization of the particular acts of individual persons, since these alone can be treated as agents in a course of subjectively understandable action"

Before I concede anything to her, I'd like to know what I actually think. Here are my verdicts, going by gut:

1. "The team decided to skip user testing" (a 3–2 poll in our Slack channel, with the dissenters going along): a perfectly good explanation of why the product failed. Fine.
2. "The crowd crushed the people at the barrier" (thinking of crowd-crush dynamics at concerts): a real explanation, and no inventory of what any individual did could replace it. Fine.
3. "The company knew for years that the firmware was faulty" (some engineers suspected, most didn't, and the relevant bug reports sat unread): rings hollow. Until you say who knew, and who decided anyway, you've said nothing. Not fine.
4. "The committee is instinctively risk-averse" (it has declined every bold proposal for a decade, although the membership has turned over completely): fine — that seems to be a fact about the committee, not about any member of it.
5. "The market punished us for shipping late": harmless shorthand in a memo headline, but not an explanation of anything, and nobody is confused into thinking the market has a mind.
6. "The union sold us out" (the leadership signed, within its authority, a deal most members opposed): not fine — it erases the split between leaders and members, which is exactly where the explanatory work lies.

I can already see the tensions. Cases 3 and 4 both ascribe a cognitive property to a group with no individual bearer, yet I reject one and accept the other; cases 1 and 6 are structurally similar — authorized bodies deciding over internal dissent — yet I accept "the team decided" and reject "the union sold us out." I suspect at least one of my verdicts has to go.

So, three questions. First, is there a principle — presumably some refinement of Weber's — that sorts acceptable group-level claims from unacceptable ones the way my gut does? State it precisely, tie each clause to the case that motivates it, tell me honestly which of my six verdicts it cannot accommodate, and if forced to choose, tell me which verdict I should surrender and why. Second, does Weber's own formulation, read strictly, even deliver the verdicts its sender wants? His stated ground is that only individuals are agents "in a course of subjectively understandable action" — which sounds like a claim about interpretive understanding rather than about ontology, causal mechanism, or the assignment of blame. Does my rejection of case 3 rest on sliding between those things, and would a test built on subjective understandability — rather than reducibility — flip my verdict on case 2, where the crowd's behavior seems understandable only statistically or physically, not from the inside? Third, what does the best candidate principle predict for two cases I haven't listed: "the recommendation engine chose to bury local news," and "the family decided to sell the house," where three siblings wanted to sell and the fourth signed only after sustained pressure?
