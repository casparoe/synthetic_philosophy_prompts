Last week I spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles -- whether two things that share absolutely all their qualitative properties could nonetheless be two things rather than one. Neither of us budged, but I realized afterward that the reason we talked past each other for three hours is that neither of us had the conceptual pieces laid out in the right order: we kept sliding between "you could never tell them apart," "there'd be no fact of the matter about which is which," and "they'd just be the same thing," as if these were interchangeable. The experience made me want to get the idea straight enough to teach, and it so happens I've volunteered to run one session of a continuing-education seminar for philosophically curious adults -- not philosophy majors, mostly engineers and lawyers -- on exactly this principle, built around Max Black's thought experiment of the two qualitatively identical spheres alone in a symmetric universe. Plan the session for me rather than teaching me the material, which I largely have: I want the pedagogy, not the philosophy lecture.

Specifically, I need the following. First, the dependency structure: which distinctions have to be in place before the principle can even be stated without confusion -- I'm assuming qualitative versus numerical identity comes first, and the contrast between the principle itself and its converse (the indiscernibility of identicals, which nobody disputes) has to be drawn early -- and in what order, with a reason for each placement. Second, the carrying example: make the case for or against building the whole session on Black's spheres, what a carrying example must have and must lack to work here (should it be a concrete near-miss first, like two peas in a pod or two mass-produced ball bearings, and only then the spheres?), and at what point the symmetry of Black's universe does its real work -- my suspicion is that the symmetry is what forces the relational-property question, and that most treatments gesture at it without making it do anything. Third, the misconception map, in the order the audience will actually generate the misconceptions: I'm expecting at least (a) the slide from epistemic indiscernibility -- couldn't be told apart -- to metaphysical identity, (b) the conviction that spatial location is itself a property that distinguishes the spheres, which needs to be confronted rather than dismissed, since the audience's instinct that "this one is here and that one is there" deserves a careful treatment of whether location can be a non-qualitative haecceity, and (c) the reading on which the whole principle is just a definition of the word "identical," making the dispute verbal. For each, tell me exactly where in the sequence to head it off and why that position in the sequence is the right one. Fourth, a single closing question I can put to the room that discriminates between someone who has grasped what the dispute is actually about and someone who has merely memorized a verdict on it. Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level -- I presume quantum indistinguishability and bundle theories of substance are on that list, but I want to know whether bracketing them costs the session anything essential.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- the one-sentence teaching strategy the whole plan implements -- and then give the plan, with no preamble and no restating of my request.
