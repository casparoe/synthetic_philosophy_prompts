Here's my running list of objections to Pascal's wager: (1) many gods -- the payoff table is underdescribed, since an anti-Pascalian god who rewards sincere disbelief gives the mirror-image expected value, and there's no principled way to assign the probabilities; (2) you can't believe at will, so the wager at most recommends going through the religious motions and hoping belief follows, which is a much weaker conclusion; (3) the infinite payoff breaks the decision theory -- any nonzero credence times infinity swamps everything, so the argument proves too much and would equally justify worshipping anyone who promises infinite reward (the fanaticism/mixed-strategy worry); (4) mercenary belief -- a belief adopted for the payout is the wrong kind of thing for a just god to reward, and might even be punished. I feel like there should be another objection that isn't just one of these in disguise. In particular, don't give me another 'what if god rewards X instead' case, since those all collapse into (1). What's the missing objection, how does it differ structurally from the four above, and do you think it actually works or does Pascal have a reply?
