Draft the joint statement that a proponent of truth-value gaps (in the style of Kripke's fixed-point approach: some sentences, including the Liar, are neither true nor false) and a dialetheist (in the style of Priest: some sentences, including the Liar, are both true and false) could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the semantic paradoxes.

Structure the document in four parts:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. Candidates you should consider, refine, or reject include: that the unrestricted T-schema, classical logic, and the existence of Liar sentences cannot be jointly maintained; that simply banning self-reference is not a solution, since the problematic sentences can arise contingently through chains of ordinary empirical claims; that any adequate account must say something about the strengthened Liar ("this sentence is not true") rather than only the plain Liar; and that Curry-style sentences ("if this sentence is true, then p") constrain a solution independently of the logic of negation. Phrase each agreed claim so that neither camp is conceding anything it actually denies -- no agreed claim may quietly presuppose that contradictions are never true, nor that excluded middle fails.

2. A disagreements table with three columns: the issue, the gap theorist's position in wording the gap theorist would accept, and the dialetheist's position in wording the dialetheist would accept. The rows should include at minimum: the status of the Liar sentence itself; which component of classical reasoning each side is willing to restrict (the behavior of negation and excluded middle versus the rule of explosion); and whether each theory can state its own classification of the paradoxical sentences in its own language without generating a new paradox (the revenge problem), which each side tends to press against the other.

3. A cruxes section. Identify the two or three sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp, and attach to each crux a concrete test both sides accept as bearing on it. One required test, which I am supplying as neutral ground: a safety regulator's file contains exactly two sentences -- sentence A, "Sentence B is not true," and sentence B, "Sentence A is true," followed by sentence C, "If this very sentence is true, then the bridge is safe." For each side, record its prediction in advance: what classification does its semantics assign to A, B, and C; does its account license concluding that the bridge is safe; and which specific feature of its conditional or its logic does the work of blocking (or absorbing) that conclusion. Both sides should also record, for this test, what result would embarrass their own view.

4. A closing paragraph stating which, if any, of the disagreements in the table the collaboration concludes are partly verbal -- for instance, whether the two camps are using "true" in the same sense when they fight over the Liar's status -- and which disagreements are genuine and load-bearing.

The deliverable is the document itself, not a commentary on it: it should read as something a Kripkean and a dialetheist could each sign, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than smoothed over.
