Hey — I'm working on a column about everyday dishonesty (the small stuff: white lies, strategic half-truths) and I need a thought experiment built to order for it. Here's the spec:

Construct a single short scenario, maybe 150-200 words, where two people each get the exact same false belief into the head of the same kind of listener, with the same stakes and the same motive — but one of them does it by flat-out lying and the other does it by saying something strictly true that merely implicates the falsehood. The whole point is that the lying/misleading distinction is the ONLY variable that changes, so be strict about it: same belief induced, same consequences, same knowledge on each speaker's side, same relationship to the listener. And keep it realistic — no philosophy-demons, no weird machines, something that could plausibly happen in an office or at a family dinner. If the misleading move is so contrived that no human would ever actually do it, my readers will bounce off it.

Once you've got the scenario, show me it does what it's supposed to. Format the response like this:

**The Scenario** — the case itself, 150-200 words
**The Verdicts** — ~150 words: how (a) the view that lying is intrinsically worse than misleading and (b) the view that there's no moral difference as long as you knowingly induce the same false belief each evaluate the two speakers. Derive the verdicts from the theories, don't just assert them.
**The Load-Bearing Detail** — ~100 words: which feature of the setup is actually doing the work of seperating the two verdicts, and what happens to the case if you strip it out.
**The Pushback** — ~100 words: how a defender of the no-difference view would attack the case (e.g., that our different gut reactions just track violations of conversational conventions rather than anything genuinely moral), and whether the scenario survives that.

One more thing: the column is for a general audience, so no Grice or "implicature" jargon in the scenario itself — save that for the analysis if you need it.
