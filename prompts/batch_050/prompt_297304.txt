I'm putting together a talk for a film-and-philosophy night at my local uni and I want to do Ex Machina. Act as a philosopher of mind who also happens to know film really well — think carefully, and maybe brainstorm two or three possible angles before committing to the reading you think is strongest.

The lens I want is sphexishness. The sphex wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the burrow, and goes inside to check things out before pulling it in; if a researcher nudges the cricket a few inches away while she's inside, she drags it back to the exact same spot and goes in to check again, and you can repeat this dozens of times and she'll never break the loop. Looks purposive until it doesn't, and the classic worry is whether our own flexibility is just a longer, more hidden loop.

Read the film through that. The obvious reading is that Ava is the test case: Caleb's job is to work out whether her flirtation and escape-planning are real agency or an extremely well-tuned sphex loop, and Nathan's experiment is rigged so the question can't be settled from behavior alone. But the reading I keep circling is that the film flips it — Nathan picked Caleb precisely because he was predictable (lonely, suggestible, a savior complex Nathan could exploit), Nathan himself runs on a rigid loop of workouts, drinking sessions and hangovers, and Ava wins by correctly modeling the humans as machines while they sit around debating whether she's one. The humans loose their own Turing test, basically.

Where does this hold and where does it strain? Things I want you to actually grapple with, not hand-wave: does the wasp analogy even fit, given that sphexishness is specifically about failing to re-plan when the environment changes, while Ava's whole skill is re-planning on the fly? Is there any point in the film where a character does break their loop — does Caleb, at the end, or does he just run the rescue subroutine Nathan predicted? Does the final sequence, where Ava walks out into the human world, give us real evidence she's more than her programming, or does the film deliberately withhold her interiority so we can't know? And what's the film's implicit answer to whether the difference is even detectable — is Caleb's mistake epistemic (he couldn't have known) or moral (he should have treated her as a someone regardless)?

Finish with what the film adds that the standard literature on mechanism and free will doesn't. What can you dramatize about sphexishness on screen that you can't establish by argument, and does Ex Machina actually earn that, or is it getting a free pass because the robot is sympathetic and the audience wants to be fooled the same way Caleb does?
