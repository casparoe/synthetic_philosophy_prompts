In my second-year philosophy seminar on paradoxes, I have one two-hour meeting on Fitch's knowability result: the derivation from the seemingly modest claim that every truth is in principle knowable to the conclusion that every truth is in fact known. The students know basic modal logic but no epistemic logic. Do not reconstruct the proof for me; plan how I should teach it. Specifically: what has to be in place before the derivation will register as a discovery rather than a trick (I am assuming Moorean sentences of the form "p and it is not known that p" must come first, with their consistency established before their unknowability is shown, but tell me if that ordering is wrong); the single example that should carry the session, including what it must have and must lack, and why an as-yet-unproven mathematical conjecture is the wrong thing to build it on; the misconceptions the students will predictably form -- my guesses are that the proof merely shows some truths happen to be unknown, that it works by self-reference like the liar, and that the only suspect premise is the distribution of knowledge over conjunction -- and for each, the point in the sequence where it will surface and how I should head it off. Finally, say how much of the response literature (restriction strategies, weakening the knowability principle itself) can fit into the remaining time without swamping the core result, and give me one closing question that would show whether they grasp that the argument threatens a substantive philosophical position rather than yielding a mere curiosity. Where the pedagogy itself is uncertain, say how confident you are in your sequencing and misconception predictions, and single out the one recommendation you are least sure of.
