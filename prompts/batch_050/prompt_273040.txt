Criminal juries typically have to be unanimous (or nearly) to convict, while legislatures, electorates and appellate panels decide by simple majority -- appellate courts even sit in odd numbers precisely so a bare majority settles it. The Condorcet jury theorem is often wheeled in to justify the majority practice: if each voter is more likely right than wrong and votes independently, a big majority is almost certainly correct. But that makes the jury's unanimity rule look backwards, since it hands a veto to the one person the theorem says is most likely mistaken. So what does each practice presuppose about the people voting? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology of unanimity rules -- do they assume jurors' errors are correlated, that false convictions cost more than false acquittals, that a lone holdout carries information a bare tally throws away? -- and of majority rules, say whether the two can be made consistent or whether one of them is resting on a mistake, and tell me concretely what would have to change either if criminal trials took the Condorcet assumptions literally, or if majority-rule institutions took the jury's caution seriously.
