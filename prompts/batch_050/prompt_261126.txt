Write a deadpan bureaucratic satire, approximately 1,000–1,200 words, in the form of an internal policy document issued by the fictional Interministerial Board for the Classification of Severe Hunger Events. The Board has been tasked with deciding when a famine is a natural disaster and when it is a political crime, but its governing principle is that the presence of any drought, blight, flood, pest, or other natural trigger establishes the event as natural, while political responsibility requires proof of a single authoritative decision explicitly intended to cause mass starvation.

The document should include:

1. A solemn foreword explaining that famine classification must remain objective, apolitical, and administratively convenient.
2. A short set of definitions distinguishing natural hazards from regrettable background conditions such as wartime inflation, hoarding, procurement rules, export policy, censorship, falsified harvest reports, restrictions on movement, and officials’ refusal to believe bad news.
3. Three formal case determinations concerning the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the famine associated with the Great Leap Forward. Each should contain enough recognizable historical substance to engage Amartya Sen’s insight that famines concern entitlements and political arrangements rather than food availability alone, while remaining concise and not resembling an encyclopedia entry.
4. An appeals procedure under which affected populations may challenge a natural-disaster classification by identifying a precise date on which the state caused each individual death, with acts of omission, foreseeability, preventable distribution failures, and institutional incentives excluded as too diffuse.
5. A concluding recommendation that future famines be avoided through improved terminology, standardized attribution forms, and clearer separation between weather and accountability.

The humor should arise from getting the underlying moral and causal issues right: multiple causation, responsibility for omissions, the difference between triggering a famine and making one lethal or unstoppable, and the way bureaucratic standards can convert political choices into immutable facts of nature. Do not deny that crop failures, plant disease, and adverse weather were real; satirize the inference from there was a natural trigger to therefore politics is exonerated. The tone should remain impeccably official throughout, with no explicit winking at the reader, no named living people, no footnotes, and no bibliography.
