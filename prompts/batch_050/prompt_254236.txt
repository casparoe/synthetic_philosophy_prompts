So I rewatched Arrival the other night (the Denis Villeneuve movie with Amy Adams, based on that Ted Chiang story) and then made the mistake of falling down an internet rabbit hole about the "block universe," and now I can't seperate the two in my head. I never studied philosophy or physics, I'm just a guy who thinks about this stuff on his lunch breaks, so bear with me.

The rabbit hole, as I understood it: there's this old argument (Putnam, and I think a guy named Rietdijk?) that relativity wrecks the idea of a universal "now." Observers moving at different speeds disagree about which distant events are happening at the same moment, so for something far away, what's still in my future could already be settled in some other observer's "now" — and if no way of slicing up spacetime is the privileged one, then the future has to already be out there, as real as the past. Time is just a dimension and we're crawling along it. I probably butchered that, but that's what I took from it.

And then it hit me that Arrival is basically this idea wearing a sci-fi costume. Louise learns the heptapod language and starts getting "memories" of her daughter Hannah — a daughter who isn't born yet and who will die young — and she has her anyway, and it costs her the marriage.

Here's what I actually want: read the movie through the block universe, seriously, in both directions. Does the film even need the language stuff? If the future already exists for everyone, then heptapod is just a telescope pointed at it, and the whole "language rewires how you experience time" theme becomes set decoration. Or is the movie saying the language is what it's like to perceive the block universe from inside, because that would make it essential instead of decoration. Also: everyone online calls Louise's situation fatalism, but the forum people kept insisting the block universe isn't fatalism — your choices still cause the future, it's just fixed which choice you make. I honestly cannot tell the difference between those two things, and I suspect the whole movie lives in that gap. Is she choosing, or just accepting, and does the film itself know the difference? There's also the General Shang phone call business where information sort of comes out of nowhere — does that fit the block universe at all, or is it a different, loopier kind of time weirdness that actually clashes with the first kind? And if the block universe turned out to be false, would the movie still make sense, or does it secretly depend on it?

Last thing: the movie wrecked me emotionally in a way none of the physics threads do. If you have a view on what the film shows about grief and time that the argument alone can't say, I want that too.

Write it as one long piece of continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Just flowing, like a long email or a letter from someone who's thought about this way too hard.
