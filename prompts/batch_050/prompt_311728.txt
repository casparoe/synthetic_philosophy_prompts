I never studied philosophy, but I keep hitting this weird thing where I can imagine dragons, time travel, even a world where pain feels like color, but if a novel calmly treats a kid’s humiliation as the thing that finally makes the family whole, my imagination stalls even though none of the facts are impossible. I started thinking of it as a borrowed-ledger problem: fiction lets me borrow the causal ledger pretty easily, but not always the moral ledger that says what counts as paid, owed, forgiven, or earned. Write the desiderata for any decent theory of imaginative resistance under that frame: what cases it has to get right, what distinctions it cannot collapse, like imagining that people approve versus finding the approval intelligible versus being pulled to share it, why genre and narration can loosen or tighten the block, why I can follow a cruel narrator without joining them, why the block sometimes melts on rereading and sometimes hardens, and why inconsistency inside a made-up world is different from mere weirdness. Say which requirements you think are real and which are just taste dressed up as theory, and whether any two of them probably cannot both be satisfied. No headings, bullets, bold, or tables, just continuous prose like an email, and please don’t lean on famous names or citations since I won’t be able to check them.
