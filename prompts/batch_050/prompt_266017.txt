So I'm a retired biophysicist — forty years of fluorescence microscopy, now mostly armchair philosophy of mind — and I've gotten stuck on something. The color phi phenomenon: a red dot flashes on the left, then a green dot flashes on the right, and what people report is one dot moving across and changing color halfway. Fine. But then I read about Dennett's two ways the brain could be pulling this off: the "Stalinesque" version, where a doctored show-trial of an experience gets staged *before* anything reaches consciousness, and the "Orwellian" version, where you really do have the naive experience first and your memory of it is rewritten so fast you can never catch the difference. And honestly I can't tell whether these are even two different theories or one theory wearing two hats.

Write me a dialogue between two equally sharp philosophers of mind — one defending the Stalinesque reading as a genuine empirical hypothesis about the timing of experience, one defending the Orwellian one — where they actually try to settle it, responding to each other's real points rather than lecturing in paralell. Some ground rules for them:

- Each must commit to a precise claim about what happened in the subject and *when* (or whether) there was an experience of the dot changing color mid-path.
- One of them should propose a tweak to the experiment — having the subject respond *during* the display rather than after it, or a version where the second dot sometimes never appears — and the other has to show why it does or doesn't tell the views apart.
- When one of them suggests the dispute might be merely verbal, the other has to push back with something better than "surely there has to be a fact of the matter."
- No strawmen: the Stalinesque defender must explain what "before consciousness" means without smuggling in a little theater with an audience, and the Orwellian must explain how a memory can misrepresent an experience that, on their own story, never quite happened.

Six to nine hundred words of dialogue. They don't have to end up agreeing — but I want to come away knowing what the disagreement is actually *about*, if it's about anything.
