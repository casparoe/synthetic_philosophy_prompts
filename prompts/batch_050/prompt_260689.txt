I'm a novelist working on a book whose main character ends up running a field hospital after a disaster, and I want her decisions to feel like they hang together, so I've been testing my own gut on cases first. Giving the last ventilator to the 30-year-old over the 85-year-old: fine. Giving it to whichever patient is more likely to pull through, age aside: fine. Choosing to save two strangers over one: obviously fine, I'd have thought. But letting one healthy patient die so her organs can save five: monstrous, no. Prioritising a patient without a disability over one with, on the grounds that saving the first buys more good years: that makes my skin crawl, even though I can't explain how it differs from the age case. And here's the one that really bothers me: my instinct says spend whatever it takes to rescue the hiker trapped on the mountain right now, but also that spending the same money per expected life saved on boring preventative stuff for nameless future patients is optional. Is there a principle (or two) that captures most of these verdicts, and which of them does it force me to abandon? And what would it tell my character to do when it's the hiker case but the rescue means the clinic runs out of antibiotics next month? Please reply in British English.
