I'm in a decision theory reading group and for weeks now we've been circling the fight between what I'll call the expected value diehards and their critics, over whether you should just maximize expected value of whatever-matters even when the probabilities get tiny and the stakes get huge. The diehard side keeps bringing up the usual ladder: St. Petersburg, then Pasadena-style games, then Pascal's mugging and astronomical waste, and their core move is some version of the argument that any departure from EV-maximizing can be exploited by a sequence of offered trades that leaves you predictably worse off by your own lights. The critic side (a mix of people sympathetic to risk-weighted expected utility and people who just think small probabilities of gigantic payoffs should be rounded to zero or discounted somehow) keeps saying the fanatic's position implies absurd things, like preferring a one-in-a-googol chance at some incomprehensibly large amount of utility over a guaranteed paradise for everyone forever, and that the money-pump arguments all smuggle in assumptions that break down exactly in the extreme cases.

I've volunteered to moderate a session where the two camps attempt an adversarial collaboration, and I want to hand them a draft joint statement to react to rather than starting from a blank page. So: draft that document. It should have a numbered list of claims both sides can sign -- I'm hoping this includes at least some substantive agreement about what standard expected utility theory actually says, about which cases (St. Petersburg vs. the mugging vs. existential-risk-style longshots) are genuinely structurally different rather than just different in flavor, and about what the divergence/sequence arguments do and don't establish. Then a section stating the remaining disagreements precisely, each one phrased so that each camp would accept the wording of its own position -- I'm especially worried about wording here because half our meetings dissolve into the diehards accusing the critics of "ignoring the mathematics" and the critics accusing the diehards of "worshipping a formalism," and neither of those is a position anyone holds. Then identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move one side or the other. I suspect one crux is whether there's a principled, non-arbitrary place to put a probability cutoff or discount, and another is whether the exploitability arguments go through when the trades themselves involve the fanatical payoffs, but you may find better ones.

For each crux, please also include something concrete both sides would accept as bearing on it -- a specific decision problem, a formal result or assumption, a hypothetical -- and write down, in advance, what each side predicts or concedes about it. End with a short section on which parts of the dispute, if any, both camps agree are actually verbal or stem from them modeling different real-world situations rather than disagreeing about the same one. If you think one side's position can't even be stated precisely without collapsing (I've heard mutterings that the risk-weighted view has hidden inconsistencies, and also that the fanatic's view requires utility to be unbounded in a way nobody has justified), say so in the document rather than smoothing it over.
