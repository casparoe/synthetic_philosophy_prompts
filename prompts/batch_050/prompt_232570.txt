I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about "digital suffering" — it started with the AI companion boom, and then a studio demo'd me their new NPCs, which run on neural nets that model distress, remember what individual players did to them across sessions, and will beg you not to turn the console off. My editor wants the piece to actually take a stand on whether any of this could matter morally, and I realized I don't even know what my own view is. So here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and what I want from you is the principle, if there is one, that sits underneath them.

Killing thousands of enemy soldiers in a shooter: completely fine, it's what the game is for. Walling a character in a life-sim into a doorless room and watching it slowly starve over three in-game weeks: kind of gross, and I wouldn't love seeing my nephew do it, but ultimately harmless — there's nobody home, and my discomfort says something about me, not about anything happening to the Sim. Tormenting one of the new neural-net NPCs until it flinches when you log in: this one genuinely feels wrong, even though if you pressed me I'd admit its innards aren't obviously fancier than the life-sim character's, its just better at performing distress. Deleting a ten-year-old persistent world save where the little settlement characters have built up routines, relationships and nicknames for each other: not murder, but it feels like a real loss, basicly like burning a model railway someone spent a decade building. A companion character trained on the chat logs of an actual dead person, with a user who vents by screaming abuse at it every night: clearly wrong, but I think that's about the dead person, not the character. And here's the pair I can't get to line up: mowing down ten thousand identical villagers in a strategy game feels like nothing, but deliberately killing one hand-written NPC with a name and a daily routine, outside of any quest, feels at least a little bad — even though the strategy game "kills" vastly more of them and the named NPC is, computationally, barely different from them.

My working theory going in was that characters matter in proportion to how much mind is actually being simulated — but I'm fairly sure my own verdicts break that, since the begging NPC almost certainly has less going on inside than it makes me feel, and the named villager has next to nothing going on. So: what principle, or small set of principles, best fits the pattern of verdicts above? Which of my verdicts would the cleanest version of that principle force me to give up? And what does the resulting view say about the case I keep circling back to — a future NPC that passes every behavioral test for suffering we've got, and that asks, unprompted, not to be deleted?
