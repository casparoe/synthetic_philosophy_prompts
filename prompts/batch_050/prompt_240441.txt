I teach an interdisciplinary seminar on decision-making for third-year undergraduates (mixed math, economics, and philosophy backgrounds, about 24 students), and I have a single 100-minute block to convey the core insight of ergodicity economics: that a gamble can have a positive expected value across parallel copies of the world while reliably impoverishing almost every individual who takes it repeatedly over time. My students all know how to compute an expectation, and that is exactly the problem — their training tells them that taking a bet with a 5% edge per round is simply rational, and I want them to feel, in their bodies and in their point totals, why that inference fails when payoffs compound multiplicatively. Design a runnable in-class game for this purpose.

Concretely, I want a two-phase structure, and I'd like you to supply the exact rules, materials, and numbers. In Phase One, everyone should play an additive gamble (fixed amounts won or lost each round regardless of current holdings) and discover that the class's individual outcomes cluster around the ensemble prediction — nothing surprising happens, which is itself the point. In Phase Two, switch to a multiplicative gamble along the lines of: each round, a fair coin flip either increases your current stake by 50% or decreases it by 40%. The per-round expected multiplier is 1.05, so the naive calculation says play forever; but the typical trajectory decays at roughly (1.5 × 0.6)^(1/2) ≈ 0.95 per round, so after twenty rounds nearly everyone should be ruined while the class's total wealth — dominated by one or two lucky outliers — has grown. Please work out the staging details: how many rounds, how students track their wealth (paper ledgers are fine, but tell me exactly what to put on the sheet), what the starting endowment should be, and what non-monetary stakes (points toward a small prize, leaderboard, etc.) keep people engaged without real money. Include one structural twist I especially want: after about ten rounds of Phase Two, pause and allow students to voluntarily form "insurance pools" that redistribute each member's gains and losses, then resume — my hope is that the pooled groups visibly out-survive the loners, letting the class discover that risk-sharing can convert a non-ergodic situation into something closer to an ergodic one, without me lecturing about it first.

Then write the debrief: a sequence of questions that walks students from what just happened (the divergence between the class total and their individual balances, the role of the outlier, why the pool worked) to the formal distinction between ensemble averages and time averages, and from there to the philosophical upshot — that expected-value reasoning silently assumes access to the ensemble, whereas a single lived life only gets one trajectory. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (e.g., students suspecting rigged coins, arithmetic errors in compounding percentages, a lucky early round convincing someone the gamble is fine, pools colluding to merge into one giant pool) and how to handle each. Finally, be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, that twenty flips is not the long run, that students knowingly play a game rather than stake their livelihoods, and that the logarithmic-utility explanation of their behavior is a story the debrief must supply rather than something the game itself demonstrates.
