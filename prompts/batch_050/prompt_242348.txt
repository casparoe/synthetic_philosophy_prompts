Professional historians observe two norms that sit uneasily together. Counterfactual speculation — asking whether the First World War would still have broken out had the Sarajevo assassination failed — is dismissed as a parlor game rather than serious scholarship. Yet the same discipline traffics freely in causal attribution: it calls certain decisions decisive, identifies turning points, and distributes responsibility among persons and circumstances, judgments that seem to commit their authors to precisely the what-would-otherwise-have-happened claims the taboo forbids. A related tension divides narrative historians, who dwell on accidents, wrong turns, and near-misses, from structural historians, for whom the great events were overdetermined by deep forces no individual could have deflected.

Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of this practice. What theory of causation do its singular causal judgments presuppose, given the official ban on the counterfactual reasoning that would test them? Is there a principled line between the counterfactuals the practice licenses and the ones it mocks, or is the taboo merely a genre convention? And does the coexistence of contingency narratives with structural explanation rest on a defensible hierarchy of fragile and robust causal processes — and can such a hierarchy survive what we know about sensitive dependence in complex systems? In your answer, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit, and sketch what historical writing would look like if rebuilt consistently on either horn: thoroughgoing structuralism, or the frank admission that all causal history is counterfactual history.
