I'm putting together the syllabus for a grad seminar I'm teaching next spring on what computer science can actually teach philosophers (working title: "theorems as arguments"), and I'm stuck on the computational complexity week. I keep a running list of places where a concept or result from complexity theory does genuine philosophical work -- not just hand-wavy "complexity is deep, man" stuff, but spots where the formalism earns its keep. Here's what I have so far:

1. The extended Church-Turing thesis (Cobham-Edmonds, feasibility thesis, whatever you want to call it) treated as an empirical, falsifiable conjecture about the physical world rather than a definition -- so that quantum computing threatens it in roughly the way Deutsch argued computation is really a branch of physics.
2. The P vs NP gap read as a formalization of the verification/discovery asymmetry -- the thought that if P=NP then the creative leap in mathematics or music appreciation collapses into mere checking, which people use to argue something substantive about creativity and intuition.
3. The tractability constraint in cognitive science (van Rooij and friends): a computational-level theory of cognition that posits an intractable problem is explanatorily defective, because the mind somehow computes it anyway, so worst-case hardness results act as a filter on psychological theories.
4. Zero-knowledge proofs as a formal model relevant to epistemology -- you can be fully convinced someone knows something while learning nothing but that fact, which puts pressure on any account of knowledge transmission or testimony that assumes convincing requires giving.

I'm fairly sure there are more I'm forgetting, and I'd hate for the seminar list to just be my own hobbyhorses. Give me one more entry -- genuinely different in structure from these four, not just "here's another problem that turns out to be NP-hard so philosophers should care." In particular, none of mine draw on relativization/oracle results, on interactive or probabilistic proof systems beyond zero-knowledge, on the worst-case vs average-case distinction, or on hierarchy theorems, so something from those territories would be especially welcome, though I'm open to being surprised. Say what the result or concept is, what philosophical question it bears on, and -- this is the part my students always skip -- what the strongest deflationary response would be, i.e. the best case that the formal result doesn't actually settle or even illuminate the philosophical question. If you think one of my four is actually weaker than I've made it sound on exactly those grounds, feel free to say so; I've had a glass of wine while writing this and my confidence is not well-calibrated.
