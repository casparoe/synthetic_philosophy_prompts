You are a professional philosopher with expertise in action theory, practical rationality, and decision theory. Please think carefully, work through the cases one at a time before synthesising, and give your answer in British English throughout.

I keep a notebook of verdicts about cases of choice without preference — situations where a person must choose between options they take to be exactly on a par. I'd like to know what principle, if any, my verdicts are tracking. Here they are:

1. At the supermarket I face two identical jars of the same jam, same price, same best-before date. I take the left one without a flicker of thought. Verdict: perfectly rational, indeed commendably efficient; anyone who stood there deliberating would be wasting their time.

2. A hungry donkey stands exactly midway between two bales of hay it cannot distinguish. Verdict: the donkey that starves is not being rational but broken; any creature that couldn't just pick would be worse off, so picking must be permissible here.

3. Two equally attractive job offers arrive the same day — equivalent salary, equivalent roles, one in Edinburgh, one in Bristol. My friend has agonised for a month and still cannot rank them. Verdict: it would be wrong of her to close her eyes and grab one. She should keep deliberating, talk to people, wait; simply plumping for a whole life feels reckless in a way the jam does not.

4. A rescue worker can save exactly one of two identical twins from a fire; they are equidistant, equally endangered. Verdict: she must choose immediately and arbitrarily — but it matters morally *how* she chooses. Grabbing whichever is nearer her left hand feels acceptable only if she doesn't know which twin is which; if she does know them both, I feel she ought to flip a coin, and merely grabbing one feels like a failure of equal respect, even though the outcome is the same either way.

5. Two competing scientific theories fit all the available evidence equally well, and a researcher must decide which to include in her grant application as her working hypothesis. Verdict: she should suspend judgement between them, or present both; arbitrarily committing to one would be epistemically irrational, in stark contrast to the jam case.

6. In a chess endgame I have calculated that two moves both lead to a forced draw. I play the first one that came to mind. Verdict: unobjectionable, like the jam.

My tentative principle was: whenever the chooser reasonably believes the options are exactly equal in value, arbitrary selection is permissible, full stop. But case 4 seems to falsify it (the *manner* of arbitrary choice matters, not just the choice), and cases 3 and 5 seem to falsify it from the other side (arbitrary selection is forbidden despite equality). Meanwhile 1, 2, and 6 seem to confirm it.

Please do the following. First, test my tentative principle against all six cases and say precisely which clauses fail and why. Second, state a repaired principle — or at most two rival candidates — that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, indicating which case motivates each clause (for example, any clause distinguishing *picking* from *choosing*, stakes from stakes, or practical commitment from belief). Third, tell me honestly which of my verdicts your preferred account cannot accommodate and must overturn, and defend the overturning. Finally, apply your preferred account to two cases I haven't listed: a voter who regards two candidates as exactly equally good, deciding whether to vote at all; and a translator who must render an ambiguous source sentence that admits of two equally faithful English translations.
