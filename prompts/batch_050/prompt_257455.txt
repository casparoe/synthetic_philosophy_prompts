I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, and I'm building a little hobby project -- basically a digital version of that game Nomic, where the players' moves are proposals to change the rules of the game itself. Rules live in a config file, and there's a meta-rule that defines how the file can be edited. While I was writing the spec I walked myself into an argument that seems to show the whole concept is broken, which is annoying because the game obviously exists and people play it. So it must prove too much. Here's the argument:

(1) Any change to a rule of a system is only legitimate if it's made in accordance with some rule of that system (that's what makes it a rule-change and not just someone scribbling on the rules).
(2) The rule governing how rules get changed (call it the amendment rule) is itself a rule of the system.
(3) So the amendment rule can only be legitimately changed in accordance with the amendment rule -- i.e., it has to authorize its own replacement.
(4) If the amendment rule can authorize its own replacement, then it was never really in charge: its replacement could just be changed again by the same method, so no rule-change rule is ever settled, and "legitimate change" bottoms out in nothing.
(5) If the amendment rule *can't* authorize its own replacement, then there's at least one rule that can't be changed, and the system isn't actually self-amending -- it's a fixed system with a fancy editing feature.
(6) Every putatively self-amending system faces this fork.
(7) Therefore no system can genuinely amend itself: every constitution, club charter, or rules-as-code setup is either incoherent or secretly frozen at the meta level.

Conclusion (7) can't be right -- constitutions do get amended, my players do change the rules. But I keep bouncing off it. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than (6)/(7), since (1) is basically the definition of a rule-governed system.

Act as an expert in philosophy of law and formal logic who's also comfortable with self-referential systems (Gödel-style stuff, fixed points, that whole area). Please think carefully step by step -- before you commit to an answer, brainstorm at least three candidate weak points in the argument and say briefly why the ones you don't pick are less plausible. Then name the step you'd reject, explain why it *looked* fine (I want to understand how it fooled me), and tell me what each standard way out has to give up. Bonus points if you can tell me what this means practically for my rule engine: should the amendment rule be able to edit itself, be hardcoded outside the file, or is there a third option I'm not seeing?
