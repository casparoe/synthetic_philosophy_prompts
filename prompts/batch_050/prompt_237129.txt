I teach a mixed philosophy-and-data-literacy seminar (14 students, one 75-minute session). Design an in-class game about Simpson's paradox that goes beyond the standard aggregate-reverses-the-trend reveal. In the first round, participants should make real allocation decisions under time pressure from summary statistics alone, score points for how their decisions turn out, and then discover that the disaggregated data reverses what the aggregate showed. But the second round should punish the lesson they just learned: here the subgroup variable is downstream of the treatment itself (or was selected on in a way that creates the split), so conditioning on it misleads and the aggregate was the right table to use. The point participants should come away with is that the paradox is not settled by any rule about which table to trust, but by causal structure the tables don't display. Give me the rules, the materials, a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money, and a debrief that maps what happened in each round onto that point. No lecture up front, and participants shouldn't hear the name Simpson's paradox until the debrief. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I expect some students to conclude that you should always disaggregate, others that statistics simply lie -- and how to handle each, and be frank about which feature of the real inferential problem a game with staged outcomes cannot reproduce.
