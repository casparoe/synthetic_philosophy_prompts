Last night a friend and I spent four hours arguing about Newcomb's problem and ended in a stalemate that surprised both of us. We started on the standard setup and quickly slid into a version that I think changes things and he thinks changes nothing: replace the infallible alien with a thoroughly mundane predictor -- an actuarial model of the sort an insurance company might run, trained on population data plus a forty-minute interview and a look at your social media history, accurate at, say, 85%. His position is that once the predictor is just statistics about you rather than magic, two-boxing becomes obviously correct, because nothing you do now influences what's already in the boxes; the correlation is a fact about the kind of person you turned out to be, not a lever you can still pull. Mine is that the argument for one-boxing never depended on the predictor being supernatural, only on the correlation being real, and that his way of dissolving the paradox proves too much -- it would also tell you to defect against your identical twin in a prisoner's dilemma, or to not bother voting on the grounds that your own behavior is merely evidence about how similar people behave. He replied that I was conflating choosing an act with choosing a disposition, and that I was welcome to become a one-boxing sort of person but that this changes nothing about what reason recommends in the single case. We agreed to spend a month thinking separately and then resume, and this is where I want your help.

What I need is a defended agenda, not a survey. Which open questions in and around Newcomb's problem are actually load-bearing for a dispute like ours, and in what order should two philosophically literate non-specialists take them up, given a month of effort and the goal of either resolving the stalemate or locating precisely the premise we disagree on? Some candidates that came up between us: whether rationality evaluates acts, policies, or dispositions (and whether that question is even well-posed independently of the case); what notion of dependence or influence two-boxing secretly relies on, and whether it survives being stated explicitly; whether the problem is really about prediction at all or merely about correlation, given the twin and voting variants; and whether any of this has consequences for real decisions -- cooperation among similar agents, collective action, medical dilemmas involving evidence about one's own dispositions -- or whether it is an artifact of contrived setups. For each question you include, tell me why it is upstream of the others (or why it is a dead end we should consciously skip), how tractable it is for people like us rather than professionals, and what would change your ordering -- a consideration, a variant, or a distinction that would move a question up or down the list. If you think our stalemate actually rests on a verbal disagreement or on neither of us having stated the other's position fairly, say that instead and show your work.
