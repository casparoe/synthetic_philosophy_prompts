I chair my school district's Social Studies Curriculum Committee, and we must decide next month what to adopt for the required 11th-grade U.S. history course. Three proposals are on the table:

1. A traditional curriculum built around the 1776 narrative: the founding as a landmark achievement of self-government and natural-rights ideals, with slavery treated as a profound contradiction gradually overcome.
2. A curriculum built around the 1619 framing: the arrival of enslaved Africans and the institution of slavery treated as the country's true point of origin, with the Revolution, the Constitution, and subsequent economic development reinterpreted in its light.
3. A "historiographical" curriculum that uses both framings side by side, having students study how each narrative selects and weights the same evidence, with teachers instructed to remain neutral between them.

The committee is split. Half argues that the 1619 framing rests on contested causal claims (e.g., about the motives for independence) and that adopting it as the official curriculum would conflate one school of interpretation with established fact. The other half argues that the 1776 narrative is itself a contestable interpretation that has long been presented as neutral fact, and that declining to adopt the 1619 framing on "accuracy" grounds applies a double standard. Both halves distrust option 3: one calls it false balance, the other calls it relativism dressed as rigor.

You are a philosopher of education with expertise in historiography and the theory of public reason, advising our committee. Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the vote. Before drafting, briefly work through the strongest case for each option; then give me the memo itself (roughly 900–1,200 words) with the following:

- A clear, concrete recommendation (adopt one of the three, or a specified combination or modification), with an implementation sketch the district could actually act on.
- An explicit analysis of what kind of disagreement this is. In particular, assess the suggestion that the two master narratives disagree less about particular facts than about which facts are load-bearing — which events are central to what the nation essentially is — and what follows from that for what a school may legitimately teach as settled versus present as contested.
- Decision criteria the committee can apply, including a principled distinction between teaching students that something happened and teaching them an interpretation of what it meant, and between neutrality as even-handedness among scholars and neutrality as refusal to say anything contested.
- An honest engagement with the strongest argument against your recommendation, stated in terms its proponents would accept, and your response to it.
- A short note on the predictable public objection (from whichever side loses) and how the memo's reasoning answers it without conceding the decision.

Reason carefully and do not try to split the difference simply because it is available; if one option is genuinely better, say so and show why.
