In the opening chapter of Bertrand Russell's The Problems of Philosophy (1912), "Appearance and Reality," I came across the following sentence:

"For most practical purposes these differences are unimportant, but to the painter they are all-important: the painter has to unlearn the habit of thinking that things seem to have the colour which common sense says they 'really' have, and to learn the habit of seeing things as they appear."

I spent my working life in colour science, and I read this with some sympathy; but it seems to me that modern psychophysics lets one strengthen Russell's argument considerably, and when I carry out the strengthening, the result appears to prove too much. Here is my version.

(1) Metamerism: two surfaces with physically different reflectance spectra can yield, for the same observer under the same viewing conditions, colour experiences that are perfectly indistinguishable. This is as well established as anything in my old field.

(2) If two experiences are perfectly indistinguishable to the subject, then whatever is directly presented in one is directly presented in the other: same phenomenal character, same thing presented.

(3) In any metameric pair, at most one of the two experiences can directly present the surface's actual reflectance property, since the two properties differ while the presentations coincide.

(4) The point generalises: for any veridical colour experience there is a possible experience, indistinguishable from the inside, in which no such surface is presented at all (a hallucination, an afterimage). By (2), what is presented in the veridical case is the same as what is presented in its non-veridical twin; and what is presented in the twin is plainly not the surface. So even in veridical perception one is directly aware of an appearance, not of the surface's colour itself.

(5) Nothing in (1)–(4) is special to colour; an analogous indistinguishable-twin construction exists for every sensible quality. Hence nothing about the external world is ever directly presented, and all perceptual knowledge is inference from appearance — just as Russell's painter, and Russell's 'real' table, suggest.

(6) But now the argument proves too much. An inference from appearance to reality can be checked only by further perception, which on this view yields only more appearance. Strictly, then, we have no knowledge of external objects at all — including the lamps, retinas, and spectrometers that establish (1). The argument saws off the branch my discipline sits on.

I would like to keep two things: that introspection is authoritative about one's own experience, and that looking at things is, ordinarily, a way of finding out how they are. Tell me which step I ought to give up; explain why that step looked innocent, particularly in light of Russell's sentence; and say what the standard positions — the disjunctivist or naïve realist, the sense-datum theorist, the representationalist, and the colour relationalist — each have to pay in order to reject it.
