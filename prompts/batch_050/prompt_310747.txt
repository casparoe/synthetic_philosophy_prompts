I am taking a seminar on classical Chinese philosophy, and we have just finished the Zhuangzi. Like everyone else, I came in knowing the butterfly dream at the very end of chapter two, but what has actually stuck with me is a much stranger passage earlier in the same chapter, which our edition prints near the beginning of "The Adjustment of Controversies." I am working from James Legge's translation, and the passage reads:

"Great knowledge is wide and comprehensive; small knowledge is partial and restricted. Great speech is exact and complete; small speech is (merely) so much talk. When we sleep, the soul communicates with (what is external to us); when we awake, the body is set free. Our intercourse with others then leads to various activity, and daily there is the striving of mind with mind. There are hesitancies; deep difficulties; reservations; small apprehensions causing restless distress, and great apprehensions producing endless fears. Where their utterances are like arrows from a bow, we have those who feel it their charge to pronounce what is right and what is wrong; where they are given out like the conditions of a covenant, we have those who maintain their views, determined to overcome. (The weakness of their arguments), like the decay (of things) in autumn and winter, shows the failing (of the minds of some) from day to day; or it is like their water which, once voided, cannot be gathered up again. Then their ideas seem as if fast bound with cords, showing that the mind is become like an old and dry moat, and that it is nigh to death, and cannot be restored to vigour and brightness. Joy and anger, sadness and pleasure, anticipation and regret, fickleness and fixedness, vehemence and indolence, eagerness and tardiness;-- (all these moods), like music from an empty tube, or mushrooms from the warm moisture, day and night succeed to one another and come before us, and we do not know whence they sprout. Let us stop! Let us stop! Can we expect to find out suddenly how they are produced?"

In discussion, a classmate argued that this passage is purely about the psychology of disputation -- that Zhuangzi is mocking the logicians and court debaters who exhaust themselves defending fixed positions, and that the sentence about sleep is just a neutral observation marking the rhythm of an ordinary day. Our professor pushed back and said the passage is doing something closer to skepticism, and that the sentence about sleep is the hinge of the whole chapter: she suggested that the frantic, opaque life of the waking mind described here is meant to weaken the privilege we normally grant waking over dreaming, so that by the time we reach the butterfly dream the reader has already lost confidence in the contrast it relies on. I can sort of see both readings, but I cannot make the details line up. If the passage is really continuous with the butterfly dream, why is the sleep sentence so brief and unemphatic, and why does the dreaming state sound almost peaceful compared with waking? Conversely, if it is just satire of debaters, what explains the final images -- moods appearing like music from an empty tube or mushrooms from warm moisture, and the double cry of "Let us stop!" -- which seem to be about the origin of mental states in general rather than about argument?

Could you help me work through this? In particular, I would like (i) an account of what the sentence about sleep and waking is doing in its immediate context, (ii) a reading of the closing similes and of what exactly the speaker is stopping -- the succession of moods, the attempt to find their source, or the striving itself -- and (iii) a judgment on whether the passage genuinely prepares the ground for dream skepticism, or whether it pursues a different point that the butterfly dream only superficially resembles. I am not asking for a survey of secondary literature, just a careful reading of these words and how they fit the chapter as a whole.
