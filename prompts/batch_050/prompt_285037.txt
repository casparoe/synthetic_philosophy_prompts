I'm putting together a seminar unit on historical fiction and biopics — what, if anything, we owe the real dead when we dramatize them. My list of the standard obligations so far: (1) don't falsify the major documented facts — invented scenes and compressed timelines are fine, rewriting what someone actually did isn't; (2) don't defame the dead — no invented crimes, affairs, or cruelties with zero basis in the record; (3) don't whitewash — don't sand a perpetrator down into a charming antihero in a way that launders the moral record; (4) don't treat real victims' suffering as free material for suspense or sentiment; (5) mark, somehow, where the record ends and invention begins. As far as I can tell that's all the usual suspects. Give me a sixth obligation that isn't any of these — not a rewording, not a special case, not a mashup of two of them — and then tell me honestly whether it survives scrunity as a genuine duty to the dead, or whether it collapses into a duty to the living (or to the audience) in disguise.
