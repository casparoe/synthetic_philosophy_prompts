I spent forty years as a working chemist and only picked up philosophy in retirement, so bear with me. I've been reading about the problem of universals and I want to figure out what I actually believe by going by gut on cases. Here's where I land:

- Electrons exist, obviously, even if we only know them through instruments.
- The number 7 exists. I can't bring myself to say my equations were about nothing all those years, and 7 would have been prime even if nobody had ever counted anything.
- Redness, as some extra thing floating over and above all the red objects, does not exist. There are red things and they resemble each other, and that's all.
- The tiger (the species, not individual tigers) exists. Evolution seems to talk about it doing things.
- The equator does not exist. It's a line we drew, nothing's there.
- The melody of Happy Birthday exists -- it survives being played in any key, on any instrument, so it's clearly not identical to any performance of it.
- The average household (2.3 kids or whatever) does not exist.

Now, 2 and 3 already feel like they're in tension -- why does 7 get in but not redness? And 5 vs 6 bothers me too: both seem like abstractions over concrete instances, yet my gut treats them oppositely. Is there one principled account of what to admit into your ontology that gets most of these verdicts, and does any principle get all of them without becoming a patchwork? If something has to give, tell me which verdict you'd overturn and why. Also, what would the resulting account say about two cases I keep flip-flopping on: genes (the type, not the molecular token) and laws of nature?
