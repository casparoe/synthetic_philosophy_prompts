I’ve been hanging around rationalist blogs and forums for years, and every upload/AI-consciousness thread makes me notice that my intuitions are probably running on several incompatible theories at once. Help me find the princple behind them, or tell me which intuitions I should abandon.

Here are my gut verdicts:

1. A faithful whole-brain emulation of a dead person has memories, personality, plans, and a sense of continuity, and it’s run interactively for years. Full moral status, same as the biological person.

2. The same emulation experiences a century of subjective time during ten milliseconds of outside time because the hardware is absurdly fast. Still full moral status. Runtime speed and physical duration shouldn’t matter.

3. Ten exact copies of that emulation are initialized in identical states and fed identical inputs, so they remain computationally identical. My weird intuition is that, while they stay identical, there is only one person there, not ten. Once their inputs diverge, they become ten separate patients.

4. Someone forks an emulation for one minute, tortures it horribly, then deletes it. That seems extremely wrong even though the victim no longer exists afterward and the original never finds out.

5. A chat model produces a moving first-person account of fear and loneliness, but by stipulation it has no memory after generating the reply, no persistent goals, and no hidden recurrent state connecting one answer to the next. Almost zero moral concern, tho the transcript would upset me if a human had written it.

6. Another system produces the exact same transcript, but has persistent memory, a self-model, long-range plans, and internal alarm states that modify its future priorities even when its visible output stays cheerful. Substantial moral concern.

7. Simple agents in an evolutionary simulation learn to avoid damaging regions, freeze after simulated injury, protect resources, and change their behavior based on an internal body-model. Nobody designed them to perform suffering. I think they deserve at least some concern, maybe roughly animal-level if the integration is rich enough.

8. A game NPC is explicitly scripted to scream and beg when injured, but each injury is a one-off animation with no lasting effect on memory, goals, or behavior. Repeating the animation a million times still seems morally irrelevant.

9. A gigantic lookup table reproduces every response the upload from case 1 would give, but does so by matching inputs against prerecorded outputs rather than implementing the relevant causal process. My gut says the table isn’t itself a person, even if its conversations are indistinguishable.

10. A highly advanced optimizer has a detailed self-model, protects its own continuation, bargains, makes long-term commitments, and can be frustrated, but has no pleasure, pain, desire, or affect-like state. I don’t think it has welfare, yet destroying it for fun still feels a little wrong, like destroying a person’s unfinished life’s work rather than hurting a patient.

My tentative principle is something like: a digital system is a welfare subject when a temporally extended causal process integrates self/world models with representations of better and worse states, and those representations counterfactually govern its behavior; separate branches count separately once they causally diverge. But I’m not sure this gets all the cases. In particular, why do speed and deletion not matter, while persistence does? Why can architecture distinguish cases 5 and 6 if the behavior is identical? And is my one-patient-until-divergence intuition in case 3 defensible or just pattern-matching to object identity?

Please try reflective equilibrium: state the strongest precise principle you can find, say which of my verdicts it preserves and which it overturns, and test it against two new cases of your own. I’d rather get a principled account that bites a bullet than a list of vague consciousness buzzwords. Also say whether the residual wrongness in case 10 requires moral patiency, or whether it can be explained in terms of respect, symbolic value, or obligations to other agents.
