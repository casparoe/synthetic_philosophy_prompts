A philanthropic foundation announces a prize: $1 million, paid today, to anyone who can genuinely form the intention, by midnight tonight, to drink a mildly unpleasant but harmless potion tomorrow afternoon. The rules are explicit: the potion's effects are temporary, the money is yours to keep whether or not you actually drink, and everyone — donor and contestant alike — knows that once tomorrow comes you will have every reason not to drink and no reason to do so. A panel of psychologists will verify tonight whether your intention is sincere. Meanwhile, the same foundation funds commitment devices — deposit contracts, locked savings accounts — on the stated rationale that a person's present intentions cannot be trusted to govern their future conduct when incentives change. What theory of intention does the prize presuppose: that intending is a mental act one can perform at will for sufficient inducement, or that an intention must be backed by reasons the agent expects to have at the moment of action? Reconstruct the implicit theory, explain whether the two programs presuppose consistent views of agency, and say what the foundation would have to change — in the prize's design or in its rationale — if it accepted that one cannot intend to do what one knows one will have no reason to do. Answer in at most 400 words.
