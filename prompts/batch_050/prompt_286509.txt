I'm co-writing a paper with a colleague I keep arguing with about the Meno problem (why is knowledge better than mere true belief?), and I want to force us to actually produce something. Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between our two camps. Camp A (mine) holds that knowledge is genuinely more valuable than true belief and that the extra value comes from its stability -- knowledge isn't easily lost when you encounter misleading evidence, the way a luckily-true belief is. Camp B (his) is the swamping camp: once a belief is true, whatever reliably produced it adds no further epistemic value of the relevant kind, any more than a great espresso machine adds value to a cup that's already good; the appearance of extra value is just us caring about truth plus a preference for good methods as a means to future truths.

Give me: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can sign, including at least what each side concedes about Gettier cases and about the instrumental value of reliable belief-forming methods; (2) the remaining disagreements, each stated in wording the side it describes would accept -- no strawmen; (3) the cruxes, and for each one a case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. For at least one crux, use this test I made up: a settlement house that, for a fee, insures your beliefs -- it pays out if a belief you hold today turns out false in five years, and it sets premiums based on how your beliefs were formed. Camp A should predict the house would rationally charge lower premiums for knowledgeable beliefs than for luckily-true ones, which shows the stability is worth something now, not just for future truths; Camp B has to either deny that or explain why what the premium tracks isn't epistemic value. Say explicitly which side that test actually favors, or whether it's inconclusive. If part of our dispute turns out to be verbal, say which part and why.
