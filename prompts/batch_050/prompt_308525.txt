In Part I of Section X ("Of Miracles") of An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, David Hume sets out the model of evidential force on which his argument against miracles is built:

"All probability, then, supposes an opposition of experiments and observations, where the one side is found to overbalance the other, and to produce a degree of evidence, proportioned to the superiority. A hundred instances or experiments on one side, and fifty on another, afford a doubtful expectation of any event; though a hundred uniform experiments, with only one that is contradictory, reasonably beget a pretty strong degree of assurance. In all cases, we must balance the opposite experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the smaller number from the greater, in order to know the exact force of the superior evidence."

A colleague and I disagree about how much this passage can bear. Consider the thesis Hume goes on to draw from it: for any reported event whose occurrence would violate a law of nature established by uniform experience, the proof against the event is, by the arithmetic described above, always at least as strong as any proof human testimony could supply, so that no testimony can make it more reasonable to believe that a miracle occurred than that the report is mistaken or deceptive. Call this the absolute thesis, in contrast to the weaker presumptive thesis that testimony for a miracle merely faces a heavy but defeasible burden. My colleague assigns the absolute thesis a credence of 0.85, regarding it as the rigorous core of the principle that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. I am at 0.3, suspecting that the passage's arithmetic equivocates between the number of confirming instances and the strength of the evidence those instances confer.

Please give me your own credence in the absolute thesis, as a single number, and show the derivation rather than asserting the result:

1. Decompose the question into the sub-questions you take to be load-bearing, assigning each a rough probability with one line of justification. At minimum, address: (a) whether the balance-and-subtract model in the passage can be made precise as a normative account of evidential force at all, given that it treats the probative weight of observations as additive and independent of their content; (b) whether the standard Bayesian repair of that model vindicates the absolute thesis or only the presumptive one; (c) whether the objection pressed most forcefully by John Earman — that sufficiently many conditionally independent witnesses, each more likely truthful than not, can drive the posterior probability of a law-violating event arbitrarily close to one — succeeds; and (d) whether the notion of a law of nature the argument requires, inductively established by exceptionless experience yet genuinely violable, is available either on Hume's own theory of causation or on a contemporary regularity or governing account.
2. State how the component probabilities combine into your overall credence.
3. Identify the sub-question to which your final number is most sensitive, and name the argument, formal result, or interpretive finding that would move it most.
4. Locate the disagreement: given my colleague's 0.85 and my 0.3, on which sub-question do we most plausibly differ, and what would each of us have to concede in order to converge?

Two constraints. First, argue from the passage quoted above — the counting and subtraction machinery — rather than from Hume's more famous maxim about testimony and the miraculousness of its falsehood; I want to know whether the machinery, not the slogan, can carry the conclusion. Second, say explicitly whether your credence would rise or fall, and roughly by how much, if the thesis under evaluation were the presumptive reading — the one arguably licensed by Hume's own concession, later in the same section, that perfectly uniform and uncontradicted testimony (his example is eight days of darkness over the whole earth, reported by all authors in all languages) ought to be received as certain.
