Suppose Leibniz were brought into a present-day philosophy seminar and confronted with the modern debate over origin essentialism: the thesis, defended by some contemporary metaphysicians, that a person's origins are essential to them, such that had your parents conceived a different child from a different sperm and egg — even one genetically identical and raised in the same home under the same name — that child would not have been you, and you could not have existed with different origins.

Reconstruct, as carefully and charitably as you can, how Leibniz would respond to this thesis, given his own commitments: the doctrine of complete individual concepts, the principle of the identity of indiscernibles, his account of possible worlds and God's choice among them, and his views on individuation. Would he accept the thesis, reject it, or regard it as resting on a confusion his system dissolves? Where in his framework would the modern debate's central intuition — that this particular child and a merely similar possible child must be numerically distinct — find its natural home, if it has one?

Then put the reconstructed Leibniz to work on three cases he never encountered:

1. Embryo selection: a clinician must choose which of several viable embryos to implant. The parents ask whether, by choosing a different embryo, they would be choosing that a different person exist, or whether they might instead be determining which origins the same future person will have had. What would Leibniz say, and would he think the question is even well-formed?

2. Identical twins: two siblings arise from a single fertilized egg that splits after conception. If origins are essential, at what moment does each twin acquire an essential origin, and how would Leibniz — who held that no two substances are exactly alike — handle a case where the divergence appears to begin after the shared origin?

3. Counterfactual biography: a person says, "If my grandparents had never emigrated, I would not exist; my parents would have married other people." Most of us accept this readily. Would Leibniz treat it as a metaphysical discovery, a trivial consequence of his system, or a loose way of speaking that needs correction?

Throughout, distinguish what Leibniz actually held from what a plausible Leibnizian might develop in response to unfamiliar questions, and flag any point where you think the historical materials genuinely underdetermine the answer. Finally, identify what you take to be the single greatest strain that the modern debate places on his system — the point where a charitable interpreter must either revise Leibniz or concede that the modern thesis captures something he cannot.
