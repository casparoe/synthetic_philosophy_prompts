I'm writing a term paper for a graduate seminar on early analytic philosophy, and I've settled on a somewhat speculative thesis: that the sixth chapter of Moore's Principia Ethica — the part where he argues that the appreciation of beautiful objects and the pleasures of personal affection are by far the greatest intrinsic goods — gives us surprisingly sharp resources for evaluating two things that didn't exist in 1903: AI companion apps (chatbots designed to simulate friendship and romantic attachment) and AI-generated art and music. My own reading is that Moore would be far more hostile to the companions than to the art, because of the role he assigns to the real existence and actual qualities of the object of one's affection: admiring a person for qualities they genuinely have is valuable, but loving what is in fact a simulation of a person seems, on his framework, to be a case of directing emotion toward an object that lacks the qualities one takes it to have. A classmate pushed back, arguing that since Moore's method of isolation makes the value reside in the whole organic unity of the appreciator's state of consciousness plus its object, the subjectively identical experience might do nearly all the work, making Moore much friendlier to AI companionship than my reading suggests.

Predict Moore's actual view. Given what he says about organic unities, the method of isolation, the contribution of belief and real existence to the value of aesthetic and personal appreciation, and his general picture of what makes the love of a person good, what would he most plausibly conclude about: (1) a person's attachment to an AI companion they know is not conscious; (2) the same attachment where the person has deceived themselves into thinking the companion has a rich inner life; and (3) the appreciation of AI-generated music or painting, where the object really exists and has the aesthetic properties in question, but no human sensibility produced it? For each case, say whether Moore would find it intrinsically valuable, less valuable than its human counterpart, positively bad, or simply not the kind of thing his framework can rank — and ground the prediction in the machinery of chapter VI rather than in generic moralizing.

Please commit to definite predictions rather than surveying the interpretive options: I want a position I can argue against in the paper, with reasons Moore himself could have endorsed. Where the 1903 text genuinely underdetermines the answer — and I expect the third case is the hardest — mark that spot explicitly and say which of his commitments you'd have to weight more heavily to resolve it, and why you weight them the way you do.
