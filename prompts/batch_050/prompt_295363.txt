I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and next month I am giving a thirty-minute talk at an interdisciplinary physics-of-information workshop, defending a position I arrived at largely through thinking about computation: that Maxwell's demon is fully exorcised by the physics of memory. My thesis is that the demon can acquire information about a gas at no necessary thermodynamic cost (measurement can in principle be thermodynamically free), that the second law is rescued entirely by Landauer's principle, and that the minimum kT ln 2 cost of erasing the demon's memory of its measurements is not merely one possible resolution but the resolution — so that "information is physical" is a substantive claim about the laws of thermodynamics and not a slogan. I will present the Szilárd-style single-molecule engine as my central case and argue that any attempt to make the measurement step carry the cost instead either relocates an equivalent cost elsewhere or quietly smuggles in an irreversible step.

The audience worries me. It contains: a statistical physicist who I gather is sympathetic to the critique that Landauer-based resolutions are circular — that you cannot derive the cost of erasure from statistical mechanics without already assuming the second law you are using it to protect, so the "resolution" begs the question; a condensed-matter experimentalist who works on information-to-work conversion with feedback and thinks in terms of fluctuation theorems, and who I suspect believes the whole demon framing has been superseded by results that treat information as just another resource in a Jarzynski-style equality, no philosophy required; and one philosopher of physics who keeps asking, at every workshop, why we should think there is a fact of the matter about which step "really" carries the entropy cost, given that the total books always balance. There will also be a few graduate students who have read Bennett's reversibility arguments and treat them as settled scripture.

Please prepare my Q&A briefing, in British English. I want the objections I will actually receive from this audience — not the ones a popular-science crowd would raise — roughly ordered by the damage each would do to my thesis if I handle it badly, each phrased in the idiom the person raising it would genuinely use (a fluctuation-theorem experimentalist does not object the way a philosopher does). For each objection: what I should concede plainly, what I should hold, my best two-minute reply, and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Where my engineer's framing — bits, memory registers, reset operations, one-way functions — is helping me and where it might be blinding me to something a physicist takes for granted, flag that too. Finish with the single question I am hoping nobody asks, and what I should say if it comes anyway.
