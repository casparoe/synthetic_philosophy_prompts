Some months ago I came across the runaway-trolley dilemma in a magazine article, and I have been chewing on it ever since, without any training in philosophy. Nearly everyone I ask says it is acceptable to divert the trolley onto a side track where it will kill one person in order to save five, but not acceptable to push one large person off a footbridge to stop the trolley, even though the arithmetic is identical. The explanation I keep encountering is that in the first case the death is a foreseen but unintended side effect, while in the second the death is the very instrument by which the five are saved: if the man on the bridge somehow survived the fall unharmed, the plan would have failed, whereas the diverted trolley saves the five whether or not the worker on the side track is hurt. I want to test whether that explanation is really what drives the judgments, or whether something else is doing the work, and for that I need a case built to order.

Construct a scenario meeting three conditions: (i) one person is seriously harmed or killed as the genuine instrument of saving several others — by the planner's own reasoning, if the one emerged unharmed, the rescue would fail; (ii) the person acting fully understands this and would privately count the plan as having failed if the one were not harmed; and (iii) despite both of these, most reasonable people still judge the action permissible, or at least markedly more permissible than pushing the man off the footbridge. Constraints: a realistic setting (a fire, a flood, a mine, a road, a factory floor), no contraptions that exist only in philosophy articles, no organ harvesting, and the scenario itself no longer than a paragraph. Then give me a matched companion case, as similar to the first as you can make it, in which the harm to the one is a mere side effect rather than the instrument, so I can hold the two side by side and compare my own reactions.

After the two scenarios, do the bookkeeping. For each case, apply the test of whether the plan still succeeds if the victim is unharmed, report the verdict you expect most people to reach, and state plainly what the pair implies about the side-effect-versus-instrument explanation: does it survive, does it need amendment, or does it break? Then argue the other side — how would a defender of that explanation attack your first case, for instance by redescribing what the rescuer actually needs (a body, an obstruction, a weight, a delay) so that the harm itself is not strictly the instrument, and is that redescription honest or a dodge? Finally, tell me which single detail of your scenario is carrying the load, and what happens to the intuition if that detail is removed. If you come to believe that no case can satisfy all three conditions at once, say so and show me where every attempt breaks down, since that would itself be an interesting result about how firm the distinction really is.
