Ok so I'm a junior and I run the physics club with two friends and I'm also on the school's lit mag (long story). Last week our club advisor was telling us about the hole argument from general relativity and I've honestly been stuck on it for days. My rough understanding: take a solution of Einstein's equations, pick some empty region of spacetime, the hole, and keep everything outside it fixed. Then you can mathematically drag all the stuff inside the hole around, like sliding furniture without touching the walls, and you get a new description that agrees with the old one everywhere outside the hole but assigns different values to each point inside. If you think spacetime points are real things with their own identities, then those are two genuinely different universes, wich means the state outside the hole doesn't determine what happens inside and determinism just breaks. The usual escape is to say the two descriptions are the same universe and the rearrangement is no more physical than switching coordinate systems. Apparently this argument derailed Einstein himself for a while, which makes me feel better about losing my whole weekend to it.

So here's what I want for the lit mag: a sestina where the form performs the argument instead of just describing it. Six end-words, shuffled in the standard sestina pattern across six stanzas, all six back in the envoi. I want the six words to play the role of the contents of the hole: every stanza has exactly the same six ingredients, but the permutation changes which word sits in which slot, like the fields getting dragged to different points while the boundary stays untouched. Two rules to keep the analogy honest. First, the words can't change meaning between stanzas, so if light is a noun in stanza one it can't become a verb in stanza three, because a dragged field keeps its value and only its address changes. Second, each stanza still has to read as a description of the same situation, the way both models agree about everything outside the hole. Then the envoi has to actually take a stand on the question: are these six stanzas six different poems that happen to share a vocabulary, or one poem told six ways? Pick an answer and commit to it. Bonus points if the fact that the sestina's permutation rule makes every later stanza fully determined by the first one feels like a quiet wink at the determinism part. I'd love it if someone who knows the hole argument could see it in the structure without any explanation attached, but also if my friends on the mag who think physics is a cult just think it's a good poem.
