Two friends of mine have been arguing about something that happened at a former employer of one of them, and they've asked me to settle it; I can't, so I'm bringing it to you. The facts are these: a junior data analyst at a logistics firm, Maya, told her director in a meeting that delivery delays were clustering around a particular warehouse's shift change. The director, without looking at her figures, said it was probably noise and moved on. Six months later an outside consultant's report made the same finding, and the firm acted on it.

The first friend calls this a textbook case of epistemic injustice: the director gave Maya less credibility than her evidence warranted because she was junior and lacked a statistics degree, and the proof is that the same claim was believed the moment it came out of a consultant's mouth. The wrong, she says, was complete at the moment of dismissal; the later vindication merely makes it visible. The second friend replies that managers must triage hundreds of claims a week and that using seniority, credentials, and track record as proxies for reliability is not prejudice but rational allocation of scarce attention; Maya had twice before raised alarms that didn't pan out, and the consultant brought both a reputation and a full report. He adds that the claim turning out to be true cannot retroactively convict the director, since the decision must be evaluated by what was knowable at the time — had Maya's claim been false, no one would call the dismissal unjust, so the injustice charge is riding on hindsight. The first friend counters that the track record argument is self-sealing: if a junior person's early warnings are dismissed without examination, she can never accumulate the track record that would earn her a hearing, so the proxy entrenches the very deficit it cites. She also insists there is a difference between weighing someone's testimony and refusing to engage it at all — the director never looked at the figures. The second friend answers that refusing to look is simply what triage looks like under time pressure, that a standard requiring managers to examine every claim would make the job impossible, and that the consultant's endorsement genuinely added information because independent verification is evidence, so the double-standard charge fails too.

Please adjudicate this disagreement point by point rather than declaring an overall winner: say who is right about whether hindsight can bear on the verdict, about whether refusing to examine a claim differs morally from discounting it, about whether the self-sealing track record objection succeeds, and about whether the consultant comparison demonstrates a double standard or rational deference to new evidence. Then say what, if anything, both friends are missing — for instance, whether a decision can be simultaneously defensible as management and wrongful to the person as a knower, and what each side would have to concede to make that coherent.

Please write your answer as continuous prose, like a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
