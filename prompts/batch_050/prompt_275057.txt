An epistemologist I know has been working through a series of cases about outright belief and statistical evidence, and I have kept a record of her verdicts. They are as follows:

1. In a fair lottery with 1,000 tickets and exactly one guaranteed winner, believing that ticket 47 will lose, purely on the basis of the odds, is rational — though she insists it does not amount to knowledge.
2. Believing, of each ticket in that lottery considered individually, that it will lose, is rational in every one of the 1,000 instances.
3. Believing that every ticket will lose is irrational, on the grounds that it contradicts something the believer already knows: that there is a winner.
4. In a fair 10-ticket lottery of the same structure, believing that one's own ticket will lose is irrational; a probability of 0.9, she holds, is not high enough for outright belief.
5. Where two separate 1,000-ticket lotteries are run, believing both that ticket A will lose in the first and that ticket B will lose in the second — including believing the two conjuncts together — is rational.
6. The author of a careful work of nonfiction believes each of its 500 claims individually, and at the same time believes, on general grounds of fallibility, that the book contains at least one error. My colleague judges this pair of attitudes, held jointly, to be perfectly rational, and regards the author's concession in the preface as a mark of intellectual honesty rather than any kind of incoherence.

Now consider a new case. A meticulous historian believes each of the 600 claims in her new book, with roughly the same confidence the nonfiction author has in his, but she is temperamentally immodest: she declines to make any preface-style concession and instead believes outright that every single claim in the book is true. Her total set of beliefs is internally consistent, since — unlike in the lottery — no one knows in advance that the book contains an error.

Predict my colleague's verdict on the historian. More importantly, identify the rule she appears to be running: is it a probabilistic-threshold view on which outright belief requires credence above some threshold between 0.9 and 0.999, with conjunctions rejected whenever their compounded probability falls below it? Or is it a consistency-based view on which any set of individually high-probability beliefs is rationally permissible so long as the set is consistent with what the believer knows — which would condemn the full lottery conjunction but acquit the historian? Say which rule better fits her track record, what each rule predicts about the historian, and give your best prediction with your reasons. Finally, name the single further case that would most cleanly discriminate between the two rules — for instance, asking her about believing that none of the first 600 tickets in the 1,000-ticket lottery will win, a belief consistent with everything known but with a probability of only about 0.55 — and explain what each possible verdict on that case would establish.
