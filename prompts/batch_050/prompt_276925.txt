I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of neuroscience, which in practice means I keep getting voluntold into metaphysics of mind reading groups. Last week I presented the argument below as basically airtight, and then someone asked why the same reasoning doesn't apply to neurons, and I've been spiralling since. Please answer as a metaphysician of mind who has seen every version of the causal exclusion argument. Think carefully, and sketch a couple of candidate weak points before committing to one — I want the actual diagnosis with the reasoning, not just a verdict on which line is guilty.

Here's the argument:

(1) Every physical event that has a cause at all has a sufficient physical cause.
(2) Mental properties aren't identical to physical properties: pain is realized one way in us, a different way in octopuses, a third way in some hypothetical silicon system, so the property of being in pain isn't any physical property.
(3) Take a case: I have a headache and reach for the aspirin. By (1), my reaching has a sufficient physical cause, some chain of neural and muscular events — call it P.
(4) If P is genuinely sufficient for the reaching, then given P, the reaching was going to happen no matter what else was true. In particular it would still have happened even if the headache hadn't been there. A sufficient cause leaves no work for anything distinct from it.
(5) So the headache made no difference to the reaching.
(6) And it can't be that the reaching has two independent sufficient causes — it isn't overdetermined like a firing squad, it's one ordinary action.
(7) So the headache qua mental property causes nothing, and the same goes for every mental property. Mental epiphenomenalism.
(8) But now notice this pattern of reasoning never mentions minds. The property of being a neuron firing is realized in particular ion channel dynamics and isn't identical to that specific molecular configuration, any more than pain is identical to a brain state. So by the same reasoning as (4)–(5), the neuron's firing makes no difference either. Nor the muscle contraction, nor the hurricane, nor the recession. Iterate downwards and nothing above fundamental physics ever causes anything. Epidemiologists and economists are literally never talking about causes.
(9) But (9)... I mean, (8) is absurd. So something in (1)–(7) is wrong, even though each of them looked fine an hour ago.

I'm fairly sure I want to keep (1), and I'd really like to keep (2) because the whole point was a nonreductive physicalism with real mental causation, and obviously I'm keeping the rejection of (8). So I guess one of (4)–(7) has to go. Which one, why did it look plausible, and what does rejecting it actually cost? Two specific things I want priced. First: does killing the bad step leave me any argument at all against interactionist dualism? Embarrasingly, that was the entire point of the exercise — I wanted closure to rule out a non-physical mind pulling levers in the brain. Second: if I say the headache causes the reaching "alongside" P, am I just counting one thing twice, and is that actually a problem or only an aesthetic one?
