I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature about conflicts inside virtual worlds — a player whose painstakingly built structure was destroyed overnight by strangers, a teenager who was systematically harassed by the same group of avatars for months, an auction-house trader who lost a collection of rare items worth real money to an exploit. Whenever I interview skeptics about why any of this deserves sympathy, let alone policy attention, I get the same response: it's just a game. The phrase is doing real work in these conversations — it's offered as a reason to dismiss the victims' complaints, not merely as a description — but my sources seem to mean quite different things by it, and some of them switch meanings mid-argument.

Before I write the piece, I want to get the slogan itself straight. What are the distinct precise claims that "it's just a game" could be making? I can already gesture at a few — that the objects involved (the structure, the items) don't really exist; that they exist but are worthless; that whatever exists there, the losses are self-inflicted because the victim could simply log off; that actions inside a fiction can't wrong anyone, the way knocking over a chess piece isn't theft — but I'm confident this list is incomplete and probably misdrawn, and I'd like the readings laid out systematically rather than as my hunches. For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something that's actually false, and — this is the part that matters for the article — whether it would actually justify dismissing the complaints in my three cases, or whether the reading people retreat to under pressure is a weaker one than the reading they started with.

One more thing: I'd rather be told plainly if the premise of this exercise is confused. If "it's just a game" isn't really a thesis at all but something else — a claim about proportion, a convention about which norms apply, a conversation-ender — say so, and if my three cases don't actually get illuminated by disambiguating the slogan, tell me that instead of politely sorting them anyway.
