I write for a magazine covering science and ideas, and my editor has killed a paragraph of mine twice now with the same rejoinder. I had flagged, in a piece on popular history writing, the sceptical worry associated with E. H. Carr and Hayden White: that any historical account is shaped by selection, narrative form, and the historian's own standpoint, so that "the facts" never simply speak. His reply is always the slogan, traced back to Ranke, that the historian's job is just to show how it actually was, as if that settled the matter. I need to know what I am actually up against before I push back a third time. Please give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion: five distinct precise claims that slogan could be making, running from the near-trivial (historians should not fabricate documents) through the substantive (history can genuinely aim at truth about the past despite the historian's situatedness) to the radical (an account can present the past unmediated, free of selection and emplotment), and for each one a verdict on whether it is defensible and whether it would actually answer the Carr-and-White objection my paragraph was making.
