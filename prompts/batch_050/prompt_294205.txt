I'm a retired experimental physicist and, late in life, I've gotten bitten by the philosophy bug. I help run a discussion evening at our local lifelong-learning institute -- about a dozen adults, mostly other retired professionals (engineers, a couple of doctors, one very sharp former accountant), and we get 90 minutes. Next month it's my turn and I want to do something on the poverty of the stimulus argument, i.e. the claim that children must bring built-in structure to language because the speech they hear is too thin and messy to explain what they end up knowing.

Here's the thing though. I don't want to lecture. In my old life, whenever someone fit a curve to sparse data, the interesting question was always what the fitting procedure had already assumed, and I want the group to feel that in their bones rather than hear it from me. So: design me an activity or game where the participants themselves try to learn some kind of miniature artificial language or pattern from a small, gappy set of examples, commit publicly to judgments about new cases, and then discover that (a) they all converged on roughly the same judgments even though the examples were compatible with several very different rules, and (b) they can't quite say why they ruled the alternatives out. I want them to have the "wait, where did that come from?" moment themselves, before anyone says the words poverty of the stimulus.

Please give me: the actual rules and materials (I can print cards, nothing fancier), a rough timeline for the 90 minutes, and some way to make the commitments binding -- small stakes or public predictions, so people can't just say afterwards "oh I was thinking that too". I'd also love a second phase, maybe with a planted role or a doctored set of examples, that shows how someone with different built-in biases would have learned a different language from the same data.

Then a debrief plan that maps what happened in the room onto the actual argument -- the underdetermination part, the convergence part, the "so the learner must contribute something" part. Two requests for the debrief. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (someone will insist they just noticed the pattern, or that the examples weren't really that sparse) and how to handle it. Second, be honest about which parts of the real debate the game can't capture -- I gather from my reading that philosophers and linguists still fight about how impoverished children's input actually is and whether fancy statistical learners can do without innate grammar-specific machinery. Please mark clearly what's more or less settled versus what's genuinely contested among the people who work on this, because I don't want to stand up there and present a live controversy as established fact, and the group will definately ask.
