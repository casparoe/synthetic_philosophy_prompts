So I'm a junior and I'm in this after-school debate society, and our coach had the idea that instead of doing normal debates we'd try writing a "joint statement" where the two sides of an argument have to agree on a document together, kind of like when scientists who disagree write a paper about what they actually disagree about. We picked the topic of whether technology is political. On one side there's kids defending the view associated with Langdon Winner, that artifacts and technical systems can themselves embody political arrangements -- like the famous example of the low bridges on the parkways to Jones Beach that allegedly kept buses (and therefore poor people and Black people) from reaching the beach, or machines designed a certain way specifically to weaken workers' bargaining power. On the other side there's kids defending the instrumentalist view, that technology is basically a neutral tool and all the politics is in the people and institutions using it -- the "guns don't kill people" style position, roughly. Both sides have been talking past each other for two meetings now and honestly its getting painful.

So: draft the joint statement these two camps would produce if they actually had to sign something together. I want a numbered list of claims BOTH sides can genuinely sign (stuff like what everyone agrees the examples do and don't show, what the naive version of each side gets wrong, what would need to be true for an artifact to count as political "by design" vs just politically used), then a disagreements section where each side's position is stated in words that side would actually accept -- no strawmen, my coach is strict about that. One thing that came up in our meetings: apparently the historical facts about the Moses bridges have been disputed, like maybe the buses were excluded by regulations anyway and the bridges weren't unusually low for their time? We couldn't resolve whether that even matters, so please address whether the two sides agree that the philosophical question depends on the empirical accuracy of the famous examples, or whether hypotheticals would do the same work.

Then the part I care most about: the cruxes. List the specific sub-questions where, if you settled them, at least one side would have to give ground -- like whether "political" has to mean intentional, whether it counts if the politics is baked in via the surrounding social system rather than the artifact itself, whether a technology having foreseeable-but-unintended political consequences is enough. For each crux, say what kind of evidence, case, or argument both sides agree in advance would bear on it, and record what each side predicts. And if you think part of this whole fight is just the two camps using words like "political" or "neutral" differently, say which part and why -- we suspect a decent chunk of it is verbal but we can't pin down where.
