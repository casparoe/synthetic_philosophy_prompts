Two friends of mine, neither of whom has studied philosophy, have been arguing about a case from the news, and I'd like a careful referee.

The case: two nurses at different hospitals each misread a patient's chart and prepared a dangerously wrong dose of the same medication. In the first hospital, a pharmacist caught the error and nothing happened to the patient. In the second, nobody caught it and the patient died. The hospital fired the second nurse and she may face criminal charges; the first nurse got a written warning.

My friend Anna says this is obviously unjust. Her reasoning, as I remember it: (1) The two nurses did exactly the same thing — same carelessness, same moment of inattention. What happened afterward was pure chance, decided by whether a pharmacist happened to be vigilant that day. (2) We don't control luck, and people should only be judged on what they control, otherwise we're punishing them for the weather. (3) So the two nurses deserve identical blame, and the difference in consequences — job loss, prosecution, a lifetime of guilt for one and a slap on the wrist for the other — is a moral scandal we've all just gotten used to.

My friend Ben pushed back through several rounds: (1) He said Anna's principle proves too much. If outcomes never matter, then attempted murder and murder are morally identical, and so are drunk driving that gets home safely and drunk driving that kills a child — and he refuses to accept a morality in which a grieving family has no more standing than a relieved one. (2) Anna replied that he's confusing the harm done with the blameworthiness of the person. The family is owed more because more was lost, but the driver himself isn't a worse person in one case than the other. The debt differs; the desert doesn't. (3) Ben then tried a different argument: the outcome isn't just luck, it's evidence. When a patient dies, we know the negligence was real and serious; when the pharmacist catches it, maybe the dose would have been caught at the next step anyway. So punishing outcomes is really punishing *certainty*, and a legal system can only act on what it can prove. (4) Anna said this still doesn't justify the *difference in felt moral judgment* — the way everyone, including the nurses themselves, treats the second nurse as a worse human being — and that even if the law has practical excuses, our private moral reactions should follow what people control. (5) Ben's last move was to ask whether Anna actually lives by her own principle: if her child were the one who died, would she really feel toward that nurse exactly what she'd feel toward a nurse whose identical error was caught? He said if the answer is no, then her principle is one she holds in theory but nobody, including her, believes in practice — and a principle nobody can actually act on might not be a principle at all.

Could you go through the exchange move by move — Anna's three opening points and Ben's five responses, including Anna's replies at steps 2 and 4 — and say at each step who has the better of it and why? I'm also interested in whether both of them are missing something: is there a way of thinking about blame, punishment, and emotion that dissolves the disagreement rather than declaring a winner, or is one side simply right?
