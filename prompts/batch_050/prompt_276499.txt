Ok so my reading group cooked up an argument last week that obviously proves too much, and after two hours of arguing we still couldn't find the bad step. Background first, since it matters: there's this idea called the unilateralist's curse -- when lots of people can each single-handedly bring something about (leaking a document, launching a project, publicly naming an alleged threat), the thing tends to get done even when doing it is net bad, because whoever acts is whoever most overestimates the upside. Fine so far. But then we wrote this:

(1) When many agents each could unilaterally do X, X happens iff at least one agent's estimate of X's value clears their personal threshold for acting.

(2) Each agent's estimate is built from noisy evidence, so estimates spread out around the true value, and the highest estimate in the group is on average an overestimate -- same math as the winner's curse in auctions.

(3) So whenever X actually gets done, the doer's estimate was probably inflated, and the more potential doers there were, the worse the inflation. A rational agent who knows all this should discount her own estimate toward the group average before acting, possibly below her threshold.

(4) But notice the flip side: the fact that nobody else has done X is itself evidence about X's value. If X were really worth doing, someone among the many capable agents would likely have done it already -- the same reason you don't expect to find a $500 bill lying on the sidewalk.

(5) And (3) and (4) are really the same move in different clothes: in both, rationality requires treating other people's non-action as evidence that can outweigh your own positive judgment.

(6) Therefore a rational agent should almost never act on a judgment that some available-but-untaken action is worth taking. Don't start the company nobody started, don't publish the objection nobody published, don't pick up the litter everyone walked past.

(7) (6) is absurd. So one of the steps above is wrong.

Two of us think the problem is (2), since real people's estimates aren't independent draws -- we read the same blogs. Two think it's the jump at (5), since in the curse case people actively deliberated and declined, whereas the litter-walkers may never have even considered acting, and those carry totally different evidential weight. I suspect it's (4), but I can't articulate why without sounding like I just really want to be allowed to start companies.

Which step do you actually reject? For each candidate, explain why it looked fine and what you'd have to give up to reject it. And please be concrete about whether the fix depends on things like: did the non-actors face the same decision and know it, do the estimates share evidence, and does the actor know how many potential actors there even are. If it turns out (6) is less absurd than I think for some domains (speculation in efficient markets?) but absurd for others, say where the line falls and why. For what it's worth I'd rather keep (1)-(2), since that's just the standard setup, and I'd really rather keep my permission slip to start the company.
