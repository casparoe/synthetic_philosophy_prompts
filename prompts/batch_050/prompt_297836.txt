I am an undergraduate in a seminar on freedom of expression, and for my final paper I am working on a passage from Chapter III of John Stuart Mill's On Liberty that has split my reading group. After remarking that even opinions "lose their immunity, when the circumstances in which they are expressed are such as to constitute their expression a positive instigation to some mischievous act," Mill writes:

"An opinion that corn-dealers are starvers of the poor, or that private property is robbery, ought to be unmolested when simply circulated through the press, but may justly incur punishment when delivered orally to an excited mob assembled before the house of a corn-dealer, or when handed about among the same mob in the form of a placard."

The group has settled on four readings of what, exactly, changes the moral status of the same opinion between the press case and the mob case, and I want to test whether the space of readings is exhausted. The readings we already have:

First, the circumstantial reading: Mill prohibits no opinion as such; only the circumstances of its expression change, so the passage shows that the liberty principle operates on the context of utterance rather than its content, and any content-based restriction is thereby ruled out.

Second, the incitement reading: the passage anticipates the modern imminence test, under which expression may be punished only where harmful action is both likely and immediate, with the mob's excitement serving as evidence of likelihood and the corn-dealer's house as evidence of imminence.

Third, the crowd-psychology reading: what matters is the medium's effect on deliberation. Print reaches readers one at a time and leaves room for reflection, whereas an excited crowd suspends individual judgment, so the regulated variable is the audience's capacity for cool deliberation, and the passage generalizes to any medium that bypasses it.

Fourth, the menace reading: the detail that the mob is assembled before the corn-dealer's house is doing the work. Addressed to an identifiable victim at his doorstep, the expression functions as a threat rather than as a contribution to opinion, and it is the threatening character, not the instigation, that forfeits immunity.

My question: can you supply a fifth reading of the passage that is genuinely different from these four — not a rewording, a special case, or a combination of them — and that the actual wording of the text supports? Point to the specific words or structural features your reading rests on, and say what it commits Mill to that the other four do not. Then give me your honest assessment of whether it holds up: does it survive a careful look at the passage, and does it deliver a different verdict from all four listed readings on at least one contemporary case, such as a widely shared post that names a private individual, or does it collapse back into one of them under pressure?
