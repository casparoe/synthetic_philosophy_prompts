I teach a mixed grad seminar (history and philosophy students) on the psychology of perpetrators, and every year we read Browning's Ordinary Men against Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners, and every year the discussion stalls in the exact same place. I'm starting to think the stall is more interesting than the books, so that's what I want diagnosed.

Quick sketch so we're on the same page. Both books lean heavily on the same material: postwar interrogation records from Reserve Police Battalion 101, a unit of mostly middle-aged, mostly unremarkable Hamburg men who shot or deported tens of thousands of Jews in occupied Poland. The famous episodes are shared evidence. At the first massacre, the battalion's commander, visibly shaken, told the men that anyone who didn't feel up to it could step out, and at first only a dozen or so of the five hundred did, though more drifted away as the day went on. Nobody who refused was shot; the worst anyone seems to have faced was a transfer or being sneered at. The men killed face to face, at close range, for months. Some took souvenir photographs. Later, hunting down escapees hiding in the forests became almost routine sport. Browning's reading: these were ordinary men, and the causal weight sits on situation and group dynamics -- authority, conformity, not wanting to look cowardly or let your comrades do the dirty work alone, gradual brutalization -- with ideology as background music, and he reaches for Milgram's obedience experiments and Zimbardo's prison study as support. Goldhagen's reading: the men killed willingly, often with cruelty beyond anything ordered, because a specifically German eliminationist antisemitism made killing Jews seem right to them; the opt-out offer proves voluntariness rather than refuting it.

Here's the stall. Everyone in the room grants all the facts. Then half say ordinary people under those pressures, read Milgram, and the other half say ordinary people don't take souvenir photos at mass graves, and nobody has moved in the thirty years since either. I have hypotheses about what's going on but no idea which is right: (a) they're answering different questions under one heading -- Browning explains how any human becomes a mass killer, Goldhagen explains why these Germans did, so no fact can settle it; (b) it's a fight about standards of evidence for motive, since both sides treat the same opt-out datum as supporting them; (c) it's a proxy moral war, since Browning sounds like exculpation and Goldhagen like collective guilt, and the empirical claims are cover; (d) there's a hidden comparative-empirical question about how cross-culturally normal enthusiastic cruelty in atrocity is, which would actually discriminate but nobody runs it.

So: which diagnosis fits, judging from what each side actually argues rather than the caricatures? Don't give me a verdict on who was right. Give me the structure. And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress -- a distinction, a comparative dataset, an agreed test for motive attribution -- or should I expect none, because this is really a dispute about what historical explanation even is? Two side questions: does the recent reputational damage to Milgram and Zimbardo (coaching, demand characteristics, archival problems) actually change anything or is it beside the point? And if the seminar framing itself is the problem -- if I've set these two up as purer opposites than they are, since Browning concedes ideology and Goldhagen concedes not every killer was a sadist -- say that too.
