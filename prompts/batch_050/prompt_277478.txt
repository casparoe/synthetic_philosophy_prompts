So here's my situation. I'm the assistant principal at a mid-sized high school, and after last years talent show (a kid did five brutal minutes about his ex-girlfriend, parents complained, the local paper picked it up, whole thing) I've somehow become the guy who pre-approves every comedy bit. Stand-up, skits, the emcee's between-act jokes, all of it comes across my desk now — roughly 30 acts a year, plus the odd pep rally.

Problem: I am not a comedy person. Ages ago I took a philosophy elective that covered the main theories of humor — superiority, incongruity, relief, and that newer benign violation idea — and I remember thinking each one captured something real, but none of them alone told you whether a specific joke is okay to tell. Now I need exactly that, as a procedure I can actually run in about ten minutes per act, with no comedy expertise, knowing my risk-averse principal has to be able to sign off on whatever I decide.

So: turn those theories into a decision procedure. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a pass or fail at each step, where the procedure stops with a hard no, and where it has to hand the call back to me because it's a values question and not a facts question. The theories pull against each other — superiority thinking would ban half of all jokes since nearly everything has a butt, while benign violation says a joke can fail by being too tame as well as too harsh — so tell me how the procedure handles that: which distinctions have to survive the simplification, which I can safely drop, and where the whole thing will predictably misfire.

One specific thing it has to handle: some jokes read totally fine to most of the audience but carry a second meaning that lands only for a smaller in-group, with plausible deniability for the teller. My hunch is incongruity theory has something to say here since the "resolution" is different for different listeners, but I don't know how to make that checkable.

Then run the finished procedure on these four actual acts:

1. A sophomore does three minutes about a specific, identifiable freshman who tripped and spilled a full lunch tray on himself last week. It killed at tryouts. The freshman was not consulted, as far as I know.
2. The chess club's skit: four minutes of dense chess in-jokes ending in a parody of a rival school's chess team. The tryout crowd was dead silent except the chess kids.
3. An emcee bit about the school bus that broke down on the interstate last month. Nobody was hurt, but it made the news and parents were genuinely scared. Two students told me it's "too soon"; the emcee says it's fine precisely because nobody got hurt.
4. A senior does an affectionate but pretty savage impression of ME — my voice, my whole thing about tucking in shirts. Honestly, I laughed. I want to approve it, and I'm aware thats exactly why it needs to go through the procedure cold. If the procedure says no, I want to hear the no.

Finally: what can't this procedure do, and which calls are still going to come down to my gut no matter what?
