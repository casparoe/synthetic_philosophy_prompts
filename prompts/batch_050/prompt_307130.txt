I am setting the weekly exercise for my graduate seminar on formal epistemology and want a handout that forces students past the standard summary of Aumann's agreement theorem. Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers: the first defends the common prior assumption as a genuine constraint on rationality, in the spirit of the Harsanyi doctrine; the second holds that the assumption is neither normatively required nor doing the real work in no-agreement results. Their dispute must centre on the following stipulated case: two traders, Ada and Bo, openly acknowledge a shared prior over every proposition about a hidden coin's bias, but Ada updates by strict conditionalisation while Bo updates by Jeffrey conditionalisation on uncertain evidence; after receiving private evidence they reach common knowledge that Ada assigns the proposition "the next toss lands heads" credence 0.8 while Bo assigns it 0.5, and both remain steadfast. The defender should argue that the case shows heterodox updating, not differing priors, is what licenses persistent disagreement, so that the common prior assumption survives intact once rationality is defined to include Bayesian conditioning; the critic should reply that this rescue renders the doctrine trivially true by definitional fiat, since it simply stipulates away the update rules actual reasoners employ, and should instead locate the real load-bearing assumption of Aumann-style results in the requirement that agents update identically, arguing that priors were never the crux. The defender must then be pushed to say whether the no-trade implications of the common prior framework survive her concession that real agents update in non-Bayesian ways, and the critic must say something constructive about what, if anything, makes disagreement between identically informed, identically updating agents irrational if not a shared prior. Neither character may caricature the other; each must respond to the argument actually made in the preceding turn. Present the dialogue as exactly five numbered exchanges, each exchange consisting of two to four turns, with no introductory framing and no concluding summary of who has won.
