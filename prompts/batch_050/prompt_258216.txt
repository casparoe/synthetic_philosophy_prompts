I’m a retired soil microbiologist who has recently become interested in philosophy of history, and I’d like some comments on a draft essay. I’m especially unsure whether the central distinctions are philosophically coherent or whether I’ve just dressed up a few metaphors in technical language. Please don’t turn this into a literature review or suggest readings; I mainly want criticism of the argument as it stands.

Draft: When Does a Volcano Make History?

Historians are rightly suspicious of saying that germs, climate, or volcanoes determine human events. That sort of claim often ignores politics, contingency, and unequal vulnerability. A drought does not itself overthrow a government; people respond to drought through institutions, markets, beliefs, and violence. Yet the opposite extreme also seems evasive. If a pathogen kills a third of a population, or an eruption changes harvests across a hemisphere, it feels strange to describe these as merely background conditions while reserving real historical causation for human decisions.

I propose that we distinguish three kinds of non-human historical actor.

First are event-actors, such as eruptions, earthquakes, and floods. These are spatially and temporally bounded intrusions. Their causal role is often dramatic but short-lived. A volcanic eruption may alter weather for several seasons, but its long-term importance depends on whether it opens what I call a causal bottleneck: a period during which established social options narrow so sharply that later developments become unusually dependent on immediate responses. An eruption that causes crop failures in a resilient, well-fed trading network may remain a footnote. The same eruption during a fiscal crisis, a war, or an epidemic may redirect an entire political system.

Second are population-actors, especially microbes and other organisms that reproduce, mutate, and spread. Unlike an eruption, a pathogen does not simply happen once and then leave its effects behind. It changes while humans change around it. This creates reciprocal selection: social practices alter which variants spread, while the spreading variants alter social practices. Quarantine, migration, sanitation, burial customs, and medical theories all become part of the pathogen’s environment. Thus a germ can have a kind of decentralized persistence that a volcano lacks. It need not intend anything, but neither is it exhausted by the immediate damage it causes.

Third are field-actors, such as climate regimes, ocean currents, soil types, and disease ecologies. These are not events but patterned distributions of possibility. Calling a monsoon system an actor may sound extravagant, since it does not intervene at a particular moment in the way an eruption does. But field-actors shape which plans are cheap, costly, repeatable, or nearly impossible. They establish what I call leverage asymmetries: situations in which modest local changes can have large effects because human activity has organized itself around a narrow climatic or ecological expectation. A farming system optimized for predictable rains is more vulnerable than one adapted to variable rains, even if both experience the same absolute deviation.

This taxonomy is meant to avoid two mistakes. The first is environmental determinism: treating non-human conditions as sufficient explanations of human outcomes. The second is narrative anthropocentrism: treating non-human causes as scenery unless they enter history through a conscious human interpretation. My alternative is that historical agency comes in degrees and kinds. Intentional agency is only one kind. A microbe lacks purposes, but it can exert selective pressure. A climate regime lacks even a single event through which to act, but it can constrain strategies across centuries.

The key test, I suggest, is counterfactual sensitivity combined with historical leverage. To call something a major historical actor, we should ask two questions. First, would relevant events probably have unfolded differently if that factor had been absent or significantly altered? Second, did the factor occupy a position where its effects were amplified by human arrangements? The first question prevents us from crediting every background condition with decisive importance. The second prevents us from treating the non-human factor as sufficient by itself. Historical causation is then relational: microbes act through bodies and infrastructures; climate acts through expectations and institutions; volcanoes act through vulnerabilities and bottlenecks.

This also suggests that non-human actors can become more historically powerful because of human action. Warming can intensify storms, but settlement patterns determine who is exposed. Trade carries pathogens, but urban crowding determines how efficiently they spread. In such cases, asking whether the cause was natural or social may be badly framed. The important unit is often a coupled process: pathogen plus shipping network, drought plus grain speculation, eruption plus fragile food reserves, or changing climate plus infrastructure built for the climate of the past.

There remains a normative danger. Talk of non-human agency can make unjust outcomes appear inevitable. If famine follows crop failure, officials may blame the weather even when hoarding, price policy, or neglect determined who starved. My taxonomy should therefore include a distinction between causal importance and moral responsibility. A drought can bear enormous causal responsibility without bearing any blame. Human agents and institutions may be morally responsible even where their causal contribution was smaller, because they could foresee vulnerability and choose whether to reduce it.

I end with a deliberately unsettling possibility: perhaps some non-human processes become historical actors only retrospectively. A mutation, drought, or volcanic winter may be initially insignificant and acquire importance because later institutions, memories, or political struggles organize themselves around it. If so, historical agency is not fixed at the moment of occurrence. It is partly constituted by the causal chains that later pass through the event. That may sound metaphysically suspicious, but history often works this way: a forgotten epidemic becomes foundational once it destroys a labor system, while a deadlier epidemic elsewhere remains marginal because social institutions absorb it.

End of draft.
