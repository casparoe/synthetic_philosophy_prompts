I write a magazine column on science and ideas, and last month I covered a small interdisciplinary workshop on consciousness research. During a panel on whether neuroscience is closing in on an explanation of experience, the moderator projected a passage from David Chalmers's 1995 paper "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness," in which Chalmers comments on the tractability of what he calls the easy problems:

"Of course, "easy" is a relative term. Getting the details right will probably take a century or two of difficult empirical work. Still, there is every reason to believe that the methods of cognitive science and neuroscience will succeed."

That sentence set off an exchange between two panelists -- a cognitive neuroscientist and a philosopher of mind -- that continued through the coffee break. I recorded it, and I reproduce the substance below because my editors and I cannot agree on who got the better of it.

(1) The neuroscientist opened by saying the passage gives the game away. Chalmers's confidence that mechanistic methods will crack reportability, attention, integration of information, and the rest can only be inductive: those methods have explained many mental phenomena, so we expect them to explain these. But induction cuts both ways. The same track record supports expecting the methods to explain experience as well, and so the line between the easy problems and the hard problem marks where we happen to be in 1995, not any principled boundary. The hard problem is just the easy problem we have not solved yet.

(2) The philosopher replied that this misreads the argument entirely. Chalmers's claim is not "mechanisms have worked before, so they will work again." It concerns what a mechanistic explanation is: an account of structure and function. For reportability or attention, a fully specified mechanism is not merely evidence of progress -- it is a complete answer, because those phenomena are defined by what a system does. For experience, a complete mechanistic story still leaves an open further question, since nothing about the execution of functions settles why it is accompanied by experience. The asymmetry lies in the logic of explanation, not in the calendar, so the induction objection never touches it.

(3) The neuroscientist countered that talk of a "complete answer" smuggles in psychology rather than logic. Whether an explanation seems complete depends on which question the questioner presses. Press "but why does this integration mechanism feel like anything?" and the explanation of integration is incomplete too; ask only the functional question about experience -- why do organisms say they are in pain, withdraw, and seek treatment? -- and experience has a perfectly easy answer. The easy/hard line is drawn by which questions we choose to insist on, and a boundary drawn by our habits of asking cannot bear metaphysical weight.

(4) The philosopher responded that even granting the point about pressing questions, the relevant difference survives: in the easy cases, any further question that remains after the mechanism is given is answerable by more of the same methods -- it is a request for additional mechanism. In the hard case, the residual question is not a request for more mechanism at all; no amount of added functional detail addresses it. The neuroscientist's last move, she said, simply restates the premise that experience is a functional concept, which is exactly what is under dispute -- and someone who denies that there is anything to explain beyond the functions owes an account of why competent thinkers persistently judge that there is, which is itself a substantive explanandum, not a victory.

I would like a careful adjudication for a piece I am writing. Please assess the four moves in order: for each, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, with a brief justification. Then address three specific questions: First, does Chalmers's professed confidence about the easy problems rest on induction, as the neuroscientist claims, or on something about the form of functional explanation, as the philosopher claims? Second, does the "which questions we choose to press" objection in move (3) actually collapse the easy/hard distinction, or does it merely relocate it? Third, what, if anything, are both panelists missing -- for instance, about the difference between explaining experience itself and explaining our judgments and intuitions about experience?

Please write your response in American English, in a polished register suitable for an intellectually engaged general readership, in roughly 700 to 900 words (the quoted passage does not count toward that length).
