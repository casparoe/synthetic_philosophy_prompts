Among people who read the same blogs I do, it is close to doctrine that evidence reconciles: two honest Bayesians who observe the same data long enough will converge, priors wash out, and persistent disagreement means someone is irrational, misinformed, or talking about something empty. I know the actual results behind this folklore — Aumann-style agreement on one side, merging-of-opinions theorems in the tradition of Blackwell and Dubins on the other — carry hypotheses that the folk version quietly drops. I want a thought experiment that makes one of those dropped hypotheses impossible to ignore.

Construct a scenario meeting all of the following specifications:

1. Two agents, Ada and Basil, are ideal Bayesians. They receive exactly the same evidence: an unending sequence of binary outcomes from a single device. Neither has private evidence, indexical information, or conceptual resources the other lacks, and they agree completely on how the hypotheses generate predictions.
2. Neither prior is dogmatic in any crude sense. Both priors have full support over the parameter space, so that no finite run of outcomes has prior predictive probability zero under either agent, and at every stage of the actual infinite sequence both agents' models remain compatible with everything observed so far.
3. The setup is physically mundane: no supertasks, no infinitely precise measurements, no predictors, no exotic physics. A competent statistician could run the experiment.
4. There is a proposition P, statable in plain language, about the actual state of the world — not about either agent's beliefs, and not a mathematical or logical truth — such that Ada's and Basil's credences in P remain at least some fixed ε > 0 apart at every time, forever, even as the evidence accumulates without bound.
5. P is settleable in principle: there is some physically possible observation, simply unavailable to the agents during the experiment, that would decide P outright. The disagreement is over a genuine fact of the matter, not an unverifiable posit.

Then do the following:

(a) Present the scenario in at most two paragraphs, intelligible to a statistically literate reader without measure theory.

(b) Demonstrate that specifications 1 through 5 hold: state both priors, trace what the evidence does and does not teach each agent, and show the credence gap in P never falls below ε — ideally, that it is exactly constant.

(c) Identify the precise hypothesis of the standard convergence results that the case violates, and explain why the usual washing-out argument gets no grip here. In particular, describe what the two agents do come to agree about, and why that agreement is exactly what the theorems actually promise.

(d) Identify the load-bearing feature of the construction and show that removing it collapses the case back into ordinary convergence.

(e) Give the strongest objection a defender of the folklore could raise — presumably that P was never the kind of proposition the theorems promised agreement about — and adjudicate whether that objection defeats the case or concedes its point. Close by stating what, exactly, survives of the claim that honest Bayesians sharing an evidence stream must eventually agree.

If you conclude the specifications are jointly unsatisfiable, do not relax one silently; name the conflicting pair and explain the obstruction.
