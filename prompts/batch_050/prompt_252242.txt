I've been thinking about van Fraassen's Reflection principle -- roughly, the requirement that your current credence in a proposition should match your expectation of your future credence in it, conditional on learning nothing but what your future credence will be -- and the sprawling literature it has generated. The standard objections are well known (the prisoner who knows he will be made to forget, cases of expected memory loss, and so on), but I'm less interested in rehashing whether Reflection holds in textbook form than in what the debate should do next. Suppose you're advising a small research group -- three or four philosophers with strong formal backgrounds, about two years of funding -- that wants to work on diachronic rationality and the norms governing how agents should relate to their own future epistemic states. Which open problems in the vicinity of Reflection should they prioritize, and in what order?

To keep the question concrete, consider the space of candidate directions: (a) Reflection-style principles for agents with imprecise or interval-valued credences, where "your future opinion" is a set of probability functions rather than a single number and it's unclear what matching your future self even means; (b) Reflection under bounded rationality and logical non-omniscience, where an agent can be certain her future self will have thought harder about the math without that fact straightforwardly mandating a present credence; (c) the relationship between Reflection failures and the possibility of self-aware Dutch books, i.e., whether an agent who knowingly violates Reflection is thereby exploitable in a way she can foresee but cannot rationally avoid, and what that shows about the normative force of coherence arguments generally; (d) Reflection for group agents or collectives, where the "future self" is an aggregate whose credences are determined by a pooling rule applied to members who may themselves change; and (e) the question of whether Reflection is best understood as a constraint on rational credence at all, or as a diagnostic of which environments make Bayesian self-trust appropriate. You may add directions of your own if I've missed something important.

What I want is a defended ranking, not a survey. For each direction you include, explain: how many other live questions in formal epistemology are downstream of it; how tractable it is for philosophers working with proof and models rather than experiments; how neglected it is relative to its importance; and what would count as genuine progress on it within two years, as opposed to wheel-spinning. Say which directions you'd explicitly deprioritize and why -- including whether any of them are popular but, in your view, unlikely to pay off. Finally, identify the single most consequential crux: the one disputed assumption whose resolution would most reshuffle the whole ranking, and what evidence or argument over the next few years would move it.
