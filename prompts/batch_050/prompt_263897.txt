I'm a mathematics undergraduate taking a seminar on the philosophy of mathematics, and a discussion from last week has been bothering me ever since. We went through Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, and our instructor made an offhand remark that if Peano arithmetic were inconsistent, we could in principle find out — an inconsistency is just a finite, mechanically checkable derivation of a contradiction — whereas its consistency, if true, admits no comparable finite certificate. Someone asked how confident we should actually be that the axioms of ordinary arithmetic are consistent, and the room split: half said the question is basically settled because the axioms are obviously true of the counting numbers; the other half pointed out that this appeal to the "intended interpretation" can't be formalized within PA itself, and that the strongest consistency proofs we actually possess (Gentzen's, using transfinite induction up to ε₀) rely on principles that are in some respects more than what they certify, so the proof only reassures you if you already trust something at least as strong as PA. A few people mentioned that even prominent logicians have harbored doubts — at least one well-known mathematician publicly suspected that PA might be inconsistent and at one point circulated a purported proof of it, later withdrawn. The instructor refused to adjudicate and told us to think about it.

Write an essay that takes this question seriously rather than dismissing it: how much credence should a reasonable person assign to the possibility that PA (or the axioms of some other foundational theory, such as ZFC) is inconsistent? Weigh the considerations on both sides — the evident truth of the axioms for small numbers and two millennia of unflagging use against the formal asymmetry between consistency and inconsistency, the epistemic circularity worry about Gentzen-style proofs, and the historical track record of systems once thought secure (naive set theory, Frege's Grundgesetze) turning out to harbor contradictions. Also address what the consequences would be if a contradiction were actually found tomorrow: in classical logic a single inconsistency trivializes the whole theory, so consider whether mathematics would collapse, be repaired by weakening the axioms, or need to migrate to a paraconsistent foundation — and say which response you think the practice of mathematics actually supports.

Don't end on a shrug. Take a definite position on how worried we should be and on what inconsistency would mean if it arrived, and justify that verdict against the strongest opposing argument. Then close with a paragraph of intellectual honesty: identify the specific argument, result, or consideration that would change your verdict — something short of actually exhibiting a contradiction in PA, since that settles the question rather than informing it — and point to the step in your own reasoning where you think error is most likely to have crept in. Aim for 800–1,000 words.
