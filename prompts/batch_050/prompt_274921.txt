I'm preparing a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who defends hedonism about well-being — the view that pleasure and only pleasure is intrinsically good for a person. Our shared text is the passage in Book II of Henry Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics where he introduces what he calls the fundamental paradox of hedonism: roughly, his observation that the conscious, direct pursuit of pleasure tends to be self-defeating, so that pleasure is most reliably obtained by those absorbed in aims other than pleasure itself, and that a rational egoist must therefore deliberately pursue things other than his own pleasure. Draft seven to nine interview questions that turn on this passage rather than merely inviting the guest to restate his view. In particular, I want at least one question pressing on whether Sidgwick's observation is an empirical claim about human motivation or something more conceptual, and what evidence could settle which; one on whether the hedonist's standard reply — that the paradox merely shows pleasure must be pursued indirectly, not that it isn't the good — quietly concedes that a correct theory of well-being can fail to be a usable guide to action, and whether that concession costs the view anything; one on whether the paradox generalizes to rival theories (desire-satisfaction, objective lists) or is distinctive of hedonism, and what follows either way; and one asking what the passage looks like from the guest's own position — whether he reads Sidgwick as reporting a genuine discovery about pleasure or as smuggling in the thought that absorption in non-hedonic ends is itself valuable. For each question, give a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give, including a follow-up for the move that the paradox is a problem for hedonism as a decision procedure but not as a criterion of value. Please write in American English.
