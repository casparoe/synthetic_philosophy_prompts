I produce a small podcast on the history of philosophy, and next month I'm interviewing a scholar of early Chinese thought who has made her reputation defending a provocative reading of Gongsun Long's White Horse Dialogue. On her reading, the infamous conclusion that a white horse is not a horse is not a sophism at all: she holds that Gongsun Long is drawing a principled distinction between what a term picks out and what it means, so that the dialogue's conclusion is an identity claim about kinds or concepts rather than the absurd class-membership claim that a white horse fails to be a horse. I find the reading attractive, but I worry it survives by being vague, and I don't want to let her coast through the interview on the general suggestion that ancient Chinese logic has been misunderstood.

Please act as an expert in early Chinese philosophy of language who has read her work and also knows the rival interpretations well -- for example, readings on which the argument exploits an ambiguity between exclusion and identity, Chad Hansen's mereological interpretation of Chinese nouns as mass-like, and deflationary readings on which the dialogue is simply a display piece of the School of Names. Think carefully first about the strongest objections to the identity-claim reading and the replies she is most likely to give, then draft exactly eight interview questions as a numbered list, with no introduction or conclusion. Each numbered item should contain one question plus a follow-up keyed to the answer she will probably offer. I want the sequence to cover the following pressure points: her handling of the dialogue's own argument about seeking a horse versus seeking a white horse in the stable, and whether that argument establishes anything stronger than a difference in what the two phrases request; whether the claim that a white horse is not a horse is trivially false if read as class membership and trivially true if read as non-identity, leaving the interesting thesis nowhere; whether attributing a distinction between sense and reference to a third-century BCE text is an anachronistic projection of post-Fregean categories; how her reading accounts for the dialogue's remarks about color and shape; what she makes of Hansen's competing mereological picture; whether the Mohist canon, which treats statements of this form as paradoxical and to be rejected, counts as contemporary evidence against her; what the reading implies about how the rest of Gongsun Long's corpus should be understood; and finally, what textual evidence would persuade her that her interpretation is wrong.
