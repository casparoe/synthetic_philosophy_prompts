In the United States, the same person whom federal law forbids from selling one of their kidneys may legally be paid, over and over, for their blood plasma; paid thousands of dollars for their eggs; paid under contract, in many states, to carry a pregnancy to term for strangers; paid to swallow an untested compound in a first-in-human drug trial; and paid to work as a commercial fisherman or a roofer, occupations with nontrivial fatality rates. The kidney prohibition is typically defended as protecting the vulnerable and keeping the human body out of commerce. Yet every permitted transaction on the list also involves the body, also involves risk, and is also taken up disproportionately by people who need the money. And the risk itself cannot be the obstacle, since we allow people to donate a kidney for nothing; living donation is regarded as safe enough to permit, just not safe enough to sell.

What view of the body, or of markets, does this pattern of permission and prohibition presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that would draw the line where the law draws it. The obvious candidates each seem to sort the cases wrongly somewhere: regeneration (plasma and eggs renew, kidneys do not) gets surrogacy and trial participation wrong; a risk threshold gets the dangerous occupations wrong; irreversibility gets the pregnancy wrong. So say whether any single coherent theory underwrites the practice, or whether several rival theories happen to license the same lines, or whether the lines are best explained by something other than a theory at all. Then say what would have to change if the implicit view, whatever it is, were replaced by a thoroughgoing welfarism aimed at the transplant waiting list, or by a thoroughgoing refusal to put the body up for sale — and which currently legal transactions each of those revisions would sweep away.
