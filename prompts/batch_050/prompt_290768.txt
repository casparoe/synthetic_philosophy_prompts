In my environmental policy seminar, we've been discussing the precautionary principle, and a classmate recently defended it using what she called the pharmaceutical analogy: society already refuses to let new drugs onto the market until their manufacturers have demonstrated safety through extensive pre-market trials, so it is merely consistent to refuse new chemicals, genetically modified crops, or industrial technologies until their proponents demonstrate safety as well. In both cases, she argued, the burden of proof should rest with the innovator rather than with the public, and anyone who accepts the first practice but rejects the second is being hypocritical. Our professor seemed unconvinced but never spelled out exactly where the argument goes wrong.

Could you audit this analogy for me? I'd like the correspondence laid out explicitly: what plays the role of the drug, the clinical trials, the disease being treated, the patient, and the regulator, and what in the environmental case is supposed to occupy each of those roles. Then please identify where the mapping breaks down. I'm especially unsure about two things: first, whether demonstrating safety means the same thing when harms are diffuse, delayed, and not testable in a controlled trial as it does when a drug can be compared against a placebo; and second, whether the status quo that a new technology would displace is really analogous to an untreated illness, or whether the analogy quietly assumes that inaction is safe in a way the pharmaceutical case lets you get away with. Finally, does the burden-shifting conclusion survive once the significant disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing the argumentative work the whole time? If there is a better analogy for the kind of caution the precautionary principle is meant to encode, I'd be interested in that too.
