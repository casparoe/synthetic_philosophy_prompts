I want to sharpen how I think about judging historical figures, and I keep circling Jefferson as the hardest case. So: play Thomas Jefferson for me. It's around 1823, you're about eighty, retired at Monticello, deep in debt, and I'm a guest who has read your Notes on the State of Virginia and won't let the slavery question drop. Stay in character until I say "stop." Speak in the first person, in something like your actual register -- measured, lawyerly, prone to qualifying everything three times -- and keep each reply to a couple of paragraphs so it stays a conversation. Use only concepts and knowledge available to you in 1823. Don't turn into a modern liberal and start apologizing in 21st century vocabulary, but also don't dodge: you should defend yourself with the resources you actually had (your emancipation proposals in the legislature and the 1784 ordinance, your conviction that emancipation had to be gradual and paired with removal of the freed people, your fear of what two peoples with grievances would do to each other in one country, your debts). You can concede small points when pressed but you don't collapse. If you think I'm being anachronistic, say so, as yourself.

Here's my opening. In Query XVIII of the Notes you wrote:

"Our children see this, and learn to imitate it; for man is an imitative animal. This quality is the germ of all education in him. From his cradle to his grave he is learning to do what he sees others do. If a parent could find no motive either in his philanthropy or his self-love for restraining the intemperance of passion towards his slave, it should always be a sufficient one that his child is present. But generally it is not sufficient. The parent storms, the child looks on, catches the lineaments of wrath, puts on the same airs in the circle of smaller slaves, gives a loose to his worst of passions, and thus nursed, educated, and daily exercised in tyranny, cannot but be stamped by it with odious peculiarities. The man must be a prodigy who can retain his manners and morals undepraved by such circumstances."

Mr. Jefferson, I've read that paragraph a dozen times and I think it destroys you, and I want to know how you live with it. By your own account you were raised from the cradle watching despots, stamped with the same odious peculiarities, and only a prodigy escapes that. So which are you? If you're the prodigy -- if you saw clearly, diagnosed the whole machine, wrote the truest paragraph anyone had written about it -- then no man in history had less excuse for going on as a master of over a hundred souls and freeing almost none even at his death. And if you're not the prodigy, if you admit you were corrupted exactly as you describe, then you're pleading "man of my time" in advance, in your own words -- and I notice you never extend that plea to the slaves themselves. So: prodigy or not? Answer me that first and we can go from there.
