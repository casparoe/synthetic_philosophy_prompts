I am a high school senior doing a one-semester independent study in philosophy and economics, and I have decided to focus it on Marx's account of alienation in the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844. In the "Estranged Labour" section, Marx writes:

"The worker becomes all the poorer the more wealth he produces, the more his production increases in power and size. The worker becomes an ever cheaper commodity the more commodities he creates. The devaluation of the world of men is in direct proportion to the increasing value of the world of things. Labor produces not only commodities; it produces itself and the worker as a commodity – and this at the same rate at which it produces commodities in general."

My project cannot be a general survey of Marx, so I need to pick two or three open questions that this passage raises and spend the semester on them. Here are the candidates I have come up with so far:

1. Whether Marx's claim here is an empirical prediction that has been falsified, a conceptual claim about the worker's relationship to what he produces, or both — and whether the truth of one part can survive the failure of the other.
2. Whether "alienation" in this passage can be made precise enough to be investigated empirically (by psychologists, economists, or sociologists), or whether it is essentially a humanistic category that resists operationalization, and whether it matters if it does.
3. Whether the passage's reasoning depends on the labor theory of value, and if so, how much of the alienation argument survives if that theory is abandoned.
4. Whether contemporary phenomena like gig-work platforms, warehouse logistics, and attention-economy social media fit the passage's model of the worker confronting his product as an alien power, or whether they represent something the passage cannot capture.
5. Whether there is a real distinction in this passage between exploitation (something done to the worker) and alienation (something happening to the worker's relation to his own activity), and whether the two can come apart in practice.

Could you help me decide which two or three of these deserve the semester, and defend the ordering? Please be explicit about the criteria you are using — for example, how central the question is to understanding the passage itself, how tractable it is for a student without graduate training in economics, how much of the secondary debate hangs on it, and how much it has already been settled. Please also say which of the candidates you would cut and why, and what considerations would change your ranking (for instance, if I were writing for a philosophy teacher versus an economics teacher, or if I had a year instead of a semester). I am not looking for a bibliography, since I will find the readings myself — I want a defended agenda and the reasoning behind it. I would like the response to be roughly 900 to 1,200 words.
