I'm in a graduate-level metaethics seminar, and we've spent the last two weeks on fitting-attitude or buck-passing analyses of value — roughly, the idea that for something to be admirable just is for there to be reason to admire it, and so on for the other evaluative properties. I keep getting tangled up on the wrong kind of reasons problem, and I want to figure out what I actually think by writing down my gut verdicts on cases and seeing what principle, if any, delivers them. So here they are. First, a demon credibly threatens to torture my family unless I admire him: I think I have a genuine and weighty reason to admire him — torture is very bad — but I deny that he is admirable. Second, my friend finally finishes a marathon after years of training: I think her effort makes her admirable, and this seems like the paradigmatic case where the reason and the value line up. Third, someone offers me fifty dollars to be afraid of a harmless house spider: I have a reason to fear it, but it isn't fearsome. Fourth, a surgeon tells me that being afraid of my upcoming operation will worsen my recovery, though the operation is in fact quite risky: here I think the operation genuinely is fearsome and there is a right-kind reason to fear it, even while the medical fact gives me some kind of reason not to. Fifth, a cruel joke told at a party: I think a joke's offensiveness can be an excellent reason not to be amused by it, and not a "wrong kind" reason at all — even though it seems to be a reason about the consequences of my amusement (what laughing expresses and endorses) rather than about the joke's comic properties. Sixth, a monstrous dictator who is a genuinely brilliant chess player: I think his chess skill is admirable, full stop, even though nothing else about him is.

The pair I keep going back and forth on is the first and fifth. My working principle, which I picked up from the secondary literature, is that right-kind reasons bear on the object of the attitude while wrong-kind reasons bear on having the attitude. That handles the demon, the spider, and the marathon cleanly. But the cruel joke seems to break it: the moral reason not to be amused looks like it's about what my amusement would do and express, not about the joke itself, so my principle classifies it as the wrong kind — yet it seems like the best reason in the world not to laugh. Meanwhile, the demon case shows the reason-to-admire and admirability come apart, and I don't want a fix so aggressive that it denies I have any genuine reason there at all (I also have the intuition, in a parallel case where the demon threatens torture unless I believe that two plus two equals five, that I have no reason whatsoever to believe it — only a reason to want to believe it — and I'd like to know whether that's a difference between belief and the attitudes or an inconsistency in me).

So: what principle, or small set of candidate principles, captures these verdicts? Please state it precisely enough that I can see which clause each of my cases is motivating, and tell me what it predicts about two cases I haven't settled: (a) an incentive to feel gratitude toward a benefactor who turned out to have helped me by accident, and (b) a reason to regret an outcome that was, in fact, the best available. And be honest about the cost: if no principled account saves all six verdicts, tell me which one you think has to go and why, rather than contorting the theory to fit them all.
