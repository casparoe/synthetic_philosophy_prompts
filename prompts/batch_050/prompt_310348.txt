Right, here's a job for you. The usual menu of objections to contested markets (kidney sales, vote selling, price gouging after a flood) is well known: exploitation of the desperate, crowding out of altruistic motives, corruption of a good's meaning, harm to third parties, background inequality. What I want to know is whether anything is left over once all of those are switched off.

So: construct a thought experiment about a market transaction that meets ALL of these constraints at once:

- fully voluntary on both sides, and both parties comfortably off — nobody is selling out of desperation, so the exploitation objection can't get started
- no third party is harmed, directly or indirectly
- the thing being sold has no sacred or constitutive meaning for either party — no kidneys, no wedding rings, no medals — so the corruption-of-meaning objection can't grip either
- realistic: something that could plausibly happen in a present-day British or American city, no sci-fi, no stipulatd monsters
- and yet most ordinary people, hearing about it, would still flinch

One paragraph for the scenario itself. Then the demonstration: walk through each standard objection in turn (exploitation, crowding out, corruption of meaning, inequality, third-party harm) and show that it genuinely fails to apply to your case — actually argued, not just asserted away. Then the interesting bit: identify which feature of the case is doing the work of producing the flinch, and say whether that feature points to a real further objection to markets or merely to a predictable misfire of disgust or status quo bias. Give the best response a markets-friendly philosopher of the Jason Brennan type would make to dissolve the case, and say whether it succeeds. Also tell me what happens to the case if you remove the load-bearing feature — does the flinch go with it?

No cheating by smuggling desperation back in through a side door (a seller who is "comfortably off" but secretly under some pressure, etc). If your scenario doesn't quite meet one of the constraints, say so honestly rather than papering over it.

At the end, say how confident you are that the case works as specified, and single out the point you're least sure of. The very last line should begin "Verdict:" followed by one sentence on whether the flinch tracks a genuine moral residue or not. British English throughout, please.
