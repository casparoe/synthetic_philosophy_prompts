So here's something that's been bugging me about how we talk and write. Science journalism and textbooks toss around generics constantly: "ducks lay eggs," "mosquitoes carry malaria," "lions have manes." Nobody complains that ducks lay eggs is literally false of all male ducks, all ducklings, and plenty of adult females -- and the malaria one is accepted even though only a tiny sliver of mosquitoes (females of particular species) ever transmit anything. But notice two things. First, "ducks are female" is a terrible generic, even though it's true of exactly the ducks that lay eggs, so it can't be raw statistics doing the work. Second, if a newspaper printed a generic about a human group with the same statistical profile -- say, some ethnic or occupational group and some rare bad behavior -- editors would (rightly, I think?) treat it as a harmful stereotype and spike it, no matter how hedged.

My question: what does this whole set of practices presuppose about how generics mean what they mean? Like, does our easy acceptance of the mosquito case presuppose that generics about natural kinds express something like a nature, a norm, or "what the kind is for" rather than a frequency claim? And if so, does the policing of human generics presuppose that people don't have natures in the relevant sense, or just that the social costs swamp whatever the generic literally says? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) behind the practice, and say what's strictly required versus what just fits comfortably -- maybe the practice only presupposes something about pragmatics, not semantics at all. Also, what would have to change in how we write and edit if we went full statistical about generics, treating them all as bare prevalence claims? Would the duck sentence become straightforwardly false, the human generics become fine, or something weirder? And if the answer is that our current practice is just incoherent, I'd rather hear that argued than have it smoothed over.
