Over the past year I've come around to the view that what matters in a life is the absence of craving and suffering, not the presence of pleasure: a calm, completely satisfied state in which nothing is wanted seems to me as good as any amount of excitement, and adding pleasure to it adds nothing. Before I commit to this or defend it to friends, I want to write down what I should actually be asking of a theory of wellbeing.

So: write the desiderata any adequate theory of wellbeing must meet. For each, state the requirement and the case or consideration behind it — which cases it must get right (someone with chronic illness and no hope of relief; a monk who wants nothing; a dreamer experiencing pure bliss who will never wake; someone whose greatest desire is fulfilled and who feels nothing), which distinctions it must respect (relief versus pleasure, satisfaction versus the mere cessation of wanting, not being bothered versus being glad), and which puzzles it must dissolve rather than ignore (why a world full of happy people strikes us as better than an empty world with no suffering in it; whether there can be a duty to create happy people at all). For each desideratum, tell me whether it looks like a genuine requirement or a prejudice smuggled in from one camp. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I have a hunch that something has to give between "we have no obligation to bring happy people into existence" and "happy lives make the world better" — and say which desideratum in the list does the most work in excluding candidate theories.

Then, briefly, score the main contenders against your list: classical hedonism, the view that wellbeing is desire-satisfaction, negative utilitarianism, and the tranquilist view I sketched above. Keep most of the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scoring can be short.
