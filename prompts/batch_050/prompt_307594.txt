For an upper-level undergraduate seminar on political philosophy (roughly 16 students, one 90-minute session), design a structured treaty-negotiation simulation built around the First Preliminary Article of Kant's Perpetual Peace (1795). The passage the activity should put under pressure is Kant's gloss on the article, which the facilitator should read aloud only at the start, without comment:

"All existing causes for a future war—although they were perhaps unknown to the contracting parties at the time—are to be regarded as entirely removed, or annihilated by the Treaty of Peace, even if they could be picked out by the dexterity of an acute interpretation from the terms of documents in the public Archives."

The activity should work as follows in outline, but I want you to fill in every detail. Divide the class into delegations from fictional states that have just fought a costly war to exhaustion, give them a fixed and uncomfortably short time to draft an actual written peace treaty, and build into the rules the temptation Kant describes: allow (but do not require) each delegation to lodge a private "reservation card" with the facilitator, recording claims they intend to revive later and the treaty language they think protects that option. Then stage a second round set some years later, in which a student playing Kant's "acute interpreter" gets to mine the archived treaty text for material justifying a renewed war—succeeding unless the treaty's drafters managed to extinguish not just the disputed claims but whole categories of future claims, including ones nobody at the table had thought of. The point participants should feel, before anyone names it, is the difference between a peace and a mere truce, and how surprisingly hard it is to draft a text that annihilates unknown causes rather than deferring known ones.

Specify: the number and composition of delegations and the interpreter role; the materials and the exact wording of the reservation-card mechanic; a minute-by-minute timeline including when the second-round war scare is sprung; how you decide whether the interpreter's excavation succeeds (a criterion the students can contest, not just facilitator fiat); and a debrief that maps specific moments of the simulation onto the quoted passage—the exhaustion that makes reservations tempting, the retrospective cleverness of archival interpretation, and Kant's remark that calling a peace "perpetual" is "pleonastic and suspicious." Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (I suspect teams will treat it as an optimization puzzle rather than feel the exhaustion, and that the interpreter role will collapse into general lawyering) and how to correct for it. Finally, give an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce: I take it the simulation can dramatize the textual problem but not Kant's claim that concluding such a peace is a duty rather than a prudential calculation, nor the institutional machinery he thinks makes the annihilation of causes credible—tell me what the facilitator must supply in lecture or discussion to cover that gap.
