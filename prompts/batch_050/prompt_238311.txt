I facilitate a fortnightly reading group on collective decision-making — about fourteen members, mostly postgraduates and a few interested professionals — and we have a 75-minute session coming up on the unilateralist's curse: the structural problem that arises whenever an irreversible action with shared consequences can be taken by any single member of a group acting alone. Even if every member is individually well informed and their estimates of the action's value are unbiased, the action will tend to be taken by whoever happens to overestimate it most, so the more people are in a position to act, the more likely the group is to end up committed to a mistake — and crucially, each individual can reason soundly and sincerely while the collective outcome is systematically skewed.

Design a structured activity that makes the group feel this rather than merely follow it on the page. The requirements:

- No lecture up front. Participants should discover the skew in their own behaviour before the idea is named. Ideally they should feel, in the moment, that acting was reasonable for them individually, and only afterwards see what the aggregation did.
- The design should isolate the epistemic mechanism (heterogeneous private estimates, each defensible in isolation) rather than letting the effect be driven by genuine disagreement in values or by a lone reckless player. The point is that nobody has to be irresponsible for the curse to bite.
- Give full rules, materials, and a payoff scheme that works with points rather than money, including how private estimates are generated so that they are genuinely noisy but unbiased.
- Run at least two rounds under different decision rules — one where any individual can trigger the outcome alone, one where triggering requires a seconder or a majority — so that the contrast is felt in the scores and not just asserted in the debrief.
- A debrief plan mapping specific moments of the game onto the structure of the curse: who acted, what their information looked like from the inside, and why the losing outcome surprised people.
- An honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run (players gaming the points, someone acting randomly for laughs, the group inferring the lesson early and self-correcting) and how to handle each.
- A frank note on which features of the real phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce — genuine conviction, real irreversibility, the absence of an announced debrief in actual cases — and what the facilitator must supply by hand.

Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested. The underlying aggregation structure (and its kinship with the winner's curse in auctions) is well established, but how far the unilateralist's curse diagnoses real-world cases — geoengineering, novel pathogen research, the release of capable AI systems — as opposed to merely redescribing them, and whether simply making people aware of the curse debiases them, are live questions. Do not present the contested parts as though they were the consensus.
