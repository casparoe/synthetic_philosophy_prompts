I work on a payments service, and a coworker used the following argument to claim that our monitoring data gives us no real grounds for trusting the service next quarter. Each step looks fine to me, but the conclusion can't be right, since it would make all of our capacity planning and on-call rotation design pointless. Here it is:

(1) Every log and uptime record we've collected is consistent with the hypothesis that the service is reliable.

(2) Define "reliably-until-March": a system is reliably-until-March if it works correctly on every day before March 1 and fails on every day from March 1 onward. Every log and uptime record we've collected is also consistent with the hypothesis that our service is reliably-until-March, since today is before March 1.

(3) If two hypotheses are equally consistent with all the available evidence, that evidence supports them equally.

(4) Therefore, our data supports "the service will fail starting in March" exactly as strongly as it supports "the service will keep working."

(5) So our historical data gives us no reason to prefer one forecast over the other.

He says the same trick works for any cutoff date and any system, so no amount of past success ever justifies expecting future success. I'm fairly confident the flaw is in (3) or in the move from (2) to (4), but when I press on it I can't articulate what distinguishes "reliable" from "reliably-until-March" without just asserting that the first is a normal property and the second is a contrived one — which he says is circular. Which step should I actually reject, why does it look innocent, and what do I give up by rejecting it? I'd like to keep (1) and (3) if possible, since they seem like the bare minimum for any data-driven engineering at all.
