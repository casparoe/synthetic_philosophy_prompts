Write the desiderata that an adequate theory of permissible betrayal would have to meet. By "betrayal" I mean the breach of a loyalty genuinely owed to a person, group, or institution, where the breach is defended by appeal to some competing moral claim — the whistleblower who violates a confidentiality agreement to expose wrongdoing, the parent who reports their child to the police, the civil servant who leaks a classified memo, the friend who breaks a confidence to protect a third party. What I want is the specification, not a theory: the cases such a theory must get right and why those cases are the hard ones; the distinctions it must be able to draw and not collapse (for example, between disloyalty and mere refusal to comply, between betraying a person and betraying an institution, between the wrongness of the breach itself and the wrongness of having entered the loyalty relationship in the first place); and the ordinary moral verdicts it must either vindicate or explain away. For each desideratum, give the consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference, and indicate which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable and which reflect the commitments of a particular camp (for instance, someone who thinks loyalty is intrinsically valuable versus someone who thinks it is only instrumentally so). Finally, if any plausible subset of these desiderata turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, if no account can simultaneously honor the intuition that some whistleblowing is heroic and the intuition that betrayal is wrong even when justified — say so explicitly and identify which requirement must be relaxed first. A list or table is fine.
