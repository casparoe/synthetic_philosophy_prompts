I'm in an upper-level philosophy of mind course, and we've just finished the unit on naturalizing intentionality — informational semantics, conceptual role, and the teleological approaches descended from Millikan and Papineau. For my term paper I need to defend a view of my own about mental content, so before I start drafting I've written down my working commitments, roughly in the order I care about them. What I want from you is not a defense of any of these but an audit: are they jointly tenable? Here they are. (1) The right theory of content will be broadly teleological: a representation means what it has the biological function of indicating, where function is grounded in selection history. I find this the most promising naturalization going. (2) A molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person, assembled by sheer accident — the Swampman case — would have beliefs and desires with content just as the original does; I can't bring myself to accept that whether I'm thinking about Vienna depends on my causal ancestry. (3) Any adequate theory must make misrepresentation genuinely possible: a state that represents X as present when X is absent must come out false, not merely unusual, and this is precisely what's wrong with crude causal covariation accounts. (4) Contents are determinate enough to do explanatory work: the frog's perceptual signal means fly, not the disjunction of fly with any small dark moving speck crossing its retina, and a theory that leaves this indeterminate has failed. (5) We can have thoughts about things nothing in our evolutionary past was ever selected to detect — quarks, transfinite cardinals, unactualized possibilities — and a theory of content must account for this.

I genuinely don't know where the weak points are, and I'd rather find out now than in the seminar. Please go through the set and identify which pairs of commitments are in real tension and which only appear to be. For each genuine tension, say how severe it is, what exactly forces the conflict (not just that the two claims feel different), and what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs me. For each merely apparent tension, show briefly why it dissolves. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all alike, and end by telling me which single revision would clean up the most of the set.

Keep the whole audit in prose, as a ranked list with short paragraphs, and hold it to at most 600 words — I'd rather have the three sharpest points done well than every pairwise combination sketched.
