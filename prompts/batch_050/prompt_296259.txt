Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. Setup: consider a society of individuals i = 1,…,n, where the alternatives are lotteries (probability distributions) over a set of social outcomes. Assume each individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so they are representable by a utility function u_i with the expected utility property; assume likewise that the social preference ordering satisfies the vNM axioms, representable by V. The Pareto condition: if every individual is indifferent between two lotteries, society is indifferent between them, and if everyone weakly prefers lottery L to M and at least one person strictly prefers it, then society strictly prefers L to M. Under these assumptions the theorem says that social utility is a weighted sum of individual utilities: V(L) = sum of a_i u_i(L), up to an additive constant. The vNM independence axiom is what forces linearity — without it you could get a maximin social ordering, say, which is not linear in individual utilities.

Does it vindicate utilitarianism? Yes, essentially. The theorem shows that any consistent social evaluation which respects individual preferences must take the additive form, and that is just the utilitarian form. Moreover the weights must be equal: the theorem derives social utility as a sum of individual utilities, and it gives us no grounds for weighting one person's utility more heavily than another's, so treating people symmetrically forces a_i equal for all i — which is classical utilitarianism (average or total, the difference is only a scaling). One might worry about fairness — e.g. a coin flip that gives one person everything versus an equal split for certain — but if the individuals themselves are indifferent between the coin flip and the equal split, Pareto says society must be indifferent too, and the additive form simply records their preferences, so the fairness objection never gets off the ground."
