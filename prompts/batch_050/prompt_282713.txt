I'm enrolled in an undergraduate course on rational choice, and our textbook presents the standard result that any agent whose preferences violate certain axioms -- transitivity, independence, continuity, completeness -- can be exploited or shown to act irrationally, so that rationality collapses into expected utility maximization. I've been stress-testing this against my own gut verdicts, case by case, and I keep finding that the theorem and my intuitions pull apart. So here is my ledger, and I'd like to know whether any coherent principle stands behind it, or whether I'm just confused in a patterned way.

First: given a choice between \$900 for certain and a 90% chance of \$1000, I take the sure thing, and I consider this perfectly sensible. But given a choice between a 0.9% chance of \$900 and a 1% chance of \$1000, I take the latter, and I consider that perfectly sensible too. I know these two verdicts jointly violate the independence axiom, and I don't care -- both feel right. Second: I would not trade my son's health for any sum of money, however large, against any improvement in my own comfort, however large. The textbook says this lexicographic ordering violates continuity, since somewhere a probability-weighted trade must tempt me. I say there is no such probability. Third: I genuinely cannot rank two lives -- continuing as a translator versus starting over as a park ranger. It's not that I'm indifferent; the question of which is better seems to have no answer for me, and I don't think I'm being irrational in declining to answer it. Fourth, and this is the one my study group argued about for an hour: suppose I preferred the concert to the gym, the gym to a quiet evening at home, and a quiet evening at home to the concert. I agree something has gone wrong, but I deny that a clever bookie could thereby drain my wallet, because I would simply refuse to keep trading -- and I think refusing is the rational move, which means the pump argument must be smuggling in an assumption about what I'd do. Fifth: having already bought the non-refundable concert ticket, I now feel I ought to go, even though, if the ticket had been free, staying home would genuinely be the better evening. The course calls this the sunk cost fallacy. I call it keeping faith with my earlier self, and I'm inclined to stand by it.

So: is there a principle of practical rationality, stated precisely, that delivers all five of these verdicts, or most of them, and can you show me where its clauses are doing the work? If no single principle does it, tell me which candidate principles get closest and which of my verdicts each one forces me to surrender -- I'd like the costs itemized rather than hidden. And two further questions. What does your best principle say about a case I genuinely can't settle: I'm offered a gamble with a 99.9% chance of winning \$10 and a 0.1% chance of destroying something I value infinitely, and my instinct is to refuse at any expected value, which seems to clash with both my willingness to take everyday tiny risks and my first verdict above. And if the answer is that several of my verdicts must go, which one should go first, and is there any respect in which the axioms themselves -- rather than my intuitions -- carry the burden of proof?
