"The Hart–Fuller debate, conducted in the pages of the Harvard Law Review in 1958, opposed two views about the relation between law and morality. H.L.A. Hart defended legal positivism: the claim that whether something counts as law is a matter of social fact alone — what has been enacted and what officials recognize — and that law and morality are therefore entirely separate domains, so that even a morally wicked rule is law in every sense that matters. Lon Fuller answered with a natural-law position: he argued that law has an inner morality consisting of eight requirements — rules must be general, promulgated, prospective rather than retroactive, clear, non-contradictory, possible to obey, relatively stable, and actually followed in practice by officials — and that a system violating these requirements fails to be law, because these requirements embody substantive justice. Their shared test case was a postwar German prosecution of a woman who had denounced her husband to the Nazi authorities under a valid Nazi statute: Hart argued that the honest response was to punish her under a retroactive statute while frankly conceding that the Nazi enactment had been law; Fuller argued that enactments of that kind were never law at all. Most commentators regard Hart as having won the debate decisively, and Fuller never published a direct answer to Hart's argument."
