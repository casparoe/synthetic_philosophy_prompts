I teach an undergraduate seminar on decision theory, and every year the liveliest session is the one on superrationality — Hofstadter's proposal that sufficiently like-minded rational agents, knowing they will all reason identically, can sometimes achieve cooperation where ordinary game theory predicts defection. To close the unit this year, I'd like a piece of satire I can distribute on the last day of class. The target is the superrationalist's confidence that symmetry of reasoning can substitute for enforcement, communication, or trust — and the way that confidence survives contact with actual human beings.

Write an Onion-style news article, headline plus roughly 500–600 words, reporting on the collapse of a fictional charity's fundraising scheme called the Concordance Draw. The rules of the draw: a philanthropist has pledged a jackpot of ten million dollars, to be split so that the final prize equals the jackpot divided by the total number of entries received; anyone may enter for free, as many times as they like, by mail. The charity's director — a devoted believer in superrational reasoning — publicly predicted that rational entrants would coordinate on near-universal abstention, so that one or a handful of entries would make somebody rich and generate wonderful publicity. Instead, the charity's mailroom has been buried under millions of entries, including submissions specifying numbers larger than a googolplex, and the resulting per-entry prize now requires scientific notation with a negative exponent. The article should treat all of this with complete deadpan seriousness, as a routine piece of local-news reporting.

The humor should come from getting the underlying concepts right, not from mocking a strawman: a specialist in decision theory should be able to tell the writer understands the symmetry assumption behind superrationality, the sense in which the draw's structure "lures" entries precisely by making cooperation so attractive, and the contrast between the superrationalist prescription and the ordinary Nash-equilibrium analysis. Please include at least one invented quotation from the charity's director, still insisting — with straight-faced technical reasoning — that the outcome vindicates the theory since everyone reasoned identically and therefore no one can complain of unfairness; at least one quotation from an entrant explaining, in earnest symmetric-reasoning terms, why he personally submitted several million entries; and at least one quotation from a weary economist or game theorist offering the conventional equilibrium perspective. No real people, institutions, or publications should be named, and the piece should never wink at the reader — the comedy must be carried entirely by the gap between the director's reasoning and the mountain of mail sacks behind him.
