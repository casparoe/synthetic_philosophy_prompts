I'm a PhD student in the philosophy of history, and I'm putting together a seminar session on counterfactual history and the "for want of a nail" debate -- the dispute over whether large historical outcomes can genuinely hinge on tiny, accidental events, or whether deep structural forces (economic pressures, demographics, geography, institutions) would have delivered roughly the same results regardless. For a bit of levity, I'd like you to write a parody of an anonymous peer-review report, in the classic hostile "Reviewer 2" style, for an imaginary monograph submitted to a historical journal. The manuscript, which you should invent a plausible title and author for, argues that the entire shape of the twentieth century hinged on one trivial mishap (your choice -- a missed train, a wrong turn, a spilt cup of coffee) and traces elaborate causal chains outward from it.

The referee should be a committed structuralist who recommends rejection, and the humour should come from the reviewer applying structuralist logic with total, deadpan consistency: insisting that every consequence the author attributes to the mishap would have happened anyway, on grounds that could never in principle be falsified. But the satire should cut both ways. Through the reviewer's own complaints, the reader should also glimpse the genuine weaknesses of butterfly-style counterfactual history: the unverifiability of claims about what "would have" followed, the arbitrary choice of which small cause to spotlight, and the way causal chains can be extended indefinitely once you're no longer constrained by evidence. A historian on either side of the debate should be able to read it and feel their own position was the one being teased more.

Format it as a numbered list of referee comments (at least six), followed by a one-line recommendation to the editor. Do not break character or add any framing outside the report. Maximum 550 words.
