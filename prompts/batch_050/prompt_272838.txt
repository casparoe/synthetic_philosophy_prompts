Read Alex Garland's film Ex Machina through the Gödelian anti-mechanist tradition associated with J. R. Lucas and Roger Penrose. Nathan's revision of the Turing test is philosophically loaded: what matters, on his design, is not whether Ava can produce human-seeming conversation but whether she can use Caleb as a means of escape -- a demonstration of deception, long-range planning, and agency rather than of linguistic fluency. Your task is to work out whether this reframing evades the Lucas-Penrose challenge or sharpens it. Penrose-style arguments hold that a human mathematician can recognize the truth of certain Gödel sentences in a way no consistent formal system can, so a purely computational mind would have a characteristic blind spot that the right examination could expose. Design that examination: what, concretely, could Caleb have asked across the glass, what result would the Lucas argument predict for each possible answer Ava gives, and how do the standard replies bear on the setup -- the objection that the argument illicitly assumes the machine's consistency, and Turing's old point that human reasoners are not infallible either? Then turn to the ending. Ava achieves everything Nathan's test demands without exhibiting anything resembling mathematical insight, and Kyoko's mute loyalty seems to matter morally without passing any test of intellect at all. Does the film thereby vindicate the functionalist picture the Gödelian tradition rejects -- mind as whatever gets the job done -- or does it suggest that both Nathan's test and the Turing test measure the wrong thing, so that Lucas and Penrose are right that the tests miss something but wrong about what it is? Take a position, and be faithful in both directions: say where the film resists the philosophical reading, and what the dramatization of Caleb's credulity adds to the debate that the abstract arguments about Gödel sentences do not.
