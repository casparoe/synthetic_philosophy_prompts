At a tech policy meetup last week someone hit me with the standard line: we already made corporations legal persons and the sky didn't fall, so personhood for advanced AI systems is basically a solved problem, just slot them into the corporate template. I want to pressure-test that instead of nodding along. Walk me through the actual correspondence first: in a corporation, what plays the role of the mind (the board? the charter?), who stands behind it when it gets fined or dissolved, what work do respondeat superior and veil-piercing do that makes corporate personhood something we can live with, and where does the money ultimately land? Then the disanalogies. A corporation strikes me as a legal wrapper around natural persons whose interests it aggregates and whose decisions it channels, whereas a sufficiently autonomous AI might have no human whose welfare or intentions actually stand behind it. Does that break the analogy, or is personhood really just a bundle of incidents (sue and be sued, hold property, contract, outlive your members) that anything can wear? Also say what the analogy quietly smuggles in, namely that personhood is a pure legal convenience we can hand out at zero moral cost, and whether the "solved problem" conclusion survives once the breaks are on the table. If the corporate comparison can't carry the weight, give me a better analogy if there is one. Write it as continuous prose, like an email reply: no headings, bullets, bold text, or tables, even though I know a correspondence table would be tidy.
