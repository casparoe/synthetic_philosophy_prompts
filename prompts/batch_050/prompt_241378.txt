When a large language model confidently states something false, virtually everyone — journalists, AI company spokespeople, and critics alike — says that it hallucinated. The word is not neutral: a hallucination is a perceptual mishap that befalls a system that is trying to see the world aright, so the term frames the model as the passive victim of a glitch, and it is routinely deployed to reassure users that the underlying competence is sound and the failure innocent. I want a rigorous audit of how far this analogy actually holds and whether the reassurance it carries survives scrutiny.

You are a philosopher of mind and language who knows Harry Frankfurt's work on lying and bullshit well and who also understands, at least roughly, how large language models actually generate text. Think carefully, and do not settle for the standard talking points on either side of the AI debate. Brainstorm privately first, then deliver only the final list.

Use two pieces of apparatus. First, the clinical literature distinguishes hallucination — a percept-like experience with no corresponding external stimulus — from confabulation — a fluent, false narrative produced to fill a gap, with no intention to deceive, as in certain amnesic syndromes. Second, Frankfurt distinguishes three relations a speaker can bear to the truth: the liar cares about the truth and deliberately conceals or contradicts it; the bullshitter is indifferent to whether what they say is true or false, saying whatever serves their purposes; and the merely mistaken speaker aims at the truth and misses. Note that the third category presupposes the existence of a truth-aimed process that has failed.

Respond with exactly six numbered items, each two to four sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the correspondence table — what in the language-model case plays the role of the sensory organ, the external stimulus, the veridical percept, and the false percept; (2) the disanalogies that matter for the reassuring use of the term; (3) the disanalogies that are genuine but irrelevant to that use; (4) what the label quietly presupposes about agency and truth-directedness, given the open question of whether model outputs are produced by anything truth-aimed at all; (5) the Frankfurt-style verdict — whether confident false output is best classified as lying, bullshit, confabulation, or error, and whether relocating the agency from the model to its designers or deployers changes the classification; (6) your bottom line — whether the reassurance the analogy was doing survives the audit and, if it does not, what label you would substitute and why.
