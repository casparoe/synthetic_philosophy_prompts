Two friends of mine got stuck in an argument after a seminar on Merleau-Ponty's account of motor intentionality, and I'd like a neutral verdict. It began with the familiar observation that skilled athletes and musicians often perform worse when they consciously attend to their own movements. Anna said this vindicates the phenomenological picture: expert performance runs on the body's own practical "I can," the habit-body that gears into the situation, and deliberate reflection is an intrusion that disrupts the skill rather than a hidden ingredient of it. Ben replied that this romanticizes expertise: top performers constantly monitor, strategize, and adjust mid-performance, so mindedness is woven into skill all the way down. Anna conceded that experts adjust, but argued that the adjustments themselves are absorbed into the body schema through training, which is why a tennis player's mid-rally correction feels nothing like a beginner's deliberation. Ben then proposed a test case: imagine a concert pianist whose piano is covertly rigged to delay each keystroke by a fraction of a second; if she adapts within a few bars without ever noticing the manipulation, doesn't that show her playing was never under reflective control, exactly as Anna claims — and if she does notice, doesn't that show monitoring was there all along? Anna said the thought experiment is rigged, because noticing a disruption only proves that reflection arrives when the body's grip breaks, not that it was steering beforehand. Ben answered that this makes her position unfalsifiable: any evidence of awareness gets reclassified as a breakdown case. At that point both accused the other of begging the question, and neither would budge. Please adjudicate: who is right about which specific point, and what, if anything, are both of them missing? Give your verdict as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
