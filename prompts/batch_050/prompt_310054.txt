So I'm prepping for a decision theory reading group and I wrote down my positions in advance so the others can poke at them. Here's what I believe:

1. Rational choice just is maximizing expected utility relative to your credences. If you can't articulate your credences in some case, that's a practical limitation, not a problem for the theory.
2. In the classic two-urn setup (one urn with a known 50/50 red-black mix, one with a totally unknown mix), I honestly would rather bet on red from the known urn than red from the unknown one, and I'd also rather bet on black from the known urn than black from the unknown one.
3. Ambiguity aversion is perfectly rational, not a bias. Facing unknown probabilities really is worse than facing known ones, other things equal.
4. Your preferences between options shouldn't change just because of how the same options happen to be described or framed.
5. In real-life decisions, like choosing between two startups to invest in, I deal with unknown probabilities by forming a rough distribution over what the probabilities might be, averaging it into one best-guess number, and then demanding a bit of a premium for the extra uncertainty.
