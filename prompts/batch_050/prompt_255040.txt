The homework question was:

Thomas Aquinas discusses tyranny in several places. Does he permit the killing of a tyrant, and if so, by whom? Answer with reference to his treatment of sedition in the Summa Theologiae and to his account of political rule in De Regno.

The student's answer was:

Aquinas's treatment of tyranny appears in two main places: De Regno, his treatise on kingship, and the discussion of sedition in the Summa Theologiae (II-II, q. 42). For Aquinas, a tyrant is a ruler who directs government to his own private good rather than to the common good; since law, properly speaking, is an ordinance of reason for the common good, tyrannical rule is a perversion of legitimate government rather than a genuine regime.

On the question of resistance, Aquinas argues in the sedition article that because tyrannical rule is unjust, those who rise against it are not guilty of sedition in the strict sense. Indeed, he turns the charge around: it is the tyrant who is seditious, since he foments discord among his subjects in order to hold on to power. From this Aquinas concludes that a private citizen, moved by zeal for the common good, may kill the tyrant, and in De Regno he cites with approval the biblical example of Ehud, who slew Eglon, king of Moab.

Aquinas does add a prudential restriction: resistance is not warranted if the tyranny is tolerable and the revolt would likely cause greater harm to the community than the tyranny itself, since peoples often suffer more from the disorder that follows an uprising than they did under the tyrant.

In sum, Aquinas endorses tyrannicide as an act of civic virtue, provided the common good genuinely requires it.
