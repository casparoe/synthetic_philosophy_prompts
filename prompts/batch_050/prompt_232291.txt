I teach philosophy at a small liberal-arts college, and I find myself in the middle of a dispute that my own training should have prepared me to resolve but somehow hasn't. I'd like advice on the substance, not on office politics.

Our department has a small seminar room on the top floor of our building. For at least fifteen years it served as overflow storage: broken chairs, obsolete filing cabinets, boxes of duplicate journals nobody wanted, a defunct radiator. Two years ago, during a sabbatical summer, I cleared it out at my own initiative. I paid for the skip, repainted the walls myself, replaced the light fittings, bought and assembled shelving, and solicited book donations from emeriti and alumni to stock it — roughly $2,000 of my own money and most of my summer. The college contributed nothing except, after the fact, a maintenance worker to fix the radiator, which cost the department perhaps $150. Since reopening, the room has become the most contested space in the building: three departments now want it for seminars, and the college's central timetabling office has proposed absorbing it into the general room pool, which would mean I book it like anyone else and frequently won't get it.

My position is that I hold some special claim on the room — not ownership, but at minimum priority of use — on the ground that the room as it now exists is largely my work. Before my intervention it was, in effect, nobody's and nothing's; I made it what it is. My colleagues make two counterarguments. The first, from a senior colleague, is that the room was always the college's, that I improved property that was never mine, and that a gift freely given can't retroactively generate a claim — she adds that she herself proposed restoring the room in 2011 and was refused funds, and that her earlier intention should count for something against my later labor. The second, from the timetabling office, is that whatever the merits, there is now acute demand: the building has only two other bookable seminar rooms, both inferior, and letting me hold priority on a room I actually use perhaps eight hours a week means the room sits idle while others go without — so my claim, if it exists, is overridden by the needs of colleagues who would use what I leave fallow.

I note, because it may matter, that the alumni donated their books explicitly "for the seminar room," and several told me they gave only because I asked.

So: Do I have a legitimate claim here, and of what kind — priority of use, a veto on reallocation, compensation if the room is pooled, or nothing at all? Whose argument among the three of us is strongest, and where do the weaker ones go wrong? I'd also like to know what difference it makes, if any, that the resource I improved was previously valueless and unused, whether my colleague's unexecuted 2011 intention has any moral weight, and whether the timetabling office's point about idle capacity defeats my claim or merely qualifies it.

Please give me a defended verdict, not a survey of considerations. I would rather be told plainly that my sense of entitlement is misplaced than be offered a balanced menu of views. And if the question as I've framed it rests on a confusion — if, say, I've misdescribed what kind of thing is even in dispute — say that directly and reframe it, rather than answering the question I happened to ask.
