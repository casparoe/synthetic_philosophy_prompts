I am a second-year undergraduate in an ethics survey course, and last week our seminar split over the standard contrast between diverting a trolley away from five people toward one and pushing a large bystander off a footbridge to stop it. When I said I could not see why the two cases should come apart morally, given that both involve one death to save five, a classmate cut in with "the end doesn't justify the means" and treated the matter as closed. Our tutor later pointed us to the historical root of the kind of reasoning my classmate was gesturing at: Thomas Aquinas's treatment of homicidal self-defense in the Summa Theologiae (II-II, Question 64, Article 7), where he argues, in paraphrase, that a single act can have two effects, only one of which is intended while the other is "beside the intention," and that the moral character of the act tracks what is intended rather than what is merely foreseen — so that killing an aggressor may be permissible when one's aim is self-preservation, provided the force is proportionate. That passage helped me see where the slogan might come from, but it also made me realize I still cannot say what the slogan actually claims.

What I would like is a disambiguation. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that "the end doesn't justify the means" could be expressing in a debate like ours. I suspect the candidates include at least: a flat denial that consequences ever bear on rightness; the double-effect distinction between intended and merely foreseen harm that Aquinas draws; a narrower claim that harm may not be pursued specifically as a means to a good end, even if it may be intended for other reasons; the Kantian thought that one may not use a person merely as a means; and a doing-versus-allowing claim about what one causes as opposed to what one permits. For each reading, say (a) whether it is platitudinous, substantive and defensible, or implausible, with a brief reason; (b) whether it actually delivers my classmate's verdict — impermissible to push, permissible to divert — since I want to know which precisification her intervention strictly required; and (c) how it relates to Aquinas's own distinction, which strikes me as closest to the second reading but perhaps not identical to it.

Two further requests. First, on the double-effect reading in particular, please address what I gather is its classic vulnerability: that "intended" and "foreseen" effects can be made to come apart or collapse together depending on how finely we describe the agent's plan (if I redirect a missile knowing civilians will die, is their death a side effect or part of my means?), so please indicate whether the reading can be stated rigorously enough to classify the footbridge case without sleight of hand. Second, conclude by saying which reading you think people who actually use this slogan most plausibly have in mind, and whether that reading, charitably interpreted, would survive being stated precisely.
