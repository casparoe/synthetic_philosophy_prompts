I'm in a reading group on philosophy of mind/cog-sci and last week a guy in the group who's way more confident than the rest of us explained Dennett's "Tower of Generate-and-Test" idea to us over beers. I took notes because I hadn't read the Dennett piece myself yet, and now that I'm skimming it I'm suspicious of a couple things he said, but I can't tell if he's wrong or if I'm just confused.

His summary went roughly like this: "Dennett says intelligence comes in four nested levels, like a tower. Darwinian creatures just get hardwired by natural selection -- blindly generate mutations, and the environment kills off the failures, so the 'testing' happens by death. Skinnerian creatures are ones that evolved the capacity for learning, so instead of waiting for genes to change, the individual tries out behaviors and keeps the ones that get reinforced -- Dennett's point is that all animal learning is basically just operant conditioning, behaviors that were rewarded before get repeated, full stop. Popperian creatures are smarter: they can generate candidate actions *in their heads* and test them against an internal model of the environment, so their hypotheses can die in their stead instead of them actually dying. And Gregorian creatures -- named after Richard Gregory -- are ones that use cultural tools, basically language and other things invented by earlier generations, which means humans and any other species with culture. Each level replaces the one below it, so once you're a Popperian creature you're not really doing Skinnerian reinforcement learning anymore. And the upshot, he said, is that human thought is mostly just a pile of borrowed cultural gadgets, so there's no such thing as a genuinely original idea or real individual insight -- it's all just cached tools handed down."

The bit about each level replacing the last one is where my alarm bells went off, since surely a creature that can simulate still has to actually learn from real feedback sometimes? And the conclusion about no original insight seems like a leap even if the rest is right. But I've never read Gregory and I'm only partway through Dennett, so maybe this is all standard. Can you go through his summary and tell me which parts a Dennett sympathetic reader would endorse, which parts are subtle distortions or conflations, and whether his big conclusion at the end actually follows from the accurate parts? Bonus points if you can tell me where each type of creature's "generate" step and "test" step actually happen, since I think that's where he might be glossing over something important.
