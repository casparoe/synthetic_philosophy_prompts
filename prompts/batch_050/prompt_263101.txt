So I work as a security engineer -- mostly internal infrastructure, access controls, that kind of thing -- and my company recently spun up a team to figure out how we're going to deploy LLM-based agents internally, and I got pulled in as "the security person." I started reading up on the AI safety literature to prepare, and I keep hitting this distinction people make between "alignment" (making the model actually want the right things) and "control" (staying safe even assuming the model might be scheming against you, via monitoring, sandboxes, untrusted models watching other models, etc.). And my gut reaction as a security person is: wait, control is just... my entire job? Defense in depth, least privilege, zero trust architecture, insider threat programs, assuming breach -- we have a whole mature discipline built around not having to trust the components.

Here's what I'd like. Translate the control-vs-alignment debate into the vocabulary of security engineering as precisely as you can. Map the control agenda's pieces onto their closest infosec counterparts -- is a trusted-monitor setup basically a SIEM with a weaker analyst? Is "untrusted model with restricted affordances" just least privilege applied to a confused deputy? Is the alignment camp's position analagous to saying "no amount of perimeter defense saves you if the admin is adversarial"? Do the mapping piece by piece, not just at the slogan level.

But the part I actually care most about is the residue report: what does NOT survive translation. Where does the analogy break down -- e.g., human insiders can't be retrained, don't have interpretable internals (sort of), and can't be copied a thousand times, but also a compromised employee doesn't get smarter every quarter. And for each piece of residue, tell me which side you think it cuts in favor of: does it show that the AI safety people are reinventing security engineering badly and should just import the field's hard-won lessons, or does it show that the standard security playbook quietly assumes things (like a bounded, non-adaptive adversary, or the ability to patch and redeploy) that won't hold for a misaligned model, so the control folks are doing something genuinely new? I have to give a recommendation to this team in a few weeks and I want to know whether my instinct that this is "just insider threat with extra steps" is right or is papering over something real.
