I am a philosophy PhD student who sits, somewhat improbably, as the graduate-student member of my university's academic integrity appeals panel. We are currently hearing a case that has split the panel, and the chair has asked each of us to submit a short written opinion before we vote. The appellant is a master's student in applied linguistics, a second-language English speaker, whose thesis chapter was flagged by the university's AI-text detection software and whose supervisor testified that the submitted prose was markedly more fluent and polished than anything she had previously produced. The finding of misconduct rests entirely on those two pillars: the detector's score and the supervisor's impression. Her defense is that she wrote every word herself, that she used only a standard grammar-checking tool as the rules permit, that her earlier drafts were rougher because they were written under deadline and revised heavily at the final stage, and — her more contentious empirical claim — that detectors like this one systematically flag non-native academic writing as machine-generated precisely because it is formulaic in the ways second-language academic English tends to be.

Her appeal also contains a second, harder-to-classify argument, and this is where the panel is genuinely stuck. She argues that even if she were guilty, the process wronged her in a way the policy cannot describe: the panel gave an automated score and a supervisor's intuition more weight than her own sworn account, and she believes her accent, her institutional juniority, and her status as an international student all depressed the credibility her testimony received. She says the wrong is not merely that she was disbelieved, but that the university's procedures made her kind of disbelief invisible — there is no box on the form, no category in the regulations, under which "my word was discounted in favor of a machine's output for reasons that have more to do with who I am than with what I wrote" can be registered. One panel member is sympathetic but objects that a detector has no prejudices and so cannot wrong anyone in the way a biased human hearer can; another thinks the integrity stakes are high enough that deference to the tool plus the supervisor is simply rational triage.

Draft the written opinion I will submit to the panel. It needs a clear bottom line: uphold or overturn, and on what grounds. But I also want it to do three further things. First, lay out general criteria a non-philosopher panel member could actually apply when weighing automated detection evidence against a person's first-person testimony — not a ban on detector evidence, but a principled account of when it should and should not outweigh a denial. Second, take seriously the question of whether discounting someone's word in favor of an algorithm can constitute a genuine epistemic wrong even granting that the algorithm itself harbors no prejudices — and, relatedly, whether it matters that the university's conceptual vocabulary lacks any term for the wrong she is trying to name. Third, give the strongest version of the losing side's case its due, including the possibility that the supervisor's comparative judgment is legitimate expertise rather than bias, and say what, if anything, the university owes this student beyond the verdict itself — a policy revision, an acknowledgment, or nothing.
