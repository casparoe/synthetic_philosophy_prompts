Here are my considered judgments about when exclusion is permissible, and I want to know what principle, if any, unifies them. In each case, take the exclusion to be legal unless the verdict says it shouldn't be.

Case 1: A homeowner refuses to let a stranger into her house, giving no reason at all. Permissible, full stop.

Case 2: A private dining club rejects an applicant because the membership committee finds his conversation boring. Unlovely, but permissible.

Case 3: A restaurant in a small town refuses to serve customers of a particular ethnicity, though other restaurants in town will serve them. Not permissible, and rightly illegal.

Case 4: A landlord with four rental units refuses tenants of a particular religion. Not permissible, even though the same landlord could permissibly refuse those tenants a room in her own home.

Case 5: A wealthy country refuses admission to would-be immigrants from a poor country, where admission would dramatically raise the migrants' lifetime prospects and impose only modest, diffuse costs on current residents. Permissible, though perhaps regrettable -- states may prioritize their own.

Case 6: A private company owns the only bridge out of a company town and begins charging residents a toll calibrated to consume most of their wages. Not permissible, despite the bridge being the company's unambiguous private property.

I am fairly confident of each verdict taken alone, but I suspect they do not cohere. Cases 2 and 3 both involve voluntary associations refusing entry to their spaces on grounds I find distasteful, yet I judge them differently. Cases 1, 4, and 6 all involve property owners controlling access, yet I judge them differently. And Case 5 looks, structurally, like either a scaled-up version of Case 2 (an association controlling membership) or a scaled-up version of Case 3 (an institution controlling access to opportunities that others need), depending on which analogy you reach for.

What I want from you: first, a precise candidate principle -- or a small set of clauses -- that captures as many of these six verdicts as possible, with each clause explicitly tied to the case that motivates it (for example, whatever distinction you draw between a club and a restaurant, or between one's home and one's rental property). Second, an honest reckoning: state plainly which of my verdicts your best principle cannot save, and tell me which single verdict you would overturn to achieve a cleaner principle, defending that choice rather than merely noting the tension. Third, apply your final principle to three cases I have not listed and give definite verdicts: a homeowners' association that gates a previously open neighborhood street; a university that refuses admission to an applicant on account of his political views; and a subnational region (say, a wealthy province) that attempts to restrict migration from poorer regions of the same country. If your principle yields a verdict on Case 5 different from mine, say so directly and tell me whether you think I should revise Case 5 or whether the fault lies in the principle.
