Last week a friend and I got into an argument about Goethe's politics that ran well past midnight and ended without resolution. Her position was that Goethe was essentially a courtier who feared the people: a man comfortably installed at Weimar, cool toward the French Revolution, and prone to retreating into the vocabulary of organic growth and self-cultivation whenever politics threatened to disturb the garden. My position was that his wariness was principled rather than self-protective. The text I kept returning to is a maxim from his Maxims and Reflections, which I will paraphrase rather than quote: Goethe says, in effect, that whatever liberates our mind without at the same time increasing our command over ourselves is pernicious. Treat the paraphrase itself as part of the problem; I am not certain I have the emphasis right, and I want the reading to turn on what that maxim can bear.

Please read Dickens's A Tale of Two Cities through the lens of that maxim, doing both sides justice. First interpret the maxim on its own terms: is it a political thesis at all, or only an ethical one that has been illegitimately drafted into politics, and what exactly would count as liberation without self-command? Then test it against the novel. Dickens seems at times to have written the maxim into fiction: the revolutionary tribunals, Madame Defarge's knitting, the grindstone scene, and the way emancipation in Paris curdles into a new and worse unfreedom all look like exhibits for Goethe. But the novel also resists the reading, and I want the resistance taken seriously: Dickens insists the Terror was authored in advance by the lawless arrogance of the ancien régime, that Madame Defarge was made by the Evrémondes rather than by some failure of self-restraint, and that people who have been crushed for generations have never been permitted the self-command Goethe's maxim presupposes. And then there is Sydney Carton, whose arc is precisely self-command achieved, culminating in a sacrifice that saves a family but leaves France exactly as it was. Does the novel thereby concede that Goethe's ethic is real but politically insufficient, redeeming individuals while doing nothing about structures, or does Carton's final vision of a future France amount to a reply?

Keep the whole response under 600 words, and end by stating plainly where the Goethean reading of the novel breaks down, if it does.
