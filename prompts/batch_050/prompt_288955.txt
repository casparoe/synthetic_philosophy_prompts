I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have been thinking about Avicenna’s flying man argument on my own. At a neighborhood discussion group, two people I know, Nadia and Owen, disagreed about how much it proves. I tried to record their exchange fairly:

1. Nadia began with the standard case: imagine a mature person created all at once, suspended in empty air, unable to see, hear, touch, or feel anything, with no limb touching another and no bodily sensations at all. Such a person could still think, “I exist.” Since that awareness does not depend on any information about a body, she said, the self must be something other than the body.

2. Owen replied that this moves too quickly from not being aware of one’s body to one’s awareness not depending on a body. A newly created person with no experiences would not have learned the concepts or language needed to think “I exist.” If we simply stipulate that the person has those abilities anyway, we may be smuggling in the very conclusion we want.

3. Nadia then revised the case. Take an ordinary adult who already has language and concepts, then temporarily block every current bodily sensation while leaving the person’s general ability to think intact. The person can suspend every belief about bodily location, shape, size, and condition, yet still recognize, “I exist.” If the self were fundamentally identified through the body, she argued, removing every bodily identification should also remove the object of self-awareness.

4. Owen answered that we can be aware of something under a very incomplete description without knowing what it fundamentally is. Someone can think about a building without knowing whether it is made of brick or steel. Likewise, the person might be directly aware of a physically constituted self while knowing nothing about that constitution. He added that if enough memories remain to preserve a continuing personal identity, bodily history may still be doing indispensable work; but if all identifying memories are removed, the person may be left only with “thinking is occurring,” not “I, this particular self, exist.”

5. Nadia objected that “I exist” contains more than “thinking is occurring.” The subject seems to grasp the very same thing as the one doubting, affirming, and thinking, even when no description of that thing is available. She called this primitive self-acquaintance and said that bodily theories of the self still need to explain how it is possible.

6. Owen said the felt unity of doubting and affirming might come from the structure of thought itself rather than from acquaintance with a nonphysical subject. At most, the revised argument shows that self-reference can be epistemically prior to bodily identification: we can know that we exist before identifying ourselves as this body. It does not show that the self could exist without a body. That would confuse the order in which something is known with the conditions on which its existence depends.

7. Nadia’s final response was that this dismissal is also too quick. Even if the argument does not prove immateriality, it may reveal a distinctive kind of self-awareness that cannot be reduced to awareness of bodily properties. Owen had shown only that this does not logically imply dualism, not that a physical theory has actually explained it.

Please adjudicate the disagreement move by move. For each of the seven moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and briefly explain why. Then give an overall verdict: Does Nadia’s revised version avoid Owen’s concept-acquisition objection? Does it establish body-independent self-awareness, the existence of an immaterial self, both, or neither? Finally, identify the strongest conclusion the flying man argument can support and the most important consideration both participants may be missing.
