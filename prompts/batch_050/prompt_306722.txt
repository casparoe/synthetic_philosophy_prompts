I've settled on a position about the ethics of randomized controlled trials, and I'd like you to map what would count against it. The position has three parts.

First, clinical equipoise — genuine uncertainty or disagreement within the expert medical community about the comparative merits of a trial's arms — is almost never actually satisfied in practice. By the time a trial is funded and approved, preliminary data, investigator incentives, and the design itself (endpoint selection, eligibility criteria, the choice of comparator) all reflect a judgment that the experimental arm is promising; the community standard then launders that judgment through the continued existence of a few skeptical holdouts.

Second, the right replacement is procedural rather than psychological. Call it the calibrated-bettor standard: a trial is ethically permissible just in case a rational forecaster with a demonstrated track record of good calibration on past trial outcomes, given everything publicly known, would not assign any arm more than a threshold probability of being strictly worse for participants than the available alternatives. Enrolling patients is then justified by the expected value of the information produced, not by anyone's ignorance.

Third, because the standard is procedural, it dissolves the classic objections to equipoise — that it collapses the moment accrual begins, that it conflicts with the physician's duty of personal care — by denying that anyone's state of uncertainty does moral work at all.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Which cases would show the calibrated-bettor standard licensing something clearly impermissible, or forbidding something clearly permissible — for instance, placebo controls where an effective treatment exists, adaptive platform trials, or trials where every arm is worse than simply not enrolling? Which assumptions could fail: that calibration on past trials transfers to new ones, that a single threshold can be non-arbitrary, that forecasts can be insulated from the same sponsor incentives that corrupted equipoise? What would a defender of the view concede as counting against it, as opposed to what critics say? And does the freedom to adjust the threshold insulate the view from refutation — and if so, is that a defect in a standard of this kind? Finally: where the standard diverges from equipoise in what it rules in and out, are those divergences evidence for it or against it?
