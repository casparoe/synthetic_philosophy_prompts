Ok so I'm TAing for a professor of Islamic theology who's been running her whole seminar on the Mu'tazila–Ash'arite debate through thought experiments, and I want to figure out what she'll say on the final exam question before I have to grade 40 papers on it. Her verdicts across the term:

1. A man grows up alone on an island, never recieves any revelation, and never feels or shows gratitude toward whatever gave him life and sustenance. Blameworthy? No. Without a revealed command there's no obligation, so there's nothing for him to violate.

2. Could God punish a sinless child forever and remain just? Yes. Injustice means overstepping into what belongs to someone else; everything belongs to God, so nothing He does to His creatures can count as injustice. He won't in fact punish the innocent, but that's because He's said He won't and His word is true — not because anything would be wrong with it.

3. Is lying wrong because God forbade it, or did God forbid it because it's wrong? Wrong because He forbade it. Full stop.

4. Abraham's command to sacrifice his son: carrying it out was right, and it was only right because of the command. Subtract the command and you have a horrifying act, not a noble one.

5. If, per impossibile, God had commanded gratuitous cruelty, would the cruelty be just? She said: by the definition of justice, yes, but the hypothetical is idle, because God necessarily wills only what is wise and so could never actually command it.

The exam case: a prophetic report, authenticated as firmly as you care to stipulate, commands the community to perform a ritual that — as far as anyone can tell — causes real suffering and benefits no one: say, the annual slaughter and disposal of a large number of healthy animals purely as an act of destruction, with the meat going to waste. Is performing it obligatory, and is the suffering now good or just?

So: (a) predict her verdict on this case, (b) lay out the candidate rules she's running — at least two fit all five verdicts, and I'd like them both plus any third that fails somewhere, with the verdict it fails on, (c) tell me which rule is the better bet and why, and (d) name the one further thought experiment whose answer would settle it between the surviving rules. The part I care about most is (b) — I want to see the rules stated precisely enough that they actually diverge on the exam case, not just gestured at with school labels.
