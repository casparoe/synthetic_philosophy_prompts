Imagine that Johannes Kepler were brought into the present and given a year to study the modern fortunes of the art he practiced. Recall the shape of his own position. He cast horoscopes for paying clients throughout his adult life, produced the official calendars and prognostications expected of a provincial mathematician in Graz, and late in life entered the service of General Wallenstein, for whom he had earlier drawn up a nativity. Yet he also attacked the judicial astrology of his contemporaries: in Tertius interveniens he deliberately positioned himself between astrology's wholesale defenders and its wholesale critics, and he tried to rebuild the art on physical and harmonic foundations, discarding whatever in the tradition he could not connect to a real celestial influence or test against experience.

What he would find today is this. On one side, a mass culture of sun-sign astrology: newspaper columns and phone apps issuing daily forecasts keyed only to the sign the Sun occupied at one's birth, Mercury retrograde as a running cultural joke, and a wellness industry in which birth charts are offered as symbolic maps of personality, often with an explicit disclaimer that the planets cause nothing at all. On the other side, a scientific consensus dismissing astrology wholesale: no known physical mechanism by which natal configurations could shape character, and controlled tests in which experienced astrologers fail to match charts to people at better-than-chance rates. He would also find software that computes any chart instantly from ephemerides far more precise than anything in his Tabulae Rudolphinae.

How would Kepler respond? Specifically: (1) Would he judge today's commercial astrology more harshly or more leniently than the popular astrology he attacked in his own day, and on what grounds? (2) Faced with the modern scientific dismissal, would he capitulate, or insist that some residue of the art still deserved a fair empirical hearing — and which of his own theoretical and methodological commitments pull each way? (3) What would he make of psychological astrology's retreat from causal claims — a sophisticated defense, or a surrender of the only thing he thought made astrology worth reforming — and of the fact that the astronomy has triumphed while the art built on it has not?

Answer in American English, in no more than 400 words. Paraphrase Kepler's writings rather than quoting them, and do not invent quotations. Close by stating what would change your mind: which evidence about his actual commitments, or which defensible alternative reading of them, would most plausibly reverse any of your verdicts, and where your extrapolation is least secure.
