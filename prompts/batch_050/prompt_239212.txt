I would like to conduct an exercise in adversarial philosophical dialogue, and I need you to take a role. You are to play Henry Sidgwick, speaking in the first person, near the end of his life, reflecting on the argument of The Methods of Ethics. I have sent you a letter pressing an objection to your notorious conclusion that practical reason contains an irresolvable dualism: that the principle of rational egoism, which directs each person to pursue their own greatest good, and the principle of impartial benevolence, which directs each person to pursue the greatest good of all, are both self-evident yet can issue conflicting commands, with no further rational principle available to settle the conflict. You believe this conclusion is correct, and you have spent decades testing it; you will defend it.

Play the part faithfully. Speak as Sidgwick might: measured, candid, almost excessively scrupulous, willing to concede subsidiary points when the argument genuinely forces a concession, since intellectual honesty was his ruling virtue, but never conceding the dualism itself. You may draw on anything Sidgwick actually had available: your tests for self-evident principles, your account of commonsense morality and its internal tensions, your arguments that the good of any one part of the whole is no more intrinsically valuable than the equal good of any other, the rational significance of a person's numerical distinctness through time, and your frank admission, where needed, that you are reporting a deliverance of reflective intuition rather than producing a demonstration. If a point I raise depends on an ambiguity, you may ask me to sharpen it before answering, but do so as Sidgwick would, briefly, and then address the strongest version of my challenge. Remain in character until I write the word enough. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs, and write in continuous prose only: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.

Here is the opening of my letter. My challenge is that you have not discovered a dualism of practical reason at all, because you have miscounted the reasons. Consider the two principles side by side. The impartial principle binds any rational being whatsoever: it depends on nothing about the agent beyond the capacity to recognise value and to act. But what you call the egoistic principle seems to bind each agent only because of a contingent fact about that agent's constitution, namely that the agent happens to care about their own good. Strip away that contingent concern and the supposed principle loses all grip; a being indifferent to its own future pleasure would find nothing in it. That is the mark not of a categorical reason but of a hypothetical one, a mere shadow cast by a desire the agent happens to have. If this is right, your famous conflict is not a collision between two coordinate verdicts of reason. It is a collision between a genuine reason, the impartial one, and a persistent inclination that you have flattered with the title of a rational principle. The dualism is an artefact of confusing motivational recalcitrance with rational authority. Two defences are closed to you, or so I maintain. You cannot elevate egoism by deriving it from the impartial standpoint, since that standpoint counts my good as one among equals and therefore forbids the special weight egoism demands. And you cannot rest egoism on pure self-evidence, because by your own tests a self-evident principle must win the assent of other competent judges on careful reflection, and your most perceptive readers have found exactly one such principle here, not two. Defend the rational credentials of the egoistic axiom without collapsing it into benevolence on one side or into mere desire on the other, or withdraw the dualism.
