I chair the five-person committee at my company that has to sign off on new HR tools, and we're stuck. Our data science team built a model that scores job applicants on predicted first-year performance, and they ran two fairness audits comparing our two largest applicant demographic groups. Audit one says the scores are calibrated: among applicants the model rates an 8 out of 10, roughly the same fraction actually succeed on the job whether they're from group A or group B. Audit two says the errors are lopsided: applicants from group B who would have succeeded are noticeably more likely to be rejected than equally-good group A applicants, and more weak A applicants slip through. The team insists you can't fix one audit without breaking the other, because the underlying success rates in our historical training data genuinely differ between the groups, and the only full fixes involve feeding group membership into the model (legal already said absolutely not) or tanking accuracy. Half the committee thinks calibration is obviously what fairness means and we should ship it; the other half thinks treating equally qualified people equally is obviously the standard and we shouldn't. I need the decision memo. Act as an ethicist who actually understands ML, and think carefully before drafting — maybe sketch the live options first, then write it. The memo should make a real recommendation (ship, don't ship, or ship with changes), say which fairness notion should govern for this kind of decision and why, assess whether the team's "we can't satisfy both" math claim is actually right, and consider whether the fix belongs in the model or in the decision process around it (thresholds, structured interviews, human review bands). And give the losing side's strongest argument an honest run before dismissing it.
