Every December, the forty-odd members of my amateur choir each receive the same private email from our treasurer: the spring concert venue costs £2,000, and she asks each of us to post her a cheque for £50, with no record kept of who gave. If too few give, she quietly covers the shortfall herself and cancels next year's extra rehearsals. Nobody ever finds out what anyone else did, and my £50 on its own makes essentially no difference to whether the target is met.

Here is where I get stuck. The choir is a strikingly homogeneous group — similar backgrounds, similar temperaments — and I strongly suspect most of the others are turning over this email in roughly the way I am right now. So it feels as though whatever I end up deciding is probably close to what most of them will decide too; on that thought, binning the letter is not really a lone free-ride but a sign that the concert won't be funded, whereas sending the cheque is a sign that it will. But another part of me objects that my cheque cannot cause anyone else's, that the similarity between us is just a coincidence I have no business reasoning from, and that the honest calculation is that £50 buys me almost nothing.

Am I entitled to treat my own choice as evidence about what the other thirty-nine will do, and if so, does that make giving the rational option? Please give the reasoning rather than only the bottom line, but end with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a single sentence stating whether I should send the cheque.
