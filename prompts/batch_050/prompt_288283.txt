ok so i'm in a third-year history of economic thought class and we're doing Aristotle on money (Politics book 1 and that passage in Nicomachean Ethics V about exchange and what makes goods commensurable). Our prof floated the idea that Aristotle basically has the first theory of value, but after doing the reading I think I can prove his whole position collapses, and i can't find where the collapse happens. Here's my argument:

1. Money exists by convention, not by nature. Aristotle says this pretty much outright -- that's why its name is nomisma, from nomos (law or convention), and why a community can just decide to swap one currency for another and make the old one useless.
2. Charging interest on loans is, on Aristotle's own view, the most unnatural way of acquiring wealth there is, because money was invented to make exchange easier, and usury makes money breed money. (The Greek word for interest, tokos, also means offspring, wich he seems to think is the tell.)
3. But if money is a pure convention, then what counts as its proper use is conventional too. A community could just as well adopt a convention under which lending at interest is one of money's standard uses -- in fact, ours did.
4. So usury can't be unnatural: there's no nature for it to offend against. You can't violate the natural function of a thing that doesn't have a nature.
5. So Aristotle's two most famous claims about money -- that it's conventional, and that usury is unnatural -- can't both be true.
6. And since he obviously won't drop the usury claim (it does a lot of work in his whole natural vs. unnatural acquisition distinction), he has to drop the conventionality claim.
7. But the conventionality claim is the one thing everyone agrees he got right, and the natural-acquisition story itself depends on money being an artificial tool invented for a purpose. So Aristotle's economics either refutes itself, or condemns lending at interest for reasons nothing else in his system can support.

My prof thinks there's an equivocation somewhere in steps 1-4 but just told me to "think harder about what 'natural' is doing," which was not helpful. My roommate, an econ major, says just reject premise 2, Aristotle was obviously wrong about interest and that whole passage is a prestige artifact. For what it's worth, I'd like to keep both the conventionality claim and the idea that there's something philosophically respectable in the natural/unnatural acquisition distinction, even if usury itself turns out to be fine -- but i can be talked out of either if the price is right. Which step do you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost?

One more thing: please be careful about what's genuinely settled in the scholarship vs. what's contested. I know people argue, for example, about whether the Ethics V passage on need holding goods together is a proto-theory of value or just a remark about money's usefulness, and about whether Aristotle is condemning commerce as such or only acquisition without limit. Don't present one side of those debates as the consensus if there isn't one.
