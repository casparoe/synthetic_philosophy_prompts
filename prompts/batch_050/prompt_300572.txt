I spent my career in geophysics and have only lately taken up philosophy in earnest, so I am used to trusting chains of reasoning and uneasy when one delivers a conclusion I cannot live with. I have been reading about Leibniz's doctrine that the actual world is the best of all possible worlds, and I worked out the following argument, which I cannot refute but also cannot accept. I would like a diagnosis, not reassurance.

(1) God is omniscient and perfectly good, and so could not have failed to create the best of the possible worlds. (2) How good a world is depends on everything it contains, including the deeds done in it. (3) The actual world, therefore, contains as much goodness as any world could; any world differing from it would be, on the whole, worse. (4) Suppose I now do something base. Then the actual world contains a base deed of mine. By (3), any world in which I instead acted decently would be worse overall. (5) So whatever I do, the alternative would have made things worse — my doing otherwise never improves the world. (6) But morality, as I naively understand it, just is the demand that I act so that things go better rather than worse; an act is wrong when the world with it is worse than the world without it. (7) Applying (5), no act of mine is ever wrong in that sense: each is already part of the optimal whole. (8) Hence the optimism of (1)–(3) abolishes the distinction between right and wrong action — or else morality was never about making things better at all.

This seems to me to prove too much: it would excuse cruelty on the ground that the world containing it is already the best going. I would prefer to keep (1) and (2) if I honestly can, and I am reluctant to say morality has nothing to do with how things turn out. But I cannot name the step that fails.

Please answer in the following sections, in this order:

Diagnosis (about 250 words): identify the single step or premise where the argument goes wrong — or, if you judge it valid, say so plainly — and explain why that step looked innocent. I want to understand what made it slippery, not merely to be told it fails.

What Leibniz could say back (about 250 words): set out the distinctions or commitments available within his own system that bear on the step you identified, without assuming I know his terminology.

The price list (about 200 words): for each premise or step one might instead reject, state what rejecting it costs — what else one could no longer say.

Verdict (about 100 words): your recommendation on which rejection is cheapest, and whether the resulting position still deserves the name Leibniz gave it.
