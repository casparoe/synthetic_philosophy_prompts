Write a satirical op-ed, roughly 550–650 words, for a general-audience philosophy magazine, in the voice of a lump of clay complaining about the statue it constitutes. The lump has recently been installed on a museum pedestal -- or rather, the statue has been installed on a museum pedestal, and the lump has come along unacknowledged -- and it is fed up.

The op-ed's grievances should be ones the lump can press without contradicting the standard constitution view, and the comedy should come from pressing them accurately. For instance: the lump existed before the statue did (it was clay on Monday; the sculptor made it into a statue on Tuesday, and yet all the press coverage dates "the artwork" from Tuesday, erasing the lump's earlier life). The lump will survive being squashed by the museum's insurance-mandated stress test; the statue will not -- and yet it is the statue whose loss the insurance policy covers, at a figure the lump notes it could never command on its own. The lump shares every molecule, every gram of weight, every cubic centimeter of its location with the statue, and yet the museum's head of collections insists on paying for two separate catalogue entries, which the lump finds either flattering or extortionate depending on the day. A conservator has explained to the lump that it and the statue are "numerically distinct but spatially coincident," and the lump has questions about what, exactly, is supposed to make them two things rather than one, given that nothing -- not weight, not shape, not history from Tuesday onward -- seems to tell them apart.

Keep the irony deadpan: the lump never doubts for a moment that the distinction is real; its complaint is that the distinction is rigged in the statue's favor. Do not let the lump slip into claiming it simply *is* the statue (that would end the column), and do not let it claim any property the statue lacks except the ones the constitution view actually grants it (earlier origin, survival under reshaping). A reader who knows the material constitution literature should recognize the counting problem, the asymmetry of persistence conditions, and the grounding problem surfacing as workplace grievances; a reader who doesn't should still find it funny as the complaint of an overlooked collaborator. End on a note that leaves the genuine puzzle standing -- some version of the question of how many things are really sitting on the pedestal -- without the lump ever breaking voice to state it as an exam question.
