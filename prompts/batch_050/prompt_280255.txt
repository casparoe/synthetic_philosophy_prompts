Last weekend I had a long and rather pointed argument with a close friend — she teaches New Testament at a seminary, I trained as an ancient historian — about whether the standard criteria of authenticity in historical Jesus research can ever settle what Jesus actually said and did. I took the negative side. Her strongest move was an analogy I couldn't fully answer at the time: the criteria, she said, work like triangulation in land surveying, where each sighting is individually unreliable but several taken together fix a position. The argument ended in a draw, but it matters more now, because next month I am presenting a paper at a joint colloquium of our religious studies department and the ancient history faculty defending that negative side. My thesis: the usual criteria — multiple attestation, embarrassment, dissimilarity, and their refined descendants — cannot, even in principle, deliver a historically secure Jesus, because each one smuggles in the very judgments it is supposed to adjudicate. Multiple attestation presupposes that our sources are independent of one another, which is precisely what source criticism cannot establish. Embarrassment presupposes that we know what would have embarrassed which early community, reconstructed from the very texts under test. Dissimilarity, applied consistently, yields a Jesus too alien from Judaism to have attracted followers and too alien from the church to have been remembered by it. My positive claim is deliberately modest: not that we know nothing about Jesus, but that the criteria function largely as after-the-fact ratifications of portraits arrived at by other means. The audience will include two senior historical-Jesus scholars whose careers rest on refined versions of these criteria and who know the literature exhaustively; one systematic theologian who regards the entire quest as a category mistake; three ancient historians who doubt the criteria would pass muster in ordinary historiography but suspect my argument proves too much; and about a dozen graduate students, half of them believers. The room is collegial but the question period is competitive. Prepare my Q&A prep: the objections I will actually get from this audience, in roughly the order they will come, each phrased the way the person most likely to raise it would phrase it, and for each one what I should concede, what I should hold, a reply of about two minutes, and where that reply is weakest. Include the triangulation objection from my friend, since I still owe her a better answer, and the objection I am hoping nobody asks — I suspect it will come from the ancient historians — with an honest assessment of whether my thesis survives it. One constraint on the form: give me all of this as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
