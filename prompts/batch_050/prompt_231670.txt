I am a novelist by trade, which is to say I spend my working life deciding what images to put in other people's heads, and this has lately made me sensitive to a move I keep encountering in political argument for which I have no name. Orwell's complaint in Politics and the English Language was about dying metaphors: stock phrases repeated so mechanically that no picture forms in the mind, leaving the writer free of the burden of meaning anything. What troubles me is the inverse failure, which strikes me as at least as corrosive. A speaker coins a fresh, viscerally concrete image -- calling a central bank's bond purchases a morphine drip for the markets, say, or describing a treaty as handing over the keys to the national house -- and the image feels so apt that the argument simply stops. Nobody asks whether the patient was ill, or what the proper dose would be; the debate proceeds entirely inside the metaphor, and the metaphor's hidden furniture (addiction, withdrawal, trespass, eviction) is thereafter treated as a discovered fact about the policy rather than as a choice the speaker made. I have watched opponents concede everything that matters by objecting that the drip is being withdrawn too quickly. Is there an established term for this specific operation? I take it to live somewhere near framing effects, near conceptual metaphor, and near what I believe is called a persuasive definition, yet none of those seems exactly right, and it is certainly not an analogy, since no comparison is ever actually argued for. If nothing established fits, please coin and define a term that marks the phenomenon off from each of those neighbors, with a boundary case or two on every side. The label matters less to me than the carving.
