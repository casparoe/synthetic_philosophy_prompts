I've been thinking about the tautology objection to the principle of natural selection: if fitness just means reproductive success, then "the fittest survive" collapses into "those who reproduce are those who reproduce," and the theory explains nothing. The standard reply is the propensity interpretation, on which fitness is a disposition to leave offspring that can fail to manifest through bad luck. I want to know whether my case-by-case verdicts add up to a coherent version of that reply, or whether I have to give some up. Here they are:

1. Two beetles with identical genotypes; one is killed by a falling branch, the other leaves ten offspring. Verdict: equally fit; the unlucky beetle's propensity simply wasn't realized.
2. A mildly deleterious mutation spreads to fixation by drift. Verdict: its bearers were less fit throughout, despite ending up more numerous.
3. A sterile worker ant that raises hundreds of sisters. Verdict: genuinely fit, despite leaving zero offspring of her own.
4. A plant whose seeds a hurricane carries to an island where they flourish, while all its mainland siblings die in a drought. Verdict: honestly, I want to say the island plant got lucky and wasn't fitter -- but I notice this is the same move I made in case 1, and here it feels less secure.
5. A salmon leaves thousands of offspring, none of which survive to reproduce; a second leaves two offspring, both of which leave many grandchildren. Verdict: the second was fitter; fitness can't stop counting after one generation.
6. Bright plumage spreads only because it hitchhikes alongside a linked allele for parasite resistance. Verdict: the plumage wasn't fitter; the genome was.

Is there a single principle behind all six? I see at least two tensions: cases 1 and 2 separate fitness from actual outcomes, but case 4 seems to reintroduce environmental luck into fitness itself; and cases 3 and 6 keep relocating the bearer of fitness from organism to gene to lineage, which may not be one property at all. So: give me a precise propensity account -- specifying what fitness is a propensity for, what bears it, over what time horizon, and relative to which reference class of environments -- that saves as many of these verdicts as possible, and say plainly which ones it forces me to abandon. Then tell me what it implies for a case I haven't settled: an organism whose trait is superb in wet years and lethal in dry ones, in a climate that fluctuates unpredictably. Is its fitness an average, a geometric mean, a function of the whole distribution, or is the question itself ill-posed?
