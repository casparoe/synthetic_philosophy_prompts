A friend in my reading group came up with this argument and its been bugging me for a week. It's about the centipede game (two players alternate choosing take or pass, pot grows each round, taking ends the game and gives you the bigger share, passing a long time is better for both than taking early, yada yada).

(1) A rational player at a node picks the move with the highest expected payoff given their beliefs about what happens after.
(2) At the last node, taking strictly dominates passing, so a rational player there takes.
(3) Working backwards: if the other player is rational and you know it, passing at the second-to-last node loses you money, so a rational player takes there too, and by induction rational players take at every node they reach.
(4) So if a player ever passes, that player was not being rational at that moment.
(5) Common knowledge of rationality = both players are rational, both know it, both know both know it, and so on.
(6) So under common knowledge of rationality, the first player takes at node one, and player 2's first node is never reached.
(7) But player 2 still needs a plan for that node -- a strategy has to say what you'd do everywhere, and anyway the node CAN be reached, empirically people pass all the time.
(8) By (4), whatever player 2 believes upon actually reaching their node has to include "player 1 just did something irrational."
(9) But player 2's own decision at that node is exactly the kind of decision (1)-(3) describe, so player 2 has to model player 1 as an expected-payoff-maximizer to figure out what passing would get them.
(10) So player 2 must simultaneously regard player 1 as irrational (to explain how the node got reached) and as rational (to decide what to do there). Contradiction.
(11) Therefore common knowledge of rationality is incoherent in games like this -- two players can't be commonly known rational while playing a centipede, and since two obviously rational people CAN sit down and play one, game theory's basic framework must be broken.

I really don't buy the conclusion but I can't find the hole. Which premise or step should I give up? The ones I'd most like to keep are (1) and (2), they seem like the core of what rational choice even means. Tell me which step goes wrong and why it looked fine, and then what it costs to reject it -- like, I know different people (Aumann-style "CKR just implies the backward induction outcome," Binmore-style skepticism about reasoning at unreached nodes, the epistemic game theory crowd) locate the problem differently, so walk me through what each of those responses has to give up and whether any of them save (1) and (2) without making game theory unfalsifiable about actual play.
