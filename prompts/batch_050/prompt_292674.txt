I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about Turing’s weird treatment of extrasensory perception in his imitation-game paper, and we still basically disagree. My friend thinks Turing was mostly being ironic: mention telepathy, gesture at how annoying it would be, then move on. I think he was making a serious methodological point—something like: if the statistical evidence for ESP were taken seriously, it would threaten the clean separation between minds that the imitation game depends on, so the experimental setup itself would have to rule it out.

Analyze the Bem precognition controversy as a test of that interpretation. For context, Bem published several experiments around 2011 that appeared to show people responding to future stimuli—like choosing where an image would later appear at above-chance rates. The paper was methodologically respectable by many standards of the time, which is exactly why it caused such a mess: attempted replications mostly failed, critics pointed to flexible analyses, questionable priors, multiple comparisons, and publication incentives, and the whole episode helped fuel psychology’s replication reforms.

Map Turing’s position onto the episode carefully. When should anomalous statistical evidence make us redesign an experiment rather than revise our background theory? Is insisting on a telepathy-proof test analogous to preregistration and blinded analysis, or is that a bad analogy because Turing was worried about contamination between subjects while Bem’s critics were worried about researcher degrees of freedom? Also, separate three things that got blurred in my argument: accepting the evidence as real, accepting ESP as its best explanation, and treating ESP as a live enough possibility that experimental design should guard against it.

Then run it backwards: does the Bem episode vindicate Turing’s apparent willingness to take ESP seriously, or show why his concession was too casual? Don’t just settle whether ESP exists.
