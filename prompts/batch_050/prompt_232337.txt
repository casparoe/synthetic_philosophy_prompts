I'm a third-year undergraduate putting together my application for a summer research fellowship in philosophy, and the proposal is the main thing the committee will judge. My idea grew out of a seminar on practical rationality where we read Kavka's toxin puzzle: a billionaire offers you a fortune if, at midnight tonight, you sincerely intend to drink a mild toxin tomorrow afternoon -- the payment depends only on whether the intention is in place at midnight, not on whether you ever actually drink, and everyone knows that come tomorrow you'll have every reason not to drink and no reason to do so. The standard discussion splits between those who think you cannot form the intention (because you know you won't act on it) and those who think you could form it but that doing so would be irrational (since intending is answerable to reasons for acting). My proposal takes a different cut at the puzzle: I want to focus on what happens when the agent pursues the intention *indirectly* -- hiring a hypnotist, taking an intention-inducing drug, or engineering circumstances so that drinking will look attractive tomorrow -- and ask whether these manoeuvres dissolve the puzzle, merely relocate it, or reveal that the original difficulty was never really about intentions at all but about the difference between causing a state of mind and adopting it for reasons. My hunch is that the indirect strategies succeed precisely by severing the connection between intending and deliberation that the puzzle's two camps both presuppose, and that this tells us something general about which mental states can be brought about "at will" and why.

Write the proposal for me, pitched at roughly the level of a ten-week, 7,000-word project. It should state the central question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer (not just "what does the toxin puzzle show?"), explain why the two standard readings of the puzzle leave my question open rather than already settling it, and lay out the approach I would take -- including why examining the indirect strategies is a better entry point than the more obvious move of just adjudicating between the "cannot intend" and "irrational to intend" camps head-on. It should also say what the possible conclusions of the project are, which considerations would decide between them, what a successful result would look like, and what would count as the project failing (for instance, if the indirect strategies turn out to collapse into the direct case, or if the general claim about reasons and mental causation proves too big to establish from this one example).
