I keep going back and forth on the Born rule problem and I want to see it actually argued out rather than summarized. So: write me a dialogue between two committed Everettians. Maya thinks the decision-theoretic program (Deutsch, Wallace, that whole tradition) basically works — a rational agent facing branching has to order their preferences according to the squared-amplitude weights, and that's all the probability anyone could want or need. Dan thinks the entire project is built on sand, because in Everettian QM every outcome occurs, full stop, so there's no genuine uncertainty anywhere, and dressing up "how much you care about your future successors" as probability just smuggles in the thing that was supposed to be proved. Both of them should be good at this — no strawmen, and each one has to respond to the other's actual last point instead of re-asserting their opening speech.

Give them a concrete occasion for the arguement: it's late, they're in the lab, and a grad student down the hall has offered them a quantum bet — a spin-measurement device pays out a nice dinner in one branch and eats their conference travel money in the other, at weights they both know. Let the abstract fight about whether "uncertainty" even makes sense in a branching world come out through the practical question of whether to take the bet and at what odds. Then, halfway through, have Dan spring his pet objection, the one he claims the decision theorists never touch: a recoherence scenario. Suppose some far-future tech could recombine the branches before the debts come due, erasing every record of which outcome "happened." Dan argues that if branches can in principle be un-branched, then rational betting weights would have to depend on contingent facts about future technology — which is absurd — so weight-based decision theory can't be constitutive of probability. Maya has to genuinely engage with this (she can bite a bullet, or press the decohered-for-all-practical-purposes vs. truly-merged distinction, or try to turn the example back on Dan — your choice), but neither of them should just fold.

Around 700-1000 words, natural speech, no lecture-length monologues — a few sentences per turn, mostly. And please write it in American English.
