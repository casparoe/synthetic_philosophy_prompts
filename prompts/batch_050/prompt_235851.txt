I’m giving a 25-minute philosophy colloquium talk called Can a Book Think If You Read It Fast Enough? and I need help surviving the Q&A.

The thought experiment is a modified version of the Einstein-brain-in-a-book setup. Imagine a room-sized “Einstein Codex” compiled from extensive observations of Einstein’s speech, notebooks, problem-solving habits, memories, moods, etc. It is not just a biography or a list of canned answers. Each entry takes a current internal ledger-state plus the conversation so far and specifies both Einstein’s next response and a revised ledger-state. A very simple electromechanical reader looks up entries, turns pages, updates the ledger, and speaks the result. The combined system can carry on a long conversation, remember what happened earlier, form new plans, get irritated, and apparently reflect on whether it is still Einstein.

My thesis is moderate but still provocative: the Codex sitting untouched on the shelf is not conscious merely because it describes or encodes a mind. But the Codex-plus-reader could be conscious while operating, provided that the overall system has the right causal and counterfactual organization. I want to argue that there is no principled line between this system and an ordinary computer implementation: neither the substrate, nor the slowness, nor the fact that much of the structure is printed in books should settle the issue. I’ll explicitly deny that ink is intrinsically conscious and that just any description of a person suffices.

The audience will be a mixed philosophy-of-mind and cognitive science crowd. There are two sophisticated functionalists who will press me on where the computation is actually happening; one enactivist/phenomenology person suspicious of disembodied input-output organization; one neuroscientist who cares about timing, recurrence, embodiment, and biological detail; and several grad students who will probably bring up the Chinese Room. Nobody there will be impressed by “but it behaves just like Einstein.”

Make me a prep sheet with the 8-10 objections I’m most likely to get from this audience, ordered by how damaging they would be. For each one:

- say who is most likely to raise it;
- phrase it the way they would actually phrase it;
- give the strongest version rather than a strawman;
- sketch a reply I could deliver in about 90 seconds;
- distinguish what I should concede from what I should hold firm on;
- flag the weakest part of my reply and any follow-up trap I should expect.

Please include at least one objection about consciousness versus mere counterfactual readiness, one about whether the reader or the book is doing the cognitive work, one about embodiment and temporal dynamics, and one about whether a finite lookup structure could really support open-ended mentality. Also tell me the question I’m hoping nobody asks, how to answer it without sounding evasive, and what distinction I should introduce in the first five minutes to prevent the discussion from collapsing into the usual China-brain talking points. No citations or reading list needed.
