I have a fifty-minute discussion section in an undergraduate political philosophy course, and my task is to teach the claim that a sweatshop job can be mutually beneficial, voluntarily accepted, and still exploitative — and that this combination poses a genuine dilemma for campaigns to shut such factories down. Don't give me the lecture; plan how I should give it. Specify the order in which the pieces must be introduced: what has to be settled about harm and baselines before the phrase mutually beneficial exploitation can even be coherent to the students, and where consent must enter so that it does real work without being allowed to settle everything. Choose the one carrying example the section should be built around: tell me whether it should be a stylized invented case or a realistic sweatshop case, and state what the example must contain (both parties genuinely better off than with their next-best alternative, no force or fraud, the employer not responsible for the workers' desperate circumstances) and what it must exclude so the puzzle isn't short-circuited. Map the misconceptions I should expect — I anticipate at least 'if both sides gain, it can't be wrong,' 'exploitation just means deception or coercion,' and 'if it's exploitative, it should be banned' — and say at which exact point in the sequence each should be headed off, since several of them will look right at first. Give me one closing question that tests whether students can distinguish the moral verdict about the employer's offer from the policy verdict about the factory, rather than whether they can recite the position. Finally, say what I should deliberately leave out at this level — for instance the choice between historical and moralized baselines, or Marxist structural accounts — and why it belongs to a later session. Commit to definite choices throughout: one order, one example, one set of cuts, each with reasons, rather than a menu of approaches for me to pick from.
