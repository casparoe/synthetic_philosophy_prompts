I am a novelist, and I have spent the better part of a year circling a debate that keeps derailing both my research and my book club. The novel I am drafting involves a protagonist raised in an honor-inflected, tightly knit community who emigrates to a loosely individualist society, and the dramatic engine is supposed to be the collision between two ways of being bad: feeling that one has done a terrible thing, and feeling that one is a terrible thing. To write this honestly I went to the literature on guilt and shame as moral emotions, and I found what looks to me like a stalled dispute.

The broad shape of it: one camp, drawing on decades of psychological research, treats guilt as the healthier and more constructive emotion — it targets behavior, motivates repair and apology, and correlates with better adjustment — while shame is global, self-condemning, linked to hiding, aggression, and various pathologies. The opposing camp, which seems to include anthropologists and some philosophers, replies that this verdict is parochial: shame is the enforcement mechanism of face-to-face societies, sustains honor codes and communal standards, and can be a form of moral perception that guilt-focused cultures have simply lost. A third, smaller group mutters that the two emotions were never cleanly separable to begin with and that the whole ranking exercise rests on a bad taxonomy. Meanwhile, everyone cites the same studies and the same ethnographic examples, and nobody appears to have moved in twenty years.

My working hypothesis — and I hold it loosely — is that the camps are not disagreeing about an emotion at all but about different objects: one side measures shame-proneness as an individual disposition in clinical populations, while the other describes shame as a public social practice, and the goods and harms of these two things need not coincide. But perhaps I am being too tidy.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on which emotion wins. Is this disagreement empirical — would better cross-cultural data settle it — or terminological, with the sides using guilt and shame to pick out different phenomena? Or is it normative all the way down, a dispute about what moral emotions are for (individual flourishing, social cohesion, accuracy of self-assessment) dressed up as a dispute about psychology? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a particular kind of study, or an admission that the question is ill-posed?

A second, smaller question matters to me as a writer: whatever the answer, can a culture deliberately shift its dominant moral emotion, or is that like choosing one's native language? Please answer in American English.
