Exam question: Explain Lewis-style modal realism in your own words and assess this objection: if every possible world is concrete and causally isolated from ours, then modal realism either makes modal knowledge impossible, since we have no causal contact with other worlds, or makes it too easy, since if a proposition is possible there is some world where it is true, so possibility seems to collapse into existence somewhere. Does the objection confuse necessity, possibility, and actuality, or does it expose a real problem? In your answer use the distinction between what exists simpliciter and what is actual for us, and say whether modal realism must claim that merely possible things are real in the same sense as actual things.

Student answer: Modal realism is the view that possible worlds are not just useful fictions or representations but are real things, roughly as real as the world we live in. The word actual is indexical: each world is actual from its own standpoint, so our world is not metaphysically privileged, only locally salient. Lewis also uses counterparts rather than strict transworld identity, which lets him say that I could have been a poet without claiming that this very person literally exists in many worlds at once. The objection is right that we have no causal contact with other worlds, but that by itself is not fatal, because Lewis thinks modal knowledge comes from recombination: we imagine rearrangements of things we already know, and the space of worlds guarantees that any consistent rearrangement is realized somewhere. So possibility does not collapse into existence simpliciter; a merely possible poet-counterpart is real, but not actual, and not existing in the same unrestricted sense as actual things. The objection does expose a real epistemological worry, though: if recombination is just a rule we stipulate, then saying every consistent recombination is realized may make possibility too cheap and explain away the evidence rather than earn it. The cleanest Lewis response is that necessity is truth in all worlds, possibility truth in some, actuality truth here, and causal isolation blocks knowledge only if all knowledge must be causal, which is too strong. Still, the view is committed to merely possible things being just as real as us, only not actual relative to us.
