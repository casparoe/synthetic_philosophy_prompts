At a meeting of my university's philosophical society — which runs its internal affairs as a game in the spirit of Nomic, with the constitution serving as a set of self-amending rules — I witnessed a dispute I couldn't settle. The constitution has four articles:

Art. 1. The Society shall meet weekly during term.
Art. 2. Any article of this constitution may be amended or repealed, and new articles adopted, by a two-thirds vote of members present, provided notice of the proposal was given at the previous meeting.
Art. 3. Article 1 shall not be amended or repealed except by a unanimous vote of all members; and this Article 3 shall not be amended or repealed except by a unanimous vote of all members.
Art. 4. This constitution is the sole and supreme rule of the Society; no act of the Society is valid unless authorized by it.

Notice having been duly given, the thirty members present (out of forty total) voted 30–0 to repeal Article 3, and then 30–0 to repeal Article 1. Afterwards, two members argued about what had just happened.

Tomas said both repeals were void. Article 3 expressly conditions its own repeal on unanimity of all members; thirty of forty is not that, so Article 3 stands, and with it the unanimity requirement protecting Article 1. The votes, however lopsided, had no legal effect.

Ines replied that Article 3's self-entrenchment is bootstrapping. The clause was originally adopted by an ordinary majority; a body cannot confer on its own decision greater authority than the body itself possesses merely by declaring the decision hard to undo. A clause cannot lift itself above the procedure that created it by its own say-so, so the unanimous vote of those present sufficed.

Tomas answered that this proves too much: every entrenched clause in every constitution was once adopted by some lesser procedure, so on Ines's reasoning no entrenchment anywhere is valid, including the ones Ines herself presumably accepts. Entrenchment just is a rule's attempt to constrain the very procedure that created it; if that is bootstrapping, it is bootstrapping all the way down, and the concept of a self-amending rulebook collapses.

Ines then offered a fallback: even granting that Article 3 binds, Article 2 authorizes adopting new articles by two-thirds of those present, with no carve-out. The members could simply have adopted an Article 5 stating that, notwithstanding any other article, Articles 1 and 3 stand repealed — and a specific, later-adopted rule prevails over an earlier general one. Tomas said this was the same repeal wearing a hat: Article 3 forbids its own repeal by any means short of unanimity, and routing the repeal through a new article changes nothing.

They both appealed to me, and I declined to rule. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: assess each of the four moves on its own merits, say who is right about the validity of each repeal, and identify what, if anything, both parties are overlooking about what it takes for a self-amending rule to bind.
