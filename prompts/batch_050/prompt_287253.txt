A friend of mine just finished a popular book about the decline of violence and summarized its argument for me like this: "The author shows that violence of every kind has been falling for centuries, and that we now live in the most peaceful era in our species' history. His key move is to measure violence per capita rather than in absolute numbers: the twentieth century's world wars killed more people than any earlier conflict, but because the world population was so much larger, the *rate* of violent death was still lower than in most pre-state societies, where archaeological evidence suggests a large share of all deaths were violent. He then argues that since 1945 there have been no wars between great powers, and that this 'long peace' shows interstate war is on its way to obsolescence, the way slavery and dueling went obsolete. He credits the decline to the growth of states, commerce, and reason, and treats the decline itself as one of the most solidly established findings in the social sciences -- one people resist only because vivid news coverage makes the present seem more dangerous than it is."

I'm inclined to trust this summary because my friend usually reads carefully, but a few things sit oddly with me. For one, "the most peaceful era in history" seems hard to square with the absolute numbers I remember from the twentieth century, no matter what the per-capita arithmetic says, and I'm not sure which of the two measures is the morally relevant one -- or whether that's a factual question at all rather than a choice about what to count. For another, the claim that seventy-some years without a great-power war *shows* war is becoming obsolete feels statistically thin to me, but I can't articulate why.

Could you audit the summary for me? Go through it claim by claim and tell me which parts an advocate of the decline thesis would accept as accurate, which parts are overstatements or conflations of the actual position (I suspect at least the strength of the obsolescence claim and the claim that the decline is uncontested are stronger than what the book argues), and what serious critics of the thesis object to on methodological grounds -- especially anything about how deaths from war are distributed statistically and what that implies for how much a run of peaceful decades can prove. Then tell me whether the conclusion my friend actually drew from all this -- that worrying about large-scale war between major powers is roughly as irrational as worrying about the return of dueling -- follows from the parts of the summary that survive correction. If the answer to "is the world becoming less violent?" turns out to depend on definitional choices (per capita versus absolute, which deaths count as violence, which baselines we compare against), I'd like you to say plainly which parts of the question have a fact of the matter and which parts don't.
