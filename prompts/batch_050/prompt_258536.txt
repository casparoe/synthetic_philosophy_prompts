"State the procreation asymmetry clearly. Explain why many people find it intuitively compelling, and discuss what you take to be the most serious difficulty facing someone who wants to accept it."

---
The procreation asymmetry is the view, most closely associated with David Benatar, that we have a moral duty not to bring into existence people whose lives would go badly, but no corresponding duty to bring into existence people whose lives would go well. Creating a miserable life is wrong; failing to create a happy life is at worst a missed opportunity, not a wrong.

The asymmetry is compelling because it matches how we actually judge cases. A couple who knowingly conceive a child destined for a short life of severe suffering do something wrong, and we blame them for it. But a couple who decide to remain childless do not wrong the happy children they might have had — there is no one there to complain. The intuition is nearly universal, which is why the asymmetry functions as a fixed point in population ethics.

The asymmetry is essentially a special case of the non-identity problem. Because the people in question do not yet exist, there is no one who can be harmed or benefited by the decision, so obligations can attach only to preventing bad outcomes, never to producing good ones.

Benatar also uses the asymmetry to argue for antinatalism: since there is never a duty to create a happy life, but there is sometimes a duty not to create a miserable one, the balance always favors not procreating, and it follows directly from the asymmetry that procreation is always wrong.

The main difficulty is that the asymmetry conflicts with total utilitarianism, which says we should maximize total happiness and so does require creating happy lives. Accepting the asymmetry means either rejecting total utilitarianism or abandoning the intuition. A further problem is that the asymmetry, combined with standard population-ethical reasoning, generates the repugnant conclusion, which most people find unacceptable.
