I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but a friend who teaches philosophy at the local university has been feeding me infinity puzzles at our weekly coffee, and I've started keeping score of her verdicts. Her calls so far: (1) Zeno's dichotomy — to cross the room I must complete infinitely many sub-intervals — she says the scenario is perfectly coherent and I simply finish; convergent series dissolve the paradox. (2) Hilbert's hotel — a fully occupied hotel with infinitely many rooms can still accommodate infinitely many new guests — she says it's strange but entirely consistent. (3) The pi-machine, a device that prints the nth digit of pi after 1/2^n minutes and so prints the entire expansion within one minute — she says this one is impossible, on the grounds that the story only works if there is a last digit for the machine to be displaying at the end, and pi has none. (4) The infinite urn: at 1/2^n minutes before noon, you add balls numbered 10n−9 through 10n and remove ball n; she says that's coherent and the urn is empty at noon, because every ball that ever goes in comes out at some definite time. (5) The counter who recites each natural number aloud, saying n at 1−1/2^n minutes, and then announces "done" at the one-minute mark — coherent, she says, since every utterance has its scheduled time and the final announcement is just stipulated as one more act.

Now she has posed me Thomson's lamp, and I want to predict her verdict before she gives it. The lamp starts off; it is toggled on at half a minute, off at three-quarters, on at seven-eighths, and so on; the question is whether it is on or off at the one-minute mark. Her track record strikes me as consistent with at least two different principles. On one reading, she rejects a scenario exactly when its description demands a first or last member of a sequence whose ordering has none — that explains the pi-machine (a demanded final digit) while acquitting the lamp, whose toggling rule never asks for a last toggle, so the lamp would be coherent but simply underdescribed at the limit. On another reading, she accepts a scenario only when the evolving state converges to, or is explicitly stipulated to have, a definite state at the limit — that also explains the pi-machine (no convergent final display) and the counter (a stipulated final act), but it condemns the lamp, whose state oscillates without settling. Both principles seem to fit cases (1) through (5), and they diverge on the lamp.

So: predict her verdict on the lamp and tell me which principle is the better reconstruction of her track record, with reasons. If you think her verdicts actually commit her to some third principle I haven't seen, lay it out and say what it predicts. Also, what further puzzle — or what variation on the lamp itself — would most cleanly distinguish the leading candidates from each other? Please separate what is genuinely settled in this territory (for instance, where the standard mathematics resolves the classical paradoxes) from what remains contested among people who actually work on supertasks — including whether the lamp's limit state is merely undetermined by the description or whether the whole setup is outright impossible — and don't present a contested position as the consensus. American English, please.
