So I co-host a small philosophy-and-TV podcast with two friends and we're doing an episode on Severance next month, and I've been assigned the "actual philosophy" segment. My plan is to read the show through the split-brain literature and the unity of consciousness debate -- commissurotomy patients, the two-hemisphere findings, the question of whether those patients house one subject or two. But the more I prep, the more I suspect the fit is worse than it looks, and I want help getting the reading right rather than just convenient.

Here's my worry. In the real split-brain cases (as I understand them) the disunity is synchronic: at a single moment, information presented to one hemisphere is unavailable to the other, the hands can act at cross purposes, and the speaking hemisphere confabulates explanations for behavior it didn't initiate. Whereas the severance procedure on the show seems to split people diachronically -- each stream (innie and outie) is internally unified, coherent, has integrated perception and action, they just don't share autobiographical memory. So is Severance actually dramatizing disunity of consciousness at all, or is it really a Parfit-style thought experiment about psychological continuity and personal identity wearing split-brain clothes? And does that distinction matter for the ethical horror of the show -- is the wrongness of severance that it creates a second subject, or that it traps the same subject, and does the show itself have a consistent answer? I'd love a reading that takes the show's own details seriously: reintegration, the overtime contigency, the Ms. Casey reveal, how innies and outies treat each other as strangers or as selves. Where does the neuroscience illuminate the show, where does the show quietly depend on a theory of mind that the actual split-brain evidence complicates, and is the honest verdict that the consciousness stuff is ultimately a vehicle for a point about work and alienation rather than the other way around?
