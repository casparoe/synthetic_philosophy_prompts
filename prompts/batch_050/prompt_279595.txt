Both empiricism and intellectualism, Merleau-Ponty argues, fail to understand skilled bodily action. Drawing on Phenomenology of Perception: (a) explain motor intentionality and the role of the body schema; (b) explain what the case of Schneider is supposed to show, and why it supports Merleau-Ponty's view over the alternatives; (c) say whether his account is compatible with computational, representation-based theories of motor control in contemporary cognitive science, and defend your answer.

And here's the student's answer I need graded:

---

Merleau-Ponty holds that both empiricism and intellectualism treat the body as an object — a thing in the world that either receives stimuli passively or gets steered by judgments. Empiricism breaks movement into reflex arcs triggered from outside; intellectualism inserts an act of mind between perception and action. Both miss that the body has its own way of being directed at the world, which he calls motor intentionality: a bodily directedness that operates before any explicit thought. The I can of the body, on his view, is more basic than the I think of reflection.

The body schema is central here. It is not a picture or map of the body that the mind consults; it is a pre-reflective system of possible postures and movements, a lived sense of where my limbs are and what is within reach. When I reach for a cup I don't compute my arm's trajectory — the cup shows up as to-be-grasped and the movement unfolds.

The Schneider case supports this. Schneider, a brain-injured veteran studied by Gelb and Goldstein, could perform concrete actions embedded in habitual contexts — grasping his comb, using his tools — but failed at abstract movements given as bare commands, like pointing to his nose with his eyes shut, unless he built the movement up through preparatory motions. Merleau-Ponty's reading is that what Schneider has lost is neither sensation nor intellect, which are relatively intact, but the function of projecting a merely possible situation — the loosening of the intentional arc that normally gathers past, future, and setting into the body's orientation. Concrete movement survives because the actual world still summons it; abstract movement fails because no actual situation answers.

This also clarifies habit. Once a habit is acquired it runs off mechanically, like a conditioned reflex — the body no longer knows what it is doing, it just repeats. Typing and driving are automatic in this sense, which is why habit poses no threat to the account: habit is precisely the non-intelligent residue of action.

On the computational question: since motor intentionality involves no representations whatsoever, Merleau-Ponty's view rules out computational theories of motor control in principle. Any model that computes trajectories from body-state representations is just intellectualism rebuilt in engineering terms, and would face the same objections.
