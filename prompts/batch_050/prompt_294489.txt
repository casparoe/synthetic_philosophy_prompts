So I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and my editor has me working on a long-read about the fights over Confederate monuments in the US -- the removals in Charlottesville and Richmond, the statue topplings during the 2020 protests, the whole "heritage vs. hate" argument that never seems to die. I'm not interested in rehashing the politics, everyones done that. What I want is to structure the piece around Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, which I read years ago and just re-read. Quick recap in case it helps: he distinguishes three ways history can serve life -- the monumental (great deeds of the past as inspiration and proof that greatness is possible), the antiquarian (loving preservation of one's inherited past, reverence for roots), and the critical (dragging the past before a tribunal, judging and condemning it so the present can live). He also insists history only has value insofar as it serves life, and warns that each mode becomes poisonous in excess.

Here's what I want from you, because it'll basically be the skeleton of the piece. Map the statue debate onto this schema with some actual discipline: which of Nietzsche's three modes is each side really practicing? The "heritage" people talk like antiquarians, but a lot of these statues went up decades after the war, in the Jim Crow and civil rights eras, which makes me suspect something else is going on -- maybe monumental history for a different subject than they admit? And is the removal side doing critical history in Nietzsche's sense, or is tearing down a statue the opposite of critical history -- refusing to judge the past and instead erasing it, since critical history for Nietzsche still requires confronting and sentencing it, not forgetting it? Then run it the other direction too: what does the episode show about the theory? Nietzsche keeps asking whether history serves life, but the statue fight suggests the real question is whose life, and his schema seems to assume a single people with a single past. Does a contested, multi-community history break his framework, or does it just multiply the bookkeeping? Finally -- he warns that an overdose of critical history shatters a community's self-confidence and leaves it unable to act. Is there anything in the last decade of American culture that confirms that worry, or does the episode suggest he got the dose-response curve wrong?

Keep it analytical rather than a book report, and feel free to tell me where my mapping above is sloppy. Also its fine if you think Nietzsche's schema is the wrong tool and only partially works -- I'd rather the piece say that honestly than force the fit.
