The question of what counts as genocide has recently become personal for me: my grandfather survived the wartime deportation of his entire village, and my family has argued for years about whether that word applies. Thinking it through, I've collected my own verdicts on standard cases, and I cannot find a principle that yields all of them.

- The 1994 killing of the Tutsi in Rwanda: clearly genocide.
- The Khmer Rouge's mass killing in Cambodia: feels like genocide to me, even though most victims were ethnic Khmer targeted as class enemies rather than as members of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group.
- The Holodomor: feels like genocide, even though the deaths came through famine policy rather than extermination orders and the intent behind it remains contested.
- The atomic bombing of Hiroshima: deliberate mass killing of civilians chosen partly to break a nation's will, yet it does not feel like genocide.
- The Great Leap Forward famine: tens of millions dead from policy, and it does not feel like genocide.
- The residential school system imposed on Indigenous children in Canada: relatively few direct killings, but an explicit aim of erasing a culture, and it does feel like genocide to me.

The problem is that some pairs look impossible to separate: Cambodia and the Great Leap both involve a state killing its own people sorted by political categories, yet my intuitions split; Hiroshima and my grandfather's deportation both involved deliberate state violence against a national group, yet my intuitions split there too. Is there a single principle — about intent, about the kind of group targeted, about whether the victims are attacked for what they are rather than for what they do, about which methods count — that delivers all six verdicts? Or is my taxonomy tracking two different things, the legal category that emerged from Lemkin's campaign and a looser moral one, in which case which of my verdicts should I give up? Either way, tell me what the best surviving principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: a siege that deliberately starves a city whose population belongs to a different ethnic group than the besiegers.
