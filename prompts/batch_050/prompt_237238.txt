I'm a PhD student in epistemology and there's an argument floating around my cohort that feels like it proves way too much, but I cant locate the weak link. (1) Moore's hand proof is a legitimate response to the external-world skeptic: the premise that he has two hands is more certain for him than any premise of the skeptical argument, so he's entitled to conclude the skeptic is wrong rather than vice versa. (2) Nothing about that legitimacy depends on it being about hands. It depends only on the structure: if a valid argument's conclusion conflicts with something I'm more confident of than I am in the argument's premises, I'm entitled to run the thing in reverse and reject a premise. (3) For basically any ambitious philosophical argument (against free will, against the self, for some wild normative theory, whatever), there's some ordinary belief I'm more confident in than I am in the conclusion. (4) So I can always legitimately flip the argument and conclude that some premise is false, without having to identify which one or why. (5) So no philosophical argument can ever rationally dislodge a confident commonsense belief. But (5) is nuts -- arguments have changed my mind before, and if (5) were true the whole discipline would just be people waving their priors at each other. And note the flipping itself is logically fine, modus tollens is valid, so I dont think the slip is there. Which premise do I give up, why did it look fine, and what does giving it up cost? If forced to choose I'd rather keep Moore's proof than (2) in its full generality, but tell me what I'm paying either way.
