Write a dialogue set in Vienna in 1934 between two thinkers: one a logical empiricist of the Vienna Circle's left wing, sympathetic to the vision (associated with Otto Neurath) of a planned economy administered on the basis of physical statistics rather than money prices; the other an economist-philosopher in the mold of the Austrian school, convinced that rational economic calculation is impossible without market prices. The occasion is the empiricist's claim that, once a society has settled its ends, organizing production to meet them is a technical problem soluble by the same empirical methods as any other engineering task. Have the Austrian press the objection that the planner's problem is not computational but epistemic — that knowledge of local circumstances, costs, and needs exists only dispersed among countless individuals and is articulated only through prices, so that abolishing the price system destroys the very data planning would require. Let the empiricist answer with characteristically logical-empiricist moves: distinguishing questions of fact from decisions about ends, questioning whether "the economy as a whole" is the sort of thing that requires a single knowing subject, and offering an explication of "rational planning" rather than a metaphysical verdict on its possibility. Both speakers should be equally able; each should respond to the other's actual arguments rather than talk past them. The dialogue should end not in agreement but at the point where the genuine locus of disagreement — whether the dispute is empirical or a clash of practical decisions — comes into view.
