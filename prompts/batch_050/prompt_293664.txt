I sit on the professional conduct committee of a national licensing body for financial advisers. Following a run of misconduct findings, the committee is deadlocked over a proposal to publish, on a permanent and searchable public register, the names of members found guilty of serious misconduct, together with a summary of what they did. Supporters call the register a deterrent and a matter of clients' right to know; opponents call it a scarlet letter. At present our sanctions are private: a reprimand, a fine, or supervised retraining, disclosed only to the complainant. Two considerations in particular divide us. The register's advocates argue that the fear of public disgrace is precisely what gives the sanction its force, and that concealing findings protects advisers at clients' expense. Its opponents argue that a sanction aimed at a person's standing rather than their conduct tends to produce concealment rather than reform: members who fear ending up on the register will hide errors, colleagues will stop reporting near-misses, and those already named, having nothing left to lose in the profession, will either leave it or reoffend. Underneath the policy dispute, as I see it, sits a philosophical one about guilt and shame: guilt condemns the act and invites repair, shame condemns the self and invites withdrawal, and the two sides of the committee seem to be disagreeing about which emotion a sanction should aim to elicit, and about whether an institution can even choose, or whether that is settled by the surrounding culture. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before next month's meeting. It should recommend one of three courses — adopt the register, keep sanctions private, or adopt a middle way you specify — and give reasoning my colleagues could actually respond to, including an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side. I am not after a literature review or citations; I want the reasoning itself. The opinion should also say, explicitly, what consideration or piece of evidence would lead it to the opposite recommendation, and identify the step in its own argument where the reasoning is most likely to fail.
