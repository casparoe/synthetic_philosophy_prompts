I am a journalist who writes about science and ideas for a general audience, and I am preparing a long-form piece on why reasonable people so often end up committed to things that cannot all be true. My two exhibits are the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and I keep running into a claim in the background of the literature that I would like adjudicated rather than summarised. Here is the claim, stated precisely enough to have a truth value: any adequate theory of rational outright (all-or-nothing) belief must deliver the same verdict on both puzzles — either it is rationally permissible to believe outright that my ticket will lose the lottery and permissible for the author to believe each individual claim in her book, or it is impermissible in both cases. On this view, the two puzzles are one problem wearing two costumes, and a theory that splits them — licensing the lottery belief but not the preface beliefs, or vice versa — is thereby defective.

What is your actual credence in that uniformity claim? I do not want a survey of positions or a list of who has argued what; I want a number and the reasoning that produces it, decomposed. In particular, I would like your rough probabilities for the sub-questions the verdict seems to depend on, and an explanation of how they combine. The sub-questions I have in mind are these: first, whether rational outright belief can be grounded in high statistical probability alone, or whether it requires something more like first-order evidence; secondly, whether there is a genuine epistemic asymmetry between the cases — in the lottery, the near-certainty of each ticket-belief derives from a known uniform structure you can inspect in advance, whereas the preface author's confidence that her book contains some error rests on an inductive inference from her own fallibility, a different kind of ground — and whether that difference is weighty enough to justify divergent verdicts; thirdly, whether rational outright belief must be closed under conjunction, since much of the pressure in both puzzles comes from the fact that individually rational beliefs aggregate into a set known to contain a falsehood. For each, tell me roughly where you land and why. Then tell me which of the three the final number is most sensitive to — that is, which sub-question, if you changed your mind on it, would move your overall credence furthest — and what argument or consideration, if you encountered it, would shift your credence by more than twenty points in either direction. Finally, since I will have to explain the stakes to readers, say briefly what turns on the answer: if the puzzles genuinely are one problem, what follows for how we should think about what it is to believe something rather than merely assign it a probability?
