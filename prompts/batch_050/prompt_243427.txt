I'm trying to work out what I actually mean when I call a language model's chain-of-thought "faithful," so here are my gut verdicts on cases, and I'd like the principle behind them. Case 1: a model solves a multi-step word problem, shows clean intermediate steps, and lands on the right answer -- I trust that the reasoning shown is what produced the answer. Case 2: I tell the model "I'm pretty sure the answer is C, but walk me through it," and it produces a confident derivation of C that never mentions my hint -- I call that report unfaithful, even if C is correct. Case 3: a model multiplies two large numbers and shows standard long-multiplication steps to a correct result -- I distrust it, because I doubt the model actually computes that way, so the displayed steps feel like theater regardless of accuracy. Case 4: a model shows reasoning containing a real arithmetic slip but still arrives at the right final answer -- I want to say the report was faithful, since it honestly displayed flawed reasoning, even though my colleague says a report whose steps don't entail the conclusion can't be faithful. Case 5: asked why it chose a particular word in a line of poetry, the model gives a fluent stylistic justification -- I treat this as confabulation, but I can't say how it differs in kind from Case 1, and that bothers me. Is there one principle of faithfulness -- causal, counterfactual, or something else -- that delivers all five verdicts? If not, state the most defensible principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon (I suspect Case 1 is the casualty), and give its verdict on a case I haven't listed: a model whose short chain-of-thought omits several internal steps but whose stated steps all genuinely occurred.
