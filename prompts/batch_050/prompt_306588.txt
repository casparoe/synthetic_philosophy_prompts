So I'm a retired chemist and in my dotage I've gotten into philosophy, mostly through a little reading group at the library. Last month we did Locke on personal identity, the memory criterion, and then that brave officer case which I gather is Reid's objection to it. My brother in law, an engineer and therefore even more insufferable than me about these things, sat down and turned the case into an argument that seems to prove something insane, and neither of us can find the mistake. Here it is. (1) A person at a later time is the same person as someone at an earlier time if and only if the later person can remember, from the inside, an experience the earlier person had. That's the memory criterion as our group understood it. (2) The old general remembers storming the fort as a young officer, so the general is the young officer. (3) The young officer remembered being flogged as a small boy, so the officer is the boy. (4) If a is identical to b and b is identical to c, then a is identical to c, and that's just logic, not a philosophical opinion. (5) So by (2), (3) and (4), the general is the boy. (6) But the general cannot remember the flogging at all, so by the criterion in (1), the general is not the boy. Now here's where my brother in law goes off the rails. He says (5) and (6) clash, (4) is untouchable, (2) and (3) are just the case as described, and since he thinks the memory criterion is the only account of personal identity that a scientifically minded person could take seriously (no souls, and bodily continuity fails in teleportation thought experiments, he says), the thing that has to give is our naive assumption that the general and the boy are one person. Generalizing: nobody survives their own forgetting. He points out that I can't rember anything from before about age four, so the toddler in my mother's photo albums strictly speaking wasn't me, and every time someone loses a memory for good, a person goes out of existence. He calls this his extinction argument and is weirdly proud of it.

I refuse to believe I've been dead since the fifties, and I also refuse to believe the general is a different person from the boy he grew out of. So tell me which step to reject and why it looked so plausible. I'd most like to keep (4), since it seems like pure logic, and I'd like to keep my conviction that I'm the same person as the kid, but if one of those has to be sacrificed I want to know what I'm paying for it. I have a suspicion that something goes wrong in how (1) gets applied across a chain of people rather than to a single pair of them, but I also have a nagging separate worry that the memory criterion is circular, since genuinely remembering something (as opposed to just seeming to) already presupposes that it was you who had the experience, and I don't know whether that worry is the same as the problem with the chain or a different problem entirely. Please sort out which of these is the actual fault line, what the standard fixes cost, and which fix you'd take. And write it as continuous prose, like an email from a friend who happens to know this material -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, none of that.
