**Mill the reluctant socialist**

Ask anyone what John Stuart Mill thought about socialism and you'll get one of two answers. Socialists claim him as one of their own; free-marketers insist he stayed a liberal to the end. I think both readings flatten what's going on in the Chapters on Socialism, the unfinished piece published after his death in 1879, and that Mill's actual position is stranger and more interesting than either side admits: he was a market socialist before the term existed, and the reason he was one is a reason most modern socialists definately don't want to hear.

Mill's move in the Chapters is to separate the socialists' diagnosis from their cure. On the diagnosis he concedes almost everything. The existing distribution of property isn't the result of some fair process of industry and thrift; it's the sedimented outcome of conquest, violence, and laws rigged in favour of whoever got there first. So the idea that current property arrangements deserve moral deference is, on Mill's telling, a joke. Private property as actually practised had never been tried in anything like its defensible form.

But then he turns on the cure. The socialists of his day mostly wanted to abolish competition, and Mill refuses to follow. Competition, he argues, does real work: it disciplines producers, sorts effort from idleness, and tells you what things are actually worth. The fact that competition under current conditions is brutal doesn't mean the alternative -- a central authority assigning everyone their task and their share -- would be less brutal, just brutal in a quieter way. His nightmare scenario is basically an all-powerful administrative hierarchy with total control over each person's life, which he thinks would be a worse servitude than anything the market produces.

So what does Mill actually want? Worker cooperatives, competing against each other in open markets. He'd already sketched this in the Principles of Political Economy, where he predicts the old relation of employers and wage-labourers will gradually give way to associations of workers who own the business and split the proceeds. This solves the exploitation problem without giving up the price mechanism, and it solves the incentive problem too, because co-op members still get paid more when the co-op does well.

Here's where I think the essay's payoff is. Mill's framework implies that the modern debate between "markets" and "socialism" is confused, because his version of socialism *requires* markets. And it also implies that he would have been fine with co-ops outcompeting traditional firms on efficiency grounds -- indeed that's the whole plan. If worker ownership is better, prove it in competition. The uncomfortable corollary, which I think modern co-op advocates mostly dodge, is that if co-ops *can't* win in open competition even after the legal playing field is levelled, Mill's own framework says they don't deserve to exist. His socialism is falsifiable in a way most socialism isn't.

One caveat worth flagging: Mill does concede that a fully communist society would be the highest form *if* human character were transformed enough to sustain it. He just thinks betting institutions on that transformation is reckless given humans as they are. Whether that concession undermines the rest of his argument is a question I leave for another day.
