I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character, a retired judge, keeps a battered copy of Adam Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments by her bedside and measures her own conduct against what she calls her inner onlooker. I want to get the philosophy right before I put it in her mouth, so I have been reading around, and I recently came across the following summary in a popular introduction to the history of ethics. It runs roughly like this: Smith holds that morality is founded on sympathy, by which he means our tendency to catch and share the feelings of people around us, rather as one catches a yawn. Because our sentiments are formed and checked in this way, each of us gradually develops an impartial spectator, an internalized representative of the community's judgment, before whom we imagine pleading our case; an action is right when that spectator approves of it. Hence, the author concludes, conscience for Smith is essentially public opinion moved indoors, and an action's being right amounts to its being the sort of thing one's society would applaud. The author adds that this explains both why the theory is so comfortable and why it has been left behind: a conscience built out of the community's actual attitudes cannot condemn that community's moral consensus, and once one sees conscience as mere socialization there is little left for an ethical theory to do.

My inclination, for what it is worth, is to trust the first half of the summary and to distrust the deflationary verdict, but I cannot articulate where the slide happens. I suspect at least two things are off: the equation of sympathy with emotional contagion, and the move from the spectator's approval to society's applause, since my character uses her inner onlooker precisely to resist what everyone around her thinks. But I am a reader here, not a scholar, and I do not want to build a novel on a hunch.

What I would like is a claim-by-claim audit of the summary: which parts a careful reader of Smith would accept as they stand, which are distortions or conflations, what the accurate version of each distorted claim would be, and whether the author's conclusions — that the theory cannot fault a settled consensus, and that it collapses into an observation about socialization — survive once the inaccuracies are corrected. Please also be candid about the answer's own exposure: name the argument or consideration that, if it went the other way, would lead you to a different overall verdict on the summary's fairness, and indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail. And please write the response in American English.
