Below is a short draft essay on the dogmatism paradox — the apparent consequence that if I know that p, I can rationally dismiss in advance any evidence against p, since such evidence must be misleading. The draft proposes a dissolution, and I would like critical comments on whether the proposal succeeds.

Draft:

The dogmatism paradox arises from a simple argument. If I know that p, then p is true; and if p is true, any evidence counting against p points away from the truth and is therefore misleading; so, knowing that p, I seem licensed to disregard future counterevidence without examining it. Since this conclusion is absurd, something must be wrong with the argument.

My diagnosis is that the argument equivocates between two kinds of "evidence against p." Call evidence of the first kind world-directed: it bears on whether p itself obtains (the readings on the instrument, the testimony of a witness). Call evidence of the second kind agent-directed: it bears not on p but on whether my belief that p was reliably formed or is being reliably maintained (news that the test I relied on was miscalibrated, or that I have taken a drug known to produce confident false memories).

The dogmatic inference is valid only for world-directed evidence: given that p, world-directed evidence against p is indeed misleading, and I lose nothing epistemically by setting it aside. But the inference fails for agent-directed evidence. From the fact that I know that p, nothing follows about the accuracy of information concerning my own reliability. That information can be perfectly accurate even when p is true — I might know that p on the basis of a method that was in fact defective but happened, luckily, to deliver the truth. Agent-directed evidence can therefore destroy my knowledge without being evidence that p is false, and dismissing it unheard is exactly the irrational stubbornness that gives the paradox its sting. The appearance of a paradox arises only when the legitimate dismissal of world-directed evidence is illicitly extended to the agent-directed kind. Most real-life "counterevidence" is of the latter sort, which is why the dogmatic conclusion feels so wrong.

Please comment on the following, in order of importance. First, does the world-directed/agent-directed distinction actually dissolve the paradox, or does it merely restrict its scope — can you construct a case of purely world-directed counterevidence whose advance dismissal still seems irrational? Second, is the proposal circular: does defining agent-directed evidence as "evidence about my reliability rather than about p" simply build in the conclusion that the dogmatic inference cannot reach it? Third, is the draft's concessive premise — that knowledge licenses ignoring world-directed counterevidence in advance — itself defensible, or does it already concede something epistemically objectionable? Finally, note any points where the argument is unclear or where a premise needs more support.
