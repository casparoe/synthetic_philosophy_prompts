Last month I served on a criminal jury for the first time, and in the same week my city council announced a citizens' assembly -- 40 residents chosen by lottery, meeting over several weekends to make recommendations on housing policy. What struck me is that nearly every advocate of such assemblies reaches for the same comparison: we already trust randomly selected ordinary people to decide whether someone goes to prison, so why not trust them to advise on rent control? The jury is doing real argumentative work here. It is not just a rhetorical flourish; it is the standard answer to the objection that a citizens' assembly is an unaccountable, unqualified body making decisions for people who never chose it. I have also seen the analogy run in the other direction: critics of sortition argue that juries work precisely because they are *not* miniature legislatures, and that borrowing the jury's legitimacy for assemblies is a kind of bait-and-switch.

So I would like a genuine audit of the analogy rather than a verdict for one side. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly -- what in the citizens' assembly plays the role of the jury pool, the voir dire, the judge's instructions, the adversarial presentation of evidence, the binary verdict, the unanimity or supermajority requirement, the fact that jurors cannot set their own agenda or choose which cases they hear. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and -- this is the part I care about most -- separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the intended use from the ones that are real but harmless. For instance: jurors answer questions of fact constrained by rules of evidence, while assembly members make value-laden recommendations on policy they may have helped frame; jury selection includes a process for striking biased jurors, while assembly lotteries rely on stratification and whoever accepts the invitation; jurors decide one case and disperse, while repeated assemblies may attract or create quasi-professional participants. Which of these breaks the load-bearing parts of the analogy, and which are cosmetic?

Finally, tell me whether the conclusion the analogy is used to support -- that a lottery-selected body deserves real (advisory or even binding) authority over policy -- survives once the significant disanalogies are on the table, or whether the jury comparison was smuggling in legitimacy the argument never earned. If there is a better analogy for what a citizens' assembly actually is, propose it and say what it illuminates and what it distorts. And please end by saying where your analysis is most likely to go wrong: which empirical or normative consideration, if it turned out differently than you assume, would most change your verdict -- for example, evidence about the quality of deliberation in real assemblies, or a different view about where democratic legitimacy comes from in the first place.
